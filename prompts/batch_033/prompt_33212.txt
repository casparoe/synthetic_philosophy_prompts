I keep noticing a pattern in moral reasoning that I can't quite put my finger on, and I'm wondering whether it has an established name in philosophy.

When asked later why they did it, they say something like "I determined that my action was morally permissible because it was consistent with an impartial principle that anyone could endorse." There's something about this answer that feels wrong — not because the action was wrong, but because the reasoning seems to misrepresent what was actually going on. The person didn't act from an impartial principle; they acted from love. And the fact that they reached for an impartial justification at all seems to suggest that something is missing from their relationship to the person they saved.

I've noticed this structure in other contexts too: someone acts from a deep personal commitment — a friendship, an artistic vocation, a religious conviction — and then, when pressed, offers a justification that translates the commitment into terms that would be acceptable to anyone, regardless of whether they share the commitment. The justification isn't false, exactly, but it seems to hollow out the very thing it's trying to defend. The original motivation didn't need that kind of justification, and the act of providing it somehow corrupts or displaces the motivation.

What I'm trying to isolate is the specific claim that, in certain cases, the very act of offering an impartial justification for a personal action is itself a kind of moral failure, even if the justification is sound and the action is right. The fact that the person felt the need to run the impartial calculation at all, rather than simply acting from the personal commitment, seems to indicate that the commitment is not what it should be.
