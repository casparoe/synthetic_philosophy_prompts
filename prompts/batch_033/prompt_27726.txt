I've been covering evolutionary biology for years and one thing that baffles me is the units of selection debate. You've got Dawkins and the gene-centrists on one side saying the gene is the real unit of selection — organisms are just vehicles, groups are epiphenomena. On the other side you've got the multilevel selection people, from Wynne-Edwards to D.S. Wilson, saying selection acts at multiple levels and the gene's-eye view is a useful bookkeeping trick but not the whole story. And then there's the paleontologists like Gould who want species selection in the mix too.

Here's what gets me: the math is the same. Everyone uses the Price equation. Everyone agrees on what happens in any given case. And yet this debate has been running since the sixties and nobody seems to have moved. My hunch is that this isn't really a biological disagreement at all — its a disagreement about what counts as an explanation. The gene-centrists think you've explained something when you've reduced it to the lowest level; the multilevel people think explanation can happen at any level where there's heritable variance in fitness. But I'm not sure that's right either.

What kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it verbal, is it about what causation means, is it two different research programs talking past each other? And if you had to say what would actually move it — a new formal result, a conceptual distinction everyone can agree on, or just waiting for one generation to retire — what would it be?
