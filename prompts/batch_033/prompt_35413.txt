My aunt died two weeks ago and I'm giving the eulogy. She was a philosopher of art and emotion — spent thirty years working on the paradox of fiction. You know the problem: why do we cry when Anna Karenina dies, or feel genuine dread during a horror movie, when we know perfectly well none of it is real? The standard line in the field, which she spent her career pushing back against, is that these are "quasi-emotions" — they feel like the real thing but they're not, because real emotions require beliefs about the world (like "this threat is actually here") and a readiness to act (like running away). She thought this was completley wrong. Her view was that the emotions we feel for fictions are entirely real, and the fact that we stay in our seats doesn't make the fear less genuine — it just means that engaging with fiction temporarily suspends certain action systems while leaving the feeling itself intact.

About half the people at the funeral will be her colleagues and grad students who know this debate cold. The other half are family — her sister, my cousins, her neighbors — who loved her but couldn't tell you what the paradox of fiction is. I want to honor her ideas without lecturing, and I want to comfort people without saying anything she would have rolled her eyes at. She had a low tolerance for sentimental sloppiness. But I also dont want to sound like I'm defending a thesis at her funeral. Can you write the eulogy? It should somehow let her actual philosophical commitments shape what gets said about grief, memory, and what it means to lose someone — and it should land for both halves of the room.
