A friend made this argument in a philosophy of computation seminar and I can't shake it. It goes:

(1) If an agent cannot, even in principle, follow a rule or procedure, then that rule can't genuinely govern or obligate them—ought implies can, roughly.
(2) For NP-hard problems, there is no known polynomial-time algorithm for every instance, and if P ≠ NP there can't be one.
(3) So a finite, bounded agent can't in general carry out the optimal procedure across all instances of an NP-hard problem.
(4) But lots of completely ordinary decisions—scheduling shifts, packing a truck, planning a route with constraints—are NP-hard optimization problems.
(5) Therefore, in those ordinary situations, agents are never rationally or morally required to optimize; at most they're required to satisfice, i.e., find some good-enough solution.
(6) And if ought implies can pushes us to satisficing there, then by parity it pushes us to satisficing for basically every interesting real-world decision, since nearly all of them have intractable structure somewhere.
(7) So normative requirements collapse into the demand to just do whatever is efficiently computable in the time you have, which seems absurd.

He swears the argument is valid. I can't find where it goes wrong, but the conclusion is too much. Which premise or step should I reject, and what do I give up by rejecting it? If I had to keep one thing, it'd be (1), but I don't know if that's compatible with the rest.
