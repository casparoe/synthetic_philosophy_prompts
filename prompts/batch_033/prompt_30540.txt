I've been working through Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* and reading LessWrong for a while now, and one phrase I keep tripping over is "jump out of the system"—sometimes people just say "JOOTS" as if it's a verb. The canonical reference is always the MU-puzzle: you start with MI, you've got your four rewrite rules, and no amount of churning inside the formal system gets you to MU; you need to step outside and notice something about I-counts and divisibility by three before you can see why it's impossible. Fine. But what I actually hear the slogan used to mean ranges from something narrow and obvious—"some formal questions can't be settled from inside the formalism"—to claims that strike me as much more ambitious, like "rationality can't evaluate its own standards," or "you can't critique an ideology from within it," or "there are problems that only some kind of non-rational leap can resolve."

I'd like you to lay out the distinct precise claims that "jump out of the system" could be expressing, ordered from the narrowest and most defensible to the broadest and most radical. For each reading, tell me: (1) whether the MU-puzzle actually demonstrates it, merely illustrates it, or doesn't touch it at all; (2) whether the claim is independently plausible regardless of what the puzzle shows; and (3) whether this is the reading that's doing the work when someone says—as I've seen more than once—something like "your framework can't evaluate its own criteria, so you need to JOOTS."

What I want is a menu of possible meanings with verdicts, not a single interpretation argued at essay length. And one more thing: if this whole JOOTS discourse turns out to be built on a misreading of what the MU-puzzle actually demonstrates, or if the slogan is so elastic that it collapses into near-vacuity once you try to pin it down, then please say that plainly rather than humoring me with a menu. I'd rather find out I'm chasing a confused idea than get a tidy answer to a bad question.
