Ok so I'm taking this course on global justice and we've been reading all this stuff about cosmopolitanism vs nationalism, like Singer on famine relief and duties to distant strangers, and then responses from people like David Miller or Yael Tamir who argue that we have special obligations to our compatriots. The thing is, outside of class I'm part of this student volunteer group that fundraises each semester and then votes on where to donate. This semester we raised about $3,000 and now we're totally stuck. Half the group wants to give it to a local homeless shelter that's literally a few blocks from campus—they're underfunded and we've actually volunteered there so we've seen the need up close. The other half wants to send the money to the Against Malaria Foundation, which is obviously super cost-effective and would probably save actual lives.

My friend Jake has been the loudest voice for the local option, and his argument basicly boils down to: "These are our neighbors, our community. We have a responsibility to take care of our own before we start trying to save the world." And honestly, when he puts it like that, it feels viscerally right? Like it would almost be a betrayal to send the money halfway across the world when there are people suffering right here. But then I think about what we've been reading in class, and it seems like from a moral point of view, a life is a life—the fact that someone happens to be born within the same arbitrary borders as me shouldn't make their suffering count more.

I guess my question is: how do I actually think through this? Is there a way to take seriously the intuition that we have special duties to people nearby or to our fellow citizens, without it just collapsing into some kind of tribalism? Or is that intuition just a bias I should try to overcome? I feel pulled in both directions and the philosophical arguments we've read feel too abstract to help me make an actual decision about three thousand dollars and a real homeless shelter.
