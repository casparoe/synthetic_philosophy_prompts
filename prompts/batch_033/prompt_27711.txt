I serve on my state's Law Reform Commission. We are reviewing the wrongful death statute, which currently limits recoverable damages to survivors' losses: loss of financial support, loss of companionship and guidance, funeral and burial expenses. A proposal before us would add a new category of compensation: damages for the decedent's own lost years — the life, experiences, and projects the deceased person would have had but for the wrongful death.

The deepest objection to the proposal is philosophical. The Epicurean argument holds that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, because there is no time at which the person exists and death is present — the harm, if there is one, has no subject at the time it supposedly occurs. If that argument is correct, then awarding damages for the decedent's own lost life is compensating someone for a loss they never actually suffered: it is a payment to the living dressed as redress for the dead. Several commissioners find this reasoning decisive; others regard it as a piece of ancient sophistry that no humane legal system should entertain.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend whether to adopt the lost-years provision, with a clear yes or no, and it should engage the Epicurean argument honestly — neither dismissing it as irrelevant nor accepting it as obviously dispositive. Address the strongest reason on the losing side. The other commissioners are judges, practicing attorneys, and law professors; they are not philosophers, but they are sophisticated readers who will notice if an argument is being dodged.
