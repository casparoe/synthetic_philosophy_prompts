The background is this. I take the simulation argument seriously: the idea that if a technologically mature civilisation could run very many detailed simulations of conscious minds, simulated minds would vastly outnumber non-simulated ones, and it would be surprising if we were not among the simulated. I am not asking you to assess the argument itself. For the purposes of this question, please treat it as establishing a substantial chance — say, between ten and thirty per cent — that we are living in a simulation.

What I am trying to work out is what this does to the claim that the far future should dominate my decisions. By "far future" I mean not the next few decades but the next thousands or millions of years, and the enormous number of people who could exist then. I have been attracted to what I have seen described as fanaticism about the far future: the claim that, because the far future could contain so much more of everything that matters, it should absorb most of my attention, money, and effort. I am trying to decide how much of that attention, money, and effort should go towards far-future concerns rather than near-future problems. The simulation idea seems to pull in several directions at once.

I can see at least these ways in which it might matter, and there may be others I have missed. One: if we are simulated, the simulation could be switched off or reset at any time, so the far future of our world is much less assured than it looks. Two: the far future inside a simulation might be computed only coarsely, or only as it is observed, so it might not be as real or as densely populated as it appears. Three: if simulated minds have less moral weight than non-simulated ones, then future simulated minds matter less — though this would seem to apply to everyone alive now as well. Four: the truly enormous future may lie in the base reality outside the simulation, where we cannot reach it from inside. Five: none of this may matter, because the expected number of future minds, simulated or not, is still so large that the far future keeps its claim on us.

I want you to go through the distinct ways in which the simulation argument might bear on how much I should prioritise the far future, rank them, and defend the ranking. For each consideration, I want to know whether it survives closer scrutiny, how large a shift in priorities it actually justifies, and what evidence or reasoning would make you move it up or down the ranking. Please do not give me references or a reading list; I am interested in your own reasoning.
