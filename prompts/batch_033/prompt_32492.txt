I'm in a graduate seminar on philosophical anthropology, and a fellow student keeps deploying "existence precedes essence" as a conversation-stopper. Last week, when someone raised the possibility that certain emotional dispositions — say, the capacity for shame or the impulse toward reciprocity — might be cross-culturally invariant and even innate, he replied: "That's exactly the kind of essentialism Sartre demolished. Existence precedes essence." When pressed, he seemed to mean something different each time: sometimes that there is no human nature, sometimes that biology places no constraints on us, sometimes just that we are responsible for what we make of ourselves. I suspect the slogan is doing more rhetorical work than philosophical work, but I want to be precise about why.

I would like you to lay out the distinct precise claims that "existence precedes essence" could be expressing — from the most modest and defensible to the most radical. For each, say whether it is plausible, whether Sartre himself would have endorsed it (engage with the texts, not with the slogan's cultural afterlife), and whether it actually supports the move my classmate was making: dismissing the claim that there might be cross-culturally invariant emotional dispositions. I want to walk into next week's session with a clear menu of readings so that I can ask him which one he is asserting and whether it does the work he thinks it does.
