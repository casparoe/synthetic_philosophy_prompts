I'm a cog sci phd student and our lab has been having the same fight for months about the color phi phenomenon. You know the setup: a red dot flashes on the left, then a green dot flashes on the right, with the right timing, and people report a single dot moving across and switching from red to green about halfway along the path. The fight is over when the color change got into the subject's experience. The Stalinesque camp says the brain delays or edits the show before it airs, so the subject never consciously experienced the uncorrected red-then-green sequence; the Orwellian camp says the subject did consciously experience the dots as they came in, and the moving-dot-with-midway-color-change report is a later memory revision, after the fact.

We've realized weve mostly been talking past each other, so we want to write an adversarial collaboration document. Draft it for us. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table. In the agreed claims, put everything a commited Orwellian and a commited Stalinesque can both sign about the phenomenon itself, about what needs explaining, and about which apparent differences are merely verbal. The agreed claims should be substantive, not just truisms. Then in the table, give the precise residual disagreement, with each side's own formulation phrased so that side would actually sign it, not a strawman. Then list the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, propose a concrete test, case, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it — think probes or interruptions at different latencies, masking one of the dots, TMS, EEG, that sort of thing — and write down each side's prediction in advance. Don't adjudicate who's right; the point is a document both camps could put their names to. If some part of the dispute dissolves on inspection, say which part and why.
