I'm a retired biologist — spent four decades in sensory neurobiology, mostly on nociception with the odd stint on the insular cortex — and I've recently started reading philosophy of mind, which feels like moving from a bench with very exact instruments into a room full of fog. A friend pressed Grahek's book on pain asymbolia into my hands, and I've been down the rabbit hole ever since. For anyone who hasn't met the case: pain asymbolia is a condition, usually following damage to the insula or anterior cingulate, where patients can still detect noxious stimuli, tell you the pain is there, rate it's intensity, point to its location, but they just don't mind it. No wincing, no withdrawal, no distress; some apparently smile while the neurologist probes them with a pin.

Now here's the philosophical fork that's keeping me up. One reading says the condition shows that pain's sensory-discriminative side (the "what" and "where") and its affective-motivational side (the "ouch", the part that makes it bad) come apart — so the badness of pain isn't built into the sensation itself but is a separable, contingent response that could in principle be peeled away. The rival reading says: not so fast. The asymbolic isnt having a full pain experience with the badness subtracted; the badness is part of what makes pain pain, so when you take it away you've removed something essential, and the case therefore doesn't show that normal pain's badness is detachable.

I'd like you to compare those two positions and then rank them, along with any third or fourth interpretation you think deserves a seat at the table, by how well they handle the phenomenology — what it's actually like to be in pain, and what the asymbolic's experience might be like. Give me a tier list from most to least compelling, with a sentence or two of justification for each placement. Please also say how confident you are in the ranking overall and single out the point you're least sure of. And if you could write in British English, that would suit an old habit from my postdoc years in London.
