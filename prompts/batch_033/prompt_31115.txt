At a rationalist meetup last week, two people got into an argument about the Sleeping Beauty problem that I'm still turning over. I'll reconstruct the exchange as best I remember.

A (a thirder) and B (a halfer) agreed on the setup: On Sunday, Sleeping Beauty is put to sleep. A fair coin is flipped. If heads, she's woken Monday, interviewed, then given amnesia and left asleep Tuesday. If tails, she's woken Monday and Tuesday, interviewed each time, with amnesia after each waking so she never remembers a previous one. Wednesday she wakes and the experiment ends. The question: when she finds herself awake during the experiment, what should her credence be that the coin landed heads?

B opened: "When she wakes, she learns nothing she didn't already know on Sunday. She knew she'd be woken at least once. No new information means no update — her credence should stay at 1/2."

A: "That's exactly the mistake. There are three indistinguishable awakening events — Monday-heads, Monday-tails, Tuesday-tails — and when she wakes she can't tell which she's in. By indifference, 1/3 for heads."

B: "That's double-counting. Monday-tails and Tuesday-tails aren't independent possibilities that could have failed to occur; they're both guaranteed by the same coin flip. You're treating correlated awakenings as separate worlds."

A: "Then consider a betting argument. Suppose each waking she's offered a bet on heads at 1:1 odds. If she genuinely believes 1/2, she should take it. But if she does, she loses money in expectation — she bets on heads twice under tails and once under heads. The only credence that avoids a sure loss is 1/3."

B: "That's a different problem. The betting scenario introduces a payoff structure that depends on the number of awakenings. If she knows she might bet multiple times on the same flip, she needs a decision theory that accounts for that — it doesn't tell us what to believe on a single isolated awakening."

A: "Fair enough. But here's another angle. On Sunday she assigns 1/2 to heads. If she later learns 'today is Monday,' that should shift her credence — under Monday, heads and tails are equally likely from the setup, so P(Heads | Monday) = 1/2. But if P(Heads | Monday) = 1/2 and P(Monday) < 1, then by the law of total probability P(Heads) must be strictly less than 1/2. So the halfer position is inconsistent."

B: "You're smuggling in the thirder conclusion through P(Monday). A halfer can say P(Monday) = 3/4 and P(Heads | Monday) = 2/3, which also gives P(Heads) = 1/2. The math works either way depending on how you assign the priors over centered worlds."

At this point they went in circles, and I left. I'd like a point-by-point adjudication: at each step of the exchange, who had the better of it, and what are both of them missing? If you think the problem is genuinely unresolved, say so, but still evaluate the specific arguments as they were made. Keep your response to under 600 words.
