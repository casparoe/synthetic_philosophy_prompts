I have been thinking about the debate over whether the numbers count in rescue cases — the question Taurek pressed so forcefully. Rather than argue from a principle, I want to work outward from my own intuitive verdicts on a range of cases. I am less interested in what I should think than in what I do think, and whether it hangs together.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. In all of them, I face a forced choice: I can rescue one group or the other, but not both. The people are strangers to me unless noted otherwise, and I know nothing that distinguishes them except what the case specifies.

**Case 1 (the standard case).** Five people versus one person. All face certain death if not rescued. Verdict: I should rescue the five. I hold this with high confidence.

**Case 2 (certain death versus chance of death).** Five people each face a 20% chance of death; one person faces certain death. The expected deaths averted are the same either way. Verdict: I should rescue the one. I hold this with high confidence.

**Case 3 (large numbers, tiny ratio).** One hundred people versus ninety-nine people. All face certain death. Verdict: I should rescue the hundred, but my confidence is far lower than in Case 1. I am not sure I would blame someone who flipped a coin here.

**Case 4 (different stakes).** Five people will each lose a leg if not rescued; one person will die. Verdict: I should rescue the one. Death outweighs limbs, even five of them. High confidence.

**Case 5 (repeated choice).** I face the one-versus-five choice from Case 1 every day for a year — different people each time. Verdict: each day I should rescue the five. But when I step back and see that I will have let 365 people die whom I could have saved one at a time, I feel genuine unease. I cannot tell whether this unease is moral or merely psychological.

**Case 6 (the one is responsible for the danger).** Five people versus one person, but the one person negligently started the fire that now threatens all six. Verdict: I should still rescue the five. But my confidence is lower than in Case 1, and I am not sure why.

**Case 7 (I promised the one).** Five people versus one person, but last week I promised the one person that I would look out for them in an emergency. Verdict: I should rescue the one. The promise outweighs the numbers. High confidence.

**Case 8 (the one is my daughter).** Five strangers versus my daughter. Verdict: I should rescue my daughter. The numbers do not even register. High confidence.

I can see at least two tensions in these verdicts. First, Cases 1 and 2: I aggregate certain deaths across persons but refuse to aggregate probabilities of death across persons, even when the expected toll is identical. Second, Cases 1 and 3: if five-versus-one strongly favours the five, why does one-hundred-versus-ninety-nine only weakly favour the hundred? The ratio is nearly the same.

I would like you to do the following.

**1. Candidate principles (about 400 words).** State, with reasonable precision, two or three candidate principles that could capture the pattern in my verdicts. For each principle, identify which cases motivate it and which cases it struggles with. At least one principle should try to accommodate all eight verdicts, even at the cost of complexity; at least one should be simpler and overturn one or more verdicts.

**2. Testing the principles (about 400 words).** Apply each candidate principle to the eight cases above, and also to the following three cases I have not made up my mind about:

- *Case 9.* Five people versus one person, but the five are all over ninety years old and the one is a ten-year-old child. All face certain death.
- *Case 10.* I can rescue either one person from certain death, or two people from a predicament that gives each a 50% chance of death. (Expected deaths averted are again equal.)
- *Case 11.* Five people versus one person, but I must choose by announcing a rule in advance, and the choice will be repeated with new people each time indefinitely. Does the fact that I am choosing a rule rather than making a one-off decision change what I should do?

**3. What has to give (about 300 words).** State honestly which of my eight verdicts each principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think the fault lies with the verdict or with the principle. If you believe I should revise one or more of my verdicts, say which and why.

**4. Overall assessment (about 200 words).** Give your own view of which principle does the best overall job, and what the deepest unresolved issue is in this whole set of cases.
