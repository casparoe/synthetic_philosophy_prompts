Okay so I've gone down the Taurek rabbit hole and I think I'm convinced. The basic picture: when you're choosing between saving one person and saving five, there's no entity called "the five" that experiences five deaths. Each of the five has a claim on you, sure, but those claims don't add up to something weightier than the one's claim. You can permissibly flip a coin. The numbers, in themselves, don't count.

I know this is a minority view and I know it makes people viscerally uncomfortable. But I want to know, as clearly as possible, what would actually count against it. Not just "here's why you're wrong" but: what would the world have to look like, or what would our moral concepts have to look like, for Taurek's view to fail? What cases or commitments does it strain on? Which of its implications are genuinely unacceptable and which are just suprising? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know whether the view has been insulated in a way that makes it unfalsifiable — and whether that even matters for a view in normative ethics.
