I'm working on a short piece about Herbert Simon's idea of satisficing, and I want a thought experiment that shows something non-obvious: a satisficing rule beating a straightforward maximizing rule even when the two agents spend the same amount on search. Please construct a decision problem to the following specifications. The setting should be ordinary — hiring is fine — with two agents facing the same pool of candidates and the same noisy screening signal. Each candidate has a true quality, and the signal an agent observes is true quality plus symmetric, mean-zero noise, so a higher signal is evidence of higher quality but is partly luck. Both agents pay the same cost per candidate screened. The satisficer sets a fixed aspiration level (a signal threshold) and hires the first candidate whose signal meets or exceeds it; the pool is large enough that the satisficer's expected number of screens is the reciprocal of the probability of passing the threshold. The optimizer screens a fixed number n of candidates and hires the one with the highest signal, which is the best expected-value use of those n observations. Choose the threshold so that the satisficer's expected number of screens equals the optimizer's n, so neither agent buys more information than the other in expectation. I want the satisficer to end up with strictly higher expected true quality than the optimizer, and I want the advantage to come from a general statistical feature of the setup, not from the aspiration level being secretly tuned to some particular candidate's true quality and not from the optimizer mishandling the observations it gathers.

After you present the case, do the following. State precisely what each rule does. Explain, in terms of the difference between the best draw from a fixed batch and the conditional mean of a threshold-exceeding draw, why the satisficer's accepted candidates carry a higher expected signal, and why the signal-to-quality mapping in your case turns that into higher expected true quality despite the equal expected search cost. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work, and say what happens if that feature is removed — for instance, if the signal became noiseless. Then tell me how a defender of maximizing would best attack the case: in particular, whether the defender would say that a sophisticated optimizer should have used a stopping rule rather than a fixed sample, and whether that reply saves maximizing or quietly concedes the satisficer's point. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the verdict — which condition, if weakened or removed, would make the optimizer win — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write the answer in American English.
