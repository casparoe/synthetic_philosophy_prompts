I'm taking a course on moral epistemology this term, and in our discussion of moral testimony last week a student shut down the conversation by saying, "You can't just take someone else's word for it on moral questions." The claim was that moral testimony is somehow defective in a way that testimony about ordinary empirical matters is not, and that this slogan captures the difference. But I've been turning it over since then and I can't pin down what precise thesis it's actually asserting. It might be a psychological claim about how people in fact form moral beliefs, or a normative claim about how they ought to, or an epistemic claim about whether testimony can transmit moral knowledge or justification, or a claim about the special role of understanding in the moral domain, or something about the relationship between moral judgment and agency. And depending on which reading you pick, the slogan either rules out moral testimony entirely, rules out only some kinds of deference, or collapses into something true but uninteresting that doesn't distinguish moral testimony from testimony about anything else. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this slogan could be making, from the most modest to the most ambitious, and say for each whether it's independently plausible and whether it actually supports the conclusion that moral testimony is epistemically defective in a way that ordinary testimony is not? I want to bring a structured analysis to the next session rather than just arguing past people who might have a different reading in mind than I do.
