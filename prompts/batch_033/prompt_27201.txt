In my graduate seminar on pain and affect, I have been trying to determine exactly what the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia establishes about the badness of pain. The following argument keeps forcing itself on me, and its conclusion seems to prove too much, but I cannot identify the step at which it goes wrong.

(1) Subjects with pain asymbolia report that they feel pain: they can locate the stimulus, describe its sharp or burning character, and rate its intensity. Yet they deny that it hurts or bothers them, and they do not wince, withdraw, or seek relief.
(2) If a subject can undergo the same pain sensation as an ordinary subject while not feeling bad at all, then the felt badness of that pain is not a property of the pain sensation itself; it is something the subject's psychology contributes in addition to the sensation.
(3) The asymbolic subject does undergo the same pain sensation as the ordinary subject; what is missing is only the additional element.
(4) Therefore the felt badness of pain is not a property of the pain sensation itself but a further evaluative or motivational response to it.
(5) Nor is there reason to treat pain as special. For any valenced experience, the natural ground for taking its valence to be intrinsic to the sensation was that the valence seemed inseparable from it; asymbolia shows that exactly this kind of apparent inseparability can come apart. By parity, then, the pleasantness of pleasure, the awfulness of nausea, and the rest are responses added to the sensations rather than properties of them.
(6) But that is too much to accept. It would mean that no felt quality is itself good or bad, that the felt badness of agony consists solely in the subject's response to it, and that a subject who simply ceased to mind their own agony would no longer be in a bad state. The badness of pain would disappear precisely in the cases where it matters most.

So the argument must fail somewhere. Which step do you reject, and what is the strongest case for retaining that step despite the pressure? And what does rejecting it commit you to about the asymbolic's report that they feel pain but are not bothered by it — in particular, must we say that the asymbolic is wrong about what they feel, or that they feel a different state, or that ordinary pain's badness is not what we took it to be?
