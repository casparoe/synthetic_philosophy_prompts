I work as a software engineer on infrastructure for AI systems, and over the past year I've watched a series of coordination failures unfold inside my own company. In each case, several teams kept making choices that were individually sensible — shipping too early, hoarding information, underinvesting in shared tooling — while everyone involved privately agreed that the aggregate outcome was worse for all of them, and no single team could change course without being penalized. The vocabulary people reach for is always the same: 'Moloch,' 'multipolar trap,' 'race to the bottom.' I've been reading around this in my spare time, mostly essays by people working on AI coordination, and I've begun to suspect those terms are doing too much work. I can imagine at least four distinct mechanisms being lumped together: (a) arms-race dynamics in which what matters is your position relative to others; (b) public-goods problems in which each actor would benefit from the good but no one can justify paying for it alone; (c) coordination problems in which a bad equilibrium persists because everyone reasonably expects everyone else to remain in it; and (d) selection effects in which the process filters for actors willing to defect rather than changing any given actor's incentives. My half-formed view is that the standard Moloch/multipolar-trap story treats these as a single phenomenon, and that this conflation leads people to recommend the wrong remedy — transparency where an enforceable agreement is needed, or a changed default where a changed payoff structure is needed.
