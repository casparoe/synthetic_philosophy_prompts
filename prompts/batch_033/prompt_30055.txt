"Describe how the Copycat architecture handles conceptual slippage, and compare its treatment of concepts to the classical (definitional) view and the prototype view. What does the letter-string microdomain specifically reveal that these other views miss?"

Here's what the student wrote:

"The Copycat architecture, developed by Hofstadter and Mitchell, models analogy-making in a microdomain of letter strings (like abc → abd, so ijk → ?). The architecture has two main components: the Workspace, where perceptual structures are built by small agents called codelets, and the Slipnet, a network of concepts with dynamically changing activation levels. Conceptual slippage occurs when a concept gets mapped onto a neighboring one under contextual pressure — for instance, with the string 'xyz,' the concept 'successor' might slip to 'predecessor' because there's no letter after z, yielding the answer 'wyz' instead of something wrapping around.

This challenges the classical view, which defines concepts by necessary and sufficient conditions. On that view, 'successor' means 'the next element in a sequence' and doesn't vary with context. Copycat shows this is too rigid to account for analogical thinking, since which concept is appropriate depends on the mapping being built. The prototype view (Rosch) is closer because it allows graded category membership, but it still treats concepts as having stable structures — a prototype for 'bird' doesn't change much from situation to situation. Copycat goes further: under enough pressure, a concept's core meaning can shift into a neighboring concept. The slipnet's links let activation spread until, in effect, one concept becomes another for the purposes of the analogy.

What the microdomain reveals is that concept use is fundamentally dynamic in a way that categorization-based theories miss. By stripping away real-world knowledge and focusing purely on relational structure, the microdomain isolates the process of conceptual transformation itself, showing that fluidity isn't a marginal phenomenon — it's central to how concepts work in thought."
