I've been thinking lately about a small game I once read about, called the dollar auction, where people bid for a dollar bill under the rule that the top two bidders both have to pay their last bid but only the winner gets the dollar. The unsettling part is that reasonable people can get trapped: once you have put in a lot, dropping out means paying for nothing, so you bid a little more to avoid that loss, the other person does the same, and the whole thing spirals. I have been rereading Macbeth with this in mind. Macbeth starts by murdering Duncan to become king, and after that each new killing looks like an attempt to make the earlier ones stick — Banquo, then Macduff's family. At one point Macbeth says something like he is so far in blood that going back would be just as hard as going forward, which struck me as almost a textbook description of the sunk-cost trap.

I would like a reading of Macbeth through the lens of the dollar auction and escalation of commitment: where the play dramatizes the trap, where the idea illuminates a scene or a choice, where the play resists the reading, and what the play shows about the trap that the bare game does not capture. In particular, I am interested in whether Macbeth is best understood as a reasonable person gradually caught by sunk costs, or whether the play gives him moments where he could have stepped back and chose not to — and whether the witches and Lady Macbeth break the analogy, since in the dollar auction the escalation is supposed to come from the structure of the game, not from outside pressure or prophecy. I am leading a discussion group on the play and want to use the dollar auction as a way in, but I do not want to force it where it does not belong.

Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
