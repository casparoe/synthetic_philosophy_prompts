Two friends of mine, Maya and Noah, got into an argument after a seminar on anthropic reasoning, and neither would concede. I was listening and couldn't sort out who was right at each step. Here is the core of what they said.

(1) Maya said the self-sampling assumption (SSA) is what drives the doomsday argument: if you treat yourself as a random draw from all humans who will ever live, then hypotheses on which humanity ends soon get a huge boost, which she took as a sign that something has gone wrong. The self-indication assumption (SIA) avoids this. If a theory says there are more observers, there are more slots for you to have been, so your existence is evidence for more observers — and that exactly cancels the doomsday effect.

(2) Noah replied that SIA buys that cancellation at an absurd price. Take two theories with equal prior that make identical predictions about everything we will ever observe, except that T1 says Earth is the only intelligent life and T2 says there are also 10^30 observers in other galaxies. SIA says T2 is 10^30 times more likely, so you would be near-certain that aliens exist without ever looking through a telescope. He said this is the presumptuous philosopher, and it shows SIA treating a non-observation as evidence.

(3) Maya answered that her own existence is not a non-observation. T2 does not just predict telescope readings; it predicts her, vastly more strongly, because it has vastly more observer-slots any one of which she could have been. She said that if this commits her to believing in aliens, so be it — the word presumptuous just expresses discomfort with where the reasoning leads.

(4) Noah said that reply shows SIA has no principled stopping point. If observers in other galaxies count, why not merely possible people who were never born, or copies in other branches of the wavefunction, or infinitely many observers in a multiverse? He said SIA either needs an arbitrary reference class, at which point it is just SSA with a gerrymandered prior, or it forces you to count possibilities so vast or ill-defined that the update becomes arbitrary.

(5) Maya said SSA has the same problem: you still have to decide whether to count future humans, chimpanzees, or your own observer-moments, and the strength of the doomsday argument depends entirely on that choice. So she did not see the reference-class problem as a point in SSA's favor.
