I'm working on a paper about bounded rationality and I've tied myself in knots. Here's where I've ended up:

(1) Rationality for creatures like us is bounded rationality. The right normative standard is satisficing — set an aspiration level, search until you find an option that meets it, and stop. Maximizing expected utility is a non-starter for real agents.

(2) But aspiration levels shouldn't be arbitrary. You should calibrate them based on the cost of further search and the likely distribution of option quality in the domain. Searching every coffee shop in the city for a marginally better latte is dumb.

(3) If you knew a strictly better option was available for negligable additional search cost, you'd be irrational not to take it. Satisficing doesn't mean settling.

(4) For an ideal agent with unlimited time and cognition, maximizing expected utility is the correct norm.

(5) Here's the bit I'm uneasy about: I don't think satisficing is just "maximizing subject to computational constraints" — a second-best approximation of ideal rationality. I think it's a genuinely different kind of norm, the kind that applies to bounded creatures.

(6) Rationality is about responding to reasons. Your cognitive and temporal limits are themselves reasons that bear on what to do.

I can feel (2), (3), and (5) pulling against each other but I can't quite articulate it, and I'm worried I've smuggled maximizing back in through (2) and (3) while (5) explicitly kicks it out. Are (4) and (5) also in tension or does that dissolve? Rank the problems by how serious they are and tell me what I'd have to give up.
