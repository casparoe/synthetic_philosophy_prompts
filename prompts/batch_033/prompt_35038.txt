I'm working on a dissertation chapter about the limits of markets, and before I go further into the literature I want to get clear on what an adequate theory of market boundaries would actually have to deliver. I do not mean a list of the usual cases people argue about; I mean the structural requirements. Write the desiderata: what any theory of when a market is impermissible or objectionable must get right — which cases it must handle, which distinctions it cannot collapse, which mechanisms it must account for, and which results from neighbouring fields it must be consistent with. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then flag any pair, or larger subset, of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied by a single theory, and say why. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the major existing views, and say what would change your mind about that judgement.

Some specifics I want you to address. First, a theory in this area has to do more than identify harmful transactions: it must explain why some transactions are objectionable even when all parties benefit by their own lights and consent is genuine. The kidney-seller and the post-disaster price-gouging cases are the obvious test here, but I also want you to consider whether the theory handles the distinction between what is objectionable and what ought to be legally prohibited — a theory that conflates the two is not doing the same job, and a theory that cannot explain why some objectionable markets should nonetheless be legal is missing something. Second, the theory must explain why some goods seem to be corrupted or degraded by market exchange in a way that others are not; the contrast between commercial surrogacy and paid childcare, or between blood donation and plasma markets, needs to be tractable within the theory even if the theory ultimately judges the distinction morally irrelevant. If the theory says all goods are commensurable, it must explain the persistent intuition otherwise without merely dismissing it as a mistake. Third, it must account for motivational crowding-out: the empirical finding that introducing market incentives can undermine non-market motivations, but not always and not for all goods. A theory that simply posits that markets corrupt without specifying the mechanism and the conditions under which it operates is not adequate.

I also want you to consider whether the theory must distinguish between the claim that a market is wrong because of what is sold and the claim that it is wrong because of who buys and sells under what background conditions — and whether a theory that draws that distinction sharply can still explain the cases where the two interact, as in markets for pollution permits or carbon offsets. And I want a requirement about the relationship between the theory's normative claims and the empirical evidence: what kind of evidence should count for or against the theory, and what would falsify it?

Be explicit about where you think the desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and where they reflect a particular theoretical commitment that a rival could reasonably reject. And, as I said, tell me what would change your mind about the overall list — which argument or consideration would lead you to restructure the desiderata, or to conclude that one of them is not a genuine requirement after all.
