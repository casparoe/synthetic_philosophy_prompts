I have just come from a long argument with a friend who maintains that Kierkegaard's attack on the daily press and on the category of the public is, at bottom, aristocratic and anti-democratic disdain, and that any attempt to put it to contemporary use merely launders that disdain. The exchange made me realise I have been preparing to defend my paper against the wrong interlocutor, and that my actual audience will press a subtler version of the same charge.

Next month I am presenting a seminar paper whose thesis is this: Kierkegaard's account of the public and the press is best read as a structural diagnosis of a particular kind of mediated sociality — one in which opinion circulates without being owned by anyone, reflection substitutes for committed action, and levelling proceeds through envy rather than through any explicit political programme — rather than as a complaint about democratic politics as such. So read, I argue, it illuminates certain features of contemporary algorithmically mediated public discourse, even though Kierkegaard himself held no democratic sympathies and even though the comparison with the present must be drawn with care.

The paper proceeds in three moves. First, it separates the empirical claim about what a certain medium does to the conditions of conviction from the normative-theological claim about the single individual. Second, it argues that the empirical claim is detachable from its theological frame and identifies a genuine mechanism. Third, it argues that this mechanism belongs to a communication regime rather than to popular government, which is why it can reappear under very different political arrangements.

The audience is a political theory workshop. Most are normative democratic theorists; several are historians of political thought of a contextualist bent. None are Kierkegaard specialists. Two work on deliberative democracy and are disposed to treat the public sphere as a normative achievement of modernity rather than as a pathology. One is a historian of nineteenth-century Europe who will be alert to any anachronism. They are not media theorists, and they will not object in the vocabulary of attention economics, filter bubbles, or epistemic vice; they will object in the vocabulary of democratic legitimacy, citizenship, the relation between the social and the political, and the dangers of anti-politics.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I am actually likely to get from this audience, ordered by how likely they are to be raised and how much damage they would do to the paper, each phrased the way a democratic theorist or a contextualist historian would actually phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than try to defend, and which point of my own I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. You may assume I know the text itself; what I need is not an introduction to Kierkegaard but a map of where the argument will be attacked. Please write the answer in British English.
