Two people in my decision theory group got really into the absent-minded driver problem the other day, and I still can't tell which of them is actually right. The setup is the usual one: two identical-looking intersections on the route, exiting at the first is worth 0, exiting at the second is worth 4, continuing past both is worth 1, and the driver can't distinguish them or remember whether he's already passed one.

Maya started by saying the only sensible solution is to continue with probability 2/3 at each intersection. Her reasoning: if the driver picks any probability p of continuing whenever he sees an intersection, his expected payoff is p²·1 + p(1−p)·4, which is 4p − 3p², and that maxes out at p = 2/3. So the driver should just decide to flip a biased coin in his head at each exit: 2/3 keep going, 1/3 exit.

Sam said that's a planning-stage answer, not an action-stage answer. Once the driver is actually at an intersection, he has information: he's at *some* intersection. If he planned to continue with probability p, then he definitely reaches the first intersection but only reaches the second with probability p, so the conditional probability that he's at the first is 1/(1+p) and that he's at the second is p/(1+p). At p=2/3 that's 3/5 and 2/5. Sam then said you should maximize expected payoff conditional on being at an intersection, not the unconditional payoff from before the trip. Plugging those probabilities in gives a different function, and its maximum is around p ≈ 0.528, not 2/3. So he said the driver who actually reaches an intersection should not stick with 2/3: the plan is not action-stable.

Maya came back saying Sam's action-stage calculation is making a weird assumption. At the second intersection, conditional on being there, continuing is just clearly worse than exiting; at the first intersection, continuing is clearly better than exiting. The reason you don't simply do the obvious thing at each is that you can't know which is which, and the same p must govern both. But Sam's own conditional probabilities show the driver should think he's more likely at the first, and the first intersection is where continuing has value. Still, she said, this doesn't get him to 2/3 from behind the wheel, because once at the second intersection, the past is already gone. She wasn't sure whether to call this a real inconsistency or just a difference between two legitimate viewpoints.

Sam replied that Maya's last sentence is the problem: if the action-stage optimum is different from the planning-stage optimum, then the driver knows he'll want to change the policy once he reaches an intersection, so a rational driver can't even coherently plan to use 2/3. If he knows he will deviate, the plan is not a plan. Maya said that's circular because Sam's action-stage calculation assumed the driver's future behavior would follow the policy p in the odds he used to update; once you let the current choice influence your odds, the whole calculation needs to be a fixed point, and 2/3 might still be the fixed point. Then they went around in circles.

They asked me to referee. Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about what—especially whether there really is an inconsistency between the planning and action stages, whether Sam's conditional-probability update is correct, and what, if anything, they are both missing? And please write your answer in American English.
