I'm a high school student enrolled in a summer philosophy of mind seminar at a nearby university. The course has covered several theories of mental representation, and we've spent the last two weeks on the language of thought hypothesis — Fodor's claim that cognition operates over syntactically structured mental representations that compose according to rules, forming something like an internal "Mentalese." We've read selections from Fodor's *The Language of Thought* and *Psychosemantics*, as well as critical responses: the regress objection (if Mentalese symbols require interpretation, who or what does the interpreting?), the challenge from connectionist networks that process information without explicit sentence-like representations, and the question of whether LOTH is an empirical hypothesis about cognitive architecture or merely a façon de parler that redescribes the very phenomenon it's supposed to explain.

For our final project, the instructor has asked each student to design one essay question for the course exam, complete with a model answer and a marking scheme. The rule is that the question must be unanswerable by simply regurgitating the lecture notes — it has to force the examinee to confront a live tension in the material and show that they can reason through it rather than just report positions.

I want my question to center on the regress objection, because it strikes me as the most philosophically interesting challenge to LOTH and because I've noticed that weaker students tend to dismiss it too quickly while stronger students see that answering it requires clarifying what LOTH is and isn't committed to. Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A-level answer from a B-level answer by naming the specific argumentative moves each must contain. The marking scheme should also identify common mistakes that would cost marks, with a brief note on why each is a mistake.
