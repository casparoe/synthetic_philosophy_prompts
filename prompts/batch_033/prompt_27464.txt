I'm a PhD student in proof theory, and I recently came across this passage in an introductory book on philosophical logic that's been recommended to undergraduates in our department:

"Intuitionistic logic is what you get when you reject the law of excluded middle. Brouwer and his followers held that mathematical truth consists in mental construction: to assert a proposition is to claim you have a proof of it. So you can't assert 'P or not-P' unless you've actually constructed a proof of P or a proof of its negation, which you often haven't. This makes every proof in intuitionistic logic constructive — you can always extract an algorithm from it — which is why computer scientists adopted it as the logical backbone of type theory and verified programming. The price is steep: proof by contradiction and double negation elimination are gone, so many classical theorems, like the intermediate value theorem and the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, simply become false. Intuitionistic logic also forces you to reject the axiom of choice, since you can't just 'choose' an element from each set without a rule that picks it out."

Something about this feels off to me, but I work on the syntactic side of proof theory — cut elimination, focusing, polarities — and haven't engaged much with the philosophical or constructive side. I'd like to know: how fair is this as a summary? Which claims would a card-carrying intuitionist or a constructive mathematician accept, which are subtly wrong or conflate distinct issues, and which are just false? I'm especially curious about the claims about the axiom of choice and about classical theorems "becoming false" — those seem like they might be overstatements, but I'm not sure.
