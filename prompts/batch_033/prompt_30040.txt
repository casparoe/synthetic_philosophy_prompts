So there's this thing I keep running into, and I can't tell if it's a named idea in philosophy or game theory or what. I'm hoping you can tell me — and I'd rather you take a definite position on what it is, even if there's some controversy, than just lay out five possibilities and leave me to sort through them.

A few years back a friend of mine sent around an email to about fifteen of us. The email said: reply to this thread within 24 hours. If exactly one person replies, that person gets a hundred bucks (he was going to venmo them). If zero people reply, or more than one, nobody gets anything. He called it a "social experiment." I sat there staring at the email and my first thought was: well, if I reply, then probably a bunch of other people will too, because they're all sitting there having the same thought process I'm having. We're all the same kind of person, we all got the same email at the same time, none of us has any special reason to think we're the unique reply-guy. So if I reply, I should expect a pile-on. So I shouldn't reply. But then I thought: wait, everyone else is going to follow exactly that reasoning too, which means nobody replies, which means I actually *should* reply because then I'd be the only one. But then everyone would think *that* too... and it spirals.

What struck me afterward wasn't the paradox itself — I know that's basically just a multiplayer version of those "guess 2/3 of the average" type games. What struck me was the *move* I kept making: I kept assuming that whatever reasoning I did, the others would do too, because we were all in symmetric positions. And that move seemed to short-circuit the infinite regress of "I think that they think that I think..." And then I started noticing myself making that same move in totally mundane situations. Like, I'm in a meeting and there's an awkward silence after the chair asks for questions. I have a question but I don't want to be the only one. But then I think: everyone else is probably thinking the same thing, so if I don't ask, nobody will. But if I do ask, maybe that gives others permission and then I'm not the only one. Or: I'm driving and I see a mattress in the middle of the highway. I could call the non-emergency line, but surely someone else already has. But then I think: everyone who sees it is thinking "surely someone else already has," so actually maybe nobody has called. The move is the same each time: I treat my own reasoning as a sample of what everyone in my position will do, and then I pick the action whose universalization gives the best outcome.

This feels adjacent to Kant's categorical imperative, but it's not really moral — it's more like a prediction engine. And it feels adjacent to Schelling points and common knowledge, but it's not quite that either, because it's about the reasoning process itself being shared, not just the information everyone has. Is there a name for this specific style of reasoning? And — this is what I really want to know — when does it actually work? Because in the email game it seems to lead to a paradox, but in the highway-mattress case it seems genuinly useful. What are the conditions that make the difference?
