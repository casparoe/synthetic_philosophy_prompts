I'm working through some issues in epistemology and I'd like a consistency check on the following set of views I find myself holding. I'm not sure where the tensions are, if any, and I'd rather not signal which ones I'd be willing to give up.

Here they are:

(1) Perceptual beliefs are prima facie justified. You do not need independent evidence that your vision is reliable before you can justifiably believe, on the basis of looking, that there is a cup on the table.

(2) Justification is closed under competent deduction. If I am justified in believing P, and I competently deduce Q from P, then I am justified in believing Q.

(3) The following reasoning pattern should not be sufficient to justify the belief that one's visual faculties are reliable: look at an object, form the perceptual belief that it is red, note that it is indeed red, and conclude that your vision got it right on this occasion. Repeat this many times over many objects, and you still have not earned justification for the general claim that your vision is reliable. Call this the anti-bootstrapping intuition.

(4) We do, in fact, have justification for believing that our perceptual faculties are generally reliable.

(5) I can justifiably believe, via introspection, that I am having a visual experience as of something red, without needing independent evidence that introspection itself is reliable.

Please go through these and identify which pairs or subsets are in tension, rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give to resolve it. Please also flag any apparent tensions that are not real on closer inspection. I want the response in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just write it as you would an email.
