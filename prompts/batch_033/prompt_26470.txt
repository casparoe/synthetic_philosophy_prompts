I'm a third-year undergraduate currently taking a seminar on two-dimensional semantics, and for my final project I need to conduct a simulated in-depth interview with a philosopher who defends the two-dimensional framework (the kind of view developed by Chalmers and Jackson in the 1990s and 2000s). The interviewee is not a specific real person -- I'm allowed to treat them as an articulate, good-faith defender of the view who answers questions in the spirit of the published literature.

I need to draft eight to ten interview questions, each with an anticipated follow-up. The goal is to push past exposition into places where the view genuinely faces pressure. My instructor has given me specific requirements for what the questions must cover, and I'd like you to write the interview script.

The questions must address at least the following:

(1) The relationship between the two-dimensional treatment of the necessary a posteriori and Kripke's original observations. The question shouldn't just ask the interviewee to restate the view; it should press on whether the framework explains the phenomenon or merely provides a formal apparatus that redescribes it. Include a follow-up that anticipates the reply that the epistemic intension does explanatory work.

(2) The conceivability argument against physicalism, which uses the two-dimensional framework. The question should zero in on the step from the conceivability of zombies (or an inverted spectrum, or absent qualia) to the metaphysical possibility of those scenarios. Specifically, press on whether the move from primary possibility to secondary possibility requires an additional premise -- something like the rejection of "strong necessities" -- that isn't itself justified by the two-dimensional framework. The follow-up should ask what independent reason we have to rule out strong necessities, beyond the intuition that consciousness is special.

(3) At least one question on the nesting problem -- the objection, pressed by Soames and others, that applying two-dimensional semantics to its own statements about primary intensions leads to an iterated structure that doesn't stabilize. The question should ask whether the framework can state its own core claims without triggering a regress, and the follow-up should press on whether the defender can stop the regress without simply stipulating that certain semantic claims are exempt.

(4) A question about the role of ideal rational reflection in fixing primary intensions. If a primary intension is supposed to map a centered world to an extension based on what ideal a priori reflection would yield, the question should ask how we are supposed to evaluate this notion of idealization -- especially since actual epistemic agents make mistakes about what is a priori. The follow-up should ask whether the view collapses into psychologism if we lower the idealization, or into vacuity if we keep it at full idealization and can never verify what the primary intension actually delivers.

(5) A question about how the framework handles indexicals and demonstratives. It's well known that "I" and "now" have primary intensions that pick out the agent and time of the centered world, but the question should press on whether this success with indexicals actually carries over to natural-kind terms and names in the way the two-dimensionalist needs it to. The follow-up should ask whether the analogy between "I" and "water" can be sustained given that the reference-fixing description for a natural-kind term is plausibly much less determinate than the rule for an indexical.

(6) A question about whether the two-dimensionalist is committed to a descriptivist theory of reference, despite attempts to distance the view from classical descriptivism. The question should frame this as a dilemma: either the primary intension of a name is given by some description, which revives Kripke's modal and epistemic arguments against descriptivism, or it isn't, in which case it's unclear what determines the primary intension. The follow-up should ask whether the appeal to "reference magnetism" or "joint-carving" properties can help here without giving up the epistemic motivations for the framework.

Finally, please add two or three additional questions of your own devising that follow naturally from the dialectic set up by the first six, making sure they continue to press the view at its genuine stress points rather than retreating to exposition.

For each question-and-follow-up pair, write a brief note (in brackets, like this) explaining what sort of answer you expect the defender to give to the initial question, so that the follow-up is calibrated to that anticipated answer. The whole thing should feel like a prepared interview that could actually be used -- not generic, not a take-home exam, but a real intellectual back-and-forth.
