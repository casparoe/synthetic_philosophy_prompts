So I've been in this reading group on AI ethics and one of the members — let's call him Dan — has been really consistent about who he blames when autonomous systems cause harm. I've been keeping track of his verdicts and now we have a new case coming up and I want to predict what he'll say. Here's what he's said so far:

1. A self-driving car with a known software bug in its pedestrian detection system hits and kills a pedestrian before the patch is released. Dan says the manufacturer is responsible, full stop. They knew and they deployed anyway.

2. A different self-driving car, thoroughly tested with no known flaws, encounters a situation no one anticipated — a child in an inflatable dinosaur costume that confuses the lidar. It swerves wrong and the child is injured. Dan says this is a geniune responsibility gap. No one is morally responsible. It's just a tragedy.

3. A military drone with autonomous target recognition is deployed by a human commander who authorizes a mission but doesn't pick specific targets. The drone misidentifies a civilian gathering as a military target and strikes it. Dan says the commander is responsible. You don't get to delegate lethal decisions to a system and then shrug when it messes up.

4. A hospital deploys an AI triage system that was trained on biased data and systematically gives lower urgency scores to Black patients. The hospital knew about the disparity but couldn't source a better system and decided the overall efficiency gains justified using it. A Black patient dies waiting. Dan says the hospital is responsible. Using a tool you know is biased is a choice.

5. A large language model gives a teenager detailed instructions for a dangerous stunt. The teen follows them and is seriously injured. The model had safety filters but they were known to be circumventable with enough effort. Dan says the company is responsible. Shipping a product with known vulnerabilities that can cause serious harm isn't that different from case 1.

So the new case we're discussing next week: a nursing home installs an AI fall-detection system that uses cameras and pressure sensors to alert staff when a resident falls. The system has a disclosed 2% false-negative rate — it misses about 1 in 50 falls. The nursing home knows this, trains staff on the limitation, and institutes hourly room checks specifically as a backup for missed detections. A resident falls ten minutes after a check, the system doesn't detect it, and the resident dies from being on the floor for fifty minutes.

What's Dan going to say? I can see at least two rules he might be running that both fit his first five verdicts, and they point in different directions here. Lay out the candidate rules, say which one you think he's actually running and why, and tell me what further case would cleanly settle which rule he's committed to.
