I am preparing an interview for a philosophy of mind podcast with a philosopher who defends the claim that the intentional stance is the correct framework for understanding the outputs of large language models — and, more provocatively, that taking the intentional stance toward an LLM licenses attributing beliefs to it, not merely treating it as if it had them. Draft eight to ten questions that push on this position from distinct angles. I want each question to press past the obvious reply, so include a follow-up keyed to the defender's most natural move.

I need at least one question on each of the following, but the questions should not announce their topic in so many words — embed the pressure point in the framing:

- The predictive-adequacy criterion and its boundaries. If treating a chess engine via the intentional stance predicts its moves, does the view license attributing beliefs about chess to Stockfish? If not, what principled criterion draws the line between Stockfish and an LLM, and why isn't that criterion ad hoc? The defender will likely appeal to the breadth or flexibility of the behavioural patterns that the stance tracks; the follow-up should ask whether breadth is a difference of kind or merely of degree, and what magnitude of breadth crosses the threshold.

- Original versus derived intentionality. The sentences in the training corpus were produced by creatures with original intentionality; the LLM reproduces statistical patterns over those sentences. Why are the belief-states we impute via the intentional stance beliefs of the system, rather than merely the system's reflecting — like a mirror — the beliefs of the authors whose texts it ingested? Anticipate a reply that the same charge can be levelled against human beings who acquire beliefs through testimony, and ask what relevant difference there is between testimonial belief-acquisition and next-token prediction over a corpus.

- The charge of behaviourism. If the intentional stance warrants belief-attribution solely on the basis of patterns in observable linguistic behaviour, the view seems to collapse into a form of logical behaviourism about the mental — a position widely thought to have been refuted by the usual counterexamples (the paralytic, the method actor, the perfect deceiver). The standard intentional-stance reply is that the stance is instrumentalist rather than reductivist: it does not claim that beliefs consist in behavioural dispositions, only that the stance is our best epistemic access to them. The follow-up should press on whether this epistemic retreat actually avoids the counterexamples, or merely relocates them: if we have no access to a belief that outstrips what the stance delivers, in what sense is there a fact of the matter about whether the paralytic has beliefs at all?

- Doxastic stability. LLMs contradict themselves across turns, reverse 'beliefs' when prompts are reworded, and confidently assert falsehoods that they 'disavow' moments later. Does a system whose doxastic profile is this unstable genuinely count as a belief-haver, or is the intentional stance here tracking something too shallow to warrant the term? The reply will likely be that human beings also hold inconsistent beliefs and shift views under framing effects. The follow-up should ask whether the frequency, indifference to truth, and lack of any integration mechanism in the LLM case constitute a qualitative rather than merely quantitative difference — and, if not, what sort of evidence could show that the instability had crossed the line.

- The question of thresholds and future systems. Does the view commit the defender to saying that no architectural improvement — no amount of world-modelling, recurrence, or grounding — could ever make the difference between 'as-if' belief and belief simpliciter, because the intentional stance is the whole story? Or does the defender allow that future systems might cross a threshold into non-stance-dependent belief? If the latter, what would that threshold be, and why doesn't it apply already? If the former, press on whether the view collapses into a global anti-realism about belief that applies to humans equally — and whether the defender is prepared to accept that consequence.

Finally, include at least one question that challenges the view from the opposite direction: if the intentional stance does license attributing beliefs to LLMs, and if those beliefs include morally salient content (e.g., beliefs about what a person deserves, or about contested historical events), does the defender incur any obligation regarding what beliefs the system is trained to have — or is that question confused because the stance tells us what the system believes without implying that it matters?
