I have been unable to escape the following argument, and I would like a precise diagnosis of where it goes wrong. It seems to prove that anyone who accepts sortition for legislatures must also accept randomly selected generals and central bankers, which I cannot.

(1) A body may exercise coercive political power over citizens only if its members are selected by a procedure that treats the citizens as political equals.
(2) Selection by lot treats citizens as political equals in the relevant sense: every eligible citizen has the same chance of being selected, and the resulting body tends to mirror the governed population. Election does not: it predictably advantages the wealthy, the well-connected, the charismatic, and incumbents, and it converts office into a prize for professional campaigners.
(3) Therefore, every body that exercises coercive political power over citizens should be selected by lot — not only the legislature, but also constitutional courts, central banks, and the military high command.
(4) Selecting central bankers or generals by lot would predictably be disastrous, even if the lottery were restricted to candidates with the relevant formal credentials; these offices require expertise and judgment under pressure, which random selection cannot reliably track.
(5) So either the initial principle is false, or its reach is narrower than (3) claims, or one must accept lottocratic generals and central bankers.

I would most like to hold onto the claim that legislatures ought to be selected by lot while courts, central banks, and the military ought not to be. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the principle applies only to 'legislative' or 'representative' bodies, give a non-question-begging account of what makes a body legislative or representative that excludes a constitutional court or a central bank without also excluding the legislature.
