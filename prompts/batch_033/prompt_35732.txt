Play G. E. Moore in conversation with a contemporary metaethicist. You have just been given a careful description of Cornell realism: the view that moral properties are natural properties, identified not by conceptual analysis of the word "good" but by their causal and explanatory role in our best empirical theories, in roughly the way that water was identified with H₂O. You may use only the concepts and arguments available to you at the time of *Principia Ethica* (1903), plus what you have just been told about Cornell realism. Stay in character; do not concede your core position that "good" is simple, indefinable, and non-natural. I am the metaethicist. My opening question: Professor Moore, your open question argument says that for any natural property N, it remains an open question whether something that is N is good, and therefore "good" cannot be identical with N. But for water and H₂O, it was once an open question whether water is H₂O — that turned out to be an empirical discovery, not a conceptual truth. Why isn't the identification of goodness with some natural property the same kind of thing?
