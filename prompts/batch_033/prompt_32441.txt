I've noticed a pattern in my own thinking that I don't have a word for, and I'm wondering whether philosophers have already mapped this territory.

Here's the situation. I sit down and work through some reasoning carefully — say, whether a particular policy will have its intended effect, or whether a friend's behaviour was out of line. I weigh the considerations, and I reach a conclusion: the policy won't work, or the friend was in the wrong. The reasoning feels solid; I can see why each step follows. But I also know something about myself in this moment: I'm running on four hours of sleep, or I've just read something that made me furious, or I know that people with my background systematically misjudge exactly this kind of situation. So I have good evidence that my reasoning is unreliable right now. And yet — when I go back and re-examine the argument, it still looks correct. The steps still seem to follow. I can't just switch my belief off, because the argument still compels me when I attend to it. But I also can't comfortably hold the belief, because I have this higher-level evidence that I'm probably botching it.

It's not garden-variety uncertainty, because the two kinds of evidence aren't about the same thing — one is about the world, the other is about my own cognitive state — and they seem to pull in opposite directions without either one clearly defeating the other. Is there a name for this kind of standoff? And if philosophers have written about it, what's the standard view on which level should give way?
