I'm a novelist, and the way I think about people is through craft: motivation, interiority, free indirect discourse, point of view, what a character feels versus what they actually do, how a backstory explains a present action, the narrator's authority over what's going on inside someone's head. From what I gather, epiphenomenalism says conscious experience is caused by the brain but never causes anything itself — its like steam rising off the engine, not part of what drives the wheels — and the paradox is that if that were true, then even my judgment that I'm conscious (and that epiphenomenalism is true) would be caused by brain processes rather than by the conscious experience itself, so the very experience I'm trying to report is never what's making me report it. That seems like a nasty self-undermining twist, but I want to see it in my own language before I believe I've understood it.

Please restate the whole position — epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment — in the vocabulary I actually use as a novelist. I mean things like: a character's inner life as the engine of their actions, motivation as causal explanation, free indirect discourse as a way of reporting experience while the character acts, the reader's standing assumption that what a character feels explains what they say and do, and the narrator's claim to know what's inside a head. Don't just decorate it with literary metaphors; I want a real translation, claim by claim if that's what it takes, even where it comes out awkward. My hunch is that this all collapses into the familiar problem of a narrator who can't be trusted about his own inner life, but I suspect that's too easy.
