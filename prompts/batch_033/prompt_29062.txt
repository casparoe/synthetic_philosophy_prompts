I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about the unity of consciousness, going by my gut on concrete cases. Here are my verdicts so far.

A split-brain patient sits in a lab. The word "spoon" is flashed to their left visual field only, so it reaches only the right hemisphere. The patient says they saw nothing. But their left hand, controlled by the right hemisphere, reaches into a bag and retrieves a spoon. I think there are two conscious visual experiences here, not one: the speaking hemisphere had no experience of the word, while the mute hemisphere did. They are not co-conscious.

Same patient, different trial: the right hemisphere is shown a snow scene and asked to select a matching card from an array; the left hand picks a shovel. The left hemisphere, which controls speech and saw nothing, is asked why. It says, "I guess I wanted to clean up." The left hemisphere is confabulating — it had no access to the snow scene. I think this shows that the left hemisphere is a distinct subject of experience, genuinely unaware of what the right hemisphere saw, yet constructing a narrative as if it were the whole person. Two subjects.

Now the same patient is at home, cooking dinner, talking with their spouse. Nothing in their behavior suggests anything unusual. I think that in ordinary life, outside the lab, this is one person — not two persons sharing a skull. I do not think the unity of the person splits when they enter the lab and reunifies when they leave.

Someone with alien hand syndrome — no commissurotomy, but damage to the corpus callosum from a stroke — finds their left hand unbuttoning their shirt while they try with their right hand to button it. The hand acts against their conscious intention. I think this is one person, one conscious subject, with a disrupted sense of agency. Not two subjects.

Someone under general anesthesia. No consciousness at all. The question of unity does not arise — there is nothing to unify.

Someone in dreamless sleep. Same verdict: no consciousness, no unity question.

Someone with dissociative identity disorder who switches between alters. The alters report gaps in memory and distinct senses of self. I think this is one person — one organism, one subject — whose self-model is fragmented. Not multiple subjects sharing a body.

Two healthy people connected by a brain-computer interface that lets each perceive some of the other's visual field in real time. I think these are unambiguously two conscious subjects, even if their experiences are partly shared.

A hypothetical: a person's cerebral hemispheres are surgically separated and each transplanted into a different donor body. Both resulting people wake up with memories of the original person's life. I think there are now two persons, and neither is identical to the original — the original person ceased to exist when the hemispheres were disconnected.

A person with an intact brain, driving a familiar route while absorbed in a podcast. They later realize they have no memory of the last ten minutes of driving, though they drove safely. I think this was one conscious subject the whole time — the driving was conscious, just not encoded in memory.

Is there a principle — about what unifies consciousness, about when two experiences belong to the same subject — that gets all of these, or close? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a split-brain patient in the lab, during a task where the two hemispheres are given conflicting instructions and the left hand and right hand simultaneously reach for different objects? If my verdicts force me to drop one, which should go, and why that one rather than another?
