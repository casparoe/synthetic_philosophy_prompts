Question: Explain Quine's argument against the analytic-synthetic distinction in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." What does Quine think the distinction amounts to, why does he think it cannot be drawn, and what does he propose in its place?

Student answer:

Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" targets two commitments of logical empiricism: the analytic-synthetic distinction and reductionism. Quine argues that these two dogmas are at root the same. The analytic-synthetic distinction holds that some truths are true purely in virtue of meaning (analytic), while others depend on both meaning and fact (synthetic). Reductionism holds that each meaningful statement can be individually translated into statements about immediate sensory experience.

Quine's argument against the analytic-synthetic distinction proceeds by examining candidate definitions of "analytic" and showing that each is circular. He starts with the idea that analytic truths are logical truths, but this only covers cases like "No unmarried man is married" and misses "All bachelors are unmarried," which requires the notion of synonymy. He then considers whether synonymy can be grounded in definition, but definitions merely report pre-existing synonymies. He considers interchangeability salva veritate, but this criterion only works in extensional languages; in intensional contexts, substitution can fail. He considers semantical rules for artificial languages, but these merely stipulate analyticity for a given language without explaining the concept. Since every attempt to define "analytic" presupposes the notion it is trying to explain, Quine concludes that there are no analytic truths — the distinction simply collapses.

Quine then argues against reductionism by introducing confirmation holism: individual statements do not face experience in isolation; only whole theories are confirmed or disconfirmed. This provides a second, independent argument against analyticity, because if no statement has its own empirical content, then the empiricist's idea of a statement true "come what may" is also undermined. In place of both dogmas, Quine proposes a pragmatic holism in which all statements are in principle revisable, though some (like logic and mathematics) lie at the center of our conceptual scheme and are revised only under extreme pressure. The analytic-synthetic distinction is replaced by a continuum of revisability.
