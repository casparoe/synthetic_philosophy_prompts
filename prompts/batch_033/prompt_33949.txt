I'm a software engineer, and I recently came across the "white horse dialogue" attributed to Gongsun Long and the School of Names, with its famous claim that "a white horse is not a horse." The obvious reading treats this as an ambiguity: "white horse" is not the same description as "horse," so the claim is trivially true if "is not" means "is not identical to," and plainly false if it means "is not an instance of." But I understand the dialogue isn't just punning, and I'd like to get clear on what it's actually doing.

Could you walk me through the argument as it's actually put forward in the dialogue and reconstruct the view of terms, properties, and categories that makes the conclusion non-trivial? In particular, I'd like to understand why the advocate distinguishes "horse" as a term that designates shape from "white" as a term that designates color, and why he thinks the fact that a request for a horse can be satisfied by a yellow or black horse while a request for a white horse cannot is meant to prove something deeper than that the two descriptions differ. What does he have to assume about how a term connects to a thing — about the relation between what a thing is and what qualities it has — for that inference to go through?

Then I'd like the other side of it. If you don't accept those assumptions and instead treat "white horse" as a way of narrowing the class of horses to those that are white — the way a type in a programming language can be a subtype of a more general type, where every value of the subtype is also a value of the supertype — is there any residue of the paradox left, or does it dissolve entirely? I want to know whether the dialogue is pointing at a real conceptual issue that a modern theory of predication still has to face, or whether its force depends entirely on assumptions most people would now reject.
