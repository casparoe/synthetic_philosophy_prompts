A survey article on medieval Islamic philosophy I was reading summarizes Avicenna's "flying man" thought experiment like this. Avicenna, anticipating Descartes by nearly six centuries, asks us to imagine a person created all at once, fully formed, suspended in the air with his vision shrouded, his limbs held apart so that they cannot touch one another, no breeze against his skin, and no memory of ever having had a body. Such a person, Avicenna says, would still be certain that he himself exists, even though he would not affirm the existence of any of his limbs, his heart, his brain, or any external thing. From this Avicenna concludes that the soul is a distinct immaterial substance, separable from the body, because one can be aware of oneself while being aware of none of one's bodily parts. The article presents this as Avicenna's answer to radical skepticism about the senses: even if all sensory input were deceptive or absent, the one thing I cannot doubt is my own existence, and the self thereby becomes the indubitable foundation on which the rest of knowledge is built. I find the comparison to Descartes suspicious, but I do not know Avicenna well enough to say where the summary runs ahead of the text. Which claims in this summary would a specialist accept, which are subtle distortions or anachronisms, and does the closing skeptical-foundationalist gloss survive a careful reading of the argument?
