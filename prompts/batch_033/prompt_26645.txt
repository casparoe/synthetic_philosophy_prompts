ok so i've been reading L.A. Paul on transformative experience and i'm trying to nail down what i actually think instead of just nodding along. here's where i've landed and i want to know if it hangs together:

(1) some experiences are epistemically transformative in Paul's sense: you literally can't know what they're like without undergoing them. having a kid, becoming deeply religious, losing your sight — these aren't just surprising or more intense than you expected, they're unknowable from the outside in a principled way.

(2) for decisions involving epistemically transformative experiences, you can't just run an expected utility calculation in the usual way, because you don't have access to the relevant utility values for the outcome you haven't experienced. the framework breaks down, not just gets fuzzy.

(3) nevertheless, people do make these decisions rationally all the time. it would be a reductio of any view that said choosing to have a child or to emigrate is necessarily arational. these are paradigm cases of major life decisions and we don't treat them as coin flips.

(4) the way out is testimony: hearing from people who've undergone the experience gives you enough of a grip on what it's like to assign utilities, even if it's imperfect. you don't need first-person phenomenal knowledge to make a rational choice — propositional knowledge from others bridges the gap.

(5) i also think that if someone undergoes a transformative experience and their values change as a result, their pre-transformation self was not necessarily wrong or irrational. the new values are genuinely new, not corrections of old errors. your past self wasn't making a mistake; they just hadn't become the person who values those things yet.

(6) but i also think that if you know, pre-transformation, that your future self will endorse the decision, that counts as a genuine reason in favor of doing it — it's not just bootstrapping or wishful thinking. "future me will be glad i did this" is a legitimate consideration.
