I'm a program officer at a foundation that makes about forty grants a year, mostly in global health and education, and I write the funding recommendations that go to our board. Last night I got into a long argument with a friend who works in institutional investing — he kept telling me that the way I evaluate proposals is a textbook case of the inside view, and that I'm making exactly the mistake Kahneman and Lovallo diagnosed: I read a fifty-page proposal, get immersed in the qualifications of the team, the elegance of the research design, and the specificity of the theory of change, and by the end I've constructed a narrative about why this particular project will succeed that is almost certainly too optimistic. His position was that I should start every evaluation by placing the proposal in a reference class of similar projects, anchoring my expectations to the base rate for that class, and then adjusting only modestly and with explicit justification for any inside-view information I think should pull me away from the anchor. He said the evidence on the planning fallacy is so overwhelming that anyone who doesn't begin with the outside view is essentially negligent.

I pushed back hard, and I still think there was something right about my side of the argument. I told him that a lot of what I do is precisely about spotting the differences that make a difference — the proposal where the team already ran a pilot and the results were strange in a way that suggests they actually understand the mechanism, the one where the principal investigator has a track record of delivering under budget in a harder context than the one we're funding, the one where the theory of change identifies a lever that nobody else in the field has thought to pull. If I just regress everything to the mean of its reference class, I'll never recommend the unusual project that actually deserves a bet, and my recommendations will be uselessly bland. He replied that this is exactly what every inside-view thinker says, and that the outside view exists precisely because people like me are systematically overconfident about their ability to pick winners from the details. We didn't resolve it, but the argument made me realize I need something more structured than my current approach, which oscillates between getting absorbed in the proposal and then, when I remember the base rates, applying them inconsistently as a kind of vague sanity check.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a grant proposal — a sequence of steps that tells me how to construct a reference class, extract a usable base rate from it, identify which kinds of inside-view information are legitimate reasons to depart from that base rate and by roughly how much, and flag the point where the procedure has taken me as far as it can and the remaining judgment is irreducibly mine. The procedure needs to be usable by someone who is not a statistician and who has maybe thirty minutes per proposal for this part of the evaluation. It should be explicit about what to do when the obvious reference class is too heterogeneous to yield a meaningful base rate, when the data for the class is thin, or when the proposal genuinely doesn't fit any existing class. Then apply the procedure to two cases: one is a randomized controlled trial of a water-quality intervention in a region where the implementing organization has an unusually strong track record but where similar WASH trials across comparable regions have shown mixed or null results; the other is a fellowship program for artists working at the intersection of computational media and disability arts, proposed by a first-time applicant with no institutional track record, where the relevant reference classes for arts fellowships are small and mostly consist of traditional discipline-based programs. I want to see where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like, and I want an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do — the cases it will mishandle and the questions it leaves for me to answer without its help.
