I've been teaching the Prince for years and my own verdicts on fortune and virtù keep pulling in different directions. I want to know if there's a principle behind them, or if I need to drop one. Here are the cases as I'd put them to myself:

Case 1: A leader inherits a stable state, changes nothing, dies in bed. I give them zero credit — they just had good fortune.

Case 2: A leader rises through sheer skill during a civil war, builds solid institutions, and then a plague hits and the whole thing collapses. I don't blame them. Fortune was just too much.

Case 3: Same as Case 2, except they'd stockpiled grain and built a primitive public health system, so the state survives the plague. I give them enormous credit — they mastered fortune.

Case 4: A leader seizes power through mass executions and terror, rules effectively for thirty years, dies in bed. I think they were effective but I won't call it virtù. It feels like a different category.

Case 5: A leader has the same skills and foresight as Case 3, but instead of a plague they face an invasion by an empire ten times their size, and they lose. I still think they had virtù, but I'm not sure whether I praise them as much as Case 3.

Case 6: A leader is flexible — cautious when needed, bold when needed — and succeeds. I praise them more than Case 3, even though both succeeded. The adaptability itself seems to matter, not just the outcome.

Case 7: A leader is cautious for twenty years and it works beautifully. Then the world changes, they stay cautious, and they lose everything. I blame them, even though they were doing exactly what had worked.

Is there one principle about the fortune-virtù relationship that gets all seven? If not, which verdict should I overturn? And what does your principle say about a case I didn't list: a leader who is genuinely adaptable, but every time they adapt, fortune shifts again before the new approach can pay off — they keep reading the moment right but the moment keeps changing mid-read?
