I'm a novelist — I spend most of my working life thinking about what it's like to be other people, so maybe it's not surprising I've got a bit obsessed with the philosophical debate about animal consciousness. I've been reading around it for a couple of years now, and I've reached a point where I'm more interested in why the debate won't budge than in the actual positions.

Here's what I see. Everyone's working with roughly the same empirical picture. We know about bee cognition — they can learn concepts, they seem to have something like play behaviour. We know about fish nociception and the fact that they behave differently when given analgesics. We know octopuses have a radically different neural architecture but behave in ways that sure look intelligent and maybe even curious. And we know about the mirror test, the various behavioural criteria people have proposed, and the limitations of all of them. Nobody disputes any of this.

And yet. Some philosophers — call them the liberals — think consciousness is phylogenetically ancient and widespread: if an animal integrates information in a certain way, there's something it's like to be that animal, full stop. Others — the conservatives — think you need the right kind of neural real estate, probably something like a mammalian cortex or at least a functional analogue, which would rule out most invertebrates and maybe fish too. Then there's a third group who think we're asking the wrong question: 'consciousness' isn't one thing, it's a grab-bag of capacities (phenomenal consciousness, access consciousness, self-awareness, sentience, etc.), and we should be asking which animals have which capacities rather than whether they're 'conscious' tout court.

What I can't figure out is what kind of disagreement this is. Is it a straightforward empirical one, where we're just waiting for better neuroscience to tell us which animals have the relevant neural signatures? Or is it a conceptual disagreement about the definition of consciousness — and if so, is it substantive or merely verbal? Or is it, as I've started to suspect, a disagreement about epistemic norms: the liberals think the default is to attribute consciousness unless we have reason not to, the conservatives think the default is to withhold it unless we have positive evidence, and neither side can justify their starting point without begging the question?

I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a summary of the literature. And I want to know what — if anything — could actually move the debate. A new experimental paradigm? A conceptual distinction that cuts the disagrement at a different joint? A formal result? Or should we just accept that the question as stated is unanswerable and reframe it? Be brutal if you need to be: if you think the whole thing is a pseudo-dispute, say so and tell me why.
