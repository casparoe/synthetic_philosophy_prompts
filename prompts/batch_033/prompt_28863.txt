I've been trying to work out what I actually believe in population ethics, and my verdicts on cases keep pulling me in different directions. I'd like you to look at my pattern of verdicts and tell me what principle, if any, captures them — or which ones I need to give up.

Here are the cases, with my gut reactions:

1. **The huge unhappy world.** A world with ten billion people, all leading excellent lives. Compare it to a world with a hundred trillion people, all leading lives that are just barely worth living — a bit more pleasure than pain, but only just. I can't accept that the second world is better. It seems worse, or at least not better.

2. **Adding a happy person.** Start with a world of a million happy people. Now imagine adding one more person, whose life is very good, without making anyone else's life any worse. It seems to me that the resulting world is at least as good as the original — maybe better, but certainly not worse.

3. **The miserable child.** A couple is considering having a child they know would have a life of unremitting suffering. I think it would be wrong for them to conceive that child. But if another couple simply decides not to have a child who would have had a wonderful life, I don't think they've done anything wrong. There's an asymmetry: we have reasons not to create miserable lives, but no corresponding reason to create happy ones.

4. **The depletion policy.** A government must choose between two energy policies. Under Policy A, the country will be moderately prosperous for two centuries and then collapse, and the people alive during those two centuries will have good lives. Under Policy B, the country will be somewhat less prosperous but sustainable indefinitely, and a completely different set of people will exist — all with good lives, and over time many more of them. No one exists under both policies, so no specific person is harmed by either choice. I think Policy B is the better choice, and I think this even though no one under Policy A can complain.

5. **Extinction.** Suppose humanity goes extinct sooner rather than later — say, a catastrophe wipes us out in a century — but everyone alive until then has a good life. Compare this to a future where humanity survives for millions of years and countless generations live good lives. I think the extinction scenario is worse. A lot worse. But I struggle to say why, given my asymmetry verdict: if we have no obligation to create happy people, why is it bad when we fail to create billions of them?

6. **The sadistic conclusion.** Imagine a population of people with terrible lives — constant suffering. Now imagine adding a much larger group of people whose lives are also terrible, but slightly less so. It seems to me that this makes the world worse, not better. But I've seen arguments that some theories imply the opposite, and I want to make sure my principle doesn't land there.

I can feel that verdicts 2, 3, and 5 are in tension — if adding a happy person makes things no worse (2), and we have no obligation to create happy people (3), then why is extinction bad (5)? And verdict 1 seems to conflict with 2 if you iterate 2 enough times. I'd rather keep verdicts 1, 3, and 5 and give up 2 if I have to, but I'm not sure whether there's a principle that lets me keep them all.

What I'm asking for: (a) the best candidate principle or small set of principles that captures as many of these verdicts as possible; (b) which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and why; (c) what the principle says about a case I haven't considered — specifically, whether a world of a million extremely happy people is better than a world of a billion people who are very happy but not quite as happy as the first group, where the total sum of well-being is higher in the second world; and (d) what consideration or argument would most likely change your mind about the principle you recommend — where is its weakest point, and what would it take for you to abandon it?
