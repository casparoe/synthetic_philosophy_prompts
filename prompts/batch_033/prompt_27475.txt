**Question:**
Explain why total utilitarianism implies the Repugnant Conclusion. Does switching to average utilitarianism allow us to avoid the Repugnant Conclusion while still holding a plausible axiology? Give reasons for your answer.

**Student’s answer:**
Total utilitarianism ranks outcomes according to the sum of individual well-being. This means a huge population where everyone has a life barely worth living can, in principle, contain more total well-being than a smaller population where everyone has very high well-being. Derek Parfit called this implication the Repugnant Conclusion, and he took it to show that total utilitarianism is deeply counterintuitive.

Average utilitarianism, which ranks outcomes by the *average* well-being in a population, straightforwardly blocks the Repugnant Conclusion. If we keep adding lives barely worth living, the average falls, so a world of such lives will never rank higher than a world of high-quality lives. So far, average utilitarianism looks better.

However, average utilitarianism has its own well-known problems. Parfit discusses two in particular. First, it falls victim to the Mere Addition Paradox: if we add a group of people whose lives are worth living but whose welfare is below the current average, average utilitarianism says the new outcome is worse, even though no one is made worse off and the added people have lives worth living. Second, it generates what Parfit dubs the “Sadistic Conclusion.” Because average utilitarianism cares only about the average, it can rank adding a group of people whose lives are *not* worth living above adding a group whose lives *are* worth living, provided the former addition raises the average. Both of these implications are at least as counterintuitive as the Repugnant Conclusion, so average utilitarianism does not give us an acceptable way out.

In sum, simply moving from total to average utilitarianism swaps one set of unacceptable implications for another, and we therefore need a more sophisticated axiology — such as a critical-level or variable-value view — to make progress.
