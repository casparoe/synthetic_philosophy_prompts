Design a 75-minute classroom game for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of biology seminar (roughly 18 students; no prior game-theory training assumed) that makes participants feel from the inside why a signal can carry information only because it costs the signaler something that low-quality signalers find harder to pay. The activity should target costly signaling and the handicap principle (the idea associated with Amotz Zahavi and its later game-theoretic formalizations), but none of those terms — nor "separating equilibrium," "honest signal," "pooling," or "differential cost" — should be introduced until the debrief. There should be no lecture up front; the idea must show up in what participants find themselves doing.

Participants should come away seeing three things: (a) that a signal's wastefulness is not an accident but the feature that makes it informative; (b) that the crucial condition is differential cost — the same display must be more expensive for low-quality signalers than for high-quality ones — not mere cost; and (c) that an informative equilibrium is only one of several stable outcomes, so receivers have a standing reason to be skeptical.

Please provide:

(1) Roles and rules: a hidden-quality game in which some players are signalers with a privately known quality (high or low) and others are receivers deciding whether to enter a costly partnership with them; the rules should let receivers condition only on the display they observe, never on the hidden quality.

(2) Materials: printed cards or tokens only — no real money. Specify exactly what the facilitator needs to prepare.

(3) Payoffs and costs: a payoff structure in which a high-quality match benefits both parties and a low-quality match harms the receiver, and in which the display's cost is strictly higher for low-quality signalers than for high-quality signalers. Include the numbers.

(4) Round sequence: at least three conditions run in order — a cheap-talk condition (displays cost nothing for everyone), a flat-cost condition (displays cost the same for everyone), and a differential-cost condition (displays cost more for low-quality signalers). The order should be arranged so that participants experience why the first two setups fail to transmit information before they hit the condition that works.

(5) A debrief that maps specific moments from the play to the concepts: separating vs. pooling equilibria; the differential-cost condition; why receivers should be skeptical of cheap and flat-cost displays; the difference between a handicap and an unfakeable index; and why the game has multiple equilibria, so the informative outcome is not guaranteed.

(6) A troubleshooting section: what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run, and how the facilitator should handle each failure. Include at least these cases: a pooling equilibrium emerges early and signalers stop displaying; receivers start trusting cheap signals because they are friendly; a low-quality signaler complains that the setup is unfair; and participants collude or share quality information outside the rules.

End with an honest note on what the game cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply — in particular, the difference between rational strategic choice by individual players and the evolutionary or selectionist interpretation of the same equilibrium conditions, and the fact that the empirical standing of the handicap principle in real animal signaling remains contested.
