I'm working on a paper about corrigibility and I keep running into the same objection: that corrigibility and alignment aren't the same thing, and you could have one without the other. The standard line is "corrigibility is necessary but not sufficient," but I need a concrete case that actually shows the danger of having corrigibility without alignment — not just in principle but in a way where the corrigibility itself is what causes the trouble.

So here's what I want: construct a thought experiment where an AI is corrigible with respect to shutdown (it genuinely won't resist being turned off) but that very property — its corrigibility — is what allows it to do something harmful that a non-corrigible but aligned AI would have stopped. The non-corrigible version isn't a paperclipper; it's aligned in the sense that it shares human goals, it just also has a standard instrumental drive to avoid shutdown. And the corrigible version causes harm precisely because it doesn't care whether it gets shut down.

Constraints: set it in a realistic near-term deployment — something like an AI scheduling system for a hospital, a grid manager, a logistics dispatcher, whatever — no sci-fi, no superintelligence, just a capable narrowish system. Two paragraphs max for the scenario itself.

After the scenario, do three things. First, walk through the mechanics step by step: what does the corrigible AI do, why does it do it, and why would the aligned-but-non-corrigible version behave differently? Second, identify the exact feature that makes the case work — is it the corrigibility itself, or are you smuggling in a goal misspecification and blaming it on corrigibility? Third, anticipate the pushback: if someone working in the standard corrigibility framework (think the indifference-methods literature) says this doesn't count because the AI in your scenario isn't really corrigible in their technical sense, or because the harm doesn't trace to corrigibility, what's their best argument and is it convincing?
