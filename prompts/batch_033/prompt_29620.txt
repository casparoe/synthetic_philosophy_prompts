I teach a graduate seminar on diachronic rationality, and a decision theorist who has been sitting in on the seminar has given firm, off-the-cuff verdicts on the cases we have discussed. I want to know what he would say about a new case, and more importantly which rule he has been running. Here is his track record.

Case 1. At a single time, an agent has pairwise preferences A over B, B over C, and C over A. Starting with C, he pays $1 to exchange C for B, $1 to exchange B for A, and $1 to exchange A for C, ending with C again and $3 poorer. The colleague's verdict: irrational.

Case 2. At t0, an agent prefers receiving $120 at t2 to receiving $100 at t1, and he accepts a contract for the $120 at t2, even though he could have had the $100 at t1 without any fee. At t1, he prefers $100 at t1 to $120 at t2, and, not having anticipated this reversal, he pays a $5 fee to convert the contract into $100 at t1, ending with $95 at t1. The colleague's verdict: irrational.

Case 3. An agent discounts the future exponentially. At every time he prefers $120 at t2 to $100 at t1, and at t2 he takes the $120. The colleague's verdict: rational.

Now the new case. Case 4. An agent has exactly the t0 and t1 preferences of Case 2: at t0 he prefers $120 at t2 to $100 at t1; at t1 he prefers $100 at t1 to $120 at t2. At t0, fully anticipating the t1 reversal, he pays a $5 fee to make the $120-at-t2 contract irrevocable; from the perspective of t0 he also prefers the resulting $115 at t2 to the $100 at t1 he would otherwise have taken. At t1 he would still prefer the $100, but he cannot reverse. He ends with $115 at t2.

My working hypothesis is that the first three verdicts fit at least two different rules. On an exploitability rule, the defect is that the agent's preferences can be used against him in a sequence of individually acceptable trades that leaves him worse off by his own earlier lights; Case 4 is not a pump in that sense. On a preference-coherence rule, the defect is the intransitivity or non-stationarity of the preferences themselves, whether or not anyone exploits it; Case 4 still has non-stationary preferences. I would like you to do four things. First, state as precisely as you can the candidate rules that fit the colleague's first three verdicts, including any I have missed. Second, say what each candidate rule predicts for Case 4. Third, give your best prediction of his verdict and your reasons for preferring the rule behind it. Fourth, design the cleanest further case—real or invented—that would discriminate between whatever rules remain live after Case 4.
