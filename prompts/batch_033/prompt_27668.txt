I'm a novelist, and I've been working on a character — a political fixer — who has made it a point of pride never to utter a false sentence, but who regularly leads people to false conclusions through careful implication, strategic omission, and what he calls "truthful misdirection." At a dinner party last month, I described the character to two friends, and they fell into a surprisingly substantive argument about whether what he does is morally on a par with lying. I took notes afterward because the exchange struck me as worth getting right, and I'd like someone to adjudicate it move by move.

(1) David opened by saying the distinction is morally empty. The fixer intends to produce false beliefs in his audience, and he succeeds. Whether he does it by uttering a false sentence or by arranging true sentences to imply a falsehood is a difference in technique, not in moral kind. If anything, he said, the fixer is worse: he's adding premeditation and cleverness to the same basic wrong, and he's likely to get away with it more often because he can always retreat to "I never actually said that."

(2) Miriam pushed back. She said that when you lie, you assert something as true and stake your credibility on it directly; when you mislead, you leave the inference to the listener. The listener retains some responsibility for what they conclude, because they had to take an additional step beyond what was strictly said. She also pointed out that misleading leaves a trail of true statements, which means a sufficiently attentive or later-informed person can reconstruct the truth, whereas a lie forecloses that possibility entirely — the record itself is corrupted.

(3) David replied that this "shared responsibility" argument is perverse — it blames the listener for trusting normal conversational implicatures that the speaker deliberately exploited. He said it's like a pickpocket blaming the victim for keeping their wallet in an accessible pocket. And the fact that a hyper-vigilant listener could theoretically see through the misdirection is irrelevant when the speaker has designed it to work on reasonable people operating in good faith. The "trail of true statements" is a forensic technicality, not a moral safeguard.

(4) Miriam answered that David's pickpocket analogy begs the question by assuming that conversational implicatures are always the speaker's responsibility in the way that explicit assertions are. She said that in many contexts — diplomacy, polite refusals, bargaining — we all rely on the distinction between what is said and what is implied, and we hold people responsible for the former more strictly than the latter. Collapsing the distinction would make a huge swath of ordinary social life morally suspect, and David's own conduct almost certainly depends on it in contexts he hasn't examined.

They went back and forth a while longer, but those four moves were the core of it. Please adjudicate: for each move, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and explain why. Then say who has the better overall position, and whether there's something important that both of them missed. Please lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
