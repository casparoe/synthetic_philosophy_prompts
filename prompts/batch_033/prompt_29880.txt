Consider a practice that is entirely routine in the natural sciences. A researcher proposes a new theory, and in support of it points to observations that were already well known and uncontroversial before the theory was formulated: a long-documented anomaly in the orbit of a planet, cited as evidence for a new theory of gravitation; the long-observed fit between certain coastlines, cited for a theory of moving continents; a previously known regularity that the new theory is then shown to entail. None of the evidence is new; only the theory is. Yet both the scientist and the wider community treat this as genuine confirmation of the theory, not as decoration, and the practice is allowed to carry real weight in theory choice. The same community often treats novel predictions as especially impressive, but it does not treat explanations of already known facts as worthless.

What theory of evidence or confirmation does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that a fact can confirm a hypothesis even when the fact was assigned probability 1 long before the hypothesis was formulated — that confirmation is a relation of fit between theory and known facts rather than an event of updating on newly learned information? Or can a Bayesian framework absorb the practice, for example by considering what probability the old evidence would have had absent the new theory, or by treating the proposal of the theory itself as the new item learned? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology as precisely as you can, say whether it is coherent, and mark clearly where the practice strictly presupposes a view, where it merely fits comfortably with one among several, and where it is underdetermined. Then say what scientific practice would have to look like if it were governed by a strictly diachronic Bayesian rule of conditionalization on which evidence already known is confirmationally inert, and identify which feature of actual scientific practice would be most costly to surrender.
