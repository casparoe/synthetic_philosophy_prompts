I'm a retired biologist and I've been working through Parfit's Reasons and Persons lately — the teletransporter stuff, what matters in survival. And its got me thinking about that Nolan film The Prestige. You know the one — Angier gets Tesla to build him a machine, and every night he steps into it, a duplicate appears across the theater, and the one who stayed in the machine falls through a trapdoor and drowns. He says the terrifying part is he never knows whether he'll be the drowned one or the one who takes the bow.

Now Parfit says if the teletransporter scans you, destroys your body, and makes a perfect copy on Mars, you survive — because what matters is psychological continuity, not numerical identity. And in the branching case where both copies live, he says both are equally you and that's fine, survival doesn't require uniqueness.

But here's what I can't shake. The Prestige treats this whole situation as absolute horror. Every night Angier is basically commiting a kind of suicide, and the film's emotional logic is clear: the copy isn't him, he's killing himself over and over. The film seems to be saying that what matters is precisley the thing Parfit says doesn't matter — being the very same organism, the same continuous stream of consciousness.

So I'm trying to figure out: is the film just philosophically confused, or is it dramatizing something Parfit's account misses? As a biologist I keep coming back to the fact that Angier's terror — not knowing which one he'll be — feels like it tracks something real, some animal fact about death and the first-person perspective that Parfit's calm analysis maybe can't touch. I'd love a reading that takes both the film and Parfit seriously and doesn't just use the film as an illustration.
