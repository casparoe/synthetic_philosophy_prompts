A friend and I recently spent an entire evening arguing about the Libet experiments, and I was struck less by the arguments themselves than by the fact that, after hours, neither of us had moved an inch. Both of us accept the same findings: in experiments where people are asked to flex a wrist or press a button at a moment of their own choosing, a slow buildup of brain activity — the readiness potential — begins several hundred milliseconds before the reported time of conscious intention, and later imaging studies have claimed to decode the choice even seconds in advance. We also agree about what the standard criticisms say: that the task is trivial and deliberately arbitrary, that introspective timing of an intention may be unreliable, that a readiness potential may be motor preparation rather than a decision, and that the experiments do not touch the kind of deliberative, reasons-responsive agency people actually care about. My friend, who works in a lab, keeps insisting that the temporal order alone — brain activity preceding the felt decision — ought to unsettle anyone who believes in free will. I keep answering that this only follows if one has already decided that free will requires conscious initiation at the last moment, which is exactly what is in dispute. We both know each other's replies in advance, and so, it seems, does everyone in the literature: the debate has been running since the early 1980s, and the positions have not fundamentally changed.

What I would like is not a verdict on whether Libet-style results undermine free will. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse itself. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a factual disagreement about what the readiness potential shows, a verbal disagreement about the meaning of "free will," a difference about standards of evidence — about what could possibly count as empirical evidence against free will — or a difference about which cases count as data in the first place, arbitrary wrist flexions versus real choices? Are the two sides answering different questions under the single heading "does neuroscience undermine free will," and if so, which questions? My own hypothesis is that it is partly verbal and partly a bedrock disagreement about whether a decision has to be consciously initiated, rather than merely consciously endorsed or accompanied, in order to count as free. I would like you to assess that hypothesis, and then say what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis: a better experiment, a sharper distinction, a reformulated question, or an honest acknowledgment that no empirical result could settle it.
