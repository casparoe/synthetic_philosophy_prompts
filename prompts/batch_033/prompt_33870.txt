In most common-law jurisdictions, citizens summoned for jury duty are legally required to serve. You cannot pay a substitute to take your place, you cannot buy your way out, and the grounds for excusal are narrow and defined by the state rather than by individual preference. The state compensates jurors only a token amount — far below market rates for their time — and employers are not always required to make up the difference. Refusal to serve can result in fines or imprisonment.

Contrast this with other civic burdens in the same jurisdictions. The state does not conscript citizens to sweep streets, teach school, or staff hospitals, even when those services are publicly funded; it hires professionals at market wages. It also does not compel citizens to vote, to attend public meetings, or to serve in the military (conscription having been abandoned in most of these countries). Jury duty appears to be an anomaly: a non-consensual, non-delegable, poorly compensated labor obligation imposed on ordinary private citizens.

What view of the relationship between the individual and the political community does this practice presuppose? If the liberal picture is that state authority is justified by protecting individual rights and that obligations are primarily voluntarily assumed, what licenses compulsory jury service? Does the practice implicitly treat political membership as carrying non-optional burdens that one cannot contract out of — a characteristically communitarian idea? Or can liberal theory justify it on its own terms, perhaps by appeal to a right to trial by jury that generates a corresponding duty to serve on the part of those who would claim that right?

And if the practice does rest on a communitarian premise, what explains the narrowness of the duty? Why jury service but not, say, compulsory voting or mandatory community labor? Reconstruct the implicit theory of political obligation that the practice presupposes, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined (so that several theories would license it). Finally, tell me what would change about the practice — and about the broader set of civic obligations — if the presupposed theory were replaced by a consistently liberal one.
