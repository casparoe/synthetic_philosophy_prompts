I just had a long argument with a friend about whether you're ever justified in voting on the basis of beliefs you haven't properly verified. He took a hard line: if you haven't read the relevant studies, understood the counterarguments, and reached a warranted conclusion, you should abstain. I pushed back that this standard would disenfranchise almost everyone, and that democratic participation sometimes requires acting on beliefs that fall short of full epistemic justification.

The argument left me thinking about the practice of democratic voting itself — not about what individual voters should do, but about what the institution presupposes. In a mass democracy, citizens are asked to form views on trade policy, public health measures, constitutional amendments — matters where even specialists disagree. The institution doesn't merely permit voting on imperfectly-evidenced beliefs; it effectively requires it, since no one can become an expert on every item on the ballot. Yet the institution also treats these votes as normatively significant — as something more than arbitrary noise.

What view of the ethics of belief does universal-suffrage democracy presuppose? Is it implicitly Jamesian, treating political choices as forced, momentous options where we must decide on incomplete evidence? Or does it presuppose something weaker — that voting is permissible not because the beliefs are justified but because an individual vote carries such low stakes that epistemic negligence at the individual level doesn't matter? Does the institution need to take a position on whether voters are blameworthy for under-evidenced beliefs, or can it remain agnostic about the ethics of belief and simply design procedures that aggregate whatever beliefs voters happen to hold? And if we took Clifford's evidentialism seriously as a civic norm, what would the practice have to look like instead — compulsory abstention on issues one hasn't studied, some form of epistemic qualification for the franchise, or something else entirely?
