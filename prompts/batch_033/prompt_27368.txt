Two friends of mine, both in a philosophy PhD program, got into an argument after an epistemology seminar. The professor had presented a version of the bank case: Hannah drives past the bank on a Friday afternoon, sees a long line, and decides to come back Saturday instead. She remembers being at the bank on a Saturday a few weeks ago. In the low-stakes version, there is no urgency — if the bank turns out to be closed Saturday, she will just go Monday, and nothing hangs on it. In the high-stakes version, she has written a large check that will bounce if not deposited by Saturday, and the resulting fees and credit damage would be substantial. Her evidence about the bank's Saturday hours is identical in both versions. The question is whether Hannah knows the bank will be open on Saturday.

After the seminar, Elena argued that Hannah knows in the low-stakes case but not in the high-stakes case. Marcus disagreed. I was sitting with them and mostly just listened. Here is roughly how their exchange went:

Elena: "The intuition is clear. In the low-stakes case, if someone asked you 'Does Hannah know the bank is open Saturday?' you would say yes without hesitating. In the high-stakes case, you would hesitate — you would say she should double-check. Since the evidence is identical, the only thing that has changed is what is at stake. So practical stakes must partly determine whether someone knows."

Marcus: "I do not think the intuition is as clear as you are making it. Hannah has the same evidence either way. If evidence is what determines knowledge, then she either knows in both cases or fails to know in both. What is changing is not her epistemic position — it is how much risk she is willing to take in acting on it. In the high-stakes case she just needs more evidence before she is comfortable acting, but that is prudence, not epistemology."

Elena: "That move severs knowledge from assertion. If knowledge is the norm of assertion — and most of us in that seminar accept something like that — then it should be proper for Hannah to say 'I know the bank is open' exactly when she knows it. In the low-stakes case it seems fine for her to say that. In the high-stakes case it does not. So either knowledge is not the norm of assertion, or stakes affect knowledge. Which one are you giving up?"

Marcus: "I am not sure the norm of assertion is knowledge. But even if it is, there is a third option: Hannah knows in both cases, but in the high-stakes case it is conversationally inappropriate to flat-out assert it because the stakes make the possibility of error too salient to ignore. It is like how you can know the food at a reception is good but it would be inappropriate to announce it during a eulogy. The norm of assertion is not the only norm governing speech."

Elena: "All right, but shift to third-person retrospective judgments. After Saturday passes and the bank really is open, we look back and say 'Hannah knew it would be open' in the low-stakes case. In the high-stakes case, even after we know the bank was open, it feels wrong to say 'she knew.' That is not about conversational propriety in the moment — it is retrospective. If knowledge were invariant across stakes, we should be equally willing to attribute it after the fact."

Marcus: "I think that is still about the context of the attributor. When we are evaluating the high-stakes case, the stakes are part of the context of our evaluation — they are salient to us as we make the knowledge ascription. So our standards for what counts as knowing go up. But that is a fact about our language, about when we are willing to apply the word 'knows,' not a fact about Hannah's epistemic state. The word 'knows' is context-sensitive, like 'tall.' But how tall someone is does not change when we raise the bar for calling them tall."

Elena: "That is a neat distinction, but I think it collapses under pressure. If 'knows' is context-sensitive in the way you are describing, then the truth of knowledge ascriptions does depend on practical factors — just via the semantics rather than via the metaphysics. But either way, what we can truly say someone knows depends on stakes. And if you are a contextualist about 'knows,' you have already conceded that knowledge ascriptions vary with practical context. That is most of what I am arguing for."

At this point they noticed they had been monopolizing the conversation and broke off, but I have been turning the exchange over in my head since. Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move: at each step, who has the better of the exchange, and what is each person missing or conceding without realizing it? Also, is there a position available that neither of them occupied but that handles the cases better than either of their views?
