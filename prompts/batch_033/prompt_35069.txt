I have been trying to sort out my views on moral luck by working through cases, and I keep finding that my verdicts pull in different directions. I want to know whether there is a principled way to draw the line, or whether some of my verdicts simply have to go.

Here are the cases as I see them.

(1) Two drivers, Adams and Blake, each drink far too much, get behind the wheel, and drive home through residential streets. Adams hits and kills a child who darts out between parked cars. Blake makes it home without incident. I think Adams deserves a harsher sentence, and I also think Adams is morally worse—not just unluckier—than Blake. But I cannot shake the feeling that this is unfair, because the difference between them was entirely outside their control.

(2) Two soldiers, Cain and Dara, are stationed at the same checkpoint. A car approaches. Both soldiers, independently and for the same reasons, decide to shoot. Cain's rifle fires; the driver dies. Dara's rifle jams; the driver survives. I have the same reaction: Cain is worse, Dara is less so, but the only difference is a mechanical failure neither controlled.

(3) Ellis grew up in a stable, affectionate home and developed into a generous, patient person. Farrell grew up in a violent, neglectful home and became mean and impulsive. I think Ellis deserves more moral credit for being the person they are than Farrell deserves blame. But I also think neither chose their upbringing, and that constitutive luck of this sort ought to undermine praise and blame equally.

(4) Geller and Hart are both mid-level managers who learn of a serious safety defect in their company's product. Geller happens to have a boss who is receptive to bad news; Geller reports the defect, it is fixed, and no one is hurt. Hart happens to have a boss who punishes messengers; Hart stays quiet, and a consumer is later injured. I blame Hart more than I blame Geller, but I would have done exactly what Hart did in Hart's circumstances.

(5) Two people, Inez and Jules, each face a moment of temptation to embezzle money from their employer. The amounts and circumstances are identical. Inez resists, through an effort of will. Jules also resists, but for Jules it is effortless—the thought of stealing simply holds no appeal. I admire Inez more than Jules, and I think Inez deserves more credit. But again, the difference between them—how strongly the temptation pulled—was not something either chose.

(6) Kane and Lee are both reckless skiers who go off-piste in an area with known avalanche risk. Kane triggers an avalanche that kills two other skiers. Lee triggers an avalanche that slides harmlessly into an empty valley. I blame Kane far more than Lee, and I think the law should treat them differently. But if I ask myself whether the outcome should make a difference to moral desert, I am not sure it should.

(7) Morgan and Nkosi are both born into wealthy families in a deeply unequal society. Morgan gives away a third of their inheritance to effective charities. Nkosi keeps the whole inheritance and lives comfortably. I praise Morgan, and I do not particularly blame Nkosi—I think Nkosi is simply doing what almost anyone in that position would do. But here the difference between them was chosen, or at least more chosen than in the upbringing case, and yet my asymmetry in praise and blame feels similar.

I am trying to find a principle—or at most a small set of principles—that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. In particular, I want to know: (a) is there a unified account of when outcome luck, circumstantial luck, and constitutive luck should affect praise and blame, and if so, what it is; (b) which of my verdicts a good principle would force me to abandon; and (c) what the principle says about a case I have not listed—a surgeon who follows the same protocol in two operations, but in one the patient dies from an unforeseeable complication and in the other the patient survives.
