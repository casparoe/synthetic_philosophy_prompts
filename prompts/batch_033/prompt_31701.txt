I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether the criminal law may punish conduct that is widely thought immoral but that, by the friend's own admission, harms no one. By the end I was defending something like Hart's harm principle and he was defending something like Devlin's legal moralism, and each of us kept reaching for a new case whenever our own principle gave out. Before I go back to that argument, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory would even have to do.

Write the desiderata for a theory of criminalization, understood as a theory of when the state is morally permitted, required, or forbidden to make conduct a crime and attach punishment, for a modern, religiously and morally pluralistic society. Structure the answer in the following named sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated.

1. The cases (roughly 600 words). Give the canonical cases an adequate theory must classify without absurdity, and say for each what a theory has to deliver about it. Include: private consensual conduct between adults (recreational drug use, selling sex, assisted dying); consensual activities that risk serious harm mainly to the participant (seatbelt and helmet laws, dangerous sports); harmless acts that offend onlookers or desecrate symbols (public blasphemy, flag-burning, desecration of a corpse, consensual cannibalism of the already dead); consensual exploitation (selling an organ, dueling, wages below a legal floor); and conduct that is individually harmless but allegedly harmful through its aggregate effects or through the moral message it sends. For each, specify what a minimally adequate theory must say about it, or at least which combinations of verdicts it cannot hold together.

2. The distinctions (roughly 400 words). State the distinctions a theory must respect and not collapse, with the reason each matters: harm to others versus harm to self; harm versus offense; wrongdoing versus harm; the enforcement of a society's shared positive morality versus the protection of the minimum conditions of social life; private versus public. Say what would be lost if the theory collapsed each one.

3. The fixed points (roughly 300 words). List the facts a theory must be consistent with: that criminal law is coercive and stigmatizing in a way other regulation is not; that the society in question is pluralistic about the good; that any legal system does presuppose some shared moral minimum; and that existing legal systems already draw lines such as consent, de minimis harm, and public order that a theory must explain rather than ignore.

4. Negotiable and non-negotiable (roughly 300 words). Mark which of the above are genuine requirements any theory must meet and which are merely preferences of a particular camp, and explain the difference. Then identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

5. Conflicts (roughly 300 words). Say whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, for example whether a theory can both respect individual liberty in the hard cases and acknowledge that a legal system presupposes a shared moral order. If something has to give, say what it is and why.

6. Scorecard (roughly 300 words). Briefly score Hart's harm principle (the only sound ground for criminalization is preventing harm to others), Devlin's legal moralism (a society may enforce the shared morality that holds it together), and Feinberg's offense principle (preventing serious offense to others can also be a legitimate ground) against the list. Keep this section short: I want the criteria, not a verdict.

Be concrete throughout: name the conduct, the statute-like rule that would or would not criminalize it, and the verdict that follows. I am less interested in which theory wins than in knowing what winning would mean.
