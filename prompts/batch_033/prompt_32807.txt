I've been teaching the Parfit teletransporter case for fifteen years now, and I've gotten pretty good at predicting where my students will land, but last week I had a disconcerting argument with a colleague in the physics department who'd never heard of the thought experiment before. I walked him through it: you step into the scanner on Earth, it records the exact state of every cell in your body, destroys you in the process, and beams the information to Mars, where a replicator builds a molecule-for-molecule copy of you out of new matter. The person on Mars wakes up with all your memories, your personality, your intentions, and goes on with your life. I asked him: is that person you? He said, without hesitation, absolutely not — the original is dead, the thing on Mars is a duplicate, end of story. No amount of fiddling with the causal details changed his mind. It was almost refreshing, actually, how little the philosophical problem had any grip on him.

So here's what I want. I'd like your actual credence — your honest, all-things-considered probability — that the person on Mars is literally you, numerically identical to the person who stepped into the scanner on Earth. Don't give me the standard moves about how it depends on your theory of personal identity. I know it depends on that. I want you to decompose the question: what would have to be true for the answer to be yes, and how likely do you think each of those things is? In particular, I want you to break it down into at least these sub-questions: (1) whether psychological continuity of the right kind is sufficient for personal identity; (2) whether the causal gap introduced by the destruction-and-recreation process matters, even if you grant that psychological continuity otherwise suffices; (3) whether there's some further fact about identity — a soul, a Cartesian ego, a brute animal-continuity requirement — that the teletransporter would violate regardless of what we think about (1) and (2). Give me a probability for each, a one-sentence justification, and then show how you combine them into your overall number. 

Then tell me: which of your sub-questions is your final credence most sensitive to — that is, if you could get a definitive answer to exactly one of them, which would shift your overall number the most? And finally, where do you think my physicist friend and I most likely disagree? My own credence on the Mars person being me is somewhere around 0.65, and his is apparently zero. Which sub-question is doing nearly all the work in that gap?
