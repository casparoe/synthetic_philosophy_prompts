My roommate keeps running this argument by me and I can't figure out whether he's doing a bit or actually broken something. The argument is the old surprise exam paradox, but he's framed it as a proof that certain kinds of announcements are literally impossible to make good on, and he's starting to apply it to things like "I'm going to break up with you sometime this month but you won't see it coming" and "the market will crash on a day nobody expects." He says it shows those statements are self-undermining in the same way. Here's the argument as he gives it:

(1) A teacher tells her class on Friday: "There will be exactly one exam next week, Monday through Friday, and it will be a surprise — meaning you won't know the night before which day it's on."

(2) Suppose the exam were on Friday. Then by Thursday night, the class would know the exam hadn't happened Monday through Thursday, leaving only Friday. So they'd know it's on Friday, which contradicts it being a surprise. So the exam can't be on Friday.

(3) Now suppose the exam were on Thursday. By Wednesday night, the class knows Friday is ruled out (by step 2), and they know Monday through Wednesday have passed without an exam, so only Thursday remains. So they'd know it's on Thursday — again, no surprise. So Thursday is ruled out.

(4) Repeat the same reasoning backward: Wednesday gets ruled out, then Tuesday, then Monday. So no day works. The teacher's announcement can't be fulfilled.

(5) But obviously a teacher can give a surprise exam. The announcement isn't logically impossible to fulfill — teachers do this. So something is wrong with the argument.

(6) But if the argument is wrong, there must be a step where it breaks. And whatever breaks it has to explain why the reasoning feels airtight going forward but somehow doesn't apply to the real situation.

He says the interesting thing is that the same structure shows up whenever someone makes a claim about a future event that depends on the listener's state of knowledge: "you won't guess my next move," "this will happen when you least expect it," etc. He thinks either the argument is valid and those claims really are impossible (which seems false), or there's a subtle mistake in the reasoning that also applies to those other cases.

I want a committed answer: which step do you reject, what exactly is the mistake, and why does the reasoning feel so compelling even though it's wrong? And does the fix you propose also handle his "breakup" and "market crash" cases, or does something different go wrong there?
