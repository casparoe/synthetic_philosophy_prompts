I am a novelist, and after decades of writing and reading fiction I still cannot settle what my emotional responses to fictional characters amount to — the paradox of fiction as I live it, rather than as it appears in the literature. I would like you to extract the principle, if there is one, behind the following verdicts of mine.

When I wrote the death of a character I had lived with for years, I wept, and I do not believe I was pretending or merely imagining weeping; the grief was occurrent and real. Yet it did not have the motivational profile of grief for an actual person: I looked for no funeral to attend and no family to console, and it left no lasting disturbance. Watching a horror film, I feel what I can only call fear — my pulse rises and I flinch — while knowing the monster does not exist and that I am safe in my seat. On a second reading of a novel I still feel suspense at a scene whose outcome I already know. I can be angry at a fictional villain, but the anger is quarantined: I feel no impulse to see him punished, and it dissolves when I close the book. A newspaper account of a real death produces a sadness that lingers and disposes me to act; a more vividly rendered fictional death moves me to tears but carries no such disposition. And a bare summary of a plot — that a child dies, for instance — moves me almost not at all, while the fully realized scene moves me deeply, though the propositional content is the same.

Is there one principle about the cognitive requirements of emotion — in particular, about what one must believe concerning the existence of an object or the occurrence of an event in order to feel something toward it — that captures all of these verdicts? If not, which verdict should I abandon, and why? Please state the principle precisely, test it against the cases above, and then tell me what it predicts for a case I have not listed: the sensation of relief at a near-miss in a story whose happy ending I already know.
