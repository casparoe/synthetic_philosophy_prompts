Question 3 (20 points). Consider Arrow's impossibility theorem for social welfare functions.

(a) Define a social welfare function, and state precisely the four conditions: unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. (6 points)

(b) State the theorem. (2 points)

(c) Using a profile with three voters and three alternatives, show that simple majority rule is defined on the unrestricted domain and satisfies weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship, yet can fail to produce a transitive social ordering. (6 points)

(d) State briefly and accurately what the theorem does and does not establish about the possibility of democratic procedures for aggregating preferences. (6 points)

Student's answer:

(a) A social welfare function is a rule that takes any profile of individual preference orderings and returns a social preference ordering. Unrestricted domain says the rule must be defined for every logically possible profile of individual orderings. Weak Pareto says that when every individual strictly prefers x to y, society strictly prefers x to y. Non-dictatorship says there is no individual i such that, for every pair of alternatives, whenever i strictly prefers x to y the social ordering ranks x above y. Independence of irrelevant alternatives says that if an alternative z is deleted from the set under consideration, the social ranking of the remaining alternatives should not change.

(b) With at least three alternatives and at least two individuals, no social welfare function satisfies all four conditions; equivalently, any social welfare function satisfying unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, and independence of irrelevant alternatives must be dictatorial.

(c) Take the profile in which voter 1 ranks A above B above C, voter 2 ranks B above C above A, and voter 3 ranks C above A above B. Majority rule yields A above B, B above C, and C above A, a cycle, so it fails transitivity. It is defined on every profile; weak Pareto is immediate; non-dictatorship holds because no single voter determines every pairwise comparison; and independence of irrelevant alternatives holds because the majority verdict on any pair depends only on how many voters rank the first above the second, which does not involve any third alternative.

(d) The theorem shows that no voting rule can meet all of Arrow's conditions, and that majority rule is essentially the unique rule that satisfies all of them except transitivity. Any democratic procedure must therefore either restrict the domain of admissible preferences or accept cycles. The theorem is best read as a fundamental limit on democratic aggregation rather than as a defect of majority rule in particular.
