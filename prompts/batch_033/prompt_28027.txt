I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Rationality and Social Knowledge" — about 18 students, mostly philosophy and economics majors, meeting for 75 minutes. I want to run an in-class game that makes them experience an information cascade: the situation where each person, observing the choices of those who acted before them, rationally decides to ignore their own private information and follow the crowd, even though the crowd's choices rest on remarkably little evidence.

Design the activity in full. Include the setup (what materials, what the students know and do not know about the state of the world), the rules (who moves in what order, what each person observes before deciding), a payoff scheme that does not require real money, and a timeline that fits a 75-minute session with multiple rounds. The activity should be structured so that cascades form naturally from the students' own reasoning — they should feel the pull of the inference themselves before anyone names it, and ideally they should be surprised, on reflection, at how little information the group's consensus actually rested on.

Then provide a debrief plan. It should map specific moments in the activity onto the formal structure: private signals, the Bayesian updating rule that a rational agent would use, the point at which the accumulated public information overwhelms any single private signal, and why a rational agent at that point should disregard their own signal even if it points the other way. Also address the following:

(a) What predictably goes wrong the first time someone runs this — students who try to be contrarian for its own sake, students who do not understand the probabilistic reasoning and treat it as a guessing game, cascades that form too quickly or never break — and how to handle each.

(b) What the activity cannot reproduce about real-world information cascades, so that I know what I will need to supply in discussion rather than expecting the game to teach it.

(c) How to use the debrief to distinguish information cascades sharply from related but distinct phenomena — conformity, reputation-based herding, and groupthink — so that students leave with a precise sense of what makes an information cascade specifically an information cascade, and why the rationality of the individual decisions is part of what makes the phenomenon unsettling.
