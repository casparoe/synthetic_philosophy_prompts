I teach a philosophy of mind course for advanced undergrads — about 15 students, we have a 75-minute session. We've been reading Dennett and Hofstadter on whether higher-level patterns (beliefs, desires, symbols) can genuinely push the physical substrate around, or whether all the causation is bottom-up and the patterns are just shorthand. You know the careenium thought experiment — the frictionless domino-like tiles in a grid where a "shiner" pattern emerges and seems to steer things, but really it's just tile-bumping all the way down. And the simmballs — the simulated baseball world where the batter's "intent to swing" seems causal but is it?

I want an in-class activity that makes them *feel* this tension physically, not just argue about it. Something where they are the substrate — they're following simple local rules — and a higher-level pattern emerges that seems to be calling the shots. Then in the debrief we can ask: were you pushed around by the pattern, or were you just following your local rule and the pattern happened to be what it looked like from above?

Constraints: the room is a seminar room with movable chairs but not a ton of open floor space. No special materials beyond paper, maybe some colored cards. The activity itself should run about 25–30 minutes, leaving plenty of time for the debrief. They shouldn't hear the words "careenium" or "simmballs" until after.

What I need from you: the full setup — the rules they follow, how the "pattern" emerges, what roles if any, how to run it step by step. Then the debrief: how to map what happened onto the philosophical distinction, the questions to ask them, the moments in the activity that correspond to specific moves in the argument. And be honest: what part of the real philosophical issue does this activity inevitably distort or miss? What will go wrong the first time I run it, and how do I recover?
