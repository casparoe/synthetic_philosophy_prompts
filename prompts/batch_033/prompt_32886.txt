I spent forty years in a field where you couldn't sign off on anything without a license, so maybe I'm primed to notice this. We wouldn't let a random person remove a gallbladder, design a bridge, or land a plane full of passengers; we demand demonstrated competence. But the very same person can vote on trade policy, nuclear energy, monetary policy, or a pandemic response, and their vote counts exactly as much as an epidemiologists. And we're not consistent about it: we deny the vote to children and, in some places, to felons, but the idea of a competence test for adults is treated as outrageous. What view of political judgment does that pattern presuppose? Does it have to assume political decisions are different in kind from technical ones, or that the franchise is about something besides getting the answer right, or that ordinary voters are secretly competent enough that the comparison is unfair? I've been reading about epistocracy and I can't tell whether our practice is committed to a view or just never worked one out. Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and tell me what would actually have to change in practice if we took the epistemic view seriously. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
