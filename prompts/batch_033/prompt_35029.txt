I am the standing rules drafter for a federation of tenant-run housing cooperatives. The federation's rulebook is amended by majority vote at quarterly member assemblies, and because the rulebook itself contains the rules that say how it is amended, once or twice a year an assembly tries to change the amendment rules themselves: raising or lowering the voting threshold, making some rule unamendable, or adding a rule about how rules may be proposed. Twice in the past decade a badly drafted amendment clause produced a year of dispute over whether a later rule change was even valid, so the federation board has asked me to draft a reusable screening procedure for amendment and entrenchment clauses before they reach the floor.

Write that procedure, grounded in what is actually known about self-reference in rule systems: the Nomic problem of a rule that governs its own amendment, and the legal paradoxes of self-amendment, self-repeal, and entrenchment. The procedure must be runnable by a cooperative secretary with no legal training in under an hour. It should walk through the questions to ask in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure returns send it back to the proposer, where it returns safe to draft, and where it hands the decision to the chairperson or a lawyer because no amount of drafting can remove the problem. In particular it must catch, and give guidance on, the following hazards: a clause that applies to itself without saying so; a clause that entrenches a rule or a voting threshold without saying whether the entrenchment is itself entrenched; a clause that would be used to amend the very rule that authorizes its own use; and a clause that can be read two ways with different legal effects. For each hazard the procedure should say whether it is a genuine paradox or only a drafting ambiguity with a determinate answer, and how to tell the difference.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals of the kind the assemblies actually bring: a proposal to make the quorum rule unamendable except by unanimous vote; a proposal, moved under the current two-thirds amendment rule, to lower the amendment threshold itself to a simple majority; and a proposal to add a rule stating that any rule may be repealed by the same vote that enacted it. Include the case where the procedure returns an answer the board will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle and must leave to someone's judgment.
