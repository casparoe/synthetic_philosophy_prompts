Please give me your credence that the Principal Principle — the claim that a rational agent's credence in a proposition, conditional on the proposition that its objective chance is x together with any admissible evidence, should equal x — is correct as a fundamental norm of epistemic rationality, not merely a useful heuristic. I am asking about the principle as applied to the kinds of objective chance that appear in our best physical theories (quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics), not about a deflationary or merely epistemic reading of "chance."

I would like your credence decomposed into at least the following sub-questions. For each, assign a rough probability and a single sentence of justification.

1. That there is a coherent, non-epistemic notion of objective chance that applies to the events our physical theories describe — i.e., that chance is a genuine feature of the world and not merely a way of encoding our ignorance in deterministic systems.

2. That, given such a notion, the correct chance-credence norm takes the form of a conditional equality (credence equals chance) rather than some other functional relationship — e.g., one that permits systematic divergence between chance and rational credence.

3. That the Principal Principle survives the problem of undermining futures: that there is a satisfactory formulation that avoids contradiction in cases where the chance of an event depends on future credences that are themselves constrained by the principle, as arises on Humean or best-system accounts of chance.

4. That the distinction between admissible and inadmissible evidence can be drawn in a principled and non-circular way — i.e., without simply defining "admissible" as "whatever evidence the principle works for."

5. That the Principal Principle, even if correct for idealized agents, is the right norm for bounded agents like us, rather than being an idealization with no normative force for non-ideal reasoners.

Then state how you combine these probabilities to arrive at your overall credence — e.g., whether you treat them as independent and multiply, or whether you model dependencies among them — and give the resulting number.

Finally, identify which sub-question your overall credence is most sensitive to, and describe one piece of evidence or argument — something we might realistically obtain — that would shift your credence on that sub-question the most, and in which direction.
