I serve on the grants committee of a small family foundation in a midsized city. Each year we distribute a fixed sum among local programs, mostly for children and families, and over the past few years I have noticed that my votes do not follow any rule I can state. They are not "do the most good with the money," and they are not "make things as equal as possible." I have no training in philosophy, and I have been trying to work this out on my own, but I keep going in circles. Below are the cases that have stayed with me, with my honest verdicts.

Case A. Two after-school programs each request $20,000. One serves children from families below the poverty line; the other serves children from comfortable families. The money would buy the same materials and reach the same number of children, but the program for the poorer families is harder to administer, so each dollar is expected to produce somewhat less measurable benefit. I vote to fund the poorer program.

Case B. A proposal would make the distribution of programs more equal by shutting down a well-attended summer program in an affluent neighborhood, with no plan to move the money anywhere useful; the original donor would simply keep the funds. No child elsewhere gains anything. I vote against it. I feel a faint pull toward "at least the gap would be smaller," but I cannot treat that as a reason to make some children worse off for nobody's benefit.

Case C. We must choose between two $20,000 proposals. One would give roughly five hundred already-comfortable seniors a pleasant museum outing. The other would pay several months of rent and a security deposit for two families who would otherwise remain homeless. The total benefit, counted plainly, seems larger for the seniors; I vote for the two families. The depth of the need outweighs the number of people helped.

Case D. A proposal would move funds from a program for the working poor — people above the poverty line but barely getting by — to a program for the destitute, people on the street. Because of how the two programs are structured, for every $100 moved, only about $15 would reach the destitute; the rest would be eaten by transition costs. I am genuinely torn. In the end I vote no; the waste is simply too large. But I notice this sits uneasily with Case C.

Case E. Two programs serve children who are both badly off, but one group is somewhat worse off than the other: families in emergency shelter versus families doubled up in unsafe housing. The money would do roughly equal measurable good for each group. I lean toward the emergency-shelter group, though not as strongly as I do when the choice is between badly off and comfortable.

Case F. A proposal would reduce the gap between upper-middle-class and wealthy families by giving extra help to the upper-middle-class children at the expense of the wealthy children, without changing anything for anyone genuinely badly off. I do not see the point. The gap between those two groups does not move me.

The closest I have come to a statement of my view is that I care about how badly off people are, not about equality as such. But that cannot be the whole story: in Case E both groups are badly off and I still lean toward the worse-off one, and in Case D I balk at waste even though the money would reach the worst off. I would like to know whether there is one principle that captures all of this, or nearly all of it. If it cannot capture everything, I want to know which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and why. I would also like the principle tested against cases I have not listed.

You are a professional philosopher who specializes in distributive justice. Please think carefully. Begin by brainstorming the main candidate principles someone in my position might be relying on; state each precisely and say which of my cases it handles well and which it handles badly. Then give your best proposal: the principle, or small set of candidates, that best fits my verdicts, stated precisely and in plain language, with the case that motivates each clause. For any verdict it cannot accommodate, say so honestly and explain what the principle says I should think instead. Finally, apply the principle to two or three new cases, including at least one where its answer is surprising or uncomfortable. Do not assume I know the academic literature; define any technical terms you use, and do not give me a reading list.
