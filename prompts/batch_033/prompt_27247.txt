So I've been talking with some friends about longtermism and someone brought up infinite ethics and now I can't stop thinking about it. The basic problem seems clear enough — if you've got infinitely many future people, or infinite value at stake across possible worlds, then adding up or comparing expected values stops making sense in the usual way. And I get why that matters if you're trying to reason about the far future or about multiverse scenarios or anything where infinities might show up. But here's my frustration: every time I try to actually read the literature, people are already deep into proposing fixes. Hyperreals! Expansionism! Value density! And I'm sitting there thinking, wait, what are we even asking a theory to do here? Like, what's the job description?

So I want to back up. Before I read one more paper that says "just use surreal numbers" or "just discount by spatial curvature" or whatever, I want someone to write the desiderata. What does an adequate theory of infinite ethics actually have to get right? I want the full list of requirements — the cases it has to handle (infinite populations, infinite time horizons, infinite value per location, mixtures of these), the paradoxes it has to dissolve or at least not make worse, the intuitive principles it can and can't sacrifice (Pareto, the idea that adding happy people shouldn't make the world worse, the idea that you shouldn't be able to get from a bad world to a good one just by rearranging locations, etc.), and which mathematical constraints are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are just someone's aesthetic preference for real numbers. And if some subset of these requirements are jointly unsatisfiable — like if you literally cannot have a total order over infinite worlds that respects both Pareto and anonymity — that's the part I most want to see, because then the whole project shifts from "find the right theory" to "decide which bullet to bite and why."

I'm not asking for a survey of existing approaches. The deliverable is the list of requirements itself, with the case or consideration behind each one, and a clear flag on which are hard constraints and which are negotiable. If you want to briefly gesture at how the main contenders fare against the list that's fine but keep it short — the thinking is in the criteria, not the scorecard. And at the very end, give me a one-sentence summary as the last line, enclosed like \answer{...}.
