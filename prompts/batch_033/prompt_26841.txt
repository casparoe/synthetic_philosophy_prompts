I've come to hold that the right diagnosis of Pascal's mugging is that expected utility theory with an unbounded utility function is rationally committed to paying the mugger, and that the only principled fix is to bound utility rather than to gerrymander the prior or to abandon expected utility reasoning whenever it delivers uncomfortable verdicts. I want to know what would count against this. Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them a defender of the bounded-utility diagnosis should themselves concede count against it. In particular, address: arguments that the defect lies in the prior, or in the very idea that a sufficiently tiny probability can be traded off against a sufficiently large payoff, rather than in unbounded utility; the costs of the bounded-utility fix itself, including its arbitrariness and the verdicts it gives in cases of unbounded but intuitively comparable value, and whether it merely relocates the problem when payoffs are infinite; and any decision-theoretic results or cases, St. Petersburg-type cases included, that sharpen, dissolve, or complicate the diagnosis. Finally, assess whether the view has been insulated by the qualification that it is only a claim about ideal rationality and not about what anyone should actually do, and whether that insulation is itself a problem.
