The debate about the semantics of indicative conditionals has been circling the same territory since at least the 1970s. Adams's Thesis — that the probability of an indicative conditional is the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent — has enormous intuitive appeal and handles a wide range of cases. But Lewis's triviality results showed that, on standard assumptions, no propositional connective can satisfy the Thesis across a nontrivial probability space. The responses have calcified into a few stable positions: the Thesis holds but conditionals don't express propositions (they are devices for conditional speech acts); the Thesis is false as a semantic claim but true as a pragmatic one; the Thesis holds but we need to revise classical probability theory; the Thesis is simply false, and our intuitions are being explained away. None of these camps has made much headway against the others in decades, and the debate sometimes feels like a ritual reenactment rather than a live dispute. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it a disagreement about the data — about which linguistic intuitions are central and which are edge cases to be explained away? Is it a disagreement about what formal constraints are non-negotiable? Is it a difference in what each side thinks a semantic theory has to deliver? Or is it something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new formal result, a different way of framing the question, or an acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed?
