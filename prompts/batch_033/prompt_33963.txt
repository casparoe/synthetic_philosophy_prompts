Ok so I've been going in circles on this thing and I want to see it played out. You know how if someone says "It's raining but I don't believe it's raining," something feels really wrong about it? But it's not like it's a contradiction — it could be true. Like, I could be wrong about what I believe, or it could be raining and I just havent looked outside yet. So why does it sound so messed up?

I've come up with two ways of thinking about it and I can't decide between them.

One way: the problem is that when you assert something, you're expressing a belief. So when you say "it's raining but I don't believe it," you're expressing both the belief that it's raining and the belief that you don't believe it's raining. And those two beliefs can't really coexist in a rational mind — not because it's logically impossible, but because if you believe p, you can't also believe you don't believe p. That's the tension.

The other way: it's not about what's in your head at all. It's about the rules of conversation. When you say something, you're committing yourself to it being true, and part of that commitment is that you take yourself to believe it. So saying "p but I don't believe p" is like making a promise and then immediately saying you can't keep it. It's a violation of the norms of assertion, not a contradiction in your beliefs.

I want to see a dialogue between someone holding the first view and someone holding the second view. Make them both sharp and make them actually respond to each other — no strawmen. I want to see where the real disagreement is and whether they end up talking past each other or if one view actually handles the cases better. Maybe throw in some variations too, like "It's raining but I believe it's not raining" or past-tense versions like "It was raining but I didn't believe it was raining." I feel like those test different things.
