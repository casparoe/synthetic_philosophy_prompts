I am preparing to interview a philosopher who defends G.E.M. Anscombe's argument, set out in her 1956 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree" and related writings, that Harry Truman was a murderer for authorizing the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The interview is for a philosophy journal, and the readership is philosophically literate but not all specialists in just war theory or Anscombe scholarship. I want the questions to press hard on the argument's weakest joints rather than letting the interviewee restate the position. Draft eight to ten questions, each of which identifies a specific premise or move in Anscombe's argument, anticipates the most natural reply the interviewee would give, and includes a follow-up that presses past that reply. I want at least one question on each of the following pressure points: the combatant–noncombatant distinction as applied to a society organized for total war, where civilians work in munitions factories and the line between military and civilian production blurs; the intention–foresight distinction as applied to the bombings, given what we know about the targeting rationale and the fact that the cities were chosen partly for their concentrated civilian populations; the counterfactual claim about the casualties a land invasion would have produced, including the question of whether the bombs were needed at all given the state of Japanese surrender feelers; the asymmetry between atomic bombing and the conventional firebombing of cities that had already occurred on a comparable scale, and whether Anscombe's argument commits her to condemning those as well; and the problem of attributing sole responsibility to Truman rather than distributing it across the chain of command, the scientists, and the broader political structure. Make sure the final question in the sequence leaves the interviewee with something they cannot dismiss by gesturing at a distinction they have already drawn.
