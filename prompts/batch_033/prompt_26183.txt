A few years ago, I joined with five neighbors to turn an abandoned lot into a community vegetable garden. We had no formal structure — no charter, no votes, no written rules. We simply started working on it together. Over time, rhythms and expectations emerged: who bought seeds, who watered when, how we decided what to plant each season. Decisions were always made by conversation, until we reached a rough consensus. It never occurred to us to formalize anything, because things ran smoothly and it genuinely felt like a shared enterprise — something we were doing together, not merely alongside one another.

Recently, two members announced that they intend to convert roughly half the garden space into a seating area with a fire pit — something more like a shared backyard for social gatherings. They have already begun pulling up vegetable beds. Three of us strongly object: the purpose of the garden, as we have always understood it, was growing food. The sixth person says she is indifferent. The two argue that since there was never any binding agreement, no one has standing to tell them what they can and cannot do with a plot they have put just as many hours of labor into as anyone else. They add that if the rest of us are unhappy, we are free to walk away — but they are not going to stop.

I am not satisfied with this, but I am struggling to articulate why, precisely because they seem right about the facts: there is no contract, no set of rules we all signed, nothing legal to appeal to. And yet I feel that something more than a preference has been violated — that the very character of what we were doing together imposes obligations they are now ignoring. Is there a philosophically coherent way to make sense of that feeling? And if there is, what follows — do the rest of us have any legitimate claim beyond simply walking away?
