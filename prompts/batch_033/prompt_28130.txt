I spent my career in neurobiology, and since retiring I've been working through the philosophy of mind literature — Dennett in particular. I recently rewatched Michel Gondry's *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* and found myself thinking about it in terms of the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model.

For anyone who hasn't seen it recently: the film's premise is a procedure that selectively erases memories — here, all memories of a failed relationship. Most of the film unfolds inside the protagonist Joel's mind as the erasure proceeds, with his memories degrading, blending into one another, and sometimes being deliberately hidden by his "internal" self in an attempt to preserve them.

What struck me is that the film seems to stage a tension between two pictures of the mind. On one hand, the very premise — target a memory, locate it, delete it — presupposes something like a Cartesian theater: memories as discrete objects presented to an inner observer who can point and say "that one." On the other, the way the erasure actually plays out — memories bleeding into each other, the self fragmenting into multiple agents with conflicting goals, the impossibility of clean excision — looks much more like the multiple drafts model, where there is no final draft, no central audience, and no neatly bounded representational items to delete.

I'd like a reading of the film that takes both the film and the Dennett seriously. Specifically:

- Where does the film's own logic commit it to a Cartesian theater, and where does it undermine that picture?
- Does the film offer anything to the philosophical debate that Dennett's arguments alone don't? That is, does the visual, narrative form do work that propositional argument cannot?
- Are there scenes where the film seems to endorse a view of consciousness that Dennett would reject, and if so, is that a flaw in the film or a challenge to the theory?
- What does the film's ending — where Joel and Clementine, having heard the tapes of each other's grievances, choose to try again — say about personal identity and narrative selfhood that connects to the multiple drafts model?

I'm writing an essay for a small philosophy-and-film group, and I want to avoid both the trap of using the film as mere illustration and the trap of forcing the theory onto scenes that resist it.
