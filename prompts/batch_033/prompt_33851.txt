I am a professor of philosophy and a member of my university's academic integrity board. We adjudicate cases of plagiarism, exam cheating, falsified data, and unauthorized collaboration, and we recommend sanctions up to and including expulsion. The board's deliberations almost always turn on character: we ask whether the student shows remorse, whether this was a one-time lapse or points to a pattern, whether the student is the kind of person who would do this again. I have taught the situationist critique of virtue ethics for years — the work of John Doris and Gilbert Harman, the Milgram obedience experiments, the Darley and Batson Good Samaritan study, the finding that discovering a dime in a phone booth dramatically increases helping behavior — and I have grown increasingly uncomfortable sitting in these hearings watching my colleagues draw exactly the kind of cross-situational character inferences that the situationist evidence calls into question.

But I cannot simply tell the board that stable character traits are a fiction and we should stop making judgments. We still have to decide cases. What I need is a decision procedure that takes the situationist critique seriously without being paralyzed by it.

Please write me a procedure our board can follow, grounded in what the situationist critique actually licenses and what it does not. The procedure should be a sequence of questions we ask about each case, in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each question. It should force us to check for situational explanations before we reach for character explanations, and it should tell us at what point the residual — what remains after we have accounted for the situation — is serious enough to warrant a sanction. It should flag the steps where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and a human being must decide.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases:

1. A first-semester freshman copied a paragraph from an online source without attribution in a paper for a course in which the professor never discussed citation standards, the class had four hundred students, and the student had no prior record.

2. A senior paid someone to write a paper for them in a small upper-division seminar whose professor had discussed academic integrity on the syllabus and in the first class session, and the student had previously been warned for minor plagiarism in another course.

3. A student with a clean prior record texted exam answers to a friend during a remotely proctored exam. The course had shifted to remote format mid-semester because of a public health emergency, the exam instructions did not address collaboration, and the student had no prior infractions.

For each case, tell me where the procedure lands, and flag whether the answer is one the board will find uncomfortable. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of cases it will mishandle, what assumptions it smuggles in, and what a virtue ethicist would say we have lost by adopting it.
