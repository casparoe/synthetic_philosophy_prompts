Here's a thing I've never managed to square. In a lot of Theravada Buddhist countries, when someone dies the family does merit transfer: they give food to monks, donate to temples, sometimes release animals, and then they formally dedicate the merit of those good deeds to the dead person, explicitly so the deceased's next rebirth goes better. But these are the same traditions that insist (a) that each person's karma is strictly their own and can't be inherited or reassigned, and (b) that there's no permanent self that carries over from one life to the next anyway. So the practice seems to presuppose that moral desert is transferable between people — or between lives — at the very same time the doctrine says that can't happen. And yet the people doing it don't seem to experience this as a contradiction.

What theory of moral desert and personal identity does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view carefully: is the merit being thought of as a debt that can be signed over, or as something causal (the ritual changes the deceased's mental state or karmic situation), or are the donor and recipient not really seperate in the relevant sense? Say which parts of the practice strictly require that view and which parts are compatible with several rival views. Then tell me what the practice would have to give up if you replaced the underlying view with a strict individual-desert, no-transfer account — just the dedication formula, or the whole idea that the living can help the dead at all? And is there a version of the no-self doctrine that makes merit transfer more coherent rather than less?
