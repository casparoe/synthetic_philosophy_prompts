I've been arguing with my friend about program equilibrium — you know, the idea from game theory that if players submit programs rather than just choosing actions, you can get mutual cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma without any external enforcement. The standard move is that each player submits a program that says something like "I cooperate if and only if your program is equivalent to mine," and then mutual cooperation becomes an equilibrium because any deviation gets punished by the conditional. So far so good.

But here's what we actually got stuck on. What does it *mean* for one program to "read" the other program? There seem to be at least two different things you could mean. One is full source-code access: you get to inspect the other player's commitment in its entirety, logic and all, before you finalize your own action. The other is black-box probing: you can only test the other player's commitment by feeding it hypothetical scenarios and seeing what it outputs, but you never get to see its internal structure. My friend thinks this distinction doesn't matter for the cooperation result; I think it might matter a lot, because probing can get you into infinite regress or self-reference problems that source-code inspection avoids.

I want you to build me a thought experiment that isolates this distinction and shows whether it actually makes a difference. Here are the specs:

- Two players in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, but they can write down conditional commitments before playing. No mediator, no AI, no exotic tech — frame it as two business partners deciding whether to invest in each other's side projects. They can each write a note that says something like "I'll invest if and only if you do X," and they exchange these notes before making their final decisions.
- Build two versions of the scenario. In the first version, each partner can read the other's full note — the whole text, all the conditionals, the logic. In the second version, each partner can only "probe" the other's note: they can ask "what would you do if I did Y?" and get an answer, but they never see the note itself. Everything else about the setup stays the same.
- Show that in the full-reading version, mutual cooperation is a program equilibrium. In the probing-only version, show that mutual cooperation fails to be an equilibrium — or at least that you can't get there with the same kind of program. Walk through the reasoning for each version, stating what each player's commitment says and why the equilibrium verdict differs.
- Then tell me which feature of the setup is doing the work. If a defender of the probing-only version wanted to argue that the full-reading version is cheating because in real life you can never fully read someone's conditional policy — you can only ever test it — would they have a point? And if you removed the probes and made the notes just unconditional declarations ("I'll invest" or "I won't"), does the whole thing collapse back into a standard prisoner's dilemma, or is there still something interesting going on?

I read the original Tennenholtz paper and some of the later stuff on Löbian cooperation but I feel like I'm still fuzzy on what the *minimal* informational requirement for the cooperation result actually is, and I think a concrete worked scenario would finally make it click.
