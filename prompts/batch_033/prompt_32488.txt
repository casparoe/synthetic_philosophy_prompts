(1) Bayesian epistemology is the right normative theory of reasoning under uncertainty — credences should be probabilities, updating should be by conditionalization, and Dutch books show why.

(2) Logical omniscience is an idealization we can't meet. No finite agent can assign probability 1 to all logical truths or keep their credences closed under logical consequence.

(3) Logical induction (in the sense of the Garrabrant et al. paper) gives us a computable procedure that approximates Bayesian reasoning over logically uncertain facts, and it's the best we've got for describing how a bounded agent should assign credences to mathematical statements.

(4) There is a fact of the matter about what credence you should have in an unproven mathematical conjecture — it's not just subjective or a matter of taste. P ≠ NP has some objective rational credence given current evidence, even if we can't compute it directly.

(5) Dutch book arguments are the right way to justify probabilistic coherence. If your credences aren't probabilities, you're vulnerable to a sure loss, and that's what makes them irrational.

(6) "Ought implies can" is true, at least for epistemic norms. You can't be required to do something computationally impossible.

(7) Probabilism — the view that credences ought to satisfy the probability axioms — is non-negotiable. Giving it up means giving up Bayesian epistemology entirely.
