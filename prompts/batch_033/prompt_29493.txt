I am a high school student writing a term paper for a class on classical Chinese philosophy, and I want to use the Mencius–Xunzi debate about human nature to analyze Philip Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment. My understanding of the two positions is roughly this: Mencius held that human nature is good, because people are born with incipient moral dispositions — compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong — that need a nourishing environment and reflection to mature; Xunzi held that human nature is bad, because people are born with desires that pull toward conflict and self-interest, and virtue must therefore be built from outside through deliberate effort, teachers, and ritual. I keep going back and forth about which view the experiment actually supports.

The experiment took ordinary college students, randomly assigned them to be guards or prisoners in a mock prison, and within days the guards were humiliating and abusing the prisoners, while many prisoners became passive and distressed; the study was ended early, well before its planned two weeks. I do not want a simple verdict about who was right about human nature. What I want is a careful mapping: what each position would actually have predicted about the guards, the prisoners, and Zimbardo himself; which features of the episode each position explains well and which it has to strain to explain; what each thinker would say the experimenters should have done once the abuse became visible; and what the episode, taken as a whole, shows about the Mencius–Xunzi debate — does it confirm one side, show that the two positions were asking different questions, or reveal something neither anticipated?
