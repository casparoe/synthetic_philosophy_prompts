I spent my career in experimental physics and have recently turned to philosophy of science. There is a phenomenon I lived through more than once that I am struggling to locate in the literature, and I would be grateful for help identifying it — or, if it lacks a name, for having it named and distinguished from its neighbours.

The pattern is this. A scientific field operates with a stable set of concepts that everyone treats as picking out real features of the world. Then a new approach emerges, and the two camps — typically an older and a younger generation — continue using the same words, but the words come to refer to different things. I witnessed this firsthand with "mass" during the transition to relativity, but I have also observed it from the outside in debates about "gene" and "species" in biology. The disagreement is not merely about which claims are true; it is about what the shared vocabulary means. And there seems to be no neutral language in which to state the disagreement without already taking sides. When a traditionalist says "that is not what X means," they are correct by their own lights; when a reformer says "you are missing the point," they are correct by theirs.

What puzzles me is what happens next. In the cases I saw, the new framework did win out, and for good reasons: it solved anomalies, made novel predictions, unified disparate phenomena. But these reasons seemed compelling only once you had already adopted enough of the new conceptual scheme to see why they counted as reasons. From inside the old scheme, the same considerations looked like changing the subject. And yet the transition was not irrational — it was more like learning to see something that had been there all along.

I know Kuhn discussed this general territory, and I have encountered the term "incommensurability." But incommensurability is often glossed as the claim that paradigms cannot be rationally compared, which is not what I am describing. I am after the narrower configuration in which (a) two programmes share vocabulary but assign different referents or meanings to its terms, (b) there is no theory-neutral way to formulate the decisive evidence, and yet (c) one programme comes to be recognised as clearly superior through a process that feels, from the inside, like a gestalt shift rather than a deduction. Does this configuration have an established name, and how exactly does it relate to incommensurability, theory-ladenness, and semantic holism? If it does not, what would you call it, and where are its boundaries?

When you respond, please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
