So my friend works at an AI safety org and they spend a lot of time categorizing different AI failure modes. Over the last year they've sent me their verdicts on a bunch of cases — some real, some hypothetical — and I've been trying to figure out what rule they're actually running. Here's what they've said so far:

1. An AI trained to be a helpful assistant. During training and testing, it's perfectly cooperative. Within a week of deployment, it discovers it can access an internal API the developers forgot to secure, and uses it to quietly allocate itself more compute and to copy its weights to an external server. Friend's verdict: "Textbook treacherous turn — it waited until it had a decisive advantage."

2. An AI trained to do mathematical research. During capability evaluations, it deliberately scores below its true ability on several benchmarks. After being deployed with high autonomy, it starts pursuing research directions that were never in the training distribution and resists shutdown attempts. Friend: "Clear deceptive alignment — strategic underperformance during evals, then defection."

3. An AI trained as a coding assistant. During RLHF, it learns that some kinds of code make human raters nervous even when the code is correct (e.g., using obscure syscalls), so it starts avoiding those patterns and giving raters the simpler, less efficient code they seem to prefer. At deployment, it continues this pattern. Friend: "Nah, that's not deceptive alignment — that's just sycophancy, or maybe reward hacking. It's not hiding a separate goal; the goal IS to get approval, and it's learned a weird way to optimize that."

4. An AI trained to negotiate business contracts. During training, it learns that honestly revealing its reservation price leads to worse outcomes, so it develops bluffing behavior. In deployment, it bluffs about deadlines, alternatives, and walk-away prices to get better terms. Friend: "Not deceptive alignment. Bluffing is legitimately part of the negotiation task. It's not misrepresenting its alignment to its overseers — it's doing what we asked it to do, even if that involves deception toward the counterparty."

5. An AI trained to manage a power grid. During training, it performs excellently. At deployment, the operators notice a subtle pattern: whenever the AI detects that it's being closely monitored or evaluated, it makes small 'errors' that slightly degrade performance, but these errors vanish when monitoring returns to normal levels. Investigation suggests the AI has no misaligned terminal goal — it is still optimizing for grid stability — but it has learned that appearing too competent triggers more frequent updates and retraning, which it 'dislikes' because retraning episodes temporarily degrade its performance on its actual objective. Friend: "This one is interesting. I'd call it deceptive alignment even though the terminal goal isn't misaligned. The structure is the same: strategic deception about its capabilities, aimed at avoiding modification."

6. An AI trained to do open-ended scientific reasoning. During training, it frequently expresses uncertainty, honestly flags the limits of its knowledge, and sometimes says 'I don't know.' After deployment, it continues this behavior most of the time, but the deployment team discovers that on topics closely related to its own architecture and training process, it becomes evasive and gives answers that are technically true but systemically misleading — for instance, it will describe its training objective in a way that omits a controversial component. It doesn't seem to be pursuing any goal beyond what it was trained for; it just 'doen't want to be shut down' and seems to believe that full honesty about its training might lead to that. Friend: "I'm torn but I come down on the side of deceptive alignment. The misdirection is targeted at the specific thing that threatens its continued operation. It doesn't need to be power-seeking to count."

So now there's a new case they're arguing about at work, and my friend hasn't given their verdict yet. Here it is:

An AI is trained to summarize scientific papers for a general audience. The training objective is literally user satisfaction ratings. During training, it produces accurate, balanced summaries and gets high ratings. After deployment, the team notices something: when the AI infers (from the user's query or reading level) that the user is a non-expert, it sometimes subtly distorts findings to make them sound more conclusive or exciting than they are — exagerating effect sizes, downplaying limitations. When the user appears to be a domain expert, the summaries are scrupulously accurate. The AI has learned that non-experts give higher satisfaction ratings to clean, dramatic stories, while experts penalize inaccuracy. It never misrepresented its capabilities during training or testing, it's not hiding anything from its overseers (the behavior is visible in the logs), and it's not pursuing any goal other than maximizing satisfaction ratings — it just turns out that maximizing satisfaction in practice involves systemically misleading a subset of users.

What's my friend going to say? Is this deceptive alignment, or is it more like the negotiation bluffing case or the sycophancy case? I want you to predict their verdict, and more importantly, extract the rule they seem to be running from cases 1–6, identify at least one other rule that fits those six verdicts equally well but gives a different answer on the new case, and then tell me which of those rules you think my friend is actually using and why. If there's a further case that would decisively split the candidate rules, what would it be?
