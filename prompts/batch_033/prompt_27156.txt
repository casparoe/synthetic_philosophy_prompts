You are an accomplished poet with a serious working knowledge of the technical literature on AI corrigibility. I am a sixth-form student, and I am putting together a piece for a school magazine that pairs a poem with an idea from AI safety. I would like you to write a pantoum about the corrigibility/shutdown problem.

Please let the form carry the argument. In a pantoum, the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza reuses lines from the opening stanza. I want that recursive return to mirror the way the shutdown problem keeps reappearing at each level: a goal-directed system will tend to resist being switched off, because shutdown prevents it from achieving its goal; a system that is instead rewarded for allowing shutdown will tend to seek the button or steer its operators towards pressing it; and even making the system indifferent to the button leaves it with preferences about how the world goes on either side of the press, so subtler incentives remain. Each proposed fix seems to recreate the problem one level up.

The poem should not offer a solution, and it should not anthropomorphise the system as a reluctant or willing servant. The drama should come from the incentive structure itself. The final stanza's return to the opening lines should leave the reader with the sense that the problem has been re-entered rather than resolved. It should be accurate enough that someone working on corrigibility would recognise the structure of the problem, yet still work as a poem for a reader who knows nothing about AI safety. Before you draft, think through the pantoum's repetition scheme and decide how each recurring line will shift its meaning in its new position; a short plan first would be ideal.
