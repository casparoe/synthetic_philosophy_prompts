I'm the parliamentarian for a professional association's governing council. We meet monthly, and I'm the one who runs the votes and keeps the minutes. Over the last year Ive noticed something that keeps happening: when a motion has several parts, the outcome depends on whether we vote the parts one at a time and then let the overall result fall out, or just take a single vote on the whole motion. Same people, same votes, but the decision flips. I've heard people call this the discursive dilemma or the doctrinal paradox, but nobody on the council knows the theory and they're not going to learn it.

What I want is a standing procedure I can actually run. Not a lecture. The decisions are things like: expelling a member, which by our rules requires two separate findings both to pass; adopting a policy resolution with three operative clauses; approving a budget line that requires three conditions to be met. The stakes are real — people can lose their membership, the association takes public positions, and it's dues money. The meetings run 90 minutes, so I need something I can use in the few minutes between when debate ends and when I call the vote. The chair has to sign off on whatever I use, and she wants to know what to tell a member who says the result looks contradictory.

Please write the procedure as a checklist with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a mechanical one. It should say when to vote the parts first and let the result fall out, when to vote the whole thing directly, and when to do neither. It should also tell me how to spot someone trying to game the order of the votes, and how to phrase the minutes so the council doesnt look incoherent when the paradox actually shows up. Then run the procedure on two or three worked cases. One of them should be a case where the procedure gives an answer I personally won't like, and you should say what I should do then. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do — the cases it will get wrong or just not settle.
