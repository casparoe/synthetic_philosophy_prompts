In a seminar on Kierkegaard's treatment of the binding of Isaac, the phrase 'the teleological suspension of the ethical' kept getting invoked as if it settled the argument. It seemed to be doing several jobs at once: describing what Abraham was doing on Mount Moriah, explaining why his readiness to kill Isaac is not simply attempted murder, and opening conceptual space for a divine command that overrides an otherwise valid moral duty. But no one in the room stopped to say which of those jobs the phrase can actually do, or what precise claim it expresses. I want to go back with the phrase disassembled. What are the distinct theses 'the teleological suspension of the ethical' could be used to assert? Lay them out as a menu. For each candidate thesis, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; whether it describes Abraham's situation from the outside or licenses Abraham's own judgment that he is justified; whether the suspension is temporary or permanent; and whether it suspends one particular duty, the ethical sphere as a whole, or the very applicability of moral concepts to the case. Then say which of these readings, if any, would actually do the work of turning Abraham from a would-be murderer into an exemplar of faith, and which reading the phrase's defenders in that room were most likely relying on without saying so.
