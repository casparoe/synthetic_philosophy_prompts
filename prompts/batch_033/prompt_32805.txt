Two people on my team at work got into it last week about the recommendation system we're building. Mike says it's fine -- we're just showing users content they'll probably like, based on what similar users liked. That's just good UX. Leah says that's way too clean: the system is optimized for time-on-platform, not user satisfaction, and it routinely surfaces outrage-fueled content because that's what drives engagement. She says that when you deliberately trigger someone's emotions to keep them scrolling, you're not persuading them of anything -- you're manipulating them. Mike pushed back and said that's an impossible line to draw: all design influences behavior, and if "influencing someone's emotions without their explicit awareness" is manipulation, then basically all advertising, all branding, and honestly most in-person conversation is manipulation too. Leah said that's a slippery slope argument and he knows it -- there's a difference between making something appealing and deliberately exploiting the fact that people have a harder time looking away from things that make them angry. Mike said "appealing" and "exploiting" are just the positive and negative spins on the same thing, and the only real difference is whether you personally approve of the outcome. Leah said no, the difference is whether you're engaging the person's capacity to reflect on what they want versus bypassing it. Mike said that makes the whole thing turn on a metaphor -- "bypassing reflection" -- that he doesn't think holds up when you look at the actual user behavior data. They went in circles and eventually asked me to weigh in. I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, and I honestly couldn't sort out who was scoring better points. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about what? I'm especially interested in whether the "bypassing reflection" criterion actually does the work Leah wants it to, or whether Mike is right that it collapses under preasure.
