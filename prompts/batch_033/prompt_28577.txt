I hold the view that natural selection makes room for reasons without reasoners: that a trait can be present because of a rationale that no agent ever represented, and that these free-floating rationales do genuine explanatory work in biology rather than merely dressing up causal talk. Tell me what would count against this position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of these a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against the view rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding. In particular, address (a) whether the position can survive the charge that the supposed rationale adds nothing over a complete causal-mechanistic account of selection; (b) whether the distinction between selection of a trait and selection for a trait is sharp enough to bear the weight the view places on it; and (c) whether such reason-ascriptions are truth-apt, or only heuristic ways of speaking we could abandon without explanatory loss. Finally, say whether the view can retreat to a deflationary reading — reasons as nothing but etiological facts about selection — and whether such a retreat amounts to giving the view up or is a defensible form of it.
