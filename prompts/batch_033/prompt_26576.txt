I'm teaching a graduate seminar on formal epistemology next term, and in the third week we tackle the lottery paradox and the preface paradox together. I want to give my students a decision tree that takes them from their initial reaction to the paradoxes to a named position in the literature, so that by the time they read the original papers they already know where they stand and what they're giving up. The tree should be built from the logical space — which premise or inference rule each position rejects — rather than from a list of famous names, and I want it to cover both paradoxes simultaneously since they put pressure on the same principle (that rational belief is closed under conjunction).

Here is what I need. The tree should open with a concrete restatement of each paradox — the fair 1,000-ticket lottery and the careful author who writes a preface apologizing for the errors that surely remain — and then ask the first diagnostic question: does the respondent think we have a genuine puzzle here, or does one of the paradoxes simply not get off the ground? From there, the branches should correspond to the available theoretical moves. At minimum, the following positions must appear as leaves: (1) rejecting that rational belief is closed under conjunction, (2) rejecting that one can rationally believe of a given lottery ticket that it will lose, on the grounds that high probability alone is insufficient for rational belief, (3) rejecting that the preface author's belief about errors is rational unless specific errors are identified, (4) accepting that a rational agent can hold inconsistent beliefs, (5) the contextualist solution on which the standards for "rational belief" shift between the ticket-by-ticket context and the whole-lottery context, (6) the stake-sensitive or pragmatic-encroachment view on which what counts as rational belief depends on what is practically at stake for the believer, and (7) the view that the paradox shows we should replace outright belief with graded credences and give up on a binary notion of rational belief altogether. Each leaf should carry a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment — the bullet the proponent has to bite. I also want the tree to flag any two leaves it cannot really pull apart (I suspect some ways of rejecting closure and some ways of accepting inconsistency look very similar when pressed, and I do not want to pretend they are cleanly distinct if the diagnostic questions cannot separate them), and to note any logically possible combination of answers that no one in the literature actually defends, with a brief diagnosis: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? The audience is philosophy graduate students who have had a course in epistemology but not in formal epistemology; they should be able to answer each node's question without knowing the labels or the literature. Aim for roughly seven to ten diagnostic questions before reaching a leaf.
