I am preparing a lecture on Russell's paradox and want to make sure I do not overstate its moral. The way I have usually put it is that the paradox shows the collection of all sets that are not members of themselves is not a set, and that the underlying difficulty is self-membership, which is why standard set theory later ruled it out by an explicit axiom. But I have become uneasy about that gloss. I know of set theories, such as Quine's New Foundations, that admit a universal set, and in such a theory the universal set is a member of itself; and in Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory the Axiom of Foundation is added as an independent axiom rather than being forced by the paradox itself. Could you explain exactly what Russell's paradox does and does not rule out? In particular, I want to know whether it refutes the unrestricted comprehension principle while leaving self-membership as such a live option, or whether self-membership is somehow the real culprit. Please separate what is mathematically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the foundations of set theory, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
