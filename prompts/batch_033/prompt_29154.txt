I've been going back and forth with a colleague about Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the idea that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition, conditional on the claim that her future credence in that proposition will be r, should itself be r. Formally: Pt(A | Pt'(A) = r) = r. The principle is often motivated by the thought that if you now believe you will later rationally assign credence r to A, you should simply adopt that credence now, on pain of a kind of epistemic incoherence. But the literature is full of apparent counterexamples: cases where you expect to forget evidence you now possess, cases where you foresee being drunk or cognitively impaired at t', cases where you anticipate gaining evidence whose significance you cannot yet assess, and so on. My colleague thinks the principle is basically dead — that the counterexamples are fatal and the principle survives at most as a rough heuristic with no normative bite. I am less sure; I suspect there is a salvageable core, perhaps by restricting the principle to futures where you have no reason to think your later self is epistemically compromised relative to your present self.
