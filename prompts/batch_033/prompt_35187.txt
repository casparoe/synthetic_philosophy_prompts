I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on practical rationality and I'm running a seminar session on Parfit's hitchhiker and rational irrationality for about 16 advanced undergrads — 75 minutes. I want them to viscerally get why you might wish you were *less* rational, not just nod along when I explain it.

Can you design an in-class game where they actually run into the problem themselves? The setup I have in mind: some kind of two-stage game with tokens, anonymous pairings, where in the first phase everyone plays as straightforward maximizers and watches cooperation collapse — drivers don't pick up, hitchhikers don't pay, everyone gets nothing. Then in a second phase, hitchhikers can optionally do something that binds them or credibly signals a disposition to keep promises, but it costs them something upfront. The tension should be: is it worth paying to tie your own hands, given that without it no one will deal with you?

Rules should be teachable in under ten minutes, no real money (points or tokens), and the whole thing plus debrief needs to fit in 75 minutes. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto Parfit's hitchhiker, maybe Kavka's toxin puzzle if it fits naturally, and the broader question of whether it can ever be rational to cultivate irrational dispositions.

Also tell me what'll go wrong when I run it — do people try to build reputations even when the setup is supposed to be anonymous? Does the binding mechanism get gamed? And be straight about which part of the philosophical idea the activity just can't reproduce, so I know what I'll need to supply myself in discussion afterward.
