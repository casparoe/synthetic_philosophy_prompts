I serve on my university's Dining Services Sustainability Committee, which is currently revising the institution's food-procurement standards. We have arrived at a substantive impasse concerning meat purchasing. The original proposal, driven largely by climate concerns, would shift our dining halls away from beef and lamb toward chicken, turkey, and farmed fish, on the grounds that ruminants have a far higher greenhouse-gas footprint per calorie delivered. But several committee members have objected that this move, while defensible on carbon grounds, may be perverse from an animal-welfare perspective: switching to chicken means killing vastly more individual animals (since chickens are much smaller than cattle), and the lived conditions of intensively raised broiler chickens are arguably worse than those of grass-fed or even feedlot cattle. A further complication has been raised by one member who has drawn attention to the problem of wild animal suffering in crop agriculture: the cultivation of grains and soy — whether fed to poultry or consumed directly by humans — kills enormous numbers of field animals (mice, voles, insects, ground-nesting birds) through harvesting machinery, tillage, and pesticide application. On some ethical accounting, pasture-raised beef, where cattle graze on land not tilled annually, may involve fewer total animal deaths and less suffering per calorie than chicken raised on feed crops. The committee has no agreed-upon framework for weighing these considerations against one another, and we need a decision memo that can move us toward a vote.

Please draft that memo. It should: (a) clearly articulate the strongest ethical arguments both for and against a beef-to-chicken shift, giving due attention to the crop-cultivation and wild-animal-suffering dimension; (b) engage seriously with the trade-off between number of lives and quality of life, and between highly visible suffering (livestock) and less-visible suffering (field animals); and (c) recommend a specific, actionable purchasing policy with a rationale that a majority of the committee could reasonably accept, even if some members remain in dissent.
