So I was arguing with my friend Marco last night — he's a grad student in the same program, and we got into this whole thing about what you should do when you find out that someone just as smart and well-informed as you disagrees with you about something. He's pretty hardcore steadfast: he thinks you can just stick to your guns, that you don't owe the other person's opinion any special weight just because they're your epistemic peer. I kept pushing him on cases — like, imagine we both do the same math problem and get different answers, or we both read the same article and come away with opposite takes. At some point he said "look, if I've already thought it through and I'm confident, why would I downgrade my confidence just because someone else came out differently?" And I realized I couldn't really make him feel the weirdness of that position just by describing cases. He needed to be inside one.

So here's what I want: I'm teaching an upper-level epistemology seminar this semester — about 15 students, we meet for 75 minutes. We're doing a unit on peer disagreement in a couple weeks (Christensen, Kelly, Elga, the usual suspects). I want to run an in-class activity that actually puts them in a peer-disagreement situation before we've discussed any of the theory. The idea is that they should experience the pull toward conciliation — or at least feel how uncomfortable it is to remain steadfast — and only afterwards we debrief and connect it to the readings.

I'm imagining something where students pair up, make some kind of judgment or estimate independently, then discover they disagree, and then have to decide what to do. Maybe there's a second round where they can revise? Maybe there are stakes involved — not real grades, but something that makes them care about being right? I want the structure to force them to confront the question: when you and your partner disagree and you both seem equally competent, is it rationally permissible to just stay where you are? A few constraints: no real money, no using their actual course grades as stakes, and it needs to fit in about 30–35 minutes (the rest of the session is debrief and discussion). The debrief should map what happened onto the conciliationist/steadfast distinction, and ideally also touch on the independance principle and the problem of bootstrapping. Also, tell me what's likely to go wrong — like, will some students just not care about being right? Will the pairs not actually see each other as peers? And be honest about what the activity can't capture: I know there's a difference between a one-shot classroom game and the kind of deep disagreement the literature is really about, and I don't want to pretend otherwise in the debrief.
