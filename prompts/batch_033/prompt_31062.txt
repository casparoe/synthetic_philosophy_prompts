I have been reading about the Ellsberg paradox on my own and keep returning to an argument that seems airtight but ends somewhere I cannot accept. I want to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

Take the usual urn: 90 balls, 30 red, and 60 that are each black or yellow in an unknown proportion. Four bets pay $100 if the named event occurs: R (red), B (black), RY (red or yellow), BY (black or yellow). When I imagine the choice, I prefer R to B and BY to RY, as most people reportedly do. Now the argument:

(1) The sure-thing principle: if two gambles agree on what happens in some possible cases, then your preference between them should depend only on the cases where they disagree.

(2) R and B agree when a yellow ball is drawn (both pay $0), so by (1) the R-versus-B comparison should depend only on what happens when the ball is red or black.

(3) RY and BY agree when a yellow ball is drawn (both pay $100), so by (1) the RY-versus-BY comparison should also depend only on what happens when the ball is red or black.

(4) When the ball is red or black, R and RY are the same bet (each pays on red and not on black), and B and BY are the same bet (each pays on black and not on red). So (2) and (3) reduce to the very same red-versus-black comparison, and (1) requires the two preferences to point the same way: R over B if and only if RY over BY.

(5) But the usual pattern is R over B and BY over RY, opposite directions. So the usual pattern violates the sure-thing principle and is irrational by its lights.

(6) In each pair, the preferred bet is the one whose odds are fixed by the setup (R's 1 in 3, BY's 2 in 3), while the other bet's odds depend on the unknown split of the 60 black-or-yellow balls.

(7) So the sure-thing principle condemns preferring known odds to unknown odds.

(8) But that same preference seems to be what ordinary prudence consists in: preferring an inspected coin to an uninspected one, a drug with a documented side-effect rate to one whose rate is unknown, a machine with a measured failure rate to one whose rate is only estimated.

(9) So the sure-thing principle condemns ordinary prudence, and a principle with that consequence is too strong.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced, I would rather keep (8) than (1), but I want to know exactly what I am paying for that choice.
