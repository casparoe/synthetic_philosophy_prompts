I'm a PhD student in AI policy, and I also sit on the safety review board at a mid-sized AI lab — mostly because they wanted someone who's read the governance literature and I wanted the stipend. Lately every proposal that crosses my desk gets framed in terms of the 'alignment tax': safety says we should do X, product says X costs too much and our competitors won't do it, and then somebody says we can't unilaterally pay the tax because the market punishes you for it. I've read enough to know the competitiveness constraint is a real idea, but I've also seen it used alot to kill pretty boring things like running a second eval suite before a launch, and I can't tell where the principle stops and the rationalization starts.

I want a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposal — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The board meets monthly and reviews four or five proposals at a time; we have two or three hours, none of us are philosophers, and the engineers mostly want a spreadsheet. The procedure should treat 'this is an alignment tax' as a claim that has to be established, not a label people get to apply. It has to handle the cases where the safety benefit is real but impossible to quantify, where the cost is mostly strategic or reputational rather than a benchmark drop, and where the measure is really about legal liability or PR rather than alignment. And it should say honestly what the procedure can't settle, where a human has to just decide.

Then run it on three recent-ish proposals: a six-week external red-teaming period before releasing a frontier model, a decision not to open-source the weights of a model that could plausibly help someone synthesize a toxin, and a safety fine-tuning stage that cuts MMLU by three points. One of those should come out with an answer the board won't like — I want to see where the procedure actually bites. And keep the whole thing in American English, please.
