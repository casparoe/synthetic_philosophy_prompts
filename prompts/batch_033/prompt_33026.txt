I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the eulogy at the funeral of my closest friend, Martin, who died two weeks ago. Martin was a Kierkegaard scholar — he taught *Fear and Trembling* for thirty years and genuinely believed that a life can contain moments when you have to suspend the ethical for the sake of something higher, something you can't explain to anyone. And he lived it. About fifteen years ago he left his wife and young daughter, moved to another country, and spent the rest of his life caring for a former student who was dying of a degenerative illness and had no one else. He told almost no one why he was going, and he never defended himself afterwards. His ex-wife and daughter never forgave him, and they'll be at the funeral. His old colleagues mostly think he had some kind of breakdown. I'm one of the few people he ever explained himself to, and I've spent years trying to work out whether he was an Abraham or just a man who broke his family and called it faith. Before he died he asked me to speak, and his exact words to me were something like: don't you dare make me sound like a nice man. Make them understand what I was trying to do.

So the room is split three ways: his daughter and ex-wife and their side of the family, a handful of academic philosophers (some Kierkegaardians, some not), and a few people like me who knew what he was actually up to. I need a eulogy that doesn't paper over what he did, that takes the teleological suspension of the ethical seriously as a real category rather than a fancy excuse, and that also doesn't turn into a lecture on *Fear and Trembling* for a room full of grieving people who never asked to read it. It has to land for his daughter without me pretending she's wrong to be angry, and it has to land for the Kierkegaardians without me softening the concept.

Could you write the eulogy? I'd like it structured as exactly five sections or moments — each one a paragraph or so — with no introduction or conclusion beyond that. Please write it in British English, and it's fine if it reads as a written speech rather than a polished essay.
