I direct the permitting division at a state environmental agency. Our enabling statute requires a full environmental impact review — a process that takes 12–18 months and costs applicants upwards of six figures — for any project that would cause "significant degradation" to water quality, but the legislature deliberately left the term undefined. We make roughly 200 significance determinations a year, and about a third are appealed to administrative law judges. We currently use a checklist of quantitative thresholds, but everyone involved knows the thresholds are arbitrary: a project that increases turbidity by 4.9% is treated as harmless while one at 5.1% triggers a multi-year review, and the hearings have become exercises in experts arguing about which side of a line we drew for convenience rather than for any principled reason.

I have read enough philosophy to know that we are living inside the sorites paradox. "Significant degradation" is a vague predicate, and any sharp cutoff we impose will generate borderline cases where the cutoff itself does the work rather than the underlying concept. But we still have to decide, and the decisions must be consistent, defensible on appeal, and honest about what we are doing.

Please write me a decision procedure for "significant degradation" that takes the vagueness of the term seriously, drawing on whatever the philosophical literature on vagueness has to offer. My analysts have no philosophy training, so the procedure must be usable by someone who knows the science but not the semantics. It should take the form of a sequence of questions or tests, with instructions for what to do at each step. It should identify which steps are mechanical and which require judgment, and it should say explicitly where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a senior official because the question cannot be resolved by the procedure alone. It should also flag the kinds of cases the procedure is most likely to handle badly, explain why, and say what to do when we encounter one. And it should be honest about what the procedure cannot do — what it leaves unsettled, what it pretends to settle but doesn't, and where we are essentially making a policy choice dressed as a finding of fact.

Then work the procedure through three determinations we face this quarter:

1. A mining operation that would increase selenium concentrations in a downstream river by 4% above baseline. The river supports a salmon run already stressed by multiple stressors, and selenium bioaccumulates in the food chain.

2. A housing development that would fill 0.3 acres of wetlands. The watershed has already lost 60% of its historical wetland acreage, and the remaining wetlands provide flood control for a downstream community of 4,000 people.

3. A small dairy farm that would add a negligible amount of nitrogen runoff — less than 0.1% of the river's total nitrogen load. The problem: it is the 400th small farm in the same watershed, and the cumulative nitrogen load from all farms is the primary driver of a growing hypoxic zone downstream.
