I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course in decision theory and formal epistemology. I would like one essay question, answerable in about 45 minutes, on the Death in Damascus problem: the case in which an agent must choose between two cities, a perfectly reliable predictor has predicted the choice, and the agent dies if and only if she goes to the predicted city. The question should include a compact statement of the case so that no prior familiarity with the label is assumed, and it should be written so that a student cannot earn a high mark merely by summarizing the evidential/causal decision theory distinction or by rehearsing the standard analysis of Newcomb's problem. It should force students to confront why the case is harder than Newcomb's problem for both theories: evidential decision theory appears to make each available act self-undermining, since conditional on performing either act, the alternative would have been better; causal decision theory cannot settle the matter by dominance, because no act is best under every possible prediction, and the probabilities of the prediction states are tied to the probabilities of the acts themselves. The question should also require students to assess at least one proposed repair, such as a ratification or deliberational fixed-point requirement, and to consider whether the mixed-strategy version of that repair genuinely solves the problem or merely stabilizes an arbitrary policy.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (total 20), a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong, and an indication of which failures separate an A from a B answer. Do not ask for or include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
