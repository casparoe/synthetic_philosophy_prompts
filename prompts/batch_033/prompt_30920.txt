I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I want to run a workshop session that lets people feel in their own choices, rather than hear from a lecturer, why a signal often has to be costly before it is believed. I have in mind Zahavi's handicap principle and the broader costly-signaling framework: the peacock's tail, the stag's antlers, the expensive watch on a wrist that can't really afford it, the initiation scar that is hard to fake. The session is for about 18 adults with no formal background in biology or economics, and I have 75 minutes. Design a runnable game in which some participants have a hidden quality — reliable versus unreliable, say, or committed versus uncommitted — and must decide how much to pay to signal it, while others must decide whether to trust the signal, with points, standing, or small non-monetary prizes as the stakes. Include at least one condition in which the signal is cheap for everyone and one in which it is costlier for the low-quality type, so that the group discovers for itself when signaling collapses into noise and when it becomes informative, before anyone names the principle. Then give me a debrief that maps what happened to the handicap principle and to the equilibrium logic of honest signaling, including the point that credibility comes from differential cost rather than average cost. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be explicit about which part of the biological idea a short human game cannot reproduce.
