Last year I began work on a novel about two moral philosophers — close friends in graduate school, now professionally estranged for over a decade — who agree, reluctantly, to co-author a paper in the form of an adversarial collaboration. The novel's centerpiece is the document they produce together, and I need the philosophical content to be credible, not the kind of caricature that makes actual philosophers wince. So I'm asking you to draft that document.

The two characters hold the following positions. The first — call her the Constructivist — argues that we can distinguish genuine moral preferences from mere biases through a process of critical reflection: we subject our initial moral reactions to consistency checks, to the test of impartial justification, and to the requirement that we be able to justify our principles to others as free and equal. What survives this process counts as a moral preference; what gets filtered out is bias. She draws on the Kantian constructivist tradition, particularly the idea that moral requirements are those we would legislate for ourselves under conditions of rational agency. The second — call him the Debunker — argues that this is wishful thinking. Critical reflection is itself shaped by the very biases it purports to filter; the impartial standpoint we imagine is populated by a thinly disguised version of ourselves; and the distinction between "preference" and "bias" is just a honorific we grant to the moral reactions we are unwilling to abandon. He draws on the empirical moral psychology of the last two decades — the work showing that disgust, in-group loyalty, and framing effects drive moral judgment in ways that reflection rarely displaces — and on the debunking tradition that runs from Nietzsche through contemporary arguments about the evolutionary origins of morality.

The adversarial collaboration should produce a single document both characters could sign. It should include: (1) a numbered list of claims they both accept, however minimal — these should not be vacuous, and they should go beyond the obvious ("morality exists," "we have intuitions"); (2) a precise statement of where they disagree, phrased so that each character would accept the wording of her or his own view; (3) an identification of the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one of them; and (4) for each crux, a concrete test, case, or piece of evidence that both would agree bears on it, with each character's prediction recorded in advance. I want the document to feel like something two smart, stubborn people actually produced after months of argument — not a tidy resolution, but a genuine narrowing of the dispute that makes the remaining disagreement exact.

Please keep the document to no more than 900 words. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
