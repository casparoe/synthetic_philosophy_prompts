I'm taking a seminar on Wittgenstein's later philosophy — we've been deep in the Investigations, especially the private language argument and all that stuff about how "an inner process stands in need of outward criteria." And independently, I fell down a rabbit hole reading Dennett's "Quining Qualia," which I'd never actually read in full before.

The bit that's been rattling around my head is the Chase and Sanborn thought experiment. You probably know it, but just in case: two coffee tasters both used to love the Maxwell House and now they both hate it. But they explain the change totally differently. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the same as it always did — his raw experience hasn't changed one bit — but his *tastes* have matured, so now he finds that same quale unpleasant. Sanborn says the opposite: his standards haven't budged, but the coffee genuinely tastes different to him now, his experience itself has changed. And Dennett's point is that there's no conceivable test, not even in principle, that could tell us whether Chase or Sanborn is right — and worse, maybe there's no fact of the matter at all. The whole idea of a private, ineffable what-it's-like that you can compare across time just comes apart under pressure.

So here's what's been bugging me. Wittgenstein died in 1951, decades before Dennett wrote that paper, so he never got to chew on the Chase and Sanborn cases directly. But reading the Investigations, it feels like he's circling around something very similar — the whole idea that we tie ourselves in knots by imagining that there's a "private object" (the sensation, the quale) that we can point to inwardly and that our words name. The beetle in the box, right?

What I'm wondering is: if you could somehow hand Wittgenstein the Chase and Sanborn cases, what would he do with them? Would he think they're a successful demolition of qualia-talk, or would he think Dennett's setup already smuggles in the very picture of inner experience that Wittgenstein was trying to dissolve? In particular, I can't decide whether Wittgenstein would see the Chase/Sanborn distinction (the whole "same experience, different judgement" vs. "different experience, same judgement" thing) as a genuine distinction we're just unable to settle, or whether he'd treat it as a pseudo-distinction born from a confused picture of how sensation language actually works. Like, would he say the problem isn't that we can't *tell* who's right, but that the question itself is malformed?

I keep going back and forth. My hunch is that Wittgenstein would be more radical than Dennett here — not just saying qualia are undetectable or epiphenomenal or whatever, but that the whole framing of Chase and Sanborn as making competing claims about a private object is already a grammatical confusion. But then I worry I'm just projecting my own reading onto him. Curious what you think, especially if you've spent time with both texts.
