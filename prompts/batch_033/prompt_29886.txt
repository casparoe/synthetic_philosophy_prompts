From that he concludes that constitutional limits aren't really anti-democratic, because the people imposed them on themselves — democracy protecting itself from its own future panics, not an outside veto. The analogy gets him a long way in arguments and I've started to think it's quietly doing all the work. I want it taken apart properly. Then tell me where the parallels strain or snap. I especially want a view on whether it matters that Ulysses was one person who consented then and there, knew roughly when the danger would pass, and only bound himself for that stretch, whereas a constitution binds people who never consented and might never get the ropes off. And give me the bottom line: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that constitutional precommitment is democratically legitimate actually survive, or was the analogy smuggling in more than it can carry? If the Ulysses picture is a bad fit, what's a better analogy for what a constitution does?
