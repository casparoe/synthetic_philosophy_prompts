I'm putting together a little case file on a colleague's views about sortition — Ruth, a political theorist in my department who works on lottocratic proposals. Over a decade of seminars and dinners I've collected her verdicts on which public decisions should be made by randomly selected ordinary citizens. She's in favour of it for criminal juries; for a one-off citizens' assembly that drafts a new constitution, with its proposal put to a referendum; for a citizens' assembly that sets binding national carbon budgets; and for a local citizens' jury deciding whether to grant a licence for a new bar in a residential street. She's against it for the central bank's monetary policy committee, for the nuclear safety regulator, for a permanent randomly selected second chamber with a veto over legislation, and for the body that decides which drugs get funded by the health service. She also said no to a standing citizens' advisory council on climate adaptation, though she admitted that one felt closer.

The case we're going to argue about next week is a one-off citizens' panel, selected by lot, convened to decide whether the health service should fund a particular expensive drug for a rare condition: a single, binding decision, with expert witnesses giving evidence but the panel doing the deciding. I can think of at least two rules that fit her track record. One sorts by whether the question is mostly about citizens' values and trade-offs rather than technical facts; the other sorts by whether the body is a one-off decision on a specific question rather than a standing regulator or policy-maker. They come apart on this new case. What do you think Ruth would say, and which rule is the better reading of her? Say what each rule predicts for the drug panel, what the closest earlier case is, and give your best guess. If the record underdetermines it, tell me a further case that would settle which rule she's running. And tell me how confident you are in the prediction, and which bit your least sure of.
