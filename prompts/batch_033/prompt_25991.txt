I used to do condensed matter physics before I retired and lately I've been working through some philosophy — Elster on Ulysses contracts, Schelling on precommitment, that whole literature about binding your future self because you know your preferences will shift or your willpower will give out. Really fascinating stuff.

Anyway I rewatched Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind last week and I can't stop thinking about it through this lens. The Lacuna procedure is basically a Ulysses contract taken to an extreme: you decide, in a moment of heartbreak, to erase someone entirely from your memory, and the future you won't even know they made that choice, let alone why. But the movie seems to show that it backfires — Joel realizes mid-procedure that he wants to keep the memories, even the painful ones, and fights to preserve them. And then at the end, he and Clementine get the tapes back, hear what they said about each other, and... choose to try again anyway.

Here's what I'm stuck on. In a standard Ulysses contract — Odysseus tying himself to the mast — you retain knowledge of the binding and the reasons for it. The whole point is that your present self is protecting your future self from a known temptation. But the Lacuna procedure destroys the knowledge itself. Is that a difference in degree or in kind? Does it stop being a Ulysses contract at all once the future self has no access to the prior decision?

And that scene where Joel is inside his own memory trying to resist the erasure — who is that, philosophically? Is it his present self changing its mind mid-decision, or is it his future self rebelling against a precommitment it never consented to? I feel like the standard models don't really have a slot for "the self that exists during the transition between selves."

I'd love a reading of the film through the philosophy of precommitment that takes both seriously. Where does the movie get something right that the philosophical literature misses, and where does it maybe lean on intuitions that dont quite map onto the standard cases? I'm not after film-studies analysis — I want to know whether the movie is actually a good argument against certain kinds of precommitment, or just a vivid illustration of a badly-designed one.
