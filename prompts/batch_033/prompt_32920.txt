I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on action theory and rational choice — 18 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the problem behind Buridan's ass before they hear the name of the paradox. The activity should involve a sequence of forced choices between two options, where the options start out clearly different and become progressively more similar, until participants face choices between options that are known to be identical. At each stage, participants must record their choice and their reason. The payoff should be real but small — for instance, the chosen option determines which of two sealed envelopes they get, each containing an identical candy, and in some rounds declining to choose means getting nothing.

Include rules, materials, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps the stages of the activity onto: (1) the original paradox, (2) the principle of sufficient reason, (3) the distinction between indifference and equipoise, and (4) whether randomizing counts as a rational resolution or merely a causal one. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply.
