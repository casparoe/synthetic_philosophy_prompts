hey, I'm taking an epistemology course this semester and we just spent a lecture on Fitch's paradox of knowability. I was trying to wrap my head around it and stumbled on this summary in a philosophy blog that gets shared a lot in my class groupchat (I think it's from one of those "big ideas explained simply" type sites). Here's what it says:

"Fitch's paradox is a proof that the principle of knowability — the idea that every truth can in principle be known — is false. The verificationists held that any genuine truth must be verifiable by someone, somewhere, at some time. But Fitch showed this leads straight to the absurd conclusion that we are already omniscient: every truth is already known. His proof works by taking a truth that nobody happens to know, like 'there is an even number of grains of sand on earth and nobody knows it.' If the verificationist is right, it must be possible to know this truth. But as soon as you try to know it — as soon as you come to believe it on good evidence — the second half of the sentence becomes false, because now someone does know the number of grains of sand. So the original truth is unknowable after all, and the verificationst principle collapses. The paradox is widely considered a decisive refutation of the idea that all truths are knowable, and most philosophers now accept that there are truths we can never know."

I'm suspicious of a few things here. For one, my professor spent a lot of time on responses to the paradox — restricting the knowability principle, revising the logic, denying the distribution axiom — which makes me think it's not exactly a "decisive refutation" that everyone just accepted. Also the grains-of-sand example feels like it's doing something weird: it's a sentence about what isn't known, and the paradox seems to hinge on that self-referential quality, but I'm not sure if the real Fitch proof needs that or if that's just a vivid illustration. And the blog equates knowability with verificationism, which my prof seemed to treat as related but distinct.

Basically: is this a fair summary? Go through it and tell me what a careful epistemologist would nod along with, what they'd wince at, and whether the conclusion — that most philosophers now accept unknowable truths — follows from the accurate parts.
