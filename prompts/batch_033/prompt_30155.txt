Norman Malcolm's Dreaming (1959) and Daniel Dennett's "Are Dreams Experiences?" (1976) defend sharply opposed answers to a question that seems, on its face, straightforward: whether a dream is something a person experiences while asleep. Malcolm argues that the ordinary concept of dreaming already settles the matter against the received view: being sound asleep is incompatible with being conscious, so a first-person dream report is not a report of an episode of consciousness that took place during sleep; it is better understood as a waking inclination to tell a certain story. Dennett, by contrast, wants to keep the claim that dreams are experiences, but only by abandoning the assumption that an experience must be a determinate, real-time mental episode: on his cassette model, the dream narrative is inserted into memory at or around the moment of waking, so questions about when the dream occurred and how long it took are not well-formed, and the dream's content need not be determinate beyond what the dreamer is disposed to report. Notice that the two positions are closer than they first appear: both reject the picture of a private mental movie unfolding in real time during sleep. The remaining disagreement is whether the result should be called an experience at all.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical. The facts about REM sleep and dream reports do not seem to decide between them, because the two sides are using different criteria for what counts as an experience: Malcolm ties experience to consciousness at some determinate time, while Dennett ties it to what it is like for the subject on waking, even if the episode has no determinate location in real time. Diagnose this disagreement. Is it verbal, conceptual, or is there a substantive question about the relation among consciousness, memory, and experience still doing the work? Engage the actual arguments on each side rather than surveying them, and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, an empirical finding, a change of question, or an argument that we should stop expecting the debate to settle.
