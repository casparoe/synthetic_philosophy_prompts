I'm writing an essay for my English class on Arthur Miller's The Crucible, and I want to read the play through a problem in moral philosophy: the idea that a person can be right about someone else's wrongdoing and yet lack the standing to blame them, because of his own wrongdoing. The play seems built around exactly this tension. John Proctor has committed adultery with Abigail Williams, and he is reluctant to expose her accusations because doing so would force him to reveal his own sin, as though his guilt disqualified him from speaking. At the same time, the people doing most of the blaming in Salem — the judges, Parris, the Putnams, Abigail herself — are corrupt, self-interested, or lying, yet their accusations carry official weight. I'd like a reading of the play through the lens of standing to blame. Specifically: does The Crucible treat a speaker's moral record as relevant to whether his accusations deserve a hearing, or does it treat the truth of an accusation as all that matters? Where does the idea illuminate Proctor's choices — his delay in coming forward, his confession of the affair in court, his final refusal to let a false confession stand — and where does the play complicate or resist the idea, for instance by showing that those with the most social standing are the ones whose blame does the most harm? I'm not asking for plot summary, and please don't try to quote the play from memory; I want an argument about what the play shows about the ethics of blame. Please begin with the reading itself, with no preface or commentary, and no restating of my question.
