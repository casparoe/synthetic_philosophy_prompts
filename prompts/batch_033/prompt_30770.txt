I'm teaching an undergraduate aesthetics course, and we've just spent two weeks on the paradox of tragedy — the puzzle that we voluntarily seek out artworks that make us feel sorrow, dread, and grief, and we count these among the most valuable experiences of our lives. We've read Hume's 'Of Tragedy', bits of the *Poetics*, and a couple of contemporary pieces — Kendall Walton on quasi-emotions and Susan Feagin on meta-response. The students grasp the moves but I want something that will stick, something that embeds the philosophical structure of the problem in a form that itself does some of the work.

I'd like you to write a Shakespearean sonnet on the paradox of tragedy. The first two quatrains should present the phenomena concretely — the weeping audience, the ticket bought, the friend told 'you must see it' — and let the sheer strangeness of the behaviour accumulate without yet reaching for diagnosis. The third quatrain should sharpen into the philosophical problem: what kind of pleasure is this, if it is pleasure at all? The rhyme scheme should pull the candidate answers into relation without reducing them to a list: catharsis as purgation, Hume's conversion of painful affect into pleasurable through the artistry of the representation, Walton's thought that we do not literally fear and pity but only make-believe that we do, and Feagin's meta-response — that the pleasure is pleasure at our own capacity to be moved by suffering. The closing couplet should not resolve the paradox — no tidy ending, no moral — but should instead do what the best philosophy and the best poetry both do: state the problem so precisely that the statement itself feels like an advance. Someone who knows the contemporary literature should recognise each position rendered in the verse; someone who does not should nevertheless find the poem moving and coherent as a poem. Title it 'The Weeping at the Matinée'.
