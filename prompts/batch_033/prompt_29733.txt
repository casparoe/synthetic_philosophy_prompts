I'm grading for a philosophy of mind course and I've got this exam answer I'm not sure how to mark. The question was:

"Some philosophers have argued that introspection is an unreliable source of knowledge about our own mental states. Explain this skeptical position and evaluate it against at least one alternative view. Is introspection reliable?"

Here's what the student wrote:

The traditional view, going back to Descartes, holds that introspection gives us privileged and infallible access to our own minds. On this picture, when I introspect I simply see my mental states as they are — I can't be wrong about whether I'm in pain, or what I'm thinking. The mind is transparent to itself.

This traditional picture has been challenged by philosophers like Eric Schwitzgebel, who argues that introspection is actually quite unreliable. Schwitzgebel points to cases where people are clearly wrong about their own mental states: someone insists they're not angry while their voice is shaking and they're clenching their fists, or someone claims to have vivid visual imagery but can't answer basic questions about what they're supposedly visualizing. He argues these aren't just occasional errors — they reveal that introspection is no more reliable than guessing about many aspects of our inner lives. Nisbett and Wilson's classic studies support this: they showed that people regularly confabulate explanations for their own decisions, confidently reporting reasons that couldn't possibly be the real causes.

On the other side, defenders of introspection like Peter Carruthers argue that while introspection isn't infallible, it's still generally reliable. Carruthers thinks we access our own minds through much the same interpretive mechanisms we use to understand others — so it's not a special inner sense — but this interpretive process works well enough most of the time. The fact that we can function socially and make plans that depend on knowing our own preferences suggests introspection can't be completely off base.

I think the skeptical view is ultimately more convincing. If introspection works the same way as reading other people's minds, and we know we're often wrong about what others are thinking, then we should expect to be often wrong about ourselves too. The Nisbett and Wilson results are particularly damning because they show error even in cases where people feel most confident. However, I'd qualify this: introspection is probably reliable enough for everyday purposes, just not for the kind of certain self-knowledge philosophers have traditionally wanted.
