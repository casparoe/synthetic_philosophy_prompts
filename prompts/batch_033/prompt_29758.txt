I'm a PhD student in philosophy and I've been sitting in on a formal epistemology seminar this term. The professor's been developing her view on imprecise probabilities by working through cases, and I've been keeping notes. Here's what she's said so far:

Case 1 (Ellsberg): Urn A has 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn B has 100 balls, unknown mix of red and black. You win £100 if you draw red. She says it's rationally permissible to strictly prefer betting on Urn A, and also permissible to be indifferent; neither preference is rationally required.

Case 2 (Complete ignorance): A coin you know nothing about except that it's two-sided. She says your credence in heads should be the full interval [0,1], and assigning a precise 0.5 is rationally impermissible — it's gratuitous precision.

Case 3 (Dilation): A coin is either fair or double-headed, with your credence that it's fair being [0.4, 0.6]. Your credence in heads on the first toss works out to [0.7, 0.8]. You toss it, it lands heads, and your credence in heads on the second toss dilates to roughly [0.57, 0.79]. She says this widening is not a bug; it's exactly what should happen when your prior is imprecise and the evidence favours the extreme hypothesis.

Case 4 (Betting): Your credence in rain is [0.3, 0.7]. A bet pays £10 if it rains and costs £6 if it doesn't, so the expected value ranges from -£1.20 to £5.20. She says it's permissible to accept the bet, permissible to reject it, and permissible to remain undecided.

Case 5 (Two ambiguous bets): Same rain credence. Bet X pays £10 if rain, Bet Y pays £10 if not. She says both bets are permissible — neither dominates the other.

Next week she's going to give her verdict on a new case, and I'm trying to work out in advance what she'll say. The new case is this: same rain credence [0.3, 0.7], but now there are three options on the table. Option A: £35 for sure. Option B: £100 if rain, £0 if not. Option C: you flip a fair coin; if heads, you get the same bet as Option B (£100 if rain); if tails, you get £40 for sure. Crucially, you have to decide whether to take Option C before flipping the coin — you're committing to the whole package.

What I want to know is: which of A, B, and C would she say are permissible? I think her verdicts so far point pretty clearly toward maximality as her decision rule rather than maxmin, but I'm not confident about how maximality handles a compound gamble like C, especially one where the randomising device is something you opt into rather than something that's already in the world. Does the coin flip in C "launder" the ambiguity in a way that changes its status relative to B, or are B and C on a par? And if her existing verdicts underdetermine the answer — if there's more than one rule consistent with Cases 1–5 that diverge on the new case — what would the competing rules say, and what further case would settle which one she's actually running?
