I'm teaching a module on the socialist calculation debate in an undergrad political economy seminar — about 25 students, mostly juniors and seniors, some of them have taken intro micro but it's not an econ-heavy group. We've got a 75-minute session and I've already lectured on the basic positions: Mises's challenge, Hayek's dispersed knowledge argument, Lange's market socialism response. They get it on paper but I can tell they don't *feel* it. They nod along when I say "the information just isn't available in one place" but then five minutes later someone asks "but couldn't a really big computer solve the equations?" and we're back to square one.

So I want to run a simulation that makes them experience the knowledge problem from the inside. Here's what I'm picturing: split the room into a Central Planning Board (maybe 5 students) and the rest are factories, resource suppliers, and households scattered across the economy. Each non-planner gets an index card with some local information — production capacities, input needs, consumer preferences, whatever — that only they can see. The planners have to set production targets and allocate inputs without being able to see those cards. They can ask questions, but there's a cost: every minute they spend gathering information is a minute they're not actually planning, and the "economy" is ticking. Then in round two we introduce something like a price mechanism — maybe the non-planners can post bid/ask prices on a shared whiteboard and trade bilaterally — and we compare outcomes.

But I'm worried the rules will be too loose and it'll just turn into a mess where nobody learns anything, or worse, where the planners do fine because the game accidentally gives them too much information. I need the activity to actually surface Hayek's point: that the knowledge is local, tacit, and *can't* be communicated upward without losing what makes it useful. And I'd like the debrief to also give Lange his due — maybe a third round where the planning board can adjust prices by trial and error and we see how that works?

Can you design this properly? I need: exact rules and what goes on the index cards (I can prep those ahead of time), a tight timeline for the 75 minutes including setup and debrief, and a clear mapping from what happened in the game to the actual arguments. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — like, what clever workaround will students invent that accidentally defeats the point, and how do I head that off. The goal is that they walk out understanding why "just gather all the data and solve the equations" isn't a serious reply to Hayek, even if they still prefer Lange's answer in the end.

Oh and please write the whole thing in American English — the course is in the US and I want the activity materials to sound natural to my students.
