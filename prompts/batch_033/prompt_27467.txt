I'm organizing a salon-style event for my department called "Paradox and Poetic Form," where each presenter pairs a philosophical puzzle with a verse form whose structure mirrors the problem's logical shape. I've been assigned Kavka's toxin puzzle, and I want to present a Shakespearean sonnet that does real philosophical work — not just a poem that mentions the puzzle, but one whose formal features track the argument.

For anyone unfamiliar: Gregory Kavka asks us to imagine a billionaire who will give you a million dollars if, at midnight on Monday, you genuinely intend to drink a merely unpleasant (not dangerous) toxin on Tuesday afternoon. The money is deposited the moment you form the intention — before you drink, and regardless of whether you later drink. On Tuesday, the money is already yours, the toxin sits before you, and you have no reason to drink it. So you won't. But if you know on Monday that you won't drink on Tuesday, can you genuinely intend to? The puzzle isn't about prediction or precommitment; the billionaire isn't scanning your brain. You just have to actually intend. The puzzle is about whether an intention can survive the knowledge that the intended action will later be irrational.

I want the sonnet to work like this: the octave (first eight lines) should present the setup — the billionaire, the offer, the apparent ease of just deciding — in a tone of almost smug confidence. The volta at line 9 should be the hinge from Monday to Tuesday, the moment the money lands and the reasons drain away. The sestet should inhabit Tuesday's point of view, where the glass sits on the table and the intention, if it ever existed, has nothing left to grip. The closing couplet should not resolve the paradox but should sharpen it — perhaps by showing that the only way to win is to be the kind of agent who doesn't think about Tuesday, which is exactly the kind of agent you cannot deliberately become.

Please take on the role of a poet with serious philosophical training. Before writing the sonnet, sketch the poem's argumentative architecture in a short paragraph: what each quatrain does, where the turn lands, and what the couplet leaves the reader with. Then write the poem in iambic pentameter with a consistent rhyme scheme. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the puzzle would recognize its precise structure — the timing, the harmlessness of the toxin, the fact that the money comes before the drinking — and would see that the poem isn't confusing it with Newcomb's problem or a prisoner's dilemma. Avoid the easy move of having the speaker simply drink the toxin out of honor; the poem should let the paradox remain a paradox.
