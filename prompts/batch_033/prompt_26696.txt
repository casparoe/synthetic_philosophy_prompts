In my early modern philosophy seminar, two students got into an argument after class about what Descartes's cogito actually proves, and neither would concede. (1) Jonas argued that the cogito establishes only that a particular episode of thinking is occurring right now: Descartes is entitled to the claim that thinking is happening, but the step to an I that owns the thought goes beyond the argument. (2) Mara replied that the argument proceeds from doubt, and doubting is something an agent does; the very form of the reasoning requires a subject, so the cogito proves at least a momentary subject of thought rather than a bare thought. (3) Jonas answered that this simply assumes that every thought requires a thinker, which is exactly the kind of metaphysical assumption the method of doubt was supposed to put under pressure; a momentary subject is either an empty label or an illegitimate import. (4) Mara responded that even a momentary subject gives Descartes the one certainty he needs at that stage, since I cannot be deceived about my own existence while I am thinking. (5) Jonas said that if the I is only momentary, then the later moves in the Meditations — the examination of the wax, the claim that the mind is better known than body — are not licensed by the cogito alone, which shows that the cogito is weaker than most readers assume. (6) Mara replied that those later moves were never meant to be licensed by the bare cogito; Descartes immediately goes on to ask what this I is, and the conclusion that it is a thinking thing is supposed to emerge from that further investigation, not from the initial conclusion. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and state who is more right overall.
