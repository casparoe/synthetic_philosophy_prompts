A mate and I were up late talking about many-worlds and he backed me into a corner I can't get out of. Here's the argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) If the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics is right, then whenever a quantum event has multiple possible outcomes, all of them happen — the world branches.

(2) From my own first-person perspective, I can only ever find myself in branches where I'm still alive. Dead-me has no observations.

(3) So if I set up a device that measures a quantum event and kills me on outcome A but spares me on outcome B, then conditional on being around to reflect on what happened, I should expect to have seen outcome B.

(4) This holds no matter how lopsided the odds are. Even if outcome A is a million times more likely by the Born rule, the only version of me doing any observing is the one in branch B.

(5) Now iterate: if I run the device a hundred times, I should expect to survive all hundred, because at each step the surviving branch is the only one I can inhabit as an observer.

(6) So it seems like, from my own perspective, I should expect to be immortal — I'll always find myself in whatever branch keeps me alive, no matter how improbable.

(7) But that's bonkers. It would mean I should be completely indifferent to physical danger. Why not play Russian roulette? Why look both ways before crossing the street? The reasoning says I'll always observe survival.

(8) Since (7) is clearly false — I can in fact die, and it's rational to fear death — something must be wrong with the argument.

I can't figure out which step to reject without giving up either many-worlds or something deep about how I reason about my own future. Where does this go wrong, and what do I have to give up if I reject that step? (Please write the answer in American English.)
