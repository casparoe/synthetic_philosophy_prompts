I just finished reading the Grand Inquisitor chapter from The Brothers Karamazov for a literature class, and I've been turning over an argument that seems to emerge from it. I've tried to reconstruct it as cleanly as I can, but the conclusion feels wrong, and I can't figure out where it goes off the rails.

Here's the argument:

(1) If freedom is intrinsically valuable, then it is valuable for everyone who possesses it, independently of whether they want it or benefit from it in any other way.

(2) The Grand Inquisitor claims, as an empirical matter, that the vast majority of human beings do not want freedom — they experience it as an exhausting burden, and they would gladly trade it for security, material comfort, and the relief of having someone else make their hardest decisions.

(3) If (2) is true, then for most people, freedom is something they do not value and do not, on balance, benefit from (since the anxiety it causes them outweighs whatever goods it brings).

(4) Something that most people do not value and do not benefit from cannot be intrinsically valuable for them. (If it were, we would have to say that a thing can be good for someone even though it makes their life worse by their own lights and brings them no benefit they can recognize — which seems like a strange kind of "good for.")

(5) Therefore, freedom is not intrinsically valuable — or at least, it is not intrinsically valuable for most people.

(6) If freedom is not intrinsically valuable, then there is nothing wrong in principle with a system that restricts freedom in exchange for happiness and security, provided the trade is genuinely in people's interests. The Grand Inquisitor's project — replacing the burden of freedom with miracle, mystery, and authority, and governing people for their own happiness — becomes morally coherent.

(7) But the Grand Inquisitor's project is not merely wrong; it is repugnant. There is something deeply objectionable about treating human beings as children who cannot be trusted with their own choices, even if the treatment makes them happy.

(8) So if (7) is right, something in (1) through (6) must be wrong.

I can't tell whether the weak link is (2) — maybe the Inquisitor is just wrong about human psychology, and most people do want freedom, even if they sometimes complain about it. Or maybe (4) is the problem — perhaps something can be intrinsically valuable for a person even if that person doesn't want it and doesn't benefit from it in any felt sense, but then I worry I'm committed to a kind of paternalism that isn't far from the Inquisitor's own position. Alternatively, maybe (6) is a non sequitur — even if freedom isn't intrinsically valuable, the Inquisitor's system of deception might still be wrong for other reasons, but then what are those reasons, and why didn't they show up earlier in the argument?

I'd like to know which step you would reject, and what rejecting it costs — what else you have to give up, and whether the remaining position is stable.
