I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been reading about epistemic logic on my own. I've come to the view that the logical omniscience assumption — roughly, that if an agent knows P, they know all logical consequences of P — is not just a convenient idealization but a fundamental mistake. I don't mean it's merely unrealistic in practice; I mean that building a model of knowledge around it distorts what knowledge is, because real knowledge is always held by computationally bounded agents, and boundedness isn't a contaminant you abstract away to get at the pure concept. The various patches I've seen — impossible worlds, awareness restrictions, explicit-versus-implicit knowledge — strike me as epicycles that preserve the omniscience framework rather than replacing it with something that takes boundedness seriously from the start.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of logic or formal epistemologist. Think carefully about this, and feel free to start with some brainstorming or to work through your reasoning step by step before giving your final answer.

What I want to know is: what would count against this view of mine? I'm not asking for the standard objections — I can find those. I want you to map out what observations, formal results, theoretical developments, or shifts in how we use the concept of knowledge would actually defeat the position, or at least seriously weaken it. Distinguish between what would be decisive, what would be merely awkward, and what defenders of my view would themselves concede counts against it. And I want an honest answer to the question: is this position insulated against counterevidence in a way that makes it more of an attitude than a claim? If so, is that a problem, or is that actually appropriate for a view about what epistemic modeling should aim at?
