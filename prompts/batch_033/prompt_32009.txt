I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, working on representationalist theories of consciousness, and I recently picked up a popular neuroscience book that devotes a chapter to dismantling the so-called hard problem. The author offers the following summary of Dennett's position on qualia — I'm paraphrasing from memory, but this captures the substance:

"Daniel Dennett has shown that the whole idea of qualia — the private, ineffable 'what it's like' of experience — doesn't hold up to scrutiny. His famous Chase and Sanborn thought experiment asks us to imagine two coffee tasters who both report that the Maxwell House blend they've been drinking for years now tastes worse. Chase says the taste itself hasn't changed; he's simply grown tired of it. Sanborn says the taste has changed — his gustatory system has undergone some physiological shift — and he no longer enjoys the new taste. Dennett's point is that there's no way to tell, even from the first-person perspective, which of them is right. Since we can't distinguish a change in our qualia from a change in our judgments about them, the whole notion of qualia as private objects of immediate awareness collapses. Dennett concludes that qualia don't exist, and that what we call consciousness is really just a set of behavioral dispositions and cognitive access relations — there's nothing more to having an experience than being disposed to report it and react to it in certain ways."

The book then uses this summary to argue that the hard problem of consciousness is a pseudo-problem, and that once we abandon qualia-talk we can get on with the neuroscience unimpeded.

I'm uneasy about this summary. I've read enough Dennett to know he vigorously rejects the charge of behaviorism, and the summary's conclusion sounds suspiciously like the kind of position he has explicitly disavowed. But I haven't read "Quining Qualia" itself carefully enough to pinpoint where the summary goes wrong, and I worry I might be projecting my own sympathies onto the critique. Could you go through this characterization sentence by sentence and tell me what Dennett would accept, what he'd push back on, and whether the book's conclusion about the hard problem survives the corrections? I'm particularly interested in whether the gap between what Dennett actually argues and what the summary attributes to him is a matter of emphasis or a matter of substance.
