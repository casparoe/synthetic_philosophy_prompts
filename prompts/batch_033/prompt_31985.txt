Here's the setup, in case you haven't seen it: Death tells a guy he's going to die tomorrow. Death is a perfect predictor of what the guy will actually do. The guy can either stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo. If he stays, Death will be in Damascus; if he flees, Death will be in Aleppo. The guy knows all this. He wants to survive.

Now the argument:

(1) Before deciding, the guy considers his options: stay or flee. If he stays, he dies. If he flees, he dies. Both options lead to the same outcome: death.

(2) In any decision problem, if all available actions lead to the same outcome, then the agent is rationally permitted to pick any action. There's no reason to prefer one over the other.

(3) So the guy is rationally permitted to just pick one — say, stay. He flips a mental coin and decides to stay.

(4) But now notice: after deciding to stay, the guy knows Death is in Damascus. He also knows that his decision to stay is what put Death there. So he asks himself: "Given that I've already decided to stay, and Death is therefore in Damascus, what should I do now?" Well, if he actually stays, he dies. If he changes his mind and flees to Aleppo, he lives — because Death is already in Damascus, and Death can't be in two places at once. The prediction was based on his original choice, and that choice is now in the past. So fleeing now would save him.

(5) But of course, if he changes his mind and flees, then by parallel reasoning he'll immediately realize that Death is now in Aleppo (because Death predicted the fleeing), and he'll have an incentive to change his mind again and stay. And so on, forever.

(6) A decision problem where every possible choice, once made, gives you an incentive to switch to the other choice is a decision problem with no stable solution. It's a deliberative loop with no exit.

(7) If a decision problem has no stable solution, then there is no rational action in that problem. Rationality requires a stable, self-ratifying choice, and there isn't one here.

(8) Therefore, there is no rational action in the Death in Damascus case.

(9) But that's absurd. The guy has to do *somethng* — stay or flee. And one of those has got to be at least as rational as the other. The idea that a well-defined decision problem could have *no* rational action is a reductio of the whole framework.

(10) So the framework that generated steps (1)–(8) must be broken. But it's just standard decision theory plus a few plausible principles about ratifiability and stability. If those principles lead to absurdity, then decision theory itself is in trouble.
