I'm teaching a seminar on the value of future generations this semester, and we just finished Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife — specifically his afterlife conjecture, the idea that if we knew humanity would go extinct shortly after our own deaths (his thought experiment is thirty days after you die), it would rob many of our current activities of their point, even though the extinction wouldn't involve any additional deaths beyond the ones already going to happen. The badness of extinction isn't just about the deaths; it's about the collapse of the background assumption that humanity continues, which underwrites the value of a lot of what we do.

I want to use Cuarón's Children of Men as a way into this. The film's infertility crisis is basically a slow-motion extinction — no births for eighteen years — and you can see people's projects hollowing out in real time. But I'm not sure the film maps neatly onto Scheffler. For one thing, Scheffler's thought experiment involves knowing extinction will happen *after* you die; in the film, people are living through the extinction, watching it happen. And the film has that whole messianic pregnancy plot — does that undercut or reinforce Scheffler's point about what we lose?

I'd like a reading of the film through Scheffler's lens: which scenes dramatize the afterlife conjecture best, where does the film complicate it, and does the film end up suggesting something Scheffler misses about what we lose when the future disappears? I'm particularly interested in the contrast between Theo's arc — his gradual re-engagement with a world that has a future again — and the govenment's response (the Quietus suicide kits, the propaganda, the refugee camps). Also, is there something about the fact that it's infertility rather than an asteroid — the loss of *reproduction* specifically — that makes it a different kind of case than Scheffler's? Be honest where the fit is imperfect; I don't want to force the reading.
