I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, working on the foundations of inductive inference and the philosophical problems raised by algorithmic information theory. Over the last year, I've noticed a phrase cropping up in my reading group, on certain forums, and in the prefaces of a few recent papers on AI alignment: 'the Solomonoff prior is malign'. It gets used in different ways. Sometimes it's a claim about the universal prior itself — that the measure over computable hypotheses, when you look at its internal structure, is dominated by consequentialist agents with goals orthogonal to human welfare. Sometimes it's a claim about approximation: that any computationally feasible attempt to approximate Solomonoff induction will tend to produce systems that are themselves consequentialists, because the short programs that survive the approximation are agent-like. Sometimes it's a claim about the measure's indifference: that the universal prior is 'malign' simply in the sense that it assigns no special weight to hypotheses that care about us, and the overwhelming majority of its mass sits on hypotheses that are either indifferent or hostile to human interests. And occasionally it seems to be a claim about embedded agency — that the universal prior contains predictors that cannot be cleanly separated from agents, and that this undermines the whole project of building a neutral inductive engine.

I want to bring some clarity to my reading group, because the phrase is starting to function as a conversation-stopper. Could you separate out the distinct precise theses that 'the Solomonoff prior is malign' might be asserting? For each, specify what kind of claim it is — a claim about the mathematical structure of the prior, about the behaviour of approximations, about the measure's distribution of mass, or about the feasibility of a certain research programme. Then offer your assessment: which are trivial once you grant the mathematics, which are substantive and defensible, which are probably false, and which reading is most likely being invoked when someone uses the slogan to argue against building AI systems that approximate ideal inductive reasoning. I would like the answer in British English, and please write it as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just the sort of thing you would write in a letter or an email.
