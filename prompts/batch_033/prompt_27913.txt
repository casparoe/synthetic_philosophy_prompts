I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have ended up convinced of a version of the Gödelian anti-mechanist position associated with Lucas and Penrose. Stated in my own terms: no single Turing machine captures human mathematical competence; equivalently, for my purposes, no one formal system whose axioms and rules a program could enumerate captures it. The argument that convinced me runs like this. Take any candidate machine M that is supposed to model my ability to recognize arithmetical truths, and suppose M is consistent and strong enough to represent enough arithmetic. Then M has a Gödel sentence G(M), a sentence that says, in effect, that G(M) is not provable in M. If M is consistent, M cannot prove G(M). But I, standing outside M and accepting that M is consistent, can see that G(M) is true. So there is an arithmetical truth I can recognize that M cannot prove. Since M was arbitrary, no such machine captures what I can do; the mind is not a Turing machine.

I want this position stress-tested, not just defended. What would have to be true for it to be wrong?

Please give me a map of the position's exposure. Walk through its load-bearing steps and hidden assumptions — for example: that I can know M is consistent; that M can be given to me as a finite object I can reason about; that my recognition of G(M)'s truth is itself a reliable, well-defined procedure; that the mind is one stable formal system rather than something that revises itself over time; and that my seeing the truth of G(M) is not itself just the operation of some larger machine. For each assumption, say what would have to be the case for the argument to fail there, and rank the failure as decisive, serious, or merely awkward.

Then separate the defeaters that a committed defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against it from the ones they would dismiss as changing the subject. Include formal results where they bear on the question — for example, results showing that one machine can prove another machine's consistency, or that out-Gödeleling a candidate system can itself be done by a Turing machine when the candidate is supplied as input. Also tell me what observations about human mathematical practice or about actual AI systems would count against the position, and what a future system would have to do to count as evidence either way.

End with an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a defect or the right shape for a claim of this kind. I would personally find it most persuasive if someone could show that the argument establishes only a limit on what I can know about my own implementation, not a limit on what I can do; weigh that possibility explicitly. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list.
