I'm a journalist working on a feature about the philosophical puzzle at the heart of any science of pleasure: what, if anything, do all pleasures have in common? The pleasure of a morphine drip, the pleasure of figuring out a proof, the pleasure of schadenfreude, the pleasure of a warm bath — the experiences feel radically different, and yet hedonists about value need them to be a unified kind. The question also matters for neuroscientists studying reward, for clinicians treating anhedonia, and for anyone who thinks well-being involves pleasure in some central way.

For the piece, I want to create a diagnostic tool — a decision tree or taxonomy — that walks a reader from their intuitive judgments about concrete cases to whichever philosophical position their judgments most closely align with. The instrument should sort people by what they actually think about the cases, not by which label they already find congenial.

The positions that should appear as leaves include at least these:

- Phenomenal/qualitative view: pleasures share a distinctive felt quality, a hedonic tone common to all of them.
- Attitudinal view: pleasure is not a sensation but a propositional attitude — being pleased that something is occurring.
- Desire-satisfaction view: pleasure just is the experience of having one's desires satisfied, or the state of wanting the experience to continue.
- Functional/neuroscientific view: pleasure is whatever state plays a certain causal role — reward learning, dopamine-mediated reinforcement, or behavioral approach.
- Representational view: pleasure is a perception-like state that represents something as good for the organism, in roughly the way visual experience represents color.
- Family resemblance view: there is no single essence; pleasures are related only by overlapping similarities, and the boundaries of the category are vague.
- Eliminativism about pleasure as a natural kind: the term "pleasure" does not pick out a unified phenomenon and should be replaced by more precise concepts in both science and philosophy.

Build the branches from the logical space rather than from the list of names. First identify the diagnostic questions that carve the space, then locate the named positions within it. Flag any logical combinations that are unoccupied, and briefly note why — whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Also flag any pairs of positions the tree cannot cleanly separate, and explain what finer-grained question would be needed to split them.

The diagnostic questions should be concrete enough that a reader without philosophical training can answer them from introspection or by thinking through a scenario. Good questions would include things like: "Could a creature with no sensory capacities at all — no sight, hearing, touch, or proprioception — still experience pleasure?" or "If a drug made you want to continue an experience you previously found neutral, and nothing about the experience's qualitative character changed, would you now be having a pleasure?" or "Can you be mistaken about whether you are experiencing pleasure — that is, could you believe you are enjoying something when in fact you are not, or vice versa?" No question should simply ask the reader which label they prefer.

The audience is educated but not specialist — readers of a magazine like The Atlantic or Nautilus. Aim for six to eight diagnostic questions before the leaves. Present the tree so that it could be rendered as a flowchart in print, with each branching node phrased as a yes/no question or a forced choice between two options. Include a brief interpretive note for each leaf, giving the reader a sense of what their position commits them to — and what its most awkward implication is.
