For as long as I have taught philosophy of science, the quarrel between physicists and philosophers over whether philosophy is of any use to science has had a peculiar character: every few years a distinguished physicist restates the prosecution case, philosophers publish rebuttals, and then the whole thing repeats with the positions untouched. Weinberg's chapter Against Philosophy in Dreams of a Final Theory (1992) argued that philosophy has never contributed to the progress of physics and that its influence, where it mattered, has mostly been a hindrance. Hawking and Mlodinow's The Grand Design (2010) declared philosophy dead on the grounds that it has failed to keep up with modern developments in science, particularly physics. Feynman, earlier, dismissed the pronouncements of philosophers of science about what science requires as naive and probably wrong. Philosophers, for their part, respond that these attacks depend on a caricature of what philosophy does: they argue that scientific practice is saturated with philosophical commitments about evidence, explanation, realism, and theory choice; that the physicists' own disputes about the interpretation of quantum mechanics or the status of the multiverse are philosophy conducted under another name; and that demanding a ledger of empirical results produced by philosophy misunderstands the kind of contribution philosophy makes.

After watching this for two decades, I have a working hypothesis, but I am not sure it is the whole story. I suspect the two sides are not answering the same question: the physicists ask whether philosophy, as an institution or a body of doctrine, has made specific discoveries that science needed, while philosophers answer by redescribing what philosophy is supposed to do. But that may be too tidy; the dispute may also be substantive, or it may be a proxy for a disagreement about who is entitled to answer the big questions. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on whether philosophy is useful. Is this a verbal disagreement about the word "philosophy"? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence, about what counts as a contribution? Is a hidden factual question doing the work, or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a case study, a change of question — or should we expect no progress, and why?

Please give the diagnosis as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and write the answer in American English.
