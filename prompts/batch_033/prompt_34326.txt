At lunch a couple of the engineers on my team got into it about Aumann's agreement theorem, and I ended up having to referee but honestly couldn't. Can you go through the whole argument and say who's right about what?

The first guy, Rob, had just read something about Aumann and was pretty fired up. His point was: the theorem shows that two rational people with the same prior can't agree to disagree — once there posterior probabilities for some event are common knowlege, they have to be equal. So when you see two smart people genuinely disagreeing about a factual question, at least one of them is either being irrational or has private information they're not sharing, or they started from different priors. He thought that makes most real-world stubborn disagreement suspicious.

The other guy, Sam, said the theorem is basically just conditional probability with extra steps. Same prior plus same information gives same posterior, sure, but that's not a deep result — it's what 'same prior' and 'update correctly' mean. And the common-knowledge setup never actually happens, so calling it a theorem about real disagreement is misleading.

Rob came back with: it's not the same information though. They have different private information. The surprising part is that they don't need to trade all their evidence, just their final numbers; if each announces their posterior and they each know the other's posterior, they can't both just stick with their number. One has to move, and if they kept trading numbers they'd converge.

Sam said the theorem only works because of the common prior assumption, and priors are the whole ballgame. If his prior says the other guy is full of it, he won't update on an announced number the way the theorem says. Common priors is doing all the work, and that's exactly the thing real people don't share.

Rob pushed back that common priors aren't crazy. He said a prior is just your subjective distribution before you condition on your own history, and there's a Dutch book argument that two people with different priors can be made to accept a set of bets that loses money no matter what. So differing priors might be a kind of incoherence, not just a difference of taste.

Sam said Dutch book arguments only show that one person's beliefs shouldn't be internally incoherent. They don't force two different people to have the same prior. Two people can each be perfectly coherent and still assign different priors, so the theorem never gets off the ground in the interesting cases.

Rob said okay, but even if you grant different priors, the theorem still bites in a weaker way: for two engineers who've seen the same codebase and the same metrics, priors are close enough that Aumann-style convergence should mostly happen, so when it doesn't, that's a sign someone is anchoring or double-counting.

Sam said that's an empirical claim about people, not a theorem. The theorem is a true mathematical statement about idealized agents, and all the interesting stuff — where priors come from, whether real people share them, whether people actually update on each other's announced numbers — is outside it. So using Aumann to call real disagreement irrational is a category mistake.

I couldn't figure out who actually won. Go through it move by move and tell me which claims are right, which are wrong, and what both of them are missing. Don't just say they're both partly right; I want a verdict on the specific moves.
