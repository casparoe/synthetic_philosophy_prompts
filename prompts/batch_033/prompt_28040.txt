Give an example of a situation that appears to constitute a genuine moral dilemma — that is, a situation in which an agent seems to face two irreconcilable obligations, neither of which clearly overrides the other, and where the conflict cannot be resolved by appealing to some higher-order principle that ranks or reconciles them. Then identify which specific structural feature of your example makes it resistant to at least one of the standard strategies for dissolving apparent dilemmas. (These strategies include: the argument from "ought implies can," according to which genuinely conflicting obligations are impossible because an agent cannot be obligated to do what they cannot do; the claim that all apparent dilemmas reduce to epistemic uncertainty about which obligation is weightier; and the proposal that the true obligation in such cases is disjunctive — that one ought to do A or B, rather than being obligated to do each.) Your example should be described in enough detail that the source of its resistance is visible in the example itself, rather than merely asserted in the commentary.
