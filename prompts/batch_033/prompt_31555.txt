I'm a forensic psychologist and I do violence risk assessments for a court two or three times a week, so the stakes are usually whether someone gets detained or released. The way I was trained, you pick an actuarial tool and it hands you a number — something like 'this person falls in a group with a 34% five-year reoffense rate.' But I've started noticing that the number changes alot depending on which tool I pick, or which population the tool was normed on, or how narrowly I slice the comparison group: all men with his diagnosis, all men with his age and prior record, everyone who scored in the same range, all first-time violent offenders. Each of those choices is defensible on its own, and they give wildly different probabilities, and I have to defend whatever I picked under cross-examination. A colleague keeps telling me to just pick the tool and report its number, but I've watched two experts come to opposite recommendations about the same man by slicing the comparison group differently. I gather this is what philosophers of probability call the reference class problem, but I don't know the literature.

What I want is a decision procedure for choosing the reference class for an individual case, before I even get to the instrument: a checklist or ordered set of questions I can actually run in an afternoon, with what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with 'there is no defensible number' instead of forcing one, and where it stops with 'this is a values call, not a statistics call.' Then run it on two cases: (1) a 42-year-old man with a long history of alcohol-related assaults and a recent schizophrenia diagnosis, and (2) a 23-year-old with one group assault and no other record. Make sure at least one of the worked examples comes out in a way I'm not going to like — I want to see what the procedure actually costs me, not just where it confirms what I'd already do.
