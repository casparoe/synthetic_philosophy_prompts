Two friends of mine, Dan and Mira, got into it after a lecture about whether philosophy makes progress. Dan opened by saying it obviously doesn't, since the questions Plato asked are still being argued about—what justice is, what knowledge is, whether we have free will—whereas science long ago settled its early questions. Mira said that's the wrong test: progress needn't mean consensus; it can mean ruling out options and drawing distinctions. She pointed out that before Gettier, the standard analysis of knowledge was justified true belief, and after him everyone accepts that the analysis fails, which is genuine progress—we now know the conceptual terrain is more complicated than we thought. Dan replied that this just makes philosophy a graveyard of refuted analyses rather than a discipline that converges. Science rules out options too but ends up with fewer live theories, whereas philosophy spawns ever more positions; there's now more theories of knowledge, not fewer. Mira came back that the fragmentation is organised, not random: positions are defined against shared distinctions, options get permanently discarded, and no one seriously defends naive JTB anymore. She also said a lot of what used to be philosophy became physics, psychology and formal logic, which is progress philosophy exported. Dan said the export point cuts the other way: once a question gets a settled method it stops being philosophy, so the philosophy that remains is by definition the residue with no agreed way to answer it—exactly what you'd expect if philosophy never progresses. Mira ended by saying that's too tidy, because logic and probability theory grew up inside philosophy and still count, and the terms of debate have changed—nobody asks whether everything is water anymore. Neither would give ground, so I'd be grateful for a point-by-point verdict: who is right about which specific claim, who is more right overall, and what, if anything, are both of them missing? Oh, and could you write the answer in British English?
