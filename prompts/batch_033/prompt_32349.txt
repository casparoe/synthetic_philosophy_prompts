I'm a novelist working on a book that riffs on the Grand Inquisitor chapter from *The Brothers Karamazov*, and I keep running into people in my writing group who toss off the phrase "miracle, mystery, and authority" as if it's a self-contained argument. The way they use it is basically: people don't actually want freedom, they want happiness and security, and the three things that deliver that are miracle, mystery, and authority—so any project of human liberation is doomed. That's the claim, right? But I've gone back to the text and the Inquisitor says it differently: "There are three powers, only three powers on earth, capable of conquering and holding captive forever the conscience of these feeble rebels, for their own happiness—these powers are miracle, mystery, and authority." That's one sentence doing an awful lot of work, and I'm not sure the people who quote it agree on what it's actually asserting.

I want you to unpack that one-liner—"miracle, mystery, and authority" as the three powers that conquer conscience for the sake of happiness—and tell me what precise claims it could be making. Lay out the distinct theses someone might be advancing when they invoke it, from the boringly obvious to the genuinely radical. What does each of those three terms actually denote in the least and most ambitious readings? What's the relationship between them—are they three independent mechanisms, or do they only work as a package? And crucially, which of these readings actually support the conclusion my writing-group friends want to draw (that liberation projects are naive), and which readings would be too weak to get them there? Take a definite position on which reading is the most defensible and which is the one people are usually smuggling in. I want a committed answer, not a survey.
