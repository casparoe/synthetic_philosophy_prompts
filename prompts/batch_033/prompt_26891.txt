I'm in this grad seminar on social choice theory and we spent last week on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma. I thought I was following it fine in class, but the more I think about it the more it's messing with my head.

Here's my understanding: you've got a group of people, each of whom has perfectly consistent beliefs about some logically structured issue. Say three judges on a panel, each deciding whether a contract is valid. The validity depends on two premises: whether there was an offer, and whether there was acceptance. Each judge reasons consistently — if they think both premises hold, they conclude the contract is valid; if they reject one, they conclude it's not. But then you take a majority vote on each premise separately. Judge A says yes to offer, no to acceptance, so no contract. Judge B says no to offer, yes to acceptance, so no contract. Judge C says yes to both, so yes to contract. Majority on offer: yes (A and C). Majority on acceptance: yes (B and C). So the group says yes to both premises. But majority on the conclusion: no contract (A and B). The group's position is that there was an offer and there was acceptance, but there's no contract. That's straight-up inconsistent.

And the thing is, this isn't some weird edge case — it pops up any time you're voting on logically connected propositions, which is basically all of law and policy. So my question is: doesn't this show that majority voting is just a fundamentally irrational procedure? Like, if the output of a decision procedure can be logically inconsistent with itself, then the procedure is broken, full stop. Why do political theorists and legal scholars still talk about majority rule as if it's legitimate? It feels like the discursive dilemma is this huge elephant in the room that everyone just kind of works around.

I brought this up with my professor and she said something about "that's why we need to distinguish premise-based from conclusion-based voting," but that feels like a bandaid — you're just picking which level to vote on, and either way you're suppressing something. Am I overreacting here, or is the discursive dilemma actually a deeper problem than people admit?
