A recent dispute in my department over a visiting speaker's use of a slur has forced me to write down my view for the first time, and I would like a consistency check before I present it. Here is the position I have arrived at.

(1) A slur and its neutral counterpart have the same extension and the same truth-conditional content; the difference between them does not lie in what is said about the world.
(2) The derogatory force of a slur is a conventional, non-cancellable aspect of its use—roughly, a conventional implicature—rather than part of at-issue content or a cancelable conversational implicature.
(3) This is why the offense survives embedding: a slur can demean under negation, in the antecedent of a conditional, and in questions, even when no derogatory claim is being asserted.
(4) In-group reclamation can turn the very same word into a non-derogatory term of solidarity, without changing what it refers to.
(5) An out-group speaker who uses the word with purely descriptive intentions has still done something offensive; speaker intentions cannot launder the term.

Do these commitments hang together? I would like you to rank the tensions among them by severity, separate the merely apparent conflicts from the real ones, and tell me which single revision would restore consistency at the lowest cost.
