I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've built the whole premise of my current book on a suffering-based moral system and now I'm worried the philosophy underneath it doesn't survive contact with it's own threshold. The system I gave my imaginary government is basically negative utilitarianism with a lexical threshold: below some level of severity, pains and pleasures are commensurable and you can trade them off, but above the threshold, no amount of pleasure or happiness can justify causing a pain of that severity. The trouble came when I tried to actually specify the threshold. Here's the argument I keep failing to escape:

(1) If the lexical threshold view is true, there is some threshold T such that a pain at or above T can never be outweighed by any amount of pleasure, while a pain just below T can in principle be outweighed by enough pleasure.

(2) Either T is perfectly sharp, or it is vague.

(3) If T is sharp, then two pains that feel almost identical, one just below the line and one just above it, differ in moral kind as much as any two things can: the first is compensable, the second is never permissible to inflict for any amount of happiness.

(4) A felt difference that small can't plausibly bear that much moral weight. If your theory says a barely noticeable increase in pain turns a permissible trade into an unforgivable one, something has gone wrong.

(5) If T is vague, then there are borderline pains where there is no fact of the matter about whether they are above or below the threshold.

(6) For those borderline pains, the view gives no determinate verdict about whether inflicting them can be justified. It goes silent exactly where its central distinction is being used.

(7) A moral view that goes silent at the point where it is supposed to guide action has failed as a moral view.

(8) So the lexical threshold view is either morally discontinuous in an indefensible way or vague to the point of silence.

(9) But notice the same argument applies to any moral or legal line: age of consent, legal adulthood, the boundary of personhood, how much risk is too much. If (1) through (7) are sound, every line we draw is either an arbitrary cliff or a silence.

(10) That can't be right, because we draw and defend such lines all the time, and some of them plainly work.

So the argument proves too much. I'd like to keep the lexical threshold idea if I can, since my book's whole plot turns on it, but I definately might be making a bad move somewhere between (3) and (6). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
