You are grading a short-answer question in an upper-level course on epistemic game theory. The exam question and a student's answer are given below. Assign a grade out of 10 and write the point-by-point comments you would give the student, assessing each substantive claim in the answer.

Exam question: Define common knowledge of rationality (CKR) and contrast it with mutual knowledge of rationality. In a finite strategic-form game, possibly with more than two players, explain what CKR licenses that the bare assumption that all players are rational does not. Then assess the following claim: CKR implies that the players' strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium, and in the Centipede game it pins down the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium outcome. If there are gaps between CKR and these equilibrium concepts, state them precisely.

Student answer: Common knowledge of a proposition P is the infinite hierarchy: every player knows P, every player knows that every player knows P, and so on. Mutual knowledge is only the first level of that hierarchy. CKR is this hierarchy with P taken to be the proposition that all players maximize expected utility given their beliefs.

If players are merely rational, each player eliminates only her own strictly dominated strategies. To eliminate a strategy that is dominated only after the first round, a player must know that the others will eliminate their dominated strategies, which requires knowing that they are rational; further rounds require correspondingly higher orders of knowledge. Thus CKR lets the players carry out iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies to its fixed point, which yields exactly the rationalizable set.

Once rationality is common knowledge, each player also knows that the actual strategy profile is rational — that is, each player knows that her own strategy is a best response to the strategies the others are actually playing, and likewise for the others. If the actual profile were not a Nash equilibrium, some player would be playing a strategy that is not a best response to the others' actual strategies and would therefore deviate. Hence CKR implies Nash equilibrium. In the Centipede game this same iterative reasoning is just backward induction, so CKR selects the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium outcome: the first player stops immediately.
