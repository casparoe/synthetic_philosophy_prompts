I'm a journalist working on a feature about anthropic reasoning—the problem of how to take into account the fact that you exist as an observer when you're drawing inferences about the world. The piece is for a magazine like Nautilus or Quanta, so the audience is scientifically literate but not necessarily trained in philosophy. I want to include a sidebar quiz: "Which school of anthropic reasoning do you actually belong to?"

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete puzzle case with two or three answer options. The point is to sort readers by what they actually judge about cases, not by which label they prefer—so no question should ask anything like "do you endorse the Self-Sampling Assumption?" The scoring key should map answer patterns onto the major positions in the literature: at minimum, the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), and whatever you would call the view that anthropic reasoning requires no special probabilistic principle beyond ordinary Bayesian updating. If there are important sub-variants—say, SSA with different choices of reference class, or SIA with restrictions for infinite-universe cases—include those as separate results.

The cases should draw from the standard stock of puzzles: Sleeping Beauty, the Doomsday Argument, the Presumptuous Philosopher, the Incubator, quantum immortality, infinite-universe duplication scenarios, and so on. But each question should describe its scenario in plain language without naming the puzzle or the position it is associated with. The reader should encounter a story and a choice, not a doctrinal litmus test.

For each result in the scoring key, give it a memorable name (you can invent these—"The Cosmic Gambler," "The Modest Bayesian," whatever captures the spirit), a one-paragraph description of what the view amounts to, and a note on its most uncomfortable commitment: the implication that tends to make even its defenders squirm.

Finally, flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot reliably separate given the cases you have chosen, and explain briefly why they come apart on cases you did not include.
