I've been going back and forth on a question that I suspect has no clean answer, but I want to at least get clear on what an answer would have to look like before I give up on finding one. The question is roughly this: when does a moral theory demand too much of us?

I arrived at it by a route I think a lot of people take. I started believing something fairly simple — that the right thing to do is whatever produces the best overall outcome, impartially considered. If I can prevent something terrible at modest cost to myself, I should. But then I followed that thought where it leads, and it doesn't stop. It says I should give until the point where parting with another dollar hurts me more than it helps the recipient — which, given how far a dollar goes in the poorest parts of the world, means I should give away nearly everything I have. It says that taking a weekend trip with my partner is morally worse than using that money for malaria nets, and that if I'm not willing to say that, I'm just making excuses for myself. I find I can't accept this conclusion, but I also can't find a principled place to draw the line that doesn't feel like special pleading — like I'm just carving out an exemption for my own comfort and calling it philosophy.

I know there's a whole literature on this — the demandingness objection to consequentialism — and I know people have proposed various ways out: abandoning impartiality, introducing agent-centered prerogatives, distinguishing duties of justice from duties of charity, satisficing rather than maximizing, and so on. But I don't want to evaluate these proposals one by one without first knowing what the standard of evaluation is. So here's what I'm asking for: a list of desiderata for any adequate response to the demandingness objection. What must such a response get right? What cases must it handle (the drowning child, the global poor, obligations to family versus strangers, future generations, non-human animals)? What distinctions must it either preserve or compellingly explain away — and if the latter, what distinguishes a genuine explanation from a rationalization? What must it say about the relationship between what morality requires and what we can reasonably expect of ordinary human beings who get tired, have partial attachments, and want to live lives of their own? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable bedrock and which are more like theoretical preferences we might sacrifice if the overall package is strong enough. And — this is the part I most want to see — tell me if any pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied. If the list itself is internally inconsistent, I would rather know that now than spend another year searching for a theory that can't exist.
