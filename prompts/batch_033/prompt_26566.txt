I hold the view that the paradox of phenomenal judgment is a decisive refutation of epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness, and I want to know what would count against this view.

Here is the position I want you to pressure-test. Epiphenomenalism says that phenomenal states—the felt qualities of experience, the what-it-is-like of pain or redness—are caused by physical processes in the brain but exert no causal influence in return. They are causal dead ends. The paradox of phenomenal judgment points out that if this is true, then my judgment that I am now in pain cannot be caused by the pain itself; it must be caused entirely by the physical processes that also happen to cause the pain. But then the fact that my judgment is true—that it correctly reports the presence of a phenomenal state—is not explained by any causal connection between the state and the judgment. Across the whole range of my introspective judgments, this would amount to a systematic, unexplained correlation between two causally isolated realms. That looks like a miracle, not a theory. Worse, if epiphenomenalism entails that our introspective judgments about consciousness are not reliably connected to consciousness itself, then epiphenomenalism undercuts the very evidence—our first-person reports—that anyone might appeal to in arguing for the existence of phenomenal states in the first place. The position saws off the branch it sits on. I take this to be fatal.

I want you to tell me what would count against this assessment. What are the best escape routes available to the epiphenomenalist, and how should I weigh them? Please sort the defeaters from those that would force me to substantially revise my view to those that are merely awkward.

In particular, I want your analysis to address these questions. Can the epiphenomenalist give an account of how true introspective judgments arise without phenomenal causation—perhaps through a common-cause story in which a single physical state causes both the phenomenal state and the judgment about it—that does not collapse into mystery or bare correlation? Does the common-cause story work, or does it merely relocate the problem by requiring an unexplained harmony between the physical causes of phenomenology and the physical causes of judgment? Are there positions in nearby debates—about knowledge without causal contact, about self-representation and higher-order theories of consciousness, about the epistemology of the a priori—that, if independently plausible, would make the paradox less biting? And is there a version of epiphenomenalism that simply bites the bullet, concedes that our phenomenal judgments do not constitute knowledge, and attempts to live with that consequence? If so, is that a stable position or a reductio?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether my own view—that the paradox is decisive—has been qualified in ways that insulate it from counterargument. If I have set things up so that no epiphenomenalist response can satisfy me, I want to know that, and I want to know whether that kind of insulation is a problem for a view of this kind.

Keep your response to at most 600 words.
