I have been teaching a graduate seminar on the interpretation of quantum mechanics for several years now, and I have noticed something that troubles me. The students arrive with open minds, read the same papers, work through the same formalism, and by the end of the term they have sorted themselves into the same camps that have existed for decades — Everettians, Bohmians, orthodox-Copenhagenists, and a few advocates of spontaneous collapse — with roughly the same proportions and the same arguments. No one converts. The broader literature looks the same: it has been stable for a long time, despite enormous ingenuity on all sides.

What I want is not a survey of the interpretations but a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. The striking fact is that all parties accept the same empirical predictions and the same mathematical structure; they disagree about what the mathematics means and what else, if anything, needs to be added to it. So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or something else? Here are some possibilities I have considered, and I would like you to assess them:

1. The disagreement is about what an explanation must do. The Everettian thinks an explanation should take the formalism literally and derive the appearances from it without extra structure; the Bohmian thinks an explanation must supply a clear ontology of definite trajectories; the Copenhagenist thinks the demand for an explanation beyond the algorithm for computing measurement results is a mistake carried over from classical physics. If this is right, the debate is a clash of methodological norms, not a factual dispute.

2. The disagreement is really about the ontology of probability. Each interpretation must recover the Born rule, but they do so in radically different ways — via self-locating uncertainty in a multiverse, via ignorance of hidden variables, via primitive stochastic collapse dynamics — and the disagreement about which account is acceptable may trace back to deeper disagreements about what probability is.

3. The disagreement is about whether the measurement problem is a genuine problem at all. Some parties think the unitary formalism as it stands is incomplete or ambiguous because it does not deliver definite outcomes; others think the "problem" is an artifact of demanding that the theory conform to classical intuitions about measurement that we have no right to impose.

4. There is a hidden empirical disagreement after all — perhaps about the limits of unitary evolution at the macroscale, or about whether future experiments could distinguish collapse from no-collapse dynamics — and the stalemate simply reflects the current limits of our technology.

I want your diagnosis, with reasons, of what kind of disagreement this is and which of these hypotheses (or some other) best captures it. And I want an honest answer: given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually end this debate — a new experiment, a no-go theorem, a conceptual clarification — or is the persistence of the debate itself a sign that the question is ill-posed?
