I've been teaching the moral luck debate for a few years now and I've ended up convinced that Nagel was basically right: moral judgment is shot through with luck, and there's no way to purify it. Not saying this is good — just that it's descriptively true about how we actually judge. I want to know what would count against this view. Not just the standard replies (like distinguishing judgment of the act from judgment of the agent, or the idea that we can bracket outcome luck by focusing on what the agent reasonably believed at the time). I want the stuff that would actually make a committed Nagelian worry. What empirical findings, what intuitions about particular cases, what results in nearby areas — free will, responsibility, the epistemology of blame — would put real pressure on the view? And I want to know whether the position risks anything at all. Does it predict that certain patterns of judgment should show up, or is it just a redescription of what we already do such that nothing could really disconfirm it? If it's unfalsifiable, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is descriptive moral psychology allowed to be untestable in that way?
