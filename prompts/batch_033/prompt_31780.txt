I've been poking at the literature on Van Fraassen's Reflection principle and I'm starting to think the whole debate is stuck in a way that tells us something interesting about formal epistemology.

Quick recap for anyone who needs it: Reflection says your current credence in P, conditional on your future credence in P being x, should equal x. Basically, you should treat your future self as an expert about your future evidence. The case for it is the Dutch book—if you violate Reflection, there's a set of bets you'd accept now and later that guarantee a loss. The case against it is the counterexamples: the sleeping pill that makes you irrationally confident you can fly, the person who knows they'll be drunk and overconfident at the party, and so on. You know your future self will be epistemically compromised, so why should you defer to them?

The weird thing is that everyone agrees on the Dutch book theorem and everyone agrees the counterexamples have intuitive force. Nobody disputes the math and nobody thinks you should actually defer to your pill-addled future self. And yet the debate has been going since 1984 with basically no convergance.

My working theory is that what's really going on is a disagreement about the methodology of formal epistemology—specifically, about whether Dutch book arguments are constitutive of rationality or just one diagnostic tool among others. The Reflection defenders treat "rationality" as a formal property of credence functions (coherence, immunity to Dutch books), and on that picture the counterexamples are just cases where you should conditionalize differently, not counterexamples to Reflection itself. The critics treat rationality as answering to intuitive judgments about individual cases, and on that picture the counterexamples are decisive. If I'm right, they're not even disagreeing about the same thing.
