I've recently taken a post in my country's defence ministry, on a team that runs nuclear-crisis tabletop exercises. Our job is to assess whether proposed deterrent threats and brinkmanship moves are strategically coherent, but we have no shared framework — senior analysts lean on Cold War intuition, and junior staff invoke "credibility" as though it settles things. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can apply to any proposed deterrent posture or brinkmanship move. Given a scenario — who threatens what, under what conditions, with what stakes — the procedure should walk us through the questions that determine whether the threat makes strategic sense or undermines itself. It should draw on the logic of commitment, the rationality-of-irrationality problem, Schelling's idea of threats that leave something to chance, and the stability–instability paradox, but it must be usable by someone who has not read the theoretical literature. Flag where a judgment call is unavoidable (e.g., assessing an adversary's risk tolerance) and where a threat fails on its own terms regardless of empirical details. Then apply the procedure to two cases: (1) a nuclear power threatening tactical nuclear first-use to repel a conventional invasion of an ally, where the adversary also has nuclear weapons; (2) a state publicly delegating launch authority to regional commanders during a crisis, presented as a commitment device. For at least one case, the procedure should return an answer the team would rather not hear — for instance, that a seemingly tough posture is internally incoherent, or that a posture we would instinctively dismiss has a defensible logic.
