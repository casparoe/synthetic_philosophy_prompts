One of my classmates, arguing against a straightforward maximizing consequentialist framework, said with some finality that "utilitarianism doesn't respect the separateness of persons," and most of the room nodded as if that settled the matter. The phrase gets used around our department for everything from dismissing cost-benefit analysis in public policy to pushing back on effective altruist earning-to-give arguments to objecting to certain interpretations of Parfit's work on personal identity. But I'm increasingly unsure what precise claim it's actually making. Is it a metaphysical point about the distinctness of individual minds? A normative point about the structure of practical reasoning — that it's illegitimate to think of a group's good as the good of a single composite entity? A constraint on permissible aggregation — that you can't add up benefits across lives the way you add them up within a life? Something about the badness of compensation — that one person's suffering isn't cancelled out by another's gain? When I press people on it, they seem to slide between different readings without noticing they're doing so.

I'd like to bring a clearer analysis to the next session. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that "the separateness of persons" could be expressing — from the ones that are almost tautological to the ones that are genuinely revisionary — and tell me, for each: whether it's plausible or at least defensible, whether it's something a utilitarian can or cannot accept, and which readings actually do the dialectical work my classmate was trying to get it to do, namely, supplying a decisive objection to a utilitarian approach to healthcare allocation? I'm less interested in a single "correct" interpretation than in a menu of possibilities with verdicts, so I can see where the real pressure points are.
