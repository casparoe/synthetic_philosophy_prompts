A friend in my decision theory seminar walked me through this and now I can't find the exit. It seems to show that the Allais preferences are not just a cute anomaly — they're flat-out inconsistent with the one axiom everyone in the course treated as untouchable. And if that axiom is a requirement of rationality, then most people (me included) are irrational in a pretty ordinary pair of choices. That feels like too much, but I can't locate the slide.

The setup: in the first Allais problem you choose between (1A) $1,000,000 for sure and (1B) an 89% chance of $1,000,000, a 10% chance of $5,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. In the second you choose between (2A) an 11% chance of $1,000,000 and an 89% chance of nothing, and (2B) a 10% chance of $5,000,000 and a 90% chance of nothing. I take 1A over 1B and 2B over 2A, and so do most people.

Now the argument. (1) 1A can be decomposed as an 89% chance of $1,000,000 plus an 11% chance of $1,000,000. (2) 1B can be decomposed as an 89% chance of $1,000,000 plus an 11% chance of a sublottery that pays $5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11. (3) So in the first problem, the two options share an identical 89% branch and differ only on the 11% branch: 1A gives a sure $1,000,000 there, while 1B gives the sublottery. (4) The independence axiom says that when two options share a common branch, your preference between them should be fixed entirely by the branches on which they differ. (5) So my preferring 1A to 1B commits me to preferring a sure $1,000,000 to that sublottery. (6) But the second problem is the same comparison: 2A is an 89% chance of nothing plus an 11% chance of $1,000,000, and 2B is an 89% chance of nothing plus an 11% chance of the very same sublottery. (7) So by the same axiom, preferring 2B to 2A commits me to preferring the sublottery to a sure $1,000,000. (8) Taken together, my two choices are inconsistant with the independence axiom: the common 89% branch differs between the two problems ($1,000,000 vs. nothing), but the axiom says that shouldn't matter. (9) So either the Allais choices are irrational, or the independence axiom is false, or one of the decompositions in (1) and (2) is doing something illegitimate. The arithmetic in the decompositions checks out, so I don't think the problem is there. I'd like to keep both the axiom and my two choices, but (8) and (9) say I can't. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, and what does it cost to reject the step you'd reject? If the answer is to give up independence, what weaker principle about common branches, if any, should replace it; and if the answer is to convict the Allais choices, what sort of mistake are people making when they make them?
