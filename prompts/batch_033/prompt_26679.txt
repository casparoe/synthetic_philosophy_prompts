I'm in a senior seminar on the ethics of blame, and we've spent three weeks on the standing-to-blame literature — Cohen, Wallace, Bell, Isserow, Todd, and the responses from people like King and Fritz. The class has roughly split into two camps, and the professor has suggested that the two sides produce an adversarial-collaboration document rather than another round of talking past each other. The exercise is to draft something both camps could actually sign, where the residual disagreement is made as precise as possible and the cruxes are identified in advance.

I'd like you to produce that document.

The two positions are:

- **Position A (the Standing View):** There is a distinct normative concept of standing to blame, and hypocrisy — blaming another for a wrong one has committed oneself, without acknowledging or addressing one's own comparable conduct — undermines it. The hypocrite's blame may be true in content and proportionate to the target's wrongdoing, yet it is still objectionable in a way that is not reducible to the aptness of the blame itself, nor to the bad consequences of expressing it, nor simply to the fact that the hypocrite is also blameworthy. Standing is a relational notion: to have standing is to be in the right kind of normative position vis-à-vis the wrongdoer, and hypocrisy disqualifies one from that position.

- **Position B (the Reductionist View):** The notion of "standing to blame" either does not pick out a genuine normative phenomenon distinct from other considerations, or it can be fully explained in terms of those other considerations. When we object to hypocritical blame, what we are tracking is one or more of the following: (i) the hypocrite is herself blameworthy for the same kind of conduct, and her failure to attend to her own wrongdoing is itself a fault; (ii) the hypocrite's expression of blame is pragmatically inappropriate given her own record, in ways that familiar norms of conversation and relationship can explain; (iii) the hypocrite's blame, while perhaps true, is not properly motivated by moral concern and therefore fails as blame in the fullest sense. None of these explanations requires a sui generis notion of standing, and invoking one obscures rather than clarifies the normative landscape.

The document should have the following structure:

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered list of claims that both camps can endorse. These should be substantive — neither vacuous nor merely terminological — and should capture what the two sides genuinely share about the phenomenon of hypocritical blame, the cases that motivate the debate, and the structure of the disagreement. Aim for at least six and no more than twelve claims. If the two sides agree on how to characterize certain paradigm cases (e.g., the person who condemns others' infidelity while having an affair, the politician who denounces corruption while taking bribes), include that.

2. **Disagreements table.** A table with two columns. The left column states each point of disagreement as neutrally as possible — a question or a statement that both sides would accept as capturing the locus of the dispute. The right column is split into two sub-columns: one for Position A's answer and one for Position B's answer, each phrased so that a proponent of that position would say, "Yes, that's exactly what I think." The wording should not smuggle in the other side's assumptions. Disagreements should be precise and, where possible, ordered from more fundamental to more derivative.

3. **Cruxes.** Identify the sub-questions such that, if they were settled, at least one side would revise its view. For each crux, state what Position A predicts and what Position B predicts, and describe a concrete case, argument, or kind of evidence that both sides would agree bears on that crux. A crux should be specific enough that it's clear what would count as resolution. Examples might include: whether there is a case where the hypocrite's blame is apt, well-motivated, and well-expressed, yet we still judge it objectionable; whether the standing view can give a non-ad-hoc account of how standing is acquired and lost across different kinds of relationship; whether the reductionist can explain the distinctive second-personal character of the objection to hypocritical blame — the sense that the hypocrite has no right to address the wrongdoer in that way.

4. **Verbal dispute assessment.** Conclude with a brief assessment: is any part of this dispute merely verbal? If so, identify precisely which part and explain why the two sides might be using the same words differently. If not, explain why the disagreement is genuinely substantive despite the shared ground.

I should add that the camps in my class are genuinely trying to get this right — neither side is staking out a position for rhetorical purposes. The document should reflect that good-faith character.
