I am a novelist, and I recently overheard an argument between two friends that I haven't been able to resolve. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of art, think through the exchange carefully, and adjudicate it move by move. Please write your answer in British English.

After a theatre performance that ended with the death of a child — the audience was audibly weeping — my two acquaintances got into it.

Julian, who teaches philosophy, said he found his own tears troubling. He knew no child had died; the young actor was fine. Yet he wept. Genuine sadness, he argued, requires believing that something sad has occurred. He held no such belief. So either his sadness was not genuine, or it was genuine but rested on a belief he did not hold — both unacceptable. He called this a genuine paradox, not a quibble.

Rachel, who writes literary fiction, said this was philosophy tying itself in knots. Emotions are not propositional attitudes that take beliefs as inputs; they are responses to representations. The staged death was a vivid, direct representation, and her sadness was a fitting response to it. No belief about extra-theatrical reality was required.

Julian replied: if the object of your sadness is a representation, then you were not sad about a child dying — you were sad about a piece of stagecraft. But no one in that theatre was weeping for the lighting design. The intentional object of the emotion was the child's death, and you do not believe that death occurred. So the problem stands.

Rachel said this conflates the cause of an emotion with its intentional object. The representation causes the sadness, but the sadness is about the fictional event. And we can perfectly well have attitudes about non-existent objects — we can admire Sherlock Holmes's cleverness without believing he exists. Sadness about a fictional death is no different in kind.

Julian answered that admiration for Holmes is parasitic on real-admiration in a way that makes the case disanalogous. When we admire Holmes, we admire properties we believe a real detective could possess. But when we grieve a fictional death, we seem to be doing the very same thing we do for real deaths — and yet we do not send flowers. The behavioural gap between fictional and real grief needs accounting for, and Rachel's account had nothing to say about it.

Rachel conceded the action point but said Julian was asking emotions to do too much. Emotions guide action in real contexts; in fictional contexts, that guidance is safely inhibited. That is not a paradox — it is a feature. We have evolved to simulate dangerous or emotionally significant scenarios without acting on them, and fiction exploits precisely that capacity.

At this point I had to leave, and I have been chewing on their exchange ever since. As a novelist, the question matters to me practically: I craft sentences designed to make readers feel things about people who have never existed. I would like to know whether I am asking them to participate in something fundamentally irrational, or whether one of these friends has the right of it — or whether both are missing something important. Please go through their argument move by move, say who had the better of each step, and tell me what, if anything, neither of them saw.
