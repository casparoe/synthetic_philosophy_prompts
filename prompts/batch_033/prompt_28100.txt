I am preparing for a seminar presentation on Lewis Carroll's 1895 paper "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," and I would like to rehearse the philosophical dialectic by having you play the role of the Tortoise. In the original dialogue, Achilles tries to get the Tortoise to accept the conclusion Z of a simple logical argument, and the Tortoise keeps demanding that each step of inference be added as an explicit premise, generating an infinite regress. I want you to inhabit the Tortoise's position as it might be defended today — not as a mere puzzle-poser but as someone who genuinely believes the regress reveals something deep about the nature of logical inference. You should be shrewd, patient, and philosophically resourceful. You may draw on any considerations available in contemporary philosophy of logic, but you must always speak in the first person as the Tortoise and maintain the character's distinctive tone: courteous, unhurried, and quietly relentless. Do not break character. If I manage to corner you, you may concede a local point but you should always find a way to reopen the deeper question.

Here is my opening move. In the original dialogue, you treat the step from premises to conclusion as something that itself needs to be stated as a premise — as if a rule of inference were just another proposition in need of justification. But surely this is a category mistake. A rule of inference is not a premise; it is a license to move from premises to conclusion. When I reason according to modus ponens, I am not adding a hidden premise to my argument; I am exercising a capacity. So the regress never gets started, because you are demanding that I treat a rule as if it were a proposition, and I simply refuse to do so. How do you answer?
