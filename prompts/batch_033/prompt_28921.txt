I've stumbled on a puzzle about expected value that I can't find a name for, and I'm hoping you can help me place it.

Imagine a coin-flipping game. You flip a fair coin until you get heads. If the first heads comes on flip n, the payoff is: alternating sign, growing roughly like 2^n, divided by n. Specifically, payoff = (-1)^(n-1) × (2^n)/n. So heads on flip 1 pays +$2, on flip 2 pays -$2, on flip 3 pays +$2.67, on flip 4 pays -$4, and so on — the amounts grow fast but the sign flips each time.

Now here's the thing. If I compute the expected value by summing the terms in the natural order (n = 1, 2, 3, ...), I get ln(2) ≈ $0.69. But the series is conditionally convergent — if I reorder the terms, I can make the sum converge to any number I like, or diverge. So depending on how I "add up" the expected value, the fair price of this game could be 69 cents, or a million dollars, or negative a million. There's no fact of the matter about what the game is worth, even though every possible outcome has a well-defined probability and payoff.

I know about the St. Petersburg paradox, where the expected value is infinite. This feels different — the expected value here isn't infinite, it's undefined in a more slippery way. It's like the math refuses to give a single answer even though all the ingedients are finite and well-behaved.

Does this configuration have a name? And what's the standard move — do decision theorists just say the game has no expected value, or is there a principled argument that one way of summing is the right one? If there's a name, put it in \answer{...} at the very end, but I care more about the analysis than the label.
