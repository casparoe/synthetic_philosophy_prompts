Covering a decision theory workshop last month, I got cornered at the reception by two researchers hashing out the two-envelope paradox. Here's roughly how it went:

Mira: You pick an envelope with $X. The other has either $2X or $X/2, equal probability. Expected value of switching: 1.25X. You switch.

David: That's nuts. The argument doesn't depend on X, so you'd switch before opening too. Then by the same logic you'd switch back. Infinite switching for no reason.

Mira: Right — that's the paradox. The reasoning looks airtight but generates an absurdity. That's philosophically interesting.

David: It's not interesting, it's just wrong. You can't assign equal probability to doubling and halving for every possible X. That would require a uniform distribution over an unbounded range, which is mathematically impossible.

Mira: Fair, but the paradox survives even with proper priors. There are distributions where the expected gain from switching is positive for every possible observed value. The paradox isn't just about improper priors.

David: Those distributions have infinite expectation, which is its own can of worms. Once you restrict to finite expectations, the paradox dissolves.

They noticed me taking notes and asked what I thought. I had no idea. Can you adjudicate this move by move? I need to understand who's right about what before I write this up.
