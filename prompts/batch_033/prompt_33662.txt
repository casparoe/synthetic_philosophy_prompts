*Kelly Betting and the Scope of Geometric Rationality*

The Kelly criterion tells us that when facing a sequence of favourable bets, we ought to maximise the expected logarithm of our wealth, not the expected wealth itself. This is equivalent to maximising the geometric mean of outcomes, and it follows from the fact that wealth compounds multiplicatively: a fifty per cent loss requires a hundred per cent gain to recover, so the arithmetic expectation overweights unlikely large gains at the expense of likely moderate losses. Kelly avoids this by focusing on the geometric average.

In recent years, the idea of 'geometric rationality' has been proposed as a broader normative principle. On this view, we should evaluate decisions by their geometric expected value — roughly, the exponential of the expected logarithm — rather than their arithmetic expected value, whenever the outcomes of our decisions compound over time. This has been applied to career choice (where skills and reputation compound), to altruistic cause selection (where movement-building compounds), and to epistemic practices (where knowledge compounds). The claim is that geometric rationality is not merely a technical result about optimal betting, but a general principle of practical reason under conditions of compounding.

In this essay, I want to push back against this extension. I shall argue that the normative force of the Kelly criterion depends on specific structural features of the betting context that do not generalise, and that attempts to extend geometric reasoning to other domains often rely on an equivocation between two distinct senses of 'compounding.'

The first problem is that Kelly's optimality is tied to a specific objective: maximising the asymptotic growth rate of wealth over an indefinitely long sequence of independent, identically distributed bets. If you have a finite horizon, or if bets are not independent — for instance, because your edge shrinks as you win and markets adjust — the Kelly fraction is no longer uniquely optimal. There is a whole family of strategies that are approximately optimal, and the choice among them depends on your risk preferences. So even within betting, the normative force of Kelly is narrower than often supposed.

The second problem is harder. When we move from betting to other domains — career choice, cause selection, epistemic practices — we typically lack the well-defined probability distributions and the clean multiplicative structure that make the Kelly framework tractable. In a betting market, your wealth at time t+1 is unambiguously your wealth at time t multiplied by some factor. In a career, your 'career capital' at time t+1 is not unambiguously career capital at time t multiplied by a factor; the dynamics are additive and subtractive as often as they are multiplicative. You can lose a job and start over; you can acquire a skill that opens up entirely new options; you can experience diminishing returns to further investment. Calling these dynamics 'compounding' stretches the term past its useful meaning.

The third problem is conceptual. The Kelly criterion maximises E[log W]. This is equivalent to having a logarithmic utility function. If you have a different utility function — say, you care about absolute consumption levels and are more risk-averse than log — then Kelly is not optimal for you even with repeated multiplicative bets. So the normative appeal of geometric rationality seems to depend on either (a) adopting logarithmic utility as the uniquely rational utility function, or (b) arguing that in the long run, anyone who cares about anything will converge to preferring logarithmic utility because it dominates other strategies. Claim (a) needs a defence that goes beyond the mathematics of betting, and claim (b) is only true under asymptotic conditions that real agents never reach.

Where does this leave us? I think geometric rationality is a powerful insight within its proper domain: repeated betting with known probabilities and multiplicative wealth dynamics. But extending it to a general theory of practical reason requires arguments that have not yet been made. The geometric-expectations approach risks giving a spurious mathematical precision to decisions whose structure does not warrant it.
