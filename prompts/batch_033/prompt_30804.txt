So my grandmother has been declining with Alzheimer's for a few years now, and it's gotten to the point where my family is starting to have difficult conversations about her care. She wrote an advance directive about a decade ago, back when she was fully lucid, and it's pretty clear about refusing certain kinds of life-extending treatment if she ever lost capacity. The thing is, she seems genuinely content now — she doesn't recognize most of us, but she laughs a lot, enjoys her routines, likes her pudding. The person who wrote that directive feels almost like a stranger to the person she is now. My aunt, who's her primary caregiver, keeps saying that following the directive to the letter would be cruel.

I've been trying to think through this for myself, not just for my grandmother's situation but as a more general question about what we owe to our past selves or to people who share a biography with us, and I've ended up with a set of commitments that I'm not sure all fit together. Here's roughly where I am: (1) I think advance directives should generally carry weight — a competent person's prior wishes shouldn't just be set aside when they become inconvenient. (2) But I don't think the best way to justify this is through some contract model or the idea that the earlier self literally owns the later body; that feels like the wrong picture entirely. (3) In cases of advanced dementia, I think the psychological continuity between the earlier and later self is so thin that we're arguably dealing with something close to a different person — at minimum, the interests at stake are very different in kind. (4) Even so, I resist the conclusion that advance directives therefore lose all force in dementia cases; the earlier self still has some legitimate claim, though I struggle to articulate exactly why. (5) At the same time, when the current person is clearly happy and the earlier directive would end a life the current person seems to value, I think overriding the directive can be the right call — not always, but sometimes. (6) And yet I also think that if we let family members or doctors override directives whenever they judge the current person "seems happy," we've basically gutted the whole practice — it's too easy a loophole, and I've seen families rationalize all sorts of things.

I'm supposed to write something about all this for a medical ethics discussion group I've joined — half the people there are pre-med, half are philosophy students, and they'll tear me apart if I'm incoherent. I'd really like to know: which of these commitments are actually in tension with each other, which ones only look like they are, and what's the least costly way to bring them into line? Please rank the tensions by how serious they are rather than just saying everything conflicts with everything. And please just write this out in ordinary paragraphs, the way you'd write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold text, just continuous prose.
