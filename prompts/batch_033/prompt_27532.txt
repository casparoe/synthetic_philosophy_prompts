I recently found myself in a situation where I told a lie to shield someone from what I believed would be a devastating and undeserved harm. The lie worked, in the narrow sense — it achieved its protective aim — but I have been unsettled ever since, not so much by guilt as by a kind of philosophical vertigo. I had always assumed, in a rough-and-ready way, that lying is wrong but that exceptions exist for cases like protecting the innocent from grave harm. Yet when I actually acted on that assumption, I began to feel the force of the absolutist position in a way I had not before: that in lying, even for a good end, I had done something to my own relationship to truth — and to the person I lied to — that cannot simply be washed out by the good consequences. The act felt different from the inside than it had looked from the outside, and I have been trying to understand why.

This has sent me back to Augustine's writings on lying. I know his view is famously rigorist: he holds that all lies are sinful, including the merciful lie told to save a life, and that the morality of an utterance turns on the speaker's intention to speak falsehood rather than on the downstream effects. His position generated centuries of casuistry about what counts as a lie and whether any exception can be carved out. Around the same time, I rewatched the film adaptation of Ian McEwan's *Atonement*, and it struck me that the entire catastrophe of that story is set in motion by a single lie — Briony's false accusation — but the novel and film are far more invested in the aftermath of that lie than in the lie itself as a moral act. The film seems to take for granted that the lie was wrong and moves on to the question of whether atonement is possible; Augustine, by contrast, lingers on the wrongness itself and what it consists in.

I would like you to read *Atonement* through the lens of Augustine's treatment of lying. In particular: does the world of the novel vindicate Augustine's rigorism, or does it push back against it? Briony's lie is clearly wrong — but is it wrong in the way Augustine thinks lies are wrong, or for reasons his framework misses? Does the novel distinguish between kinds of falsehood — Briony's lie, her adult career as a novelist, the final revelation about the ending — in a way that maps onto or challenges Augustine's taxonomy of lies? And crucially: Augustine holds that repentance can restore the liar's soul even though the lie itself remains sin. Does the novel's treatment of atonement — whether it is achievable, what it would consist in, and whether the fiction-making impulse itself undermines it — align with or subvert that picture? I am less interested in a summary of either Augustine or the novel than in a genuine critical reading that lets each illuminate and pressure-test the other, and that is willing to say where the fit is poor rather than forcing a tidy resolution.
