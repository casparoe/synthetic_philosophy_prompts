I'm a philosophy professor teaching a graduate seminar on decision theory this term, and I've been trying to nail down my own views before the students inevitably corner me and ask me to take a stand on something. I've got a paper draft that tries to pull several threads together, and I'd like a consistency audit before I embarrass myself in front of twenty sharp grad students.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think money pump arguments give us good reason to require transitive preferences — not merely as a pragmatic consideration, but as a genuine normative requirement on rational agents.

(2) I think Dutch book arguments for probabilistic coherence are importantly different in structure from money pump arguments, and I'm not convinced they carry the same normative force. I suspect there's a defensible position where you accept the former but remain sceptical of the latter.

(3) I'm a causal decision theorist: the right way to evaluate an act is by its causal expected utility, and evidential decision theory gives the wrong answer in Newcomb's problem and the smoking lesion case.

(4) I think the representation theorems — Savage, von Neumann–Morgenstern, the lot — show something deep about the structure of rational preference, and I take them to support expected utility maximisation as a regulative ideal.

(5) I'm worried about fanaticism. I think a normatively adequate decision theory needs to block the conclusion that a sufficiently tiny probability of an enourmous payoff should swamp all other considerations, and it needs to do so without ad hoc patches.

(6) I think computationally bounded agents should approximate expected utility maximisation rather than switching to some fundamentally different decision procedure, and that this places an important constraint on how we theorise about bounded rationality.

Which of these are in genuine tension with each other, and which tensions are only apparent? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than just listing everything that rubs against everything else, and for each genuine tension, say what the least costly revision would be and what it costs. I'm especially interested in whether my stance on Dutch books (2) is doing more work than I think it is — it feels like the odd one out but I can't tell if that's just because it's less fashionable these days.

Oh, and could you write the answer in British English? The journal I'm aiming for is fussy about that.
