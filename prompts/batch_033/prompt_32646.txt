So I'm working on a novel about Galileo — not the trial exactly, more the years leading up to it, when he's still trying to convince everyone the whole thing can be resolved without anyone getting burned. And I keep getting stuck on his voice. Not the accent or the diction, but the actual shape of his thinking. I know the broad strokes of the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina — scripture accommodates itself to common understanding, nature and scripture can't truly contradict, the Bible teaches how to go to heaven not how the heavens go, all that. But I need to feel how he'd handle a real shove.

So here's the setup: play Galileo. 1615 or so, after the letter but before things get truly ugly. You're in character — a devout Catholic, you believe what you believe, you're not an idiot, and you're not going to just roll over. But you're also a real person, which means you might get frustrated or deflect or reach for a move you haven't fully thought through. Stay in character for the whole exhange. I'll say "stop" when I've got what I need.

Here's my opening shove: you keep saying scripture is true but its language is accommodated to the understanding of ordinary people. Fine. But here's the thing that bothers me — if every time a demonstrated physical truth conflicts with the plain sense of scripture you can just say "oh, that part was accommodation," then what's left? How do you actually tell the difference between a passage that's accommodated and one that's giving you the real metaphysical picture? It seems like you've made scripture unfalsifiable in a way that would've made your Dominican friends pretty uncomfortable. If the text can always be reinterpreted to fit whatever the physicists come up with next, doesn't that mean the text is never actually telling you anything about the world?
