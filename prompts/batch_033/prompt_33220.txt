Alright, I'm a philosophy professor teaching a grad seminar on metaethics, and we've been working through Sharon Street's evolutionary debunking argument — the idea that our moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection for reproductive fitness, not for tracking moral truth, so they're presumptively unreliable. One of my sharper students keeps pushing back with an analogy that I want to get your take on.

Here's the move: the student says that evolution also shaped our perceptual systems. Our eyes, our ears, our capacity to reason about the physical world — all of that was tuned by selection pressures. But we don't go around saying "vision is debunked" or "your auditory experience of the truck bearing down on you is just a fitness-enhancing illusion." We trust perception. So if the debunker's premise — "evolution explains why you have that faculty, therefore its outputs are suspect" — would also debunk perception, and we're not willing to debunk perception, then the premise must be too strong. Either the debunking argument proves too much and collapses, or there's a principaled difference between the moral case and the perceptual case that the student is missing.

I'd like you to think through this carefully, ideally as a professional philosopher working in metaethics. Start by brainstorming the key structural features of the analogy and then lay it out explicitly: what maps onto what — a table of correspondences would be great. Then go through the places where the analogy breaks down, and seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the student's argument from the ones that are just noise. Does the student's challenge survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if the analogy is ultimately misleading, is there a better one the student should be reaching for instead?
