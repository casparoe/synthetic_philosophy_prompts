I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I sit on its Editorial Standards Board. We must decide a case that has split the Board. Eight months ago we published a long feature by a senior science reporter arguing that a controversial Alzheimer's drug should be approved on the strength of its Phase 3 trial data. After publication we learned that, while reporting and writing the piece, the reporter held an undisclosed consulting arrangement with the drug's manufacturer. The reporter does not dispute the arrangement, but says it concerned an unrelated matter, did not influence the piece, and is irrelevant to whether the article's claims are true; they have offered to walk the Board through every table and statistical argument. We must decide whether to append an editor's note stating that, because of the undisclosed conflict, readers should treat the article's conclusions with reduced confidence.

The Board is split. One faction argues that the undisclosed tie is higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of the reporter's judgment — and that retaining full confidence in the article's conclusions in the face of such evidence would be epistemically akratic. The other faction argues that the piece must be judged on its first-order merits: if the data and arguments are sound, a conflict of interest does not make them unsound, and a publication should not require a reporter to disavow a conclusion the first-order evidence still supports merely because of evidence about the reporter. The dispute, at bottom, concerns how first-order and higher-order evidence should interact, and whether the reporter's stance is rational steadfastness or epistemic akrasia.

Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — publish the note, withhold it, or publish a narrower disclosure — with a time frame, and it should take seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than merely restating our conflict-of-interest policy.
