I'm a regular on a discussion forum where people read LessWrong, the APA blog, and similar venues, and a thread recently blew up over whether "intelligence" is a family resemblance concept in roughly Wittgenstein's sense. Two of the most careful posters — call them N and K — went back and forth for several rounds, and I'd like an expert philosophical analysis of who's right about what. Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works on concepts and the philosophy of mind, think through each move carefully, and then deliver a point-by-point adjudication. Write in American English.

Here's the exchange, lightly edited for clarity:

**N:** Wittgenstein's point about "game" applies straightforwardly to "intelligence." A chess engine, a crow extracting larvae with a stick, a human proving a theorem, and a language model generating plausible text — what single property do they all share? If you try to find one, you'll either make it so broad it's vacuous ("they all process information") or so narrow it excludes clear cases ("they all have general problem-solving ability," which the chess engine lacks). The concept hangs together by overlapping similarities — family resemblance — not a common essence. And that's fine. Concepts don't need essences to be useful.

**K:** That's giving up too early. There is a common core: intelligence is the capacity to achieve goals across a wide range of environments. The chess engine achieves the goal of winning across many board states; the crow achieves the goal of getting food across many physical puzzles; the human achieves goals across essentially any environment. The fact that the mechanisms differ doesn't mean the property isn't unified. "Cutting" is unified by its function even though scissors, lasers, and water jets share no physical property.

**N:** But "wide range of environments" is doing family-resemblance work in disguise. What counts as "wide"? A thermostat achieves its goal across a range of temperatures — is it a little bit intelligent? If you say no, you need a threshold, and any threshold will be arbitrary in exactly the way family resemblance predicts. The boundaries will be fuzzy and contested, which is the whole point.

**K:** The threshold isn't arbitrary — it's set by the complexity of the environment relative to the complexity of the system. A thermostat has exactly one degree of freedom matched to one environmental variable. Intelligence requires the system to be internally simpler than the environment it navigates, yet still capable of achieving goals across that environment. That's a crisp property: the system compresses the environment into a model that guides action. That's what all your examples do and the thermostat doesn't.

**N:** That's a genuinely interesting criterion, but now you've excluded things we clearly call intelligent. A human navigating a familiar commute isn't compressing anything at that moment — the environment is simpler than the system in that case. Is the human not being intelligent during the drive? Or take an expert who has memorized thousands of chess positions and just pattern-matches: the internal complexity might exceed the relevant environmental complexity. You're either forced to deny these are intelligent performances or to retreat to saying the underlying capacity is intelligent, not the performance — which concedes that the concept applies to different cases in different ways, i.e., family resemblance.

**K:** The capacity/performance distinction isn't a concession to family resemblance; it's standard in any dispositional concept. A car is still fast when it's idling at a red light because it has the capacity for speed. Likewise, the human on the commute is exercising a capacity that was built by compression, and the chess expert's pattern-matching is the output of a compressed model. The concept applies uniformly: intelligence is the possession of a compressed world-model that enables goal-achievement across varied environments. That's one property, not a family of overlapping similarities.

**N:** I think you've just relocated the fuzziness. "Possession of a compressed world-model" — how compressed? How varied must the environments be? You've replaced one vague threshold with two. And the move from "the system compresses" to "the system possesses a compressed model" smuggles in representational commitments that don't obviously apply to, say, the crow. At some point, insisting there must be an essence starts to look like insisting that a rope must have a single fiber running its whole length.

**K:** The rope analogy is tendentious. Some concepts really do have essences — "water is H2O" was a discovery, not a stipulation. The fact that pre-theoretic usage was fuzzy didn't mean water was a family resemblance concept; it meant we hadn't yet identified the underlying property that usage was tracking. Intelligence could be the same: usage is fuzzy, but there's a real property — efficient cross-domain optimization — that our usage is imperfectly tracking. The fuzziness is epistemic, not metaphysical.

**N:** That's a possible view, but now you owe an account of why we should believe the property is there to be discovered rather than constructed. For water, we had independent evidence of a hidden structure — chemistry gave us H2O. For intelligence, you're positing a hidden structure to save the intuition that the concept must have an essence. That's backwards. And notice: even if there is a unified underlying property, the everyday concept "intelligence" might still be a family resemblance concept, in the same way the everyday concept "water" included stuff that wasn't H2O. So we might both be right: you about the metaphysics, me about the concept.

I'd like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each move (I count roughly eight), say whether the point is strong, weak, or somewhere in between, and explain why. Then give an overall assessment: who is more right, and what — if anything — are both parties missing? In particular, I'm interested in whether N's final move (distinguishing the concept from the underlying property) successfully dissolves the disagreement or just relocates it.
