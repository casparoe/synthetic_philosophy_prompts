I manage a materials-qualification laboratory at a company that makes safety-critical aircraft fittings. My team repeatedly has to decide, on the basis of accelerated bench tests, whether a batch of material has a dispositional property — for example, whether a new aluminum alloy is susceptible to stress-corrosion cracking, or whether a coated part is flame-retardant. A negative test result is ambiguous in ways our current protocols do not handle: the test conditions can destroy the very disposition we are trying to detect (a fink — say, the test's thermal cycle anneals away the brittleness we are screening for), or they can block the manifestation while the disposition remains (a mask — say, an inhibitor in the test solution suppresses the cracking that would otherwise occur). The engineers who would run the procedure have materials-science training but no philosophy background, the procedure must be runnable in under an hour, and I sign off on the final call. Please write a decision procedure they can run after a negative test to decide whether the right conclusion is that the batch lacks the disposition, that the disposition was finked, or that it was masked. Ground the procedure in the philosophical work on dispositions, finks, and masks, but do not cite or quote anything: the deliverable is a usable procedure, not a literature review.

Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and write in American English. The five items should be: (1) the questions to ask in order; (2) what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (3) where the procedure stops with the conclusion that the batch lacks the disposition rather than that the result is inconclusive; (4) the procedure run on two hypotheticals, one a fink case and one a mask case, including at least one where the procedure forces a conclusion the lab would rather not reach; and (5) the points at which the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and an engineer must decide.
