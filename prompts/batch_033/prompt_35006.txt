I'm a high school student in a philosophy club, and we've been working through Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge. I've put together an argument that seems to force a choice between the tracking theory and something almost everyone accepts — and the tracking theory appears to lose. But I suspect I'm missing something, because serious philosophers still take Nozick seriously. Here's the argument:

(1) Nozick's sensitivity condition: S knows that p only if, were p false, S would not believe that p. (This is the condition meant to handle Gettier cases: in a Gettier case your belief is true and justified, but you would still have held it even if it had been false, so you don't know.)

(2) I know that I have hands. Call this H.

(3) I know that H entails that I am not a handless brain in a vat being deceived into thinking I have hands. Call this not-BIV.

(4) Epistemic closure: If S knows p and S knows that p entails q, then S knows q (or is at least in a position to know q by competently performing the deduction).

(5) From (2), (3), and (4): I know not-BIV.

(6) But my belief in not-BIV fails the sensitivity condition. If not-BIV were false — if I were a handless brain in a vat — I would still believe not-BIV, since the BIV scenario is designed to be experientially indistinguishable from normal life.

(7) From (1) and (6): I do not know not-BIV.

(8) Contradiction between (5) and (7).

Nozick's own move is to reject (4): knowledge is not closed under known entailment. He accepts that I know H, I know H entails not-BIV, but I don't know not-BIV. But here is where the argument seems to prove too much. If closure fails, then competent deduction from known premises does not always yield knowledge. And if deduction doesn't reliably extend knowledge, it's hard to see how we know much of anything beyond what we directly track — after all, most of what we take ourselves to know comes from reasoning from things we already know. That is not just the skeptical result that we don't know we're not brains in vats; it threatens to undermine deduction as a knowledge-extending practice altogether. If the tracking theory forces us to give up on that, shouldn't we take the whole thing as a reductio of the tracking theory?

I want a committed answer: which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Don't just lay out the options — take a definite position and defend it.
