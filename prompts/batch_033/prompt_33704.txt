I lead the analytics group at a state public-health agency. A large share of what reaches my desk is a claim of the form "the data we already collect shows X": a newly proposed risk factor, a new explanation for a long-running trend, a new outbreak signature, a reanalysis of years of surveillance data that supposedly supports a mechanism nobody had proposed when the data were collected. These claims feed decisions about interventions, advisories, and where we spend scarce analytic time, so we see them constantly and the stakes are high. Some of them are plainly hypotheses built after staring at the data; some are genuinely new theories that happen to predict patterns already sitting in our databases. My analysts are statistically competent but have no formal epistemology, and I need them to stop treating those two kinds of claim as if they were the same. I keep seeing the same failure mode: a hypothesis that was read off the data is given the same credit as one that stuck its neck out.

I know the shape of the problem from Bayesian confirmation theory: the problem of old evidence. If the evidence is already known with probability one, conditioning on it cannot move the probability of the hypothesis, yet in practice old data sometimes plainly should support a new theory. I also know the usual repairs are something like counterfactual priors and the distinction between prediction and accommodation. What I do not have is a procedure my analysts can run.

Write me a decision procedure for deciding, when someone claims that old data supports a new hypothesis, whether and how strongly the old data should count. It should be a reusable checklist in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "no — this is not evidence," where it stops with "yes — this is evidence, with roughly this strength," and where it hands the question to a person because judgment cannot be eliminated. Ground it in what Bayesian confirmation theory actually says about old evidence, but translate: an analyst should not need to know the theory to run it, and the whole thing must be runnable in under a day by someone with a master's-level statistics background and produce documentation a scientific review committee can sign off on.

The procedure should preserve the distinctions that actually do the work. In particular, it must separate and give my analysts a test for each of the following:
- whether the hypothesis was formulated before or after the data were examined;
- whether the analysis itself was pre-specified or chosen after looking — the test statistic, the subgroup, the time window, the threshold;
- whether the hypothesis makes a risky, precise prediction about the old data, or is compatible with nearly anything the data could have shown;
- whether a counterfactual prior can be reconstructed honestly: what a reasonable analyst would have assigned to P(data | hypothesis) versus P(data | not-hypothesis) if the hypothesis had been on the table before the data were seen;
- whether the old data were used to build the hypothesis in the first place, so that using them again as support is double-counting.

It should also handle the hard cases explicitly: the old data are the only data there will ever be; the analyst cannot honestly reconstruct what they would have believed beforehand; the hypothesis is vague enough to accommodate almost anything but is dressed in precise language.

Then run the procedure on three cases:
1. An internal analyst proposes that our existing hospitalization database supports a new causal claim about a common exposure, after having run several hundred associations and found one that meets a conventional significance threshold; the hypothesis as stated was written down only after the significant association was found.
2. A modeler derives, from an independently motivated transmission model, a precise quantitative prediction about the shape of a lag structure in historical surveillance data. The prediction is present in data collected years before the model existed, and it was specific enough that it could easily have failed.
3. A mixed case of your own construction: the hypothesis is partly built from the old data but also makes one moderately risky prediction, and the analyst's own sense of what they would have believed beforehand is not trustworthy.

The first case should produce an answer the proposing analyst will not like; the third should force the procedure to hand the question to a person. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, where the procedure will predictably misfire, and what it cannot do — in particular, what it cannot determine about hypotheses that were formed with the data in view. Also say where the procedure stops being epistemology and becomes decision theory: how strongly the old data should count is the procedure's business; whether to act on it, given costs and values, is not.
