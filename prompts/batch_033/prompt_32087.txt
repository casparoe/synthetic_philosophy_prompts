I'm a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and I've been trying to get clear on the Searle–Dennett debate about original versus derived intentionality for a seminar paper. The more I read, the more I suspect the debate has been running in place for decades, and I want to know whether that's right and, if so, why.

Here's the landscape as I see it. Searle argues that the intentionality of human thought — its aboutness, its directedness at objects and states of affairs — is intrinsic to certain biological processes. By contrast, the intentionality of words, pictures, and computational states is merely derived: it exists only relative to the interpretive practices of beings with original intentionality. Dennett counters that this distinction won't do the work Searle wants. His central move, as I understand it, is the two-bitser argument: you can't purchase original intentionality by adding a little more derived intentionality — say, by hooking a symbol-manipulating system up to cameras and robot arms, or by giving it an evolutionary history. Either the whole notion of original intentionality is an illusion, or everything that passes Searle's test for derived intentionality already has as good a claim to originality as we do. The two sides seem to agree on a great deal: they agree on the physical facts about brains and computers, they agree that thermostats and paramecia don't have beliefs, and they agree that intentional explanation is indispensable. And yet they remain deadlocked.

I have a working hypothesis, but I'm not confident in it. My hypothesis is that the debate isn't really about the metaphysics of intentionality at all. It's about what it means to explain intentionality. Searle wants an explanation that identifies a natural kind with causal powers that constitute aboutness, and he thinks only biology delivers that. Dennett wants an explanation that shows how the patterns we call intentional emerge from non-intentional processes, and he thinks Searle's demand for a causal account of aboutness is confused. If that's right, then the two sides are answering different questions — Searle is doing constitutive metaphysics, Dennett is doing something closer to explanatory reduction — and the impasse reflects a deeper disagreement about what philosophical work the concept of intentionality is supposed to do.

But I'm not sure my diagnosis is correct, and I'd like an expert assessment. Please approach this as a professional philosopher of mind would: first, lay out the commitments each side actually holds, being careful not to caricature either position. Then, diagnose the structure of the disagreement. Is it substantive or verbal? Is it a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, a disagreement about what counts as evidence in philosophy of mind, or two different projects sharing a vocabulary? In giving your diagnosis, please separate what is broadly settled among philosophers of mind from what remains genuinely contested — don't present a contested position as the consensus. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. If none is possible, say why. If a specific kind of empirical result, conceptual distinction, or shift in framing could move things, spell that out. Think through the options carefully before settling on a diagnosis.
