For a piece I'm writing on long-running disputes in philosophy that seem immune to resolution, I want to understand the debate over Bradley's regress as it bears on the unity of the proposition. The core question, as I understand it, is this: a proposition appears to be something more than a mere list of its constituents — the object and the property, or the individual and the universal, are somehow combined into a truth-evaluable whole. But what does the combining? If you posit a relation — an instantiation relation, or a copula, or the logical form of the proposition — to do the work, you seem to need a further relation to connect that relation to the original constituents, and so on. That is the regress.

The debate has been running since Bradley's own writings in the 1890s, and it has been engaged by Russell, Wittgenstein, Frege, Bergmann, Armstrong, and many others since, yet no resolution has emerged. Some philosophers think the regress is vicious and shows that propositions cannot be built from independently specifiable constituents. Others think the regress is benign — that it is generated only if one makes an avoidable assumption, or that it is an infinite but non-vicious structure akin to a mathematical series. Still others think the whole framing is a mistake, that it arises from treating propositions on the model of objects and their parts, or from confusing semantics with metaphysics. The positions are well mapped; what I cannot tell is whether the debate has a genuine engine or is simply replaying itself.

I want a diagnosis of why this dispute persists. In particular:

Is this a substantive disagreement, or are the two sides using terms like "proposition," "constituent," and "unity" in different enough ways that they are not really contradicting one another?

To what extent is the disagreement driven by different background pictures of what an explanation in metaphysics must look like — for instance, whether one is permitted to treat the unity of a proposition as primitive, or whether one must always analyze it into further elements?

Is there a hidden formal or empirical question at the bottom of this, or is the dispute ultimately about bedrock intuitions concerning relations, composition, and explanation that neither side can argue for without begging the question?

And given your diagnosis: what, if anything, would count as progress? Is there a distinction that could be drawn, a formal result that could be proved, or a way of reframing the question that would move things forward? Or is this a debate where no progress should be expected, and if so, why?

I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a bibliography. I am asking for a structural diagnosis of the disagreement itself — what kind of argument it is, why it has not ended, and what it would take to end it.
