I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on personal identity, and I've gotten myself into a mess with an argument I built for a seminar paper. The conclusion is totally nuts but I can't figure out which step to blame, and my advisor just keeps saying "interesting, keep digging." I'd love a fresh set of eyes on this.

Here's the argument:

(1) Suppose I step into a teletransporter that scans my brain and body, destroys them, and assembles a molecule-for-molecule replica on Mars. The replica wakes up with all my memories, personality quirks, half-finished projects, and so on. It seems to me — and to most people I've talked to — that I survive this. The Mars person is me.

(2) Now tweak the case: the machine scans me, creates the Mars replica, but the "destroy" function glitches. Now there's me on Earth and a replica on Mars, both psychologically continuous with the pre-scan person. They can't both be me — identity is one-to-one. But the relation between pre-scan me and the Mars replica is identical in both cases (scan, then build). So if I survive as the replica in case 1, I survive as the replica in case 2. But that means I am two people at once, which is impossible.

(3) Parfit's move, as I understand it, is to say: look, what matters in survival isn't identity — it's psychological continuity and connectedness (Relation R). In case 2, what matters obtains twice. You don't literally survive as two people; rather, survival-matters is a relation that can branch. Identity is irrelevant.

(4) But here's the problem. If identity drops out as irrelevant in the fission case, it should drop out everywhere — the fission case just makes vivid what's true in all cases. That means when I wake up tomorrow, the person in my bed isn't strictly identical to me. They're just psychologically continuous. Identity through time was never there; we just didn't notice because fission doesn't happen in ordinary life.

(5) And if the person waking up tomorrow isn't me, why should I care about them in the special way I care about my own future self? If the relation grounding my concern is just psychological continuity, and I can stand in that relation to other people (shared memories, deep empathy, etc.), then the line between prudence and altruism dissolves. But that's fundementally wrong — my concern for my own future feels completely different from my concern for others.

(6) So the argument seems to force a choice: either (a) I don't survive the normal teletransporter — it kills me, contra (1) — or (b) the special concern I have for my future self is a mistake, a kind of irrational bias. Both are hard to swallow.

Where does this go wrong? If you had to reject one step, which one and what's the price? I'm tempted to bite the bullet on (1) and say the teletransporter kills you, but I'm worried about what that means for ordinary survival — am I dying every night when I fall asleep?
