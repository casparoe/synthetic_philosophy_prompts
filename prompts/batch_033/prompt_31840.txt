I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and my reading group kept going around in circles on something last week. We were arguing whether the move that gets the right answer in counterfactual mugging also forces you to pay in XOR blackmail, which everyone in the room agrees is a reductio. I'd like a diagnosis.

Let me set up the two cases. In counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin. In the actual branch it comes up tails, and Omega asks you for $100. In the other branch, if it had come up heads, Omega would have paid you $10,000 iff it predicted that you would have paid in the tails case. The usual functional-decision-theory/updateless line is that you should pay: your decision function in the tails branch subjunctively controls what Omega's predictor does in the heads branch, so the policy "pay when asked" has positive expected value across branches.

In XOR blackmail, a very reliable predictor sends you a letter saying only: I selected you because exactly one of these two claims is true — either you have embarrassing property P, or you will pay me $100. You don't know whether P is true. If you pay, you get evidence that P is false; if you don't pay, you get evidence that P is true. But paying has no causal effect on P or on the selection. The standard anti-Evidential-DT line is that paying here is a pure money pump: you're just buying good news about yourself.

Now here's the argument we couldn't kill:

1. In counterfactual mugging, you should pay, because the output of your decision function in the tails branch subjunctively controls Omega's prediction in the heads branch.
2. The only apparent difference between that dependence and the one in XOR blackmail is that in XOR blackmail the predictor's rule makes your action informative about P, rather than about a different payoff branch. But in both cases the reason-giving force comes from the fact that a predictor models your decision function under a stated rule.
3. If the CM dependence is reason-giving, then the XOR dependence is reason-giving too.
4. So XOR blackmail requires paying.
5. But paying in XOR blackmail is a pure money pump: after paying you are $100 poorer, and P's truth value is exactly what it was. A theory of rational choice that requires paying for no causal effect is exploitable.
6. So either the CM verdict is wrong, or the anti-EDT verdict in XOR blackmail is wrong, or there's a hidden equivocation in the move from 2 to 3.

I'd like to keep the functional decision theory verdict in counterfactual mugging, but I don't want to just assert that step 2 is false without being able to say what the actual difference is. Which premise should I reject, why did that premise look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I also want to know whether the distinction between subjunctive dependence and evidential correlation is treated as settled among people who work on this, or whether it's still genuinely contested.
