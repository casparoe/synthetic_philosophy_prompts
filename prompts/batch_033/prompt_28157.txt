I've been trying to get clear about when a decision process counts as rational even though the agent doesn't attempt to find the best available option — what Herbert Simon called satisficing. I have firm verdicts on a range of cases, but I cannot tell whether they hang together under a single principle. I would like you to extract the principle (or small set of candidate principles) that best captures my pattern of verdicts, state it precisely, show how it handles each case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to revise. Please also apply the resulting account to a further case I have not listed.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

**1. The restaurant.** I am in an unfamiliar neighborhood, hungry, and I walk into the first restaurant that looks clean and has a menu I like. I do not check reviews or look at other options. Verdict: perfectly rational.

**2. The car.** I need a reliable sedan. I test-drive three models from different manufacturers, all within my budget, and buy the one I liked best. I do not research every sedan on the market. Verdict: rational.

**3. The oncologist.** A patient with an aggressive cancer is offered a treatment plan by the first oncologist they see. The patient accepts without seeking a second opinion, even though doing so would be easy and the stakes are life and death. Verdict: not rational.

**4. The dissertation topic.** A PhD student in philosophy picks the first dissertation topic their advisor suggests, without exploring alternatives. The topic is fine, but the student never seriously considers whether a different topic would be more fruitful for their career or intellectual development. Verdict: not rational — even though the search costs here are low in one sense (just thinking), the decision shapes years of their life.

**5. The retirement fund.** A 30-year-old chooses a target-date retirement fund from their employer's list. They do not compare expense ratios across funds, do not investigate whether a different allocation would suit them better, and do not learn what a target-date fund actually is. Verdict: rational. The default is reasonable and the cost of acquiring the relevant financial literacy is high relative to the likely improvement.

**6. The marriage.** Someone marries the first person they ever date seriously, having never reflected on what they want in a partner or whether the relationship has problems they are ignoring. Verdict: not rational. This feels different from the restaurant case, but I am not sure why — in both cases the agent stops at the first acceptable option.

**7. The public contract.** A city council needs to choose a contractor for a bridge repair. They take the lowest bid that meets the published specifications, without investigating the bidders' track records or the quality of their past work, even though that information is publicly available and the council has staff who could review it at no additional cost. Verdict: not rational.

**8. The emergency room triage.** An ER doctor, overwhelmed with patients, assigns treatment priority using a simple heuristic (roughly, "worst vital signs first") rather than computing expected outcomes for each possible ordering. Verdict: rational — indeed, more rational than trying to optimize would be.

The tension that bothers me most is between cases 1 and 6. In both, the agent takes the first acceptable option without further search, yet I judge one rational and the other not. I suspect the difference has something to do with the stakes, the reversibility of the decision, and perhaps whether the agent's aspiration level was itself set responsibly. But case 5 is high-stakes and I still call it rational, so stakes alone cannot be the answer. And case 4 is at least somewhat reversible — one can change dissertation topics — yet I still judge it irrational.

Please give me the best principle you can that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, explain which clause of the principle handles which case, and tell me which of my verdicts (if any) the principle requires me to abandon. Also, apply the principle to a case I have not listed: someone choosing a political party to support by adopting the platform of whichever party their parents supported, without examining alternatives.
