I'm a novelist, and somewhere in the middle of a book about a translator who starts seing analogies everywhere I got hooked on those little letter-string puzzles—the kind where you're shown abc turning into abd and then asked to change another string the same way. I've started keeping a list of my gut answers, and they feel completely obvious one at a time but they refuse to line up under any single rule, which is probably exactly what the book is about, so I'd like to know what's actually going on.

Given abc → abd, I say ijk should become ijl: the last letter gets bumped to its successor. Given abc → abd, I say xyz should become xya, because z has no successor so I wrap around to a. Given abc → abd, I say kji should become kjj; I still bump the last letter even though the string runs backwards. Given abc → abd, I say aabbcc should become aabbdd—the whole doubled c-group turns into a doubled d-group, not just the final character. Given abc → abd, I say iijjkk should become iijjll, same group logic. But given abc → abd, I say abcabc should become abdabd; I change both copies of abc, not just the final c. And here's the one I keep going back and forth on: given abc → abd, for xyzxyz I sometimes want xyzxya (only the final z wraps) and sometimes xyaxya (both copies change, the way I changed both copies of abc).

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about the xyzxyz case, and which of my verdicts would it force me to drop or revise? Dont just tell me there's no single rule; if the best account is a small set of competing principles, state them precisely and say what each predicts for a case I haven't listed. And please, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just continuous prose, like you're writing me a letter.
