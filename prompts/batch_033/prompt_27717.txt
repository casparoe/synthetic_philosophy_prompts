I am auditing a graduate seminar on rule-following, and I tried to write out the Kripkenstein argument as a clean numbered chain. The result disturbs me, because it seems to prove far too much, and I cannot tell whether the problem is a bad inference or a premise I am unwilling to pay for. I would like a diagnosis, not a survey.

Here is the argument as I have it. (1) My past use of the plus sign is finite: I have computed only finitely many sums in my life. (2) That finite record is compatible both with my having meant the addition function and with my having meant the quus function, where quus agrees with addition for every pair of numbers smaller than 57 and yields 5 otherwise. (3) So my past behavior does not by itself determine whether I meant plus or quus. (4) My dispositions do not determine it either, since I am disposed to make computational mistakes, to tire, and eventually to stop answering, and in any case a disposition says how I would answer, not how I ought to answer. (5) Introspection is no help, because I possess no inner state that settles, for the indefinitely many sums I have never considered, which continuation is the correct one. (6) These are the only kinds of fact that have been seriously proposed to constitute meaning, and none of them settles the matter. (7) There is nothing special about me or about addition; the same reasoning applies to every speaker and every expression. (8) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about what anyone means by anything. (9) But if there is no fact of the matter about what anyone means, then the argument itself has no fact of the matter about what it means, so the conclusion cannot be something the argument truly establishes. (10) The argument therefore proves too much: it seems to leave no stable position from which even the skeptic can state the skeptical result.

I find (8) unacceptable, but each of (3), (4), (5), and (6) looks hard to give up, and the seminar's recurring appeal to the skeptical solution — accepting (8) while replacing truth conditions with assertability conditions — strikes me as merely relocating the problem to step (9). Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit one to? If the argument is valid, say which premise is least costly to surrender and explain the price. If it contains a subtle flaw, name the step and explain why it looked like a good inference. In either case, address the skeptical solution directly and say whether it is a stable resting point. Please answer in American English.
