Last night a friend and I had a long argument about a former colleague of ours, Daniel, who was recently caught diverting money from the small business he ran with his brother. My friend does not deny that what Daniel did was wrong, but he insists that Daniel is not ultimately to blame for it. His reasoning went like this. Daniel's decision to take the money issued from his character: his impulsiveness, his way of weighing risk against loyalty, his susceptibility to temptation. Those traits were formed by his genes, his upbringing, and the accidents of his life, none of which he chose. So Daniel is not responsible for the kind of person he is. But if he is not responsible for the kind of person he is, my friend argued, he cannot be responsible for what that kind of person does. When I replied that Daniel had made choices along the way that shaped his character, my friend asked whether Daniel was responsible for the traits that made those choices, and so on backwards. He claimed the chain never bottoms out: at each step, the self that chooses is itself the product of unchosen causes. I objected that this proves too much, because if the argument works it seems to dissolve blame for everyone, not just Daniel. But I could not find the flaw in the regress itself. Was my friend's argument sound? If it is not, where exactly does it go wrong? And if it is, what should we conclude about blame and praise?
