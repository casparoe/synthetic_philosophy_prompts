I work as a software engineer on a team that builds and operates autonomous delivery robots in a mid-sized city. Recently one of our robots struck a pedestrian in a crosswalk, breaking her leg. I have been asked to contribute to the internal review, and I keep getting stuck on the same question: who is responsible?

The robot's navigation policy is learned. No engineer ever wrote an instruction telling it to enter the crosswalk at that moment. The failure involved a novel combination of sensor inputs that did not appear anywhere in our training or test data. The reward function and training pipeline were built by a different team than mine, and the hardware was supplied by an outside vendor. A remote supervisor was monitoring eight robots simultaneously and had roughly half a second between the robot's decision and the collision—no realistic chance to intervene. Our team followed all of the company's documented procedures, and an external audit found no regulatory violations.

None of this is meant to excuse what happened. But every candidate for blame—the engineers, the supervisor, the vendor, the managers who approved the deployment schedule—appears to have done what could reasonably have been expected given what they knew at the time. Still, saying "nobody is responsible" feels wrong, and I suspect the company's insistence on finding a responsible party is pushing us toward answers that are convenient rather than true.

I am not looking for legal advice. I am trying to understand whether my underlying assumption is mistaken: that when a serious harm occurs, the right response must involve locating one or more humans who failed in some identifiable way. Is that assumption sound? If it is not, how should someone in my position think about accountability when a system's behavior emerges from a learned model rather than from any specific human decision?
