Ive been going down a rabbit hole with game theory puzzles and got into an argument about the prisoner's dilemma — the one-shot kind where two people can stay quiet or rat on each other and can't communicate. I always thought ratting was obviously right, because no matter what the other person does, ratting gets you less time. But then someone showed me this argument and I can't find the hole in it.

(1) Say the two players are both perfectly rational, have exactly the same information, and know all of that about each other, including knowing that the other is rational, and knowing that they know it, all the way down.

(2) If two perfectly rational people are in exactly the same situation with exactly the same information, they'll pick the same move.

(3) So in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, either both stay quiet or both rat.

(4) Both staying quiet is better for each player than both ratting.

(5) So each player should think: if I stay quiet, my partner will too, and we both get the light sentence; if I rat, my partner will rat too, and we both get the heavy one. So I should stay quiet.

(6) But ratting dominates staying quiet: whatever the other player does, ratting gets you a shorter sentence than staying quiet.

(7) So either ratting isn't actually the rational move here, or one of the steps above is wrong.

The person I argued with said this line of thought has a name, superrationality, but they got vague when I asked what you have to give up to reject it. I'd like to keep something like (2) for cases where the two players are literally running the same code, or are exact copies of the same person, but I don't want to be forced to cooperate with a total stranger just because we're both rational. Is there a principled way to keep (2) in some cases and not others, or is that cheating? Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
