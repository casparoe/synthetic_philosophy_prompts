I'm a science journalist and I sit on the advisory board of the Halpern Foundation, which funds "consciousness studies" — historically that's meant philosophy of mind, neuroscience of consciousness, and a bit of contemplative science. We've got a grant proposal that's split the board right down the middle.

The applicant wants to investigate why humans have the intuition that they're conscious in the first place. The experiments would probe things like: why people find the "hard problem" compelling even when they can't articulate it, why we attribute phenomenal states to ourselves so readily, whether the intuition that there's "something it's like" to be us survives certain kinds of cognitive debiasing, and whether children aquire it gradually or have it from the start. The applicant explicitly frames this as the "meta-problem of consciousness" — explaining our phenomenal intuitions rather than explaining phenomenal consciousness itself.

Four board members say this is squarely within our remit: it's about consciousness, just studied through the lens of why we *think* we have it. Four others say it's cognitive psychology with a fancy name, and if we fund this we'll have to fund any study on metacognition, self-report bias, or theory of mind. The chair is abstaining and wants a decision memo from the rest of us.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend fund, don't fund, or fund with conditions — with clear reasoning that takes the strongest point on the losing side seriously. The bottom line needs to be something the foundation can actually act on (e.g., "fund but require the applicant to revise the framing" or "reject but invite resubmission to our psychology panel").

And write it in British English — the foundation's London office gets shirty about American spelling.
