Over the last few months I keep hearing the same picture of how thinking works, and it's started doing real argumentative work in my friend group. The picture is that a mind is a population of ideas, and thinking is just natural selection running inside one skull: ideas are like organisms, producing variations is like mutation, criticizing or testing them is like selection, and the ones that survive are the good ones. One friend in particular—he works in product design—uses this to argue that the right way to solve hard problems is to generate lots of variants and kill the bad ones fast (brainstorm, prototype, fail early) rather than trying to reason your way to a single well-designed answer. He even ties it to that Dennett ladder of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures: a Popperian creature is supposed to be one that lets its hypotheses die instead of dying itself, so his line is that the more of your thinking you can turn into selection among ideas, the smarter you get.

I'm not sure the analogy is doing the work he thinks it is, but I also can't say exactly where it gives out. I'd like an audit of how far it holds. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the genome, the organism, the population, the environment, fitness, mutation, recombination, and the breeder if there is one. Then walk me through the places the mapping breaks down and looses its grip, and seperate the disanalogies that matter for his practical conclusion from the ones that are just cosmetic. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—stop designing, start generating and selecting—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. Also say what would change your verdict: what argument or consideration would flip you from "mostly a useful analogy" to "mostly misleading" or the other way, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail.
