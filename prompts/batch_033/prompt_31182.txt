Before I re-enter the material-constitution debate, I want something the papers rarely supply: a clean statement of what a theory in this area is actually answerable to. Write the desiderata. Begin from the core case — a clay statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it. Squashing destroys the statue but not the lump; the lump can survive changes the statue cannot; yet the two coincide, share all their matter, and are indistinguishable by any ordinary physical test. An adequate theory must either make sense of two distinct things coinciding in this way or show that the appearance of two things is misleading.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a commitment of one camp rather than a constraint on all comers. At a minimum, address the following.

1. Constitution and identity. The theory must say what the relation between the statue and the clay is, and how it bears on identity: are they one thing or two? If constitution is not identity, the theory owes an account of how it differs; if it is identity, the theory must explain away the apparent differences.

2. Leibniz's Law. If the statue and the lump are distinct, there must be some property one has and the other lacks, and the theory should say which. If they are identical, the theory must explain why substitution fails in the squashing case without making identity incoherent.

3. The modal profile. The theory must get the persistence conditions right: the statue is destroyed by squashing, the lump is not. It must do this without gerrymandering — without simply stipulating sortal essences to fit the case, unless it defends a general doctrine of sortal essentialism.

4. The grounding problem. If the two are distinct but share every categorical and microphysical property, what makes one a statue and the other a lump? What grounds their different kinds and persistence conditions? A theory must supply a ground — form, history, intention, sortal concept, something — or argue that no ground is needed.

5. Counting and sortals. The theory must explain our counting practices: we say one statue and one lump, yet hesitate to say two objects in the one place. It must either vindicate that hesitation or explain it away.

6. Mereological extensionality. Classical extensional mereology says sameness of parts suffices for identity; the statue and the lump appear to be a counterexample. The theory must reject, restrict, or reinterpret extensionality, and say what the cost is.

7. Generality. The theory must extend beyond artifacts, or justify treating artifacts as special. Persons and their bodies, organisms and the parcels of matter composing them at a time, and similar cases should be handled or explicitly set aside with a reason.

8. Proliferation. If the clay and the statue are two things, what about the aggregate of clay molecules, the aggregate of atoms, the arbitrary undetached parts of the clay? The theory needs a principled account of which coincident candidates are objects, or a defense of the abundance.

9. The formal features of constitution. If constitution is a real relation, the theory should specify at least its irreflexivity, asymmetry, and whether it is transitive; if it is not a real relation, the theory should say what talk of constitution amounts to.

Then two structural questions. First, I suspect a subset of these cannot be jointly satisfied — roughly, the modal asymmetry, a non-proliferating ontology, and an absolute identity relation form an inconsistent triad, and any theory must give up one. Say whether that is right, and what the escape routes are. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the main existing positions, and give a brief scorecard: how do the coincidence view, the dominant-kind view, four-dimensionalism, and mereological nihilism fare against the list? Keep the scorecard compressed; the criteria are what I want.
