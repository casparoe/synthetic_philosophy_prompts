I'm a junior studying philosophy and comparative literature, currently enrolled in a seminar called "Meaning and Interpretation" where we've been working through the debate between intentionalists, anti-intentionalists, and hypothetical intentionalists — Beardsley and Wimsatt on the intentional fallacy, Barthes on the death of the author, Levinson on hypothetical intentionalism, and a few contemporary pieces on the ethics of interpretation. The seminar has been rich but abstract, and I'm now facing the same issues in a much messier form.

I serve as an associate editor for our university's undergraduate literary magazine, which publishes fiction, poetry, and creative nonfiction twice a semester. We receive about sixty submissions per issue, accept roughly twelve, and for each accepted piece we write a short editor's note — a paragraph situating the piece, describing what we take its preoccupations to be, and sometimes commenting on a specific interpretive crux. Every issue generates two to five disputes with authors who say we've misread their work. The disputes fall into three rough categories: authors who want us to revise an editor's note to reflect their intended meaning; authors who appeal a rejection on the grounds that we missed what they were really doing; and authors who object during copyedits when we suggest changes that would alter what they intended a sentence to convey, even if the sentence as written doesn't seem to do that work.

Our editorial board of eight people splits predictably. The English majors tend to say the author's stated intention is authoritative — who would know better? The philosophy students on the board tend to say the text is all we have and the author's post-hoc commentary is just one more interpretation among others. A few of us try to argue for something like hypothetical intentionalism but we can't agree on what that actually means for a given decision, and in practice we end up voting case by case, which means the outcome depends more on who shows up to the meeting than on any consistent principle. The editor-in-chief has asked me, since I'm the one taking the seminar, to write up something we can actually use.

What I need is a decision procedure — a step-by-step set of questions an editor can run through — that determines how much weight to give an author's stated intentions in each of our three recurring situations: (a) revising an editor's note, (b) reconsidering a rejection, and (c) adjudicating a line-level dispute during copyedits. The procedure should be usable by someone who hasn't read any of the theoretical literature. It should translate whatever you take to be sound in that literature into concrete tests, and it should be explicit about where a step requires a judgment call that no checklist can eliminate. It should also warn us about the kinds of cases where the procedure will predictably give an answer that we, or the author, will find unsatisfying or unfair.

Please run the procedure on these three cases, which are real disputes from our last two issues:

1. A short story about a gentrifying neighborhood includes a Black character who speaks exclusively in dialect, commits a theft, and is the only character shown attending church. Three board members read the story and described it as trading in racial stereotypes; two others found it unobjectionable. The author, when we raised the issue before acceptance, wrote back a long email arguing that the character was intended as a critique of how gentrification narratives flatten Black characters into moral exemplars or villains, and that the story deliberately deploys stereotype in order to undermine it from within. The story was accepted, and now we are drafting the editor's note. Half the board wants the note to mention the stereotype concern and the author's response; the other half says that if the intended subversion isn't legible on the page, we're doing the author's publicity for them rather than describing the text. What does the procedure say?

2. A poem we accepted contains a line that our editor's note described as an oblique meditation on grief. The poet wrote to us saying the line is in fact a direct allusion to the Srebrenica massacre, which transforms the poem's apparent subject from private loss to political violence. None of the board members recognized the allusion. The poet wants the note revised to reflect this. What does the procedure say?

3. We rejected a novella that the board found meandering and symbolically overelaborate. The author sent a detailed, genuinely sophisticated five-page document laying out the symbolic architecture of the piece — a structure of interlocking allusions to medieval bestiaries that, if noticed, would turn the apparently arbitrary events into a unified allegory. No reader caught this during review. The author is asking us to reconsider the rejection. What does the procedure say?

Finally, please tell me honestly what the procedure cannot handle — the cases it's blind to, the kinds of texts or disputes where it will misfire, and the residual questions it can't settle but that an editorial board still has to answer.
