At my school's philosophy club, two members got into an argument about the example of two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who have both lost their taste for the same coffee. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and that what changed is his reaction to that taste; Sanborn says his reaction never changed and that the coffee's taste itself has changed. Priya defended the example: the two reports are genuinely different claims, and each taster is in a position to know which is true of himself because his own experience is directly known to him. Jordan replied that the two claims cannot both be true — either the coffee changed or it did not — so if direct access to experience is reliable, at least one taster is badly wrong about something he supposedly knows directly. Priya answered that this only shows memory of an experience is fallible, not that current experience fails to be directly known. Jordan said that once fallible memory is admitted, the private, directly known taste-quality — the quale — does no explanatory work: everything we can check is public, and the quale drops out. Priya countered that dropping out of the explanation is not the same as being incoherent; there is still a fact about how the coffee tastes to each taster right now.
