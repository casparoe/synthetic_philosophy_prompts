I hold the following view: as AI systems are integrated into the core of human epistemic practices — not merely as search engines or calculators, but as systems that generate explanatory narratives, propose research directions, evaluate evidence, and mediate our access to entire bodies of knowledge — they produce a distinctive kind of epistemic erosion. The erosion is not the familiar loss of a skill (like memorization) that can be compensated for by a tool. It is a loss of understanding: the capacity to grasp why a conclusion follows, to navigate the space of possible alternatives, to recognize when a result is surprising or anomalous, and to integrate new knowledge into a coherent picture. The mechanism is that understanding requires active cognitive engagement with the structure of a domain — the kind of engagement that is precisely what gets handed off to the AI. The result is a knower who can reliably produce or recognize correct answers but cannot be said to understand the domain those answers come from. Call this the Erosion Thesis.

What would count against the Erosion Thesis? I want a systematic map of the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically:

1. What empirical findings would directly test the thesis, and what would falsification look like? Are there studies — or could there be studies — that distinguish the loss of understanding from the loss of mere performance ability, and what would it mean if such studies found no erosion of understanding despite heavy AI dependence?

2. What conceptual distinctions might show the thesis is confused? For instance, does the thesis conflate individual understanding with distributed or collective understanding? If understanding can be genuinely distributed across a human-AI system, does that defuse the worry, or does it merely relocate it? Does the thesis presuppose an overly internalist picture of understanding that the extended mind literature has already undermined?

3. What would a clean counterexample look like? Are there domains — mathematics, chess, programming, medical diagnosis — where heavy reliance on AI tools has clearly not eroded, and perhaps even deepened, human understanding? If so, what features of those domains make them different, and do those features generalize to the cases the Erosion Thesis is worried about?

4. What would defenders of the Erosion Thesis themselves concede as counting against it? For example, would they grant that the thesis is overstated if it turns out that AI tools, when designed with explanation and transparency in mind, actually improve understanding by making structure more visible? Would they concede that the thesis is time-bound — true of current AI but not of AI in principle?

5. Has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability? The worry is that every apparent counterexample — every case where AI seems to deepen understanding — gets re-described as not involving the right kind of dependence, or the right kind of understanding, or the right kind of AI. If the thesis has been insulated in this way, is that a problem for a view of this kind (a structural claim about the conditions for understanding), or is it characteristic of philosophical claims that operate at a level of abstraction where direct empirical refutation is not the right standard?

I want an honest answer, including the possibility that the Erosion Thesis turns out to be mostly wrong, or that it is true but only under conditions so narrow as to be uninteresting. I would also like to know whether there is a nearby thesis — more modest, more precise — that survives scrutiny better, and what that thesis would be.
