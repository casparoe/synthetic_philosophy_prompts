I've been going back and forth on A Clockwork Orange — the Burgess novel or the Kubrick film, take your pick — because I want to use it to think through a real clash between Buddhist ethics and consequentialism. The Ludovico treatment makes Alex physically incapable of violence, and the state's case for it is purely about consequences: fewer future victims, a reformed offender. But the book/film treats this as a horror even though it stops him hurting people. From a Buddhist angle, where moral quality hangs on intention (cetanā) and on the underlying roots of greed, hatred, and delusion rather than outward behavior, what exactly has the treatment accomplished? It seems to leave the unwholesome roots in place and just layer on a new aversion — which is itself a form of dosa. Is a conditioned reflex against violence morally worth anything at all, or is it karmically inert in a way the state doesn't grasp? And could a Buddhist consequentialist consistenly back the Ludovico technique, or does the Buddhist insistence on mental purification rule it out even when the outcome looks good? I'd like a reading that pinpoints where Buddhist ethics and consequentialism genuinely diverge here, says whether the work itself takes a side rather than just staging the debate, and is honest about the places where the Buddhist reading of Alex's situation gets strained.
