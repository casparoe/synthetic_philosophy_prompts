I'm on the animal care and use commitee at my university, and we're genuinely split. Our policy only covers vertebrates, but a neuroscience lab has submitted a protocol that would use around 250,000 adult fruit flies over two years in a learning experiment: the flies are food-deprived to motivate them, some get their wings clipped, and at the end they're killed by freezing. Three members want to treat this as the test case for extending our oversight to insects. Their argument is the scale argument: even if the chance a fruit fly is sentient is tiny — they say one in a thousand, maybe one in ten thousand — when you multiply that by the number of insects used in research and agriculture every year, the expected amount of suffering is enormous, so the cost of being wrong is high and a precautionary policy is warranted. The other side says that's a category mistake: fruit flies show nociceptive responses, but nociception isn't sentience, and if we go down this road the committee ends up reviewing every Drosophila stock center and pest-control contract on campus while the mice and rats that we know suffer get less attention. I have to draft the short decision memo we'll circulate before next Thursday's meeting. I need a concrete recommendation — approve the protocol as is, approve with conditions (say which ones), or pause it while we adopt some interim invertebrate policy — with a time frame, and it has to engage the scale argument on its own terms rather than just calling it absurd. Also, I'd like the memo to say honestly where its reasoning is most likely to fail and what empirical finding about insects would actually flip the recommendation.
