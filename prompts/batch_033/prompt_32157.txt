I hold that the difference principle — roughly, that social and economic inequalities are just only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged — is the correct principle of distributive justice, and I accept it as Rawls argues for it from the original position. I want to know what would count against it.

I'm not looking for a list of objections for their own sake. What I want is a map of the position's exposure: which evidence, arguments, possible cases, or empirical findings should make me revise or abandon it, sorted by how decisive they would be. I'm especially interested in what a Rawlsian should themselves concede as counting against the view — not merely what Nozick or luck egalitarians say, but what the internal architecture of the theory makes it vulnerable to. For each defeater, tell me whether it is fatal, severely damaging, or merely awkward, and whether the standard Rawlsian reply genuinely meets it or merely deflects it.

I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. The difference principle is embedded in a whole system — the original position, the two principles and their lexical ordering, the idea of the basic structure, the assumption of a closed society, and so on. When a critic hits one part, the Rawlsian can shift to another. Has the view become a fortress of epicycles, and if so, is that a problem for a theory of this kind, or is it what we should expect from a systematic normative theory? Distinguish insulation that is principled from insulation that is ad hoc.
