I recently read a popular article about paradoxes of rationality, and the author gave the following summary of Douglas Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery:

"Hofstadter imagined a game where twenty strangers are told that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person gets a billion dollars. If nobody sends one, or if two or more send one, nobody gets anything — and the players cannot communicate. The puzzle is that if everyone is perfectly rational and knows everyone else is perfectly rational, they will all reason identically. So either everyone sends or nobody sends, and both outcomes are losing. Hofstadter called this symmetry problem 'superrationality': you have to act as if your own choice determines what every other equally rational player will do. But this doesn't help — if you decide to send, you reason that everyone will send, so you lose; if you decide not to send, you reason that nobody will send, so you still lose. Rationality therefore gives no answer; the dilemma is genuinely undecidable. Hofstadter's Luring Lottery, a variant that Scientific American actually ran, illustrates the same paradox. The takeaway is that in any one-shot cooperation problem with identical agents, standard game theory is helpless, and you need something like Kant's categorical imperative — act only on a maxim you can will as a universal law — to break the deadlock."

I've spent a fair amount of time on rationalist blogs and forums, so I'm familiar with Newcomb's problem, timeless decision theory, and the broad debates around decision theory, but I haven't read Hofstadter's original columns. Something about this summary strikes me as off — in particular, the claim that superrationality consists in acting as if your choice determines everyone else's, and the conclusion that the dilemma is therefore undecidable, feel like they might be getting the concept backwards. But I don't trust my own read of the summary enough to know exactly where the errors are.

Go through this summary and tell me what's accurate, what Hofstadter or a defender of his view would push back on, and whether the article's conclusions — that rationality gives no answer, that standard game theory is helpless, and that the resolution is essentially Kantian — follow from anything Hofstadter actually argued.
