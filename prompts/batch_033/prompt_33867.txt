Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis, and I came away unsure which of us had the fairer picture of it. My friend summarized the thesis roughly like this: "Fukuyama's point is simple. With the collapse of Soviet communism, liberal democracy and market capitalism had defeated every serious rival, so history — understood as the long struggle between fundamentally incompatible political systems — had come to an end. What remained was only the slow, uneventful spread of liberal democracy to the rest of the world; conflict would continue, but merely as mopping-up operations at the margins. Fukuyama did allow one possible remaining alternative, Islamic fundamentalism, but dismissed it as a desperate throwback with no real future. The subsequent rise of China, religious politics, and ethnic war has plainly refuted the thesis." I have a nagging sense that this is a caricature — I recall Fukuyama making more qualified claims about what "end of history" means and about what would happen afterward — but I cannot say exactly where the summary goes wrong, or whether the parts that are right still support the conclusion that the thesis has been refuted. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence: which parts would a sympathetic reader of Fukuyama accept, which are overstatements or conflations, and which are simply mistaken? Then say whether the final verdict — that later events have refuted the thesis — follows from the accurate parts of the summary. Keep your answer to at most 600 words.
