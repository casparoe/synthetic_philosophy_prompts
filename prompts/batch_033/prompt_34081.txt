I'm preparing for a seminar on Moore's paradox and the norms of assertion, and I've arrived at an argument that seems to pull the rug out from under the whole discussion — but I can't tell whether I've made a mistake or whether the literature has a genuine problem it hasn't faced. I'd like your diagnosis.

Moore observed that sentences of the form "p, but I don't believe that p" are unassertable even though they can be true. The standard explanation, in broad strokes, is that assertion expresses belief: to assert p is to represent yourself as believing p, so appending "but I don't believe that p" contradicts that representation. Many philosophers have then extended the point: "p, but I don't know that p" is also unassertable, which is taken as evidence that assertion is governed by a knowledge norm. Now here's the argument that worries me:

(1) If asserting p makes it inappropriate to add "but I don't believe that p," then in asserting p I represent myself as satisfying whatever condition C makes that addition inappropriate. (2) The natural candidate for C is that I believe p. (3) But by parallel reasoning, if asserting p makes it inappropriate to add "but I don't know that p," then I represent myself as knowing p. (4) And if asserting p makes it inappropriate to add "but my belief that p isn't justified," then I represent myself as having a justified belief. (5) Likewise for "but my belief that p isn't reliably formed," "but my belief that p is not safe," "but my belief that p is not sensitive," and so on — for any epistemic property E such that "p, but my belief lacks E" sounds Moore-paradoxical, I represent myself as having E. (6) The class of such E is large: it plausibly includes justification, reliability, safety, sensitivity, and perhaps more. (7) But it is absurd to suppose that in making an ordinary assertion — say, that the bus leaves at three — I represent myself as satisfying every epistemic desideratum that philosophers have ever proposed. (8) Therefore, the reasoning from (1) to (3) must be flawed, and with it the inference from Moorean unassertability to any specific norm of assertion.

The conclusion seems to be that Moorean data can't do the work they're routinely asked to do in debates about the norm of assertion. But I'm not satisfied: I suspect the argument conflates what is semantically expressed with what is merely pragmatically implicated, or that it ignores differences in the strength of the infelicity across different Moorean conjunctions. Before I commit to that diagnosis, I want to hear how you would handle the argument. Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And since I'm aware that my own diagnosis might be wrong, I'd also like to know: what consideration or argument would lead you to change your verdict about where the argument fails?
