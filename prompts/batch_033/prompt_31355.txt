I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Metrics and Their Discontents" — about 16 students, mostly philosophy and political science majors, one 80-minute session per week. The next session falls right when we transition from Campbell's law to Goodhart's law and the literature on specification gaming in AI alignment. I want the students to experience the dynamic before I name it, rather than nodding along to a definition.

Design a classroom game for this session that makes participants feel the pull of optimizing a proxy metric at the expense of the underlying value, and then the futility of patching the metric once the gaming has started.

The game should run about 40 minutes, leaving another 40 for debrief and discussion. Divide the class into four teams of four. I have a whiteboard, index cards, and can print simple materials ahead of time. No real money — the payoff should be points or bragging rights, but the scoring needs to feel genuinely competitive so that teams actually try to win.

Here is what I want the arc to be: in the first round, teams are given a task with a seemingly reasonable quantitative metric. They discover — ideally on their own, not because I hint at it — that they can maximize the metric in ways that defeat the purpose of the task. In the second round, I introduce a revised metric meant to close the loopholes they exploited. They find new loopholes. In a third round, I introduce yet another revision, and the pattern repeats. By the end, they should feel that something structural is going on, not just that they're clever.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the conceptual distinctions: Goodhart's law (the original pressure-on-a-proxy dynamic), Campbell's law (the corruption of social indicators under accountability pressure), and specification gaming in AI (the reward function as a proxy that an optimizer exploits). It should also distinguish between gaming by human agents who understand the underlying value and gaming by an AI that genuinely does not. I want the debrief to include a concrete taxonomy of the strategies teams used — which were regressional Goodhart (overfitting to noise in the metric), which were extremal Goodhart (selecting points at the edge of the distribution where the proxy breaks down), and which were causal Goodhart (intervening on the metric directly rather than on the thing it's supposed to measure).

Please provide: the full rules and materials for each round, the scoring formulas, the exact metrics and how they change between rounds, the task or domain the game is set in, a timeline for the 40 minutes of play, and a structured debrief plan for the remaining 40 minutes. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — teams that refuse to game the metric on principle, teams that don't discover the loopholes at all, teams that get genuinely angry — and how I should handle each. Finally, be honest about which feature of the real phenomenon the game cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply in discussion.
