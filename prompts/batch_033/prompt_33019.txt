I'm in a philosophy of mind course and we just did a unit on wireheading and the problem of reward tampering, and I'm trying to figure out what principle actually captures my intuitions because they're kind of all over the place. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases:

- Someone gets an electrode implanted in their nucleus accumbens and spends basically all their waking hours pressing the button. This seems obviously bad, like a ruined life.
- Someone takes an SSRI for clinical depression and reports feeling more satisfied with their life. Totally fine, good for them.
- A runner who chases the runner's high and that's part of why they run. Fine, that's just how motivation works.
- Someone who starts using heroin recreationally and their life gradually contracts to just seeking the next dose. Bad, and I want to say it's bad in the same way the electrode case is.
- A Buddhist monk who trains for decades to feel deep contentment with almost no external stimulation. This seems admirable, not a ruined life at all.
- Someone takes a hypothetical pill that makes them feel exactly as satisfied as if they'd written a great novel, but they've actually produced nothing. This feels wrong, like a lie even if no one else is involved.
- A person who works hard for years to build a business, succeeds, and feels deep satisfaction. Obviously fine.

I can't find the thread. At first I thought it was about whether the feeling is connected to something real that you accomplished, but the monk isn't accomplishing anything external and I still admire that. Then I thought maybe it's about whether you're still functioning in the world, but the SSRI user and the monk might both be functional, and honestly the electrode user might be functional between presses too. I also thought maybe it's about whether the feeling tracks a true belief about your life, but that doesn't seem to explain the difference between the monk and the heroin user.

Is there a single principle that makes sense of these verdicts, or at least most of them? And which ones do I have to give up if there isn't? Also, what does the principle you come up with say about a case I keep going back and forth on — someone who uses a non-addictive, side-effect-free technology that just permanently sets their hedonic baseline to "mildly content" regardless of what happens to them?
