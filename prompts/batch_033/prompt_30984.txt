I'm a software engineer by training — no formal philosophy background — and I currently serve as the outside-industry member on the program committee of a small theory-of-computation conference. We have a submission that has split the committee. The paper describes a concrete relativistic setup: a computer in a Malament-Hogarth spacetime, in which one observer can in finite proper time receive the results of infinitely many computational steps executed along another worldline. The authors argue that such a machine could decide the halting problem and that this refutes the Church-Turing thesis; they want the paper accepted as a main-track result. Two reviewers say the paper is simply confused: the Church-Turing thesis is a claim about effective computation in the mathematical sense, or an explication of the informal notion of an algorithm, and no story about exotic spacetime can touch it. Two other reviewers say that dismissal is too quick: on a widely held reading the thesis is also an empirical claim about what any physical device can compute, and a concrete physical counterexample is exactly the kind of thing that would refute it. The chair has asked me to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate. It should recommend one of the following: accept as a main-track paper, accept only as a short position note with revisions, or reject. Begin with the memo itself — no preface or commentary — and make its first sentence the recommendation. Then give the reasoning. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the side we do not take, and if it recommends revisions it should say specifically what the authors would need to change. I care more about getting the underlying conceptual distinction right than about the conference's acceptance rate, but the memo does need a clear, actionable bottom line that both the authors and the other reviewers could respond to.
