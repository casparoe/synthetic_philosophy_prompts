Two members of my decision-theory reading group got into an extended argument about the centipede game, and I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move. The setup: a centipede game with 100 rounds. Two players alternate choosing Pass or Take. The pot starts at $2 and grows by $1 each round. If a player Takes at round n, she gets $(n+1) and the other gets $(n−1). If both always Pass, they reach the end and split $101 and $100. Both players know the payoffs and know that both are rational, self-interested, and know that each other knows this.

Here's the exchange, which I wrote down as faithfully as I could:

(1) David: Backward induction gives a unique solution. At round 100, player 2 can Take $101 or Pass for $100 — she Takes. So at round 99, player 1 knows player 2 will Take at 100, so player 1 can Take $100 or Pass for $99 — he Takes. This unravels all the way back: at round 1, player 1 should Take immediately. It's airtight.

(2) Mira: The conclusion is absurd on its face. Two moderately cooperative but non-strategic players walk away with roughly fifty dollars each. Two "perfectly rational" players, on your logic, walk away with one dollar and nothing. A theory of rational choice that makes rational agents systematically poorer than ordinary reasonable ones has failed at its job.

(3) David: That's just wishing the game were different. Given the actual payoffs, if you Pass on round 1, you're choosing a move that loses you money against a rational opponent. The fact that you'd prefer a different game doesn't make Passing rational in this one.

(4) Mira: But backward induction requires common knowledge of rationality all the way to the end. Suppose player 1 Passes on move 1 anyway. Player 2 now observes an event inconsistent with player 1's perfect rationality. She must revise her model — maybe player 1 is irrational, or believes she is irrational, or is playing a different game. Either way, player 2 can no longer confidently run the backward induction from round 100, so she might reasonably Pass on round 2. And player 1, anticipating exactly this, might reasonably Pass on round 1. The backward-induction argument undermines its own premise the moment anyone deviates.

(5) David: That just pushes the problem back. If player 1 knows player 2 will reason that way, then player 1 knows that Passing on round 1 won't induce cooperation — because player 2 will see the deviation and think "player 1 is trying to exploit my willingness to cooperate, so I should Take." The whole thing unravels again, just with extra steps.

(6) Mira: You're assuming the only rational response to a deviation is to Take. But if both players have even a tiny amount of uncertainty about each other's type — say a 1% chance the other is an unconditional cooperator — then Passing early can be rational because it builds a reputation and induces further Passing. The "airtight" argument only works in a model with perfect common knowledge of rationality, a model that nobody actually inhabits and that may not even be coherent once you examine what happens off the equilibrium path.

(7) David: Those are all ways of changing the game. Adding uncertainty or social preferences makes it a different game. The original puzzle is about what ideal rationality requires in the centipede game as stated, with the assumptions as stated. And the answer is still: Take on round 1.

They went in circles after this and I couldn't resolve it. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the seven moves, rate it as clearly right, mostly right, a tie / unclear, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and give a one-sentence justification. Then state who is more right overall and identify the deepest unresolved issue between them.
