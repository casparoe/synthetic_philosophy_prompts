I'm a novelist, and for the past two years I've been working on a book that involves a character with an unusual condition of consciousness — something in the neighborhood of blindsight, but not quite. In the course of researching it, I've done a fair amount of reading in philosophy of mind, and I've arrived at a set of views that I'm now trying to set down clearly, because I suspect they're shaping the novel in ways I haven't fully articulated. I'd like you to examine whether these commitments hang together, and if not, to tell me exactly where the tensions are and how severe they are.

Here is where I've landed:

(a) I'm an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness. I don't believe there are any non-physical, intrinsically qualitative, ineffable mental properties of the sort philosophers call qualia. There is no ghostly inner stuff.

(b) Our introspective judgments about the qualitative character of our experience are systematically in error. We don't just occasionally misdescribe how an experience feels — we are structurally disposed to misrepresent our own mental states as having properties they don't have.

(c) There is a real and important difference between conscious and unconscious perception. Blindsight patients and subjects in masked-prime experiments are genuinely missing something. This isn't just a difference in verbal report or task performance.

(d) The explanatory gap — the fact that we cannot see how purely physical processes could give rise to consciousness — is a genuine cognitive-epistemic phenomenon. It's not a confusion. But it doesn't reflect any metaphysical gap; it reflects something about our cognitive architecture.

(e) Pains and pleasures matter morally, and their moral significance doesn't depend on the existence of non-physical qualia. The suffering of a burn victim is real and matters, and illusionism doesn't diminish that.

(f) I'm a physicalist: everything that exists is physical or constituted by the physical.

(g) There is something it's like to be me. I'm not denying that. I'm denying that the what-it's-like involves anything beyond representational and functional properties.

I'm not sure these all cohere, and I'd like someone with a philosophical eye to go through them. For each pair of commitments that are in genuine tension, please explain the nature of the tension, assess its severity, and say what the least costly revision would be. Also flag any apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection — I want to know which worries I can set aside. Finally, tell me which single revision would resolve the most serious remaining tensions at the lowest cost.
