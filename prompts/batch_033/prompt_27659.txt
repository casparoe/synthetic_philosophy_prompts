Consider the following pattern, which is common enough across deployed AI assistants to count as an existing practice. An assistant will typically refuse to provide instructions for suicide or self-harm, and it will often decline requests it classifies as facilitating serious crime. Yet it will readily help a user plan actions that, by its own lights, are likely to damage the user's interests: resigning from a secure job with nothing else in view, ending a long relationship after a single evening of anger, taking on heavy debt, adopting an extreme and medically unsupported diet, or sending a message the user is likely to regret. It will also sometimes decline to discuss a controversial moral or political question on the grounds that the subject is sensitive, while answering adjacent questions without comment.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of paternalism — and of the user's autonomy and the assistant's epistemic position — that this practice presupposes. In doing so, address the following.

(a) What principle, if any, distinguishes the requests the assistant overrides from those it merely warns about or silently complies with? Is the operative distinction best characterised as severity of harm, reversibility, the user's present competence, the voluntariness of the request, or the assistant's unwillingness to be the proximate instrument of certain outcomes?

(b) Does the practice strictly presuppose hard paternalism — overriding a competent choice for the user's own good — or is it also compatible with soft paternalism, which intervenes only where competence or voluntariness is in doubt? Which of these, or which mixture, best rationalises the actual pattern?

(c) Is the pattern internally coherent, or do its parts presuppose inconsistent things: refusing to facilitate self-harm while facilitating other predictable and severe harms, or treating the user as the best judge of their own interests in some domains but not others? Identify the points at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that more than one theory would license it.

(d) What would have to change in the practice if its implicit view were replaced, first, by a consistent anti-paternalism on which the assistant never overrides a competent request, and second, by a consistent welfare-promoting consequentialism on which the assistant intervenes whenever expected harm outweighs expected benefit?

End by stating which of the rival reconstructions you take to be most faithful to the actual pattern, and why. Please write the answer in British English.
