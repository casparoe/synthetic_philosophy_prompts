I've been covering the philosophical literature on hard choices and incommensurable values for my publication, and I've noticed a pattern that seems worth diagnosing. The debate between those who think values can be genuinely incommensurable and those who think all values are in principle comparable has been running for decades, and as far as I can tell, the central arguments on both sides were on the table by the early 2000s. Since then, the literature has grown, but the dialectic hasn't moved: the same move-and-countermove sequences appear in paper after paper, and I don't know anyone whose mind has been changed by them.

Here is the debate as I understand it. The incommensurabilist says that when you face a choice between, say, a career that is intellectually stimulating and one that allows you to support your family generously, there is often no fact of the matter about which is better — not even that they are equally good, because improving one option slightly would not break the tie. The options are incommensurable: they cannot be placed on a single scale of value. The comparabilist replies that this gets the phenomenology wrong in the other direction: if the options were truly incomparable, rational choice between them would be arbitrary, but we do not experience hard choices as arbitrary. Moreover, the comparabilist argues, the incommensurabilist's argument from the failure of small improvements to break a tie just shows that the comparative relation is vague, not that no comparison exists. The incommensurabilist counters that vagueness is itself a comparative relation — it still requires that the options sit on the same scale — and that the real issue is whether all values reduce to a single dimension.

What strikes me, reading this literature, is that both sides can accommodate the same data. Both can explain why hard choices feel hard, why they feel different from choices between clearly ranked options, and why small improvements do not always resolve them. Both can account for the fact that we do, in practice, make hard choices without feeling that we are acting irrationally. The disagreement seems to be about what is doing the explanatory work: the metaphysics of value, on one side, or the limits of our epistemic access to value, on the other. And I cannot see what new evidence — empirical, formal, or conceptual — would decide between these two explanations.

My hypothesis, and I offer it as a hypothesis, is that this debate is not really about value at all. I suspect it is a disagreement about the norms governing a theory of practical reason. The comparabilist wants a theory that preserves the formal structure of maximization — that is, a theory in which rational choice is always choice of the best option, even if we cannot always identify it. The incommensurabilist wants a theory that takes the first-person experience of deliberation seriously and does not posit ranking facts that play no role in actual decision-making. If that is right, the two sides are answering different questions: one is asking what the structure of value must be for rational choice to be formally well-behaved, and the other is asking what the structure of value must be for our experience of deliberation to be intelligible. And if that is right, the impasse is not a sign that the question is hard but that it is two questions conflated.

But I am not a philosopher, and I want to know whether this diagnosis holds up. Is there a genuine, unacknowledged empirical or formal question at the bottom of this debate — something about the structure of preference, or about the cognitive processes underlying choice, or about the semantics of comparative terms — that could, if investigated, break the impasse? Or is it indeed a disagreement about the aims of theorizing, and if so, is there any argument that could persuade one side to adopt the other's aims, or is this a bedrock normative disagreement about what a theory of practical reason is for? And given whichever diagnosis is correct, what would actually count as progress?
