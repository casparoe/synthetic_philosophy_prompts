I sit on the public communication committee of a regional health authority. We must decide whether to replace the written patient-information notices in our clinics and outreach materials with a unified picture-based sign system, on the model of Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE project. Our patient population includes speakers of more than a dozen first languages, and the committee is split. One side holds that a properly constructed picture language is more nearly universal than any written language and can be made neutral among languages and cultures. The other side holds that pictures are not a language in the required sense: a picture can show a particular figure or object, but it cannot state, deny, qualify, or modalize in the way a public notice must, and the appearance of neutrality is itself an editorial choice, since every simplification of a human figure and every selection of what to depict encodes a decision about what matters.

I spent last evening arguing about exactly this with a friend. His position was that Neurath's dream of a picture language relocates rather than removes the need for interpretation: a pictogram of a cigarette with a red slash does not negate the way a sentence does, because the slash is itself a learned convention, and once you grant that such conventions must be taught and can be misread, the claim that pictures are self-evidently universal collapses. I found the argument harder to answer than I expected, and it has made me think our committee is relitigating a philosophical dispute rather than merely a design dispute.

Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the picture system, reject it, or adopt it under specified conditions — with a time frame, and with reasons the committee can actually act on. It should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it. It should also treat the philosophical question as part of the practical one: say clearly what a picture system of this kind can and cannot do as a vehicle of public communication, and how that bears on the decision. Write in your own words; do not include quotations, footnotes, or references.
