I'm putting together a practice exam for my high school philosophy club's unit on causation, and I'd like one essay question on causation by omission, to be written in 45 minutes and marked out of 20. It should be the kind of question that can't be answered by reciting the acts/omissions distinction or the counterfactual definition of causation. Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must include. The question should force students to work through the selection problem with a case like this: a lifeguard on duty and an ordinary bystander both watch a swimmer drown and neither acts. On a but-for test, both omissions qualify as causes of the death, yet we ordinarily single out the lifeguard. Students should have to decide whether the difference between the two omissions is a causal fact or a normative matter of duty, and therefore whether causation by omission is a genuine causal relation or a disguised way of assigning responsibility. The marking scheme should reward an answer that states the counterfactual test accurately, shows how it overgenerates in the lifeguard/bystander case, separates the causal question from the responsibility question, defends one side of the debate, and replies to at least one objection — for instance, the fact that we still explain outcomes by citing what someone failed to do. It should also identify specific misunderstandings that cost marks, such as treating the lifeguard's duty as itself a causal fact without argument, or thinking the case refutes the but-for test rather than showing it needs to be supplemented.
