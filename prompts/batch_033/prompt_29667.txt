I'm a high school student who runs a small prediction market among friends — we bet small amounts on student council elections, whether the football team makes playoffs, which teacher leaves next, that kind of thing. I set the initial odds and adjust them as people place bets. I've read enough to know about Dutch book arguments: if the odds you offer don't satisfy the probability axioms, someone can construct a set of bets you'd accept individually that together guarantee you lose money no matter what happens. I want to turn that principle into a procedure I can actually use.

Give me a checklist I can run in under ten minutes before I post a new market. It should catch the most common ways my odds could be incoherent — not just that probabilities of mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes should sum to one, but subtler failures like inconsistency between conditional and unconditional odds, or between odds on events where one is a subset of another. The procedure needs to work when my probabilities are rough estimates, not numbers I'm deriving from data, and it should mark clearly which steps are mechanical checks and which are judgment calls I can't eliminate.

Then apply the procedure to this case: I'm setting odds on three events for the upcoming student council election — (A) the incumbent wins, (B) the challenger wins, and (C) the incumbent wins but gets less than 55% of the vote. My gut estimates are 50%, 40%, and 25% respectively. Walk me through what the procedure catches, what it doesn't, and whether the remaining judgment calls matter enough that I should change how I set odds in the first place.
