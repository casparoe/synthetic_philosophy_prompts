Question: "Present the Sleeping Beauty problem. Explain the main argument for the halfer position and the main argument for the thirder position. Then assess which position is better supported by considerations of rational betting, and why."

Student's answer:

"The Sleeping Beauty problem asks what credence Beauty should assign to a fair coin's having landed heads when she wakes up during an experiment. On Sunday, Beauty is told the setup: a fair coin will be flipped. If heads, she will be awakened on Monday only. If tails, she will be awakened on Monday, given a drug that erases her memory of the awakening, and then awakened again on Tuesday. When she wakes, she knows the setup but does not know what day it is or whether she has been awakened before. The question is what her credence in heads should be upon awakening.

The halfer says 1/2. The main argument, due to David Lewis, is that Beauty learns nothing new when she wakes up. She already knew on Sunday that she would be awakened at least once, regardless of the coin flip. Since she receives no new evidence, and her prior credence in heads on Sunday was 1/2, her credence upon awakening should remain 1/2. Some halfers also argue that the coin is fair and the experimental setup does not change the physical probability of heads, so there is no reason to deviate from 1/2.

The thirder says 1/3. The main argument, due to Adam Elga, is that when Beauty wakes up, there are three possible centered worlds she could be in — heads-Monday, tails-Monday, and tails-Tuesday — and by the principle of indifference, these three possibilities are equally likely. Since only one of them involves heads, her credence should be 1/3.

The betting argument supports the thirder. If Beauty bets $1 on heads at 1:1 odds each time she wakes, she loses money in expectation. In the heads case (probability 1/2), she wins $1 once. In the tails case (probability 1/2), she loses $1 twice. Her expected net gain is (1/2)($1) + (1/2)(–$2) = –$0.50. This shows that a credence of 1/2 is a losing credence for guiding action. Since rational credences should guide rational action, the thirder position is better supported."
