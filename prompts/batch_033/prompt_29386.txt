In my biology class we read Gould and Lewontin's paper on spandrels. Their central move, as I understood it, is that a trait can look designed, be useful, and still not be an adaptation, because it may be a byproduct of how something else is built — like the triangular spaces under the dome of San Marco. I tried to extend that thought, and I ended up with a conclusion I do not believe. I have written the argument out with numbered steps so that the failure can be located precisely.

(1) To call a trait an adaptation for a function F is to make a historical claim: the trait became common because natural selection favored it for contributing to F, rather than its merely having that use now.

(2) For any trait and any proposed function, non-adaptive alternatives are always available in principle: the trait could be a spandrel, a byproduct of selection on some other trait; it could have spread by genetic drift; or it could be a side-effect of developmental or architectural constraints.

(3) A claim about an unobserved evolutionary past is justified only if the competing alternatives that are consistent with what we observe have been ruled out, or at least shown to be much less probable.

(4) For most traits, the evidence that would rule out the spandrel, drift, and constraint alternatives — past effective population sizes, the fitness differences among the variants that actually existed, the developmental architecture at the trait's origin — is unavailable, and in many cases unrecoverable.

(5) Therefore, for most traits, the claim that they are adaptations is unjustified.

(6) But that would make us unjustified in calling the vertebrate eye's lens an adaptation for focusing light, or hemoglobin an adaptation for oxygen transport.

(7) Since (6) is unacceptable, at least one of (1) through (4) must be false.

Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (6), but I want the honest price, not a reassuring answer.
