I've been turning over a version of Buridan's ass that seems to prove far too much, and I can't locate the misstep. Here's the argument:

(1) A choice is rational only if the agent has a reason that favors the chosen option over the alternatives.
(2) In a perfect tie — two options identical in every respect the agent cares about — there is no reason favoring either over the other.
(3) So in a perfect tie, neither choice is rational.
(4) The same applies when options are merely on a par or incomparable: if no reason decisively favors one over the others, no choice among them is rational.
(5) But parity and incomparability are pervasive. Choosing between two equally good job candidates, two equally appealing vacation destinations, or two equally worthwhile charitable causes are not exotic edge cases; they are the stuff of ordinary life.
(6) So rational choice is impossible across a vast swath of ordinary decisions.
(7) But we do make such choices, and we do not ordinarily regard ourselves or others as irrational for doing so.
(8) Therefore, at least one of the premises must go.

I'm drawn to rejecting (1) — perhaps rationality doesn't require a favoring reason, only the absence of a defeating reason against what you pick. But then I worry: if rationality permits any arbitrary selection among tied options, does it also permit coin-flipping your way through all the hard choices in life? And if not, what distinguishes a tie from a hard choice?

Tell me which premise you would reject, and what rejecting it costs. If you think the argument contains an equivocation or a scope shift rather than a false premise, identify it and explain why it looked innocent.
