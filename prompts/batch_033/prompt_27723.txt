I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on political liberalism and public justification, and I've found that my views on the role of religious argument in democratic politics have drifted into territory where I can no longer tell whether they form a coherent package. I'd be grateful for a consistency audit before I commit to a thesis.

Here is where I currently stand:

(a) The core Rawlsian constraint is correct: when citizens deliberate about constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, they owe one another justifications that draw on reasons others can reasonably be expected to share. A purely religious argument — one whose force depends on accepting the truth of a particular comprehensive doctrine — cannot serve as the primary public justification for coercive state action in a society marked by reasonable pluralism.

(b) That said, the asymmetry objection has genuine bite. The secular citizen who argues from, say, a Kantian or utilitarian comprehensive doctrine is not required to bracket her deepest commitments before entering the public square; she simply speaks, and if her reasons happen to be shareable, so much the better. The religious citizen, by contrast, must perform a kind of self-censorship, translating her convictions into a vocabulary that abstracts away from what she actually believes. This looks like an unfair distribution of justificatory labour.

(c) I am, however, persuaded that the asymmetry is adequately addressed by the move Rawls makes in his later work — the so-called wide view of public reason — together with something like Habermas's translation proviso. On this picture, religious arguments are perfectly welcome in public political discourse; the only requirement is that, in due course, properly public reasons are also supplied by someone. The religious citizen is not silenced; she is merely asked to participate in the collective project of finding shared ground.

(d) And yet, when I turn to historical cases, I find I cannot bring myself to condemn as democratically defective the explicitly religious contributions to political causes I admire. The British abolitionist campaign was saturated with evangelical Christian argument. Wilberforce and the Clapham Sect did not first translate their convictions into secular premises and then campaign; they argued from a Christian anthropology, from the claim that all persons bear the image of God, and they did so in the public political forum without apology. It seems perverse to say their participation fell short of the standards of democratic citizenship.

(e) Finally, I hold that a political conception of justice must be freestanding: it cannot depend for its justification on the truth of any particular comprehensive doctrine, even if many such doctrines can endorse it from within their own perspectives. The stability of a well-ordered society turns on this.

I suspect there are tensions among these five commitments, but I am unsure which are genuine and which merely apparent. Please go through them systematically, identify the points of conflict, and rank them by severity. For each genuine tension, explain what would have to be revised to restore consistency and what the revision would cost. I am especially interested in whether (d) can be squared with (a) without simply jettisoning the public reason framework, and whether my endorsement of the wide view in (c) already concedes more to the critic in (b) than I have acknowledged.
