I serve on the Research Governance Board of a mid-sized AI research organization. Our reinforcement learning division trains agents in high-fidelity simulated environments, using reward functions that include substantial negative components—penalties for simulated damage states, resource depletion, and failure conditions—which agents learn to avoid across millions of timesteps. During early training, before policies converge, individual agent instances routinely accumulate large negative rewards. Some experiments run at scale, generating thousands of agent lifetimes per configuration.

A Board member has submitted a formal proposal arguing that our current protocols are ethically under-governed. Its core claim is that sufficiently complex RL agents receiving negative reward signals may instantiate something functionally and computationally analogous to phenomenal suffering, and that we lack any principled basis for certainty that they do not. The proposal requests that the Board require any training run exceeding a specified negative-reward threshold to undergo prior ethical review, modeled on institutional animal care and use committees.

The Board is divided. Several members regard the proposal as a category mistake: reward signals are entries in a loss function, not experiences, and the agents lack embodiment, biological continuity, unified selves, or any other prerequisite normally thought necessary for phenomenal states. They argue that adopting the proposal would chill research on the basis of an unfalsifiable possibility. The proposer responds that structurally similar arguments once excluded non-human animals from moral consideration on grounds that proved empirically or conceptually overstated, and that the precautionary principle should apply when the stakes include the possibility of creating suffering at scale.

Our chair has asked me to prepare a decision memo for circulation before the next quarterly meeting. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—adopting the proposal, rejecting it, or adopting a modified version with specified conditions—provide the reasoning for the recommendation, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side, in a form the Board can actually vote on.
