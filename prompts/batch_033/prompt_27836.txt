I recently had a long argument with a friend about the Ellsberg paradox, and I've been turning one point over in my mind. As I understand it, the paradox demonstrates that people who prefer betting on an urn with a known 50/50 composition over an urn with an unknown mix of red and black balls are violating Savage's sure-thing principle. Since the sure-thing principle is a foundational axiom of rational choice, it seems to follow straightforwardly that ambiguity aversion is simply a mistake—a deviation from rationality that we should correct. This would mean that in any real-world decision under ambiguity, the rational course is to assign subjective probabilities to the unknown possibilities and proceed with standard expected utility maximization, treating the ambiguity as if it were mere risk. My friend insisted this conclusion doesn't follow, but I couldn't see where the logic breaks down. Can you help me identify the flaw?
