I help organize a small philosophy of mind discussion series at a public library, and we are about to record an episode with a philosopher who defends a broadly Malcolm-style view of dreaming. Her position is that it is a conceptual confusion to suppose that dreams are experiences occurring during sleep, and that a dream report is not a report of a private episode but a waking performance governed by the practice of dream-telling. The topic has become unexpectedly personal for me: after several weeks of unusually vivid nightmares, I started keeping a dream journal, and I keep catching myself assuming that what I write down each morning is a record of something that actually happened while I slept — which is precisely the assumption our guest thinks is confused.

I want the interview to push well beyond a restatement of the view. Please draft eight to ten questions, including at least one question on each of the following, and for each question include a follow-up keyed to the reply our guest is most likely to give: (a) the argument that a person cannot intelligibly assert of herself that she is asleep, and therefore cannot ground the idea that she is aware of dreaming while it occurs; (b) the claim that dream-telling is not a memory report, and what the relation between the report and the previous night would then be; (c) the challenge posed by lucid dreaming, especially the sleep-laboratory results in which subjects signal with prearranged eye movements during REM sleep; and (d) whether Dennett's thought experiment in which a dream is recorded during sleep and later played back to the waking person shows that the question whether dreams are experiences is intelligible after all, even if it should turn out to be answerable in the negative. The questions should turn on the actual structure of the Malcolm-style position and anticipate the standard objections rather than asking for introductory exposition.
