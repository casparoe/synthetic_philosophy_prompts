For my upper-division philosophy of mind seminar, I need a decision tree on what makes pain bad. We've been using pain asymbolia as our central puzzle case — you know, these patients (usually from insular or cingulate lesions) can tell you a sensation is painful, locate it, rate its intensity, distinguish sharp from dull, but they genuinely don't mind it. They smile through it. It doesn't bother them. So whatever makes normal pain bad, they're missing it, while keeping the sensory-discriminative component intact. It's basically the perfect wedge.

I want the decision tree to start from a really basic question like "Is the badness of pain part of the sensation itself, or is it something seperate from the sensation?" and branch from there. The leaves should include at least: perceptualism (pain represents bodily damage, and the badness is in the represented content — like the redness of a perceived apple), imperativism (pain is a command to stop doing whatever you're doing), evaluativism (pain is a direct perception of badness or disvalue), the attitudinal view (badness is just our conative reaction — we want it to stop, and that wanting is what the badness consists in), and whatever you call the view that the badness is intrinsic to the quale itself (the view pain asymbolia seems to refute, but some people still defend it by saying asymbolics have degraded or incomplete pain, not the real thing).

Each branch node should be a question someone could answer without knowing the jargon. Don't just ask "are you an imperativist?" — ask about the case. So for instance: "When you feel a pain in your hand near a hot stove, is the pain telling you something about your hand, or is it telling you to do something?" That kind of thing.

Flag any positions your tree can't separate — I suspect imperativism and evaluativism are hard to pull apart using just asymbolia as a test case, so tell me if that's right and what additional case would split them.

Also flag any logical combination of answers that doesn't have a defender in the literature, and say whether you think that's because the position is incoherent, just deeply unattractive, or merely underexplored. I want students to see the logical space, not just the historically occupied positions.

Format: Give me the tree itself first (use indentation or numbering, whatever's clearest in plain text). Then a section called "How to use this" (about half a page) explaining how an instructor would walk students through it in a seminar — where they'll stumble, which nodes generate the most argument. Then a final section called "The asymbolia challenge" (about half a page) on whether pain asymbolia actually settles anything or whether the defender of intrinsic badness has a plausible reply that keeps their view alive.
