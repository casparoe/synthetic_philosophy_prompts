I am preparing an essay on the relationship between Kelly betting and what might be called geometric rationality, and I would appreciate your comments on the following sketch of its central argument. I aim to submit this to a general philosophy journal, so the argument must be accessible to readers unfamiliar with the technical literature while remaining philosophically rigorous.

**Working Title:** "Geometric Rationality and the Normative Force of Repeated Gambles"

**Central Claim:** The Kelly criterion is not merely a technical rule for maximizing long-run wealth in iterated betting contexts; it encodes a distinctive normative conception of practical rationality—one in which the relevant measure of an option's choiceworthiness is its expected geometric growth rate rather than its arithmetic expectation. I argue that this conception, which I call geometric rationality, has underappreciated implications for decision theory, the theory of risk, and the interpretation of utility functions.

**Outline of the Argument:**

1. **The setup.** Consider an agent facing a sequence of independent, identical gambles with positive arithmetic expectation. Standard decision theory prescribes maximizing expected utility and therefore recommends taking each gamble in full. Yet following this prescription in a multiplicative setting (where wealth compounds across trials) leads, with probability approaching one, to worse outcomes than betting a fixed fraction of one's bankroll according to the Kelly rule over sufficiently many trials. This is the familiar point that maximizing arithmetic expectation in multiplicative settings produces volatility drag and, in the limit, near-certain ruin relative to the Kelly policy.

2. **From description to prescription.** The standard response treats the Kelly result as a descriptive fact about long-run trajectories, not a normative challenge to expected utility theory. I argue this distinction is unstable. If an agent knows with certainty that they will face a long sequence of independent gambles, the fact that one policy yields near-certain ruin while another yields near-certain enormous gains seems normatively decisive. The usual escape—insisting that agents should simply have sufficiently concave utility in wealth—misses the deeper point. An agent with logarithmic utility and the Kelly bettor converge on the same behavior, but the Kelly bettor's rationale is structural rather than psychological. Geometric rationality claims that the iterative, multiplicative structure of repeated risk generates normative reasons independently of an agent's risk attitudes.

3. **The independence axiom and complementarity.** Here I develop what I take to be the most philosophically interesting part of the argument. In a multiplicative setting, the value of a gamble at time t depends on the outcomes of gambles at times 1 through t−1. This induces a form of complementarity between temporally separated choices—earlier outcomes change what later bets amount to in a way that makes choices non-separable. The independence axiom, in its standard formulations, excludes precisely this kind of interdependence. I suggest this reveals a tension: either we accept that the normative status of a choice depends on its embedding in a temporal sequence (and thus reject the independence axiom in dynamic, multiplicative settings), or we maintain independence and accept that rationality can recommend policies that are stochastically dominated in the limit.

4. **Broader implications.** If geometric rationality is correct, we have grounds for a rethinking of risk aversion in economic theory. Rather than treating risk aversion as a feature of preferences (curvature of the utility function), we might understand it as a rational response to the multiplicative structure of real-world wealth dynamics. This would provide a non-psychological foundation for a range of risk-averse behaviors currently explained by positing concave utility, and it would recast several debates about the normative status of expected utility theory.

**Potential Objections I Anticipate:**

- The argument depends on agents knowing they will face repeated gambles; in many real-world decisions, this is not transparently the case.
- The Kelly criterion itself can be derived within expected utility theory if one permits logarithmic utility, so geometric rationality may collapse into standard theory after all—the dispute would reduce to which utility function is rationally mandated.
- There is a growing literature on ergodicity economics that makes structurally similar points about the difference between time averages and ensemble averages; my argument must distinguish itself from this work or risk redundancy.

I have two specific areas where I would particularly value comments.

First, does the argument in section 3 genuinely identify a tension with the independence axiom that goes beyond existing critiques of independence in dynamic choice settings? I worry that the non-separability I identify may turn out to be a special case of a more general phenomenon already well discussed in the literature on dynamic consistency.

Second, I am concerned that the argument's force depends on an ambiguity between two senses of "rationality"—procedural rationality (following rules that are justifiable ex ante) and outcome rationality (systematically achieving good results). Is there a satisfying way to bridge these senses without simply stipulating that the latter reduces to the former, or must geometric rationality ultimately commit to a consequentialist picture of rational norms?
