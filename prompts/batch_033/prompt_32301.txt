I've been chewing on this argument and I can't find the flaw, but the conclusion seems too strong. Can you help?

(1) When we deliberate well, we sometimes come to see that we had a reason for something all along — a reason we hadn't previously noticed or formulated. (2) This makes no sense unless the reason existed before we noticed it. (3) So at least some reasons exist independently of any reasoner having them — they're "free-floating" rationales.

(4) But if there can be one free-floating reason, there's no principaled barrier to there being vast numbers of them — reasons that no one has ever articulated, reasons attached to actions no one has ever contemplated. (5) Among these undiscovered reasons, there will be some that bear on every ordinary action you and I perform. (6) So for any action you take, there may be weighty undiscovered reasons against it — reasons you can't access even in principle, since discovering them would require conceptual resources you lack.

(7) If for any action you take there may be inaccessible reasons against it, then you can never be justified in believing you've acted rightly. (8) A normative framework that precludes justified belief about right action is self-undermining: the whole point of reasons is to guide action, and guidance requires at least the possibility of knowing where you stand.

(9) So (3) must be false. Reasons can't be free-floating. (10) But then we're forced to say that when we "discover" a reason, we're actually creating it — which seems to get the phenomenology of moral discovery completely backwards.

Where does this go wrong? And if I have to reject a premise, which one costs the least?
