I have long been persuaded by the view that the self is something like a center of narrative gravity: not a substance, not a particular thing in the brain, but an abstractum — a kind of useful fiction that emerges from the stories we tell about ourselves and that others tell about us. The self, on this picture, is real in roughly the way a center of gravity is real: it is not a physical particular, but neither is it nothing; it earns its keep by organizing and predicting a complex set of psychological and behavioral phenomena. I do not think the view entails eliminativism about the self, but it does deny that there is a single, unified, persisting entity that does the thinking and the feeling in anything like the way a Cartesian ego would.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not just what its critics have said, but what its defenders ought to concede as genuine pressure points — the phenomena, arguments, or results from philosophy, psychology, or neuroscience that should most trouble someone who accepts the narrative-gravity picture. I would like you to identify the most serious defeaters and rank them from fatal to merely awkward, with a defense of the ordering.

I am interested in several families of challenge. Are there features of first-person experience — the unity of consciousness at a moment, the sense of being an agent rather than a character, the phenomenology of self-reference — that the view cannot adequately describe without distorting the phenomena? Does the view struggle with cases where narrative breaks down, such as severe amnesia, dissociative identity disorder, or advanced dementia, and if so, does that reveal something about the self that the view misses, or does it merely show that the narrative machinery can break? Are there arguments from the metaphysics of persistence — about what it takes for a self to survive changes in narrative — that put pressure on the view's implicit criteria of identity over time? And is there a risk that the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: that whenever someone points to something that seems to resist narrativization, the defender can simply broaden what counts as a narrative? If the view is well insulated, I want to know whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, or whether it is not in the business of making risky empirical predictions in the first place.

I am less interested in a survey of the critical literature than in a map of the view's real exposure: which defeaters its defenders would themselves concede carry weight, which the view can comfortably absorb, and which would genuinely require abandoning it or substantially revising its core commitments.
