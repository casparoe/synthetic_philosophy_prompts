You are a cognitive scientist who works on the language–thought interface. You are comfortable with the distinction between grammaticalized distinctions (obligatory and automatically encoded) and lexical ones (optional), and with the difference between perceptual or categorical tasks and amodal or epistemic tasks. Think carefully and brainstorm candidate rules before committing; do not settle for the first rule that fits.

My colleague Dr. Mara Ellison studies whether the language a person speaks changes non-linguistic cognition — tasks using button presses or pointing, with no spoken or written response. Across six studies she has reached these verdicts:

1. Grammatical aspect: speakers of a language whose verbs obligatorily mark whether an event is completed or ongoing were better than controls at remembering whether actions in short videos had been completed. Verdict: effect.
2. Grammatical spatial frame: speakers of a language with no relative spatial vocabulary, in which spatial statements must use cardinal or geographic orientation, outperformed controls on a non-linguistic pointing task after being rotated. Verdict: effect.
3. Grammatical noun class: speakers of a language in which every noun belongs to a noun class that adjectives and verbs must agree with sorted pictures of objects by similarity along class lines more than controls did. Verdict: effect.
4. Lexical evidential vocabulary: speakers of a language with only optional words for marking information source, such as a particle meaning apparently or a construction meaning I heard it said, were no better than controls at a non-linguistic source-memory task in which they judged whether they had directly seen or only inferred each test item. Verdict: no effect.
5. Lexical causal vocabulary: speakers of a language with optional causal connectives but no grammatical marking of causation were no better than controls at non-linguistic causal judgments about launching-event animations. Verdict: no effect.
6. Lexical mental-state vocabulary: speakers of a language with optional mental-state words but no obligatory marking showed no advantage on a non-linguistic false-belief task. Verdict: no effect.

She is now planning a seventh study: the same non-linguistic source-memory task as study 4, but with speakers of a language that grammatically requires evidential marking on every clause — direct, inferred, or reported. The task includes verbal shadowing to block silent labeling.

I want to know what to expect her to predict. I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the rule or rules she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her six verdicts equally well, and which further study would best settle which rule she is actually committed to.

Please answer as a numbered list with exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Use these five items:
1. State the two or three most plausible rules consistent with all six of her verdicts, and mention any further rule-family that also fits.
2. For each rule, say what it predicts about the seventh study.
3. Give your best prediction for the seventh study, with the reasons for preferring that rule, and say honestly how much her track record underdetermines the prediction.
4. Identify the closest analog among her first six studies and explain why it is not decisive.
5. Propose the single further study that would most cleanly discriminate between the rules, and state what each rule predicts for it.
