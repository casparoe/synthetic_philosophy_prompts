I am a novelist, and a character I am writing has talked himself into an argument that lets him dismiss, in advance, any challenge to his convictions, and I find I cannot refute it as cleanly as I would like. The argument runs: (1) If I know that p, then I know that any evidence against p is misleading. (2) If I know that a piece of evidence is misleading, then I am rationally entitled to set it aside. (3) Therefore, if I know that p, I am rationally entitled to set aside any evidence against p. (4) But this cannot be right, since it would give any knower a standing license for dogmatism about everything they know. Where does the argument go wrong? I would most like to keep (1), which seems to follow from the factivity of knowledge; I am less attached to (2), but I want to know what rejecting either premise commits me to, and whether some weakened version of the conclusion survives. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked so plausible, and what the standard repairs each cost.
