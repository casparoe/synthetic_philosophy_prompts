You are a philosopher of language and visual representation, with a strong background in early twentieth-century philosophy of science. Work through the question slowly. Begin by reconstructing the practice from the details below, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag any point where you think the practice is philosophically underdetermined rather than committing to a single view.

Otto Neurath's picture-language project — the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics, later called Isotype — aimed to communicate social and economic facts through standardized pictograms: simplified, repeatable figures to show quantities, flat colors, no perspective, no individual variation, and an explicit ambition to be intelligible across languages and to readers with little formal education. Neurath presented the system as an instrument of unified science and democratic enlightenment, and he and his collaborators treated the pictograms as a more direct, more rational, and less divisive vehicle for facts than prose. Yet Neurath elsewhere denied that science rests on a neutral, theory-free observation basis and insisted that all knowledge is fallible and revisable.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? In particular, what theory of pictorial meaning, if any, does Isotype presuppose? Does it commit itself to the idea that a simplified picture can be transparent or 'natural' — that the link between a pictogram and the fact it displays can be read off without prior training — or is it better reconstructed as a conventional symbol system whose intelligibility is learned like a language? Does the practice presuppose that social and economic reality is the kind of thing that can be exhaustively presented in standardized visual units, and does that carry a quiet commitment to something like physicalism or the unity of science? And how should we understand the apparent tension between the project's claim to neutrality and the fact that its editorial choices — which categories to display, what counts as 'unemployment', which contrasts to make salient, which simplifications to adopt — seem to encode substantive social and political judgments? Reconstruct the implicit theory of representation and of social facts, say whether the practice's parts presuppose consistent things, and tell me what would have to change in a picture-language project built on a rival view of representation, such as one that treats pictograms as thoroughly conventional or as unavoidably theory-laden.
