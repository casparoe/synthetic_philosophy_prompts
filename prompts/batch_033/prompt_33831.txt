I am preparing a lecture on Dostoevsky's "The Grand Inquisitor" chapter from *The Brothers Karamazov* for an upper-division philosophy and literature seminar, and I have been struggling with a particular stretch of the Grand Inquisitor's monologue. I would like help understanding the internal logic of the passage, because I suspect there is a move in it that my students will spot and that I am not yet prepared to handle.

The passage I have in mind is the one in which the Inquisitor tells Christ that Christ's great error was to prize human freedom above human happiness. The Inquisitor's argument, as I reconstruct it, runs roughly as follows: when Christ rejected the three temptations in the wilderness—turning stones to bread, commanding a miracle by throwing himself from the temple, and accepting worldly rule—he rejected the very instruments by which human beings could have been made content. The Inquisitor says that by refusing to give people bread, Christ demanded that they come to him freely and for the sake of something higher than their bellies. But the vast majority of people, the Inquisitor insists, are too weak to bear this freedom; they find it a torment, not a gift. They would gladly trade their freedom for certainty, for someone to tell them what to do, for bread and mystery and authority. And so the Church, in the Inquisitor's telling, has corrected Christ's work: it has taken freedom off people's hands and given them the happiness they actually crave, at the price of their liberty.

Here is what I find puzzling. The Inquisitor seems to be making two claims about the relationship between freedom and happiness that sit uneasily together. On the one hand, he treats freedom and happiness as straightforwardly opposed: freedom is a burden, and removing it makes people happy. On the other hand, he also seems to treat freedom as a kind of good that only a few elite souls can properly enjoy, while the many are constitutionally incapable of it. So is the Inquisitor saying that freedom is intrinsically bad for most people, or that it is a genuine good that is simply beyond their capacities—like a food that is nourishing but indigestible to all but the strongest stomachs? And if the latter, then what exactly is the Church doing when it rescinds freedom: is it withholding a real good paternalistically for people's own sake, or is it declaring that the good was never really a good for them in the first place? I can see the text pulling in both directions, and I want to know whether this tension is a flaw in the Inquisitor's reasoning—a kind of equivocation that Dostoevsky put there deliberately to undermine him—or whether it reflects a genuine philosophical difficulty about the value of autonomy when autonomy makes people miserable. I would be grateful for a close reading that traces the conceptual structure of the passage and helps me see what is actually being argued, so that I can present it clearly and anticipate the objections it will provoke.
