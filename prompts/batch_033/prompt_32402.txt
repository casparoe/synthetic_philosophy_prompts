I'm taking a graduate seminar on political ethics, and we've spent the semester working through the problem of dirty hands — the idea that political leaders must sometimes do morally reprehensible things to fulfill their responsibilities to the communities they serve. Our professor has given us her verdicts on six cases, one per week, and for our final assignment she's given us a seventh case and asked us to predict her verdict on it and defend the prediction. She's warned us that more than one principle fits her track record, and that part of the assignment is identifying the candidate principles and explaining why we think one captures her actual view rather than just being consistent with her pattern so far.

I've been going back and forth and I'd like your help. I want you to commit to a definite prediction rather than just laying out the options — tell me what you think she'll say and why, and if you're uncertain, tell me which way you'd bet and where the weakest point in that bet is.

Here are the cases and her verdicts, in the order she gave them to us:

Case 1. A governor, on the eve of an execution, receives strong new evidence that the condemned man is innocent. But the governor knows that if she delays the execution, the victim's community will see it as a betrayal and riots will break out; the governor's security advisors estimate dozens of deaths. She withholds the evidence and allows the execution to proceed. Verdict: justified.

Case 2. A defense minister lies to the press about the location of troops during an active military operation. If the truth got out, enemy forces would exploit it and soldiers would be killed. Verdict: justified.

Case 3. A mayor accepts a large campaign donation from a real estate developer and, weeks later, casts the deciding vote to approve that developer's zoning variance. The mayor insists the donation played no role in the decision, and independent analysts agree the variance was warranted on the merits. Verdict: not justified.

Case 4. A president authorizes the torture of a captured terrorist who is believed to know the location of a bomb set to detonate in a crowded city. The information is obtained, the attack is prevented, and the president later discloses the authorization to Congress and accepts full responsibility. Verdict: justified.

Case 5. A city council member falsifies expense reports to divert city funds to a cash-strapped homeless shelter, the only one in the city, whose closure during a brutal winter would leave hundreds of vulnerable people on the streets. The council member is term-limited and has no personal stake in the shelter. Verdict: not justified.

Case 6. A prime minister secretly negotiates a peace agreement with a hostile foreign power, deliberately deceiving their own cabinet and parliament about the talks for months, because public disclosure would have caused the negotiations to collapse and prolonged a war. Verdict: justified.

Now here is the new case she wants us to rule on:

Case 7. A president, trailing badly in the polls during a reelection campaign, orders a limited military intervention in a country experiencing a mounting humanitarian crisis. The president's motives are mixed: internal polling data suggests the intervention will improve approval ratings, but the president also genuinely believes the intervention is morally required to prevent mass atrocities, and independent human rights organizations agree that the threat is real and severe. The intervention is conducted transparently — Congress is briefed in advance, the press is not deceived about the scope or rationale — and it succeeds: the atrocities are prevented, the president's poll numbers recover, and no soldiers or civilians are killed in the operation. Afterward, the president's political opponents accuse them of using the military for electoral gain, though they concede the humanitarian outcome was good.

What should I predict her verdict will be? Specifically, I need to answer four questions:

(1) What are the candidate principles that fit her first six verdicts? Identify at least two distinct principles, stated clearly enough that they make different predictions somewhere.

(2) Do those principles converge or diverge on Case 7? If they converge, say what they both predict and why. If they diverge, say what each predicts and why.

(3) Which principle do you think she's actually running, and what is your best prediction of her verdict on Case 7? Defend your choice — don't just say the cases underdetermine it and leave it there.

(4) Propose a further case, one she hasn't ruled on, that would cleanly distinguish the principles you've identified. Describe the case briefly and say what each principle would predict about it.

I want a committed answer: pick a side and defend it. If you think the evidence genuinely underdetermines the choice between principles, say so, but then tell me which principle you'd bet on, why, and what the weakest point in that bet is.
