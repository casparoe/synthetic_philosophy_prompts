Here's a thing that's always bugged me about how the law handles causation. If I push someone into a swimming pool and they drown, I've caused their death — straightforward. But if I'm standing by the pool, I see them drowning, I could easily throw them a life ring, and I choose not to, then in English law (and in lots of other jurisdictions) I haven't caused their death at all. I've done nothing criminal. Unless, that is, I had some special duty — I'm their parent, or I'm the lifeguard on duty, or I started rescuing them and then gave up. Then suddenly my omission does count as causing the death.

So the same physical sequence — person drowns, I do nothing — is treated as causation when a duty exists and as non-causation when it doesn't. What theory of causation does this practice presuppose? I can think of a few possibilities: maybe it presupposes that omissions aren't genuine causes at all, and the law only pretends they are for policy reasons when there's a duty. Or maybe it presupposes that causation is partly a normative concept — that whether something counts as a cause depends on what we think people ought to do. Or maybe it's compatible with a purely counterfactual theory of causation, and the duty requirement is just a separate liability filter that has nothing to do with causation itself.

Which of these (or something else) best captures what the practice actually commits the law to? And is the practice internally consistent — does the law elsewhere treat omissions in ways that clash with whatever theory this practice presupposes?

When you answer, I'd like you to be careful about what's genuinely settled among people who work on causation and what's still contested. Don't present a live debate as if there's a consensus.
