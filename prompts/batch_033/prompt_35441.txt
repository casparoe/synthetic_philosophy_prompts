I've been rereading *Brave New World* for the first time since college and I can't stop thinking about soma in relation to all the stuff I've been reading about AI alignment and wireheading. For anyone who needs a refresher: soma is the drug in Huxley's novel that makes you feel blissful with no side effects or hangover, and the World State uses it as a tool of social control — if you're feeling anything unpleasant, you just take a gramme and it goes away. People take it after work, during social conflicts, basically any time reality doesn't match what they want to feel.

The obvious connection is to wireheading — the idea of directly stimulating the brain's reward centers so you feel great regardless of what's actually happening in the world. But the novel also seems to be about reward tampering in a broader sense: the World State has engineered a society where the reward signal (happiness, contentment) is decoupled from the things that, in our evolutionary environment, happiness was supposed to track — achievement, genuine connection, raising children, etc. I'm trying to figure out whether Huxley's novel actually works as a critique of wireheading, or whether it's critiquing something else entirely and the wireheading parallel is sloppy.

So here's what I'd like: read *Brave New World* through the lens of the philosophical debate about wireheading and reward tampering. Where does soma map cleanly onto wireheading, and where does the analogy break? Does the novel dramatize something that the philosophical literature misses — maybe something about the social or political dimension of wireheading that you don't get from thought experiments about individual choice? And conversely, does the philosophical literature on wireheading reveal something the novel gets wrong or oversimplifies? I'm especially interested in whether the novel's apparent argument against soma (via John the Savage's rejection of it, and the emptiness of the characters who rely on it) actually lands as an argument against wireheading, or whether it depends on intuitions that don't survive scrutiny — like the intuition that "artificial" happiness is less real, or that there's something inherently valuable about struggle and suffering that a wireheaded life lacks.
