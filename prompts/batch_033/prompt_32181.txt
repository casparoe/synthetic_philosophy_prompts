So I just spent like two hours arguing with a mate about Kripke and whether there really are necessary a posteriori truths, and honestly my brain is mush. But it reminded me of this argument I watched two people have at a philosophy meetup a while back, and I'm curious what you'd make of it. Here's the back-and-forth as best I remember it.

Miriam kicked things off by saying that Kripke was basically right: "water is H₂O" is a necessary truth we only know through experience. She said once we discovered water's chemical structure, we realised it couldnt have been otherwise — anything that isn't H₂O simply isn't water, full stop.

James pushed back. He said that's just stipulating. If the clear liquid in our lakes and taps had turned out to be XYZ, we'd say "well, water is XYZ after all" — not "oh, there's no water anywhere." So the necessity just tracks how we've decided to use the word, not some deep metaphysical fact.

Miriam said that was missing the point about rigid designation. "Water" picks out the actual substance around here. Since that substance is H₂O, the word refers to H₂O in every possible world. So "water is H₂O" is necessary — we just needed science to learn what water is.

James came back with the Twin Earth thought experiment. He said Putnam's intuition that XYZ isn't water is just one intuition — the Twin Earthlings have the opposite intuition and they're not obviously wrong. The whole setup, he said, just presupposes our way of using "water" gets to settle the question.

Miriam said it's not about privileging anyone, it's about how reference works. "Water" was introduced to refer to the natural kind in our environment. The Twin Earthlings' word refers to a different kind. Different word, different proposition.

James then said: so the necessity of "water is H₂O" depends on the contingent fact that we introduced the word around H₂O and not XYZ. But then the necessity itself is contingent — if history had gone differently, a different sentence would count as necessary. He said that's a pretty odd kind of necessity.

Miriam replied that this confuses sentences with propositions. The proposition that water is H₂O is necessarily true, period. The fact we might have used the sounds "w-a-t-e-r" for something else doesn't touch that.

James said the proposition is only available because of the actual reference-fixing. Strip away the linguistic decisions and what's left that's necessary? Just that H₂O is H₂O — which is trivial and not what anyone was arguing about.

They went round a few more times but that's the core of it. I'd love an adjudication — who's right about what, and is there something both of them are missing?
