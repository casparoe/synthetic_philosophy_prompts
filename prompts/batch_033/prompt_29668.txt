I've come to accept Moritz Schlick's view in his 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life" that the meaning of a life consists in play — not play in the sense of frivolity or leisure, but activity done for its own sake, which carries its purpose and value within itself rather than borrowing them from an external goal. On this view work becomes meaningful only insofar as it turns into play: it need not be pleasant, and it may be strenuous, but the doing itself must justify the effort, and the effects, however useful, are not where the meaning lies. Schlick couples this with the thought that looking for a "purpose" of life is already a mistake — the problem dissolves once one stops searching for a goal beyond activity and finds meaning in the present doing — and he ties the idea to youth, to childlike absorption, and to a joy he distinguishes from mere pleasure.

What I want is a map of what would count against this view. I don't mean the obvious objection that some lives look meaningful without being playful, or playful without being meaningful; I want the specific experiences, cases, and arguments that should move someone who actually holds the position, sorted by how decisive they are. Which lives does the view misdescribe, and which features of meaningful activity does it assimilate too cheaply? Does it secretly collapse into hedonism, or into a phenomenological mood rather than a substantive claim about what makes a life go well? Where does the play/work distinction get gerrymandered? And has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — every apparent counterexample reclassified as not really play, or as really play despite appearances — and if so, is that a defect for a view of this kind?

Structure your answer as follows:

1. The view and its commitments (roughly 150 words). State the position precisely, including the play/work distinction, the joy/pleasure distinction, and the claim that the search for a purpose of life is misguided.

2. Where it is exposed (roughly 200 words). Say what the view predicts about the texture of a meaningful life that rival accounts — purpose-given, achievement-based, relational — do not, and identify the kinds of cases that would test it.

Include at least one concrete kind of life or experience, and say which single consideration would be most persuasive to someone already sympathetic to the view.
