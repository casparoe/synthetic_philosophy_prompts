I run the quality lab at a small manufacturer that makes precision parts, and every quarter I have to sign off on how we validate our measurement equipment — calipers, micrometers, thermocouples in the heat-treat ovens, a coordinate measuring machine, hardness testers. The rule on the books says an instrument can only be checked against something more trustworthy than it is, but in practice the thing it's checked against got its own trustworthiness from the same family of instruments, or from a standards lab whose authority rests on the same kind of chain one level up. My techs keep bringing me the same two moves. One is: take twenty readings with the instrument, show they cluster tight, and declare it accurate — as if internal consistency proves it's tracking the true value. The other is: check instrument A against instrument B, then cite the fact that they agree as evidence that B is fine, even when A's status was the very thing in question. One engineer tells me this is textbook circularity and the measurements are worthless; another says all callibration is circular somewhere and the real question is just where you're willing to stop, so my job is to pick a stopping point and stop apologizing for it.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run when someone proposes a validation — something a competent tech with no epistemology background can run in under an hour on a single proposal. It should lay out the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure says "this is circular and doesn't count," where it says "this is circular but harmless," and where it has to stop and hand the call to a person because the remaining question is about risk tolerance or cost rather than anything a procedure can settle. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) validating a new digital caliper against an old micrometer that hasn't itself been checked in years but that everyone in the shop trusts; (2) validating a thermocouple by comparing it to a second thermocouple from the same batch, on the grounds that if they agree they're probably both fine; (3) validating the CMM using gauge blocks whose calibration certificate expired but which are treated like heirlooms. Make sure at least one case comes out in a way I won't like. Start with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one-sentence version of the procedure — and then give the reasoning, no preamble, no restating the question. Also say how confident you are in the main claims and single out the point you're least sure of.
