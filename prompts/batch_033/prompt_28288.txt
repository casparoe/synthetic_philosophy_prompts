So I was at a dinner thing last week and two friends got into it over the two-envelope paradox — you know, the one where there's two envelopes, one has twice as much money as the other, you pick one and then you're offered the chance to switch. The paradox is that it seems like you should always switch, but then by symmetry you'd switch back, which is nuts.

Here's roughly how it went:

Dan said the paradox shows that expected value reasoning breaks down when you don't have a well-defined prior. You can't just say the other envelope has a 50% chance of being double and 50% chance of being half — that move implicitly assumes a uniform distribution over all possible dollar amounts, which isn't a proper probability distribution.

Maya pushed back: she said the 50-50 isn't an assumption about how the envelopes were filled, it's about your epistemic situation. You picked one of two envelopes at random. So whatever amount you see, the other envelope is the one you didn't pick, and given the setup, it's equally likely to be the smaller or larger one. That's just from the random choice, not from any prior over amounts.

Dan said that can't be right because if you actually open the envelope and see, say, $10, you now have evidence about which envelope you picked. If the pair was ($5, $10) you'd see $10 half the time conditional on that pair; if it was ($10, $20) you'd see $10 half the time. But those two pairs aren't equally probable — that's where the prior comes in. The 50-50 breaks as soon as you condition on the observed amount.

Maya said okay, but what if you don't open the envelope? Then you have literally no information, and the symmetry argument says switching can't help. But the expected value calculation still seems to say switching is +EV. So the paradox survives even without opening. The problem isn't about conditioning on an amount — it's deeper.

Dan said no, if you don't open, the expected value of switching is actually zero, because the two possibilities (you have the smaller vs. larger) cancel. The apparent +EV comes from a sleight of hand where X means different things in the two branches of the calculation.

Maya said that's exactly her point — the sleight of hand is the real issue, not the prior. And the interesting question is why the sleight of hand is so compelling. She thinks it's because we're used to cases where the amount in your envelope is independent of whether you have the larger or smaller one, and here it's not: if you have the larger amount, the other envelope has half; if you have the smaller, the other has double. The "X" in the expected value formula is doing double duty.

At that point they realized they might actually agree more than they thought, but neither could quite articulate where the remaining disagreement was. I was lost by then.
