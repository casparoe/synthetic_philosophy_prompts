I have been reading Epicurus’ Letter to Menoeceus in translation, and there is a stretch of argument that I find much harder than it first appears. The passage runs:

"Accustom yourself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply awareness, and death is the privation of all awareness; therefore a right understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life an unlimited time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality."

A little later he continues:

"Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not. It is nothing, then, either to the living or to the dead, for with the living it is not and the dead exist no longer."

I understand the surface claim, but I do not understand the actual joints of the reasoning. In the first sentence, he seems to be making a psychological or therapeutic point — we should train ourselves to believe something — but then the second passage is phrased as a strict consequence: when death arrives, we no longer exist, so death is "not" for us. What exactly does "nothing to us" mean here — is it a claim about harm, about subjective awareness, about value, or about all three? And what suppressed premises are needed to get from the temporal fact that death and we do not overlap to the evaluative conclusion that death is not an evil? I would like help reconstructing the argument step by step, distinguishing what Epicurus is entitled to conclude from what he is merely recommending as a therapeutic practice, and saying whether the two passages are doing the same work or different work.
