Two members of my department's ethics reading group got into an extended argument last week about the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and neither would concede. I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move.

Here's roughly how it went.

[A] opened by saying that hedonism about well-being — the view that pleasure is the only intrinsic good — faces a basic problem: pleasures are so diverse that it's unclear what, if anything, they all share. The pleasure of a warm bath, the pleasure of solving a proof, the pleasure of schadenfreude, the pleasure of listening to music, the pleasure of eating — these feel nothing alike phenomenologically. If there's no common property, then calling them all "pleasures" is just a labeling trick, and hedonism hasn't earned the right to treat them as a unified category of value.

[B] replied that this is a familiar objection but it's overblown. There is a common property: all pleasures are mental states that the subject desires to be in, or would desire to be in under the right conditions. That's the unifying thread. The heterogeneity of how they feel is irrelevant — what matters is the functional role they play in our psychology.

[A] pushed back: that's not hedonism anymore, or at least not the kind that made the view attractive in the first place. If the common property is just "being desired," then why call the view hedonism rather than a desire-satisfaction theory? The original appeal of hedonism was that pleasure feels good in a way that's intrinsically and obviously valuable. You've kept the label but swapped out the content.

[B] said that's too quick. Even on the desire-based account, what makes a state a pleasure is that it's a state you want to be in for its own sake, not for some further consequence — and that's still a distinctive, unified kind of psychological relation. The attitudinal theory of pleasure holds exactly this: pleasure is an attitude of pro-attitude toward an occurrent state. It doesn't require a uniform phenomenological "feel." And this is still recognizably hedonistic — it says the good is constituted by a certain kind of positive stance toward one's experience.

[A] responded that this just relocates the heterogeneity problem. If pleasure is a pro-attitude, then we need to ask: what do all pro-attitudes have in common? The pro-attitude I take toward the taste of coffee and the pro-attitude I take toward finishing a difficult project seem just as heterogeneous as the original pleasures were. You've replaced one diverse category with another and declared victory by fiat.

[B] countered that this misses the point of the attitudinal view. The view doesn't claim that pro-attitudes share a further phenomenological property — it claims that the unity is at the level of the attitude itself: all pleasures are instances of the same kind of mental act, namely taking a favorable stance toward some object. The diversity of the objects explains the diversity of how the pleasures feel, but the attitude type is the same. That's like saying all beliefs are the same kind of mental act — asserting something as true — even though believing different propositions feels different.

At this point [A] said the analogy to belief actually cuts the other way. Beliefs are unified by their content — they all aim at truth — not by some introspectible "belief-ish feel." But the attitudinal hedonist can't say pleasures aim at truth or any other single standard of correctness. A pro-attitude toward a good meal and a pro-attitude toward a beautiful proof don't share a common aim in the way beliefs share truth. So the analogy doesn't secure the unity you need.

The discussion ended there with both of them frustrated. I'd like you to go through each move and assess who has the better of it at each stage — not just who wins overall, but where each argument lands and what both parties might be missing. Please assign yourself the role of a philosopher working in ethics or philosophy of mind, take a moment to map out the logical structure of the exchange and identify the key claims at stake, and then render a point-by-point verdict.
