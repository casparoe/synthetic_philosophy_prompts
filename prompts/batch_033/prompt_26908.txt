I've been attending a philosophy reading group at my company — mostly engineers trying to broaden our horizons — and we recently worked through the rule-following paradox from Kripke's book on Wittgenstein. I have to admit I'm struggling to see why this is treated as a deep problem rather than something straightforward.

As I understand it, the skeptical challenge goes like this: suppose I've never added any numbers larger than 57. My past behavior is consistent with my having meant ordinary addition, but it's equally consistent with my having meant "quaddition," defined as x ⊕ y = x + y when x and y are both below 57, and x ⊕ y = 5 otherwise. Since all my past uses are compatible with both interpretations, the skeptic says there's no fact about me that determines which one I really meant. But from where I sit, this just looks like the standard problem of fitting a function to a finite set of data points — something we deal with all the time in software. You can't uniquely pin down a function from any finite number of input-output pairs; that's exactly why we write explicit algorithms instead of just providing test cases. The real specification of addition isn't my history of past outputs, it's the algorithm I've learned — something like the recursive definition of addition on the natural numbers. Once you have the algorithm, it determines every output, including for inputs I've never encountered before.

So here's what I don't get: why isn't the answer to the paradox simply "the algorithm, not the behavioral history, is what fixes meaning"? And if the response is that the algorithm itself faces the same skeptical regress (how do I know I was following that algorithm rather than some deviant variant?), then doesn't the whole thing just collapse into a generic skepticism about whether anything determines anything — not a special problem about meaning or rules at all? I feel like I'm missing the move that makes this a genuinely hard philosophical puzzle rather than a dressed-up version of "you can't extrapolate from finite samples with certainty." What am I not seeing?
