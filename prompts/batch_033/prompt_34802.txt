There's an analogy that keeps showing up in arguments about when AI assistants should refuse or quietly override a user's request, and it's started to bug me because everyone reaches for it and noone actually stress-tests it. The analogy is the bartender. Someone points out that an assistant might be asked for help with something self-destructive, say drafting the message that ends a marriage, or a plan to go off their meds without telling anyone, or help hiding a gambling habit from a spouse. And the reply is always: a good bartender doesn't just give the customer what they ask for. If the customer is visibly drunk, the bartender cuts them off, refuses to serve, maybe calls a cab, and overrides the customer's immediate preference because the bartender is better placed to see the harm coming and the customer's judgment is the very thing that's impaired. So the assistant is like the bartender, and refusing or pushing back is just ordinary professional judgment rather than objectionable paternalism or censorship. The analogy is doing a lot of work in that argument, and I want someone to actually audit it the way a LessWrong thread would audit a bad analogy, rather than gesture at it.

What I'd like is a proper mapping: what plays the role of the patron, the bartender, the intoxication, the cut-off, the bar's licence and legal exposure, the hangover the next morning, the fact that a bartender only ever sees you in one narrow context, whatever else seems load-bearing. Then I want the places the mapping breaks, split into the disanalogies that actually matter for the intended conclusion and the ones that are just surface. And then the bottom line: does the conclusion that assistants should sometimes refuse or override a request for the user's own good survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the bartender carrying the whole argument? If there's a better analogy (a GP, a lawyer, a friend, a sober companion, whatever) say what it would license that the bartender doesn't.

Please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables. I know a correspondence table is the obvious move here, so do the mapping in prose instead. And British English throughout, please.
