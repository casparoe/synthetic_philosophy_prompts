I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been working through the computationalism literature with a growing sense that the debate has been running in place for decades. One camp holds that cognition is computation over syntactically structured mental representations; another replies that computational descriptions are not intrinsic to physical systems and that, on permissive readings, nearly any physical system implements nearly any computation; a third camp tries to salvage the claim with a mechanistic account on which a system genuinely computes only when its states are manipulated according to a rule defined over representation-like vehicles. The parties seem to accept the same neurophysiological findings and even the same formal results, yet none of them moves.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not empirical but conceptual: the sides are using different success conditions for the claim that the brain is a computer — one requiring contentful representations, another requiring an objective mapping from physical states to computational states, another requiring only that a computational model earn its keep as an explanation at some level. Is that right? Or is there a hidden empirical question in the background (for instance, whether neural dynamics are stable and vehicle-invariant enough to satisfy the mechanistic account), a hidden formal question (what constraints would block trivial implementations), or a difference about what counts as an explanation in cognitive science in the first place?

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a survey or a verdict. Is it verbal, substantive but about standards of explanation, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a reframing of the question — or should we stop expecting the impasse to resolve? Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would push you toward a different diagnosis, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
