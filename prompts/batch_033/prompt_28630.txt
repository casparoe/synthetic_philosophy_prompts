I'm a PhD student in philosophy presenting a paper at a workshop hosted by a centre for legal theory. My thesis is that causation by omission is not genuine causation: when we say that a lifeguard's failure to rescue caused a swimmer's death, or that a doctor's failure to prescribe antibiotics caused a patient's deterioration, we are making a normative judgement about responsibility, not reporting a causal fact. I draw on the metaphysics of causation — particularly the contrast between difference-making accounts and production or mechanism-based accounts — to argue that omissions lack the features that make something a cause in the world, and that the law's reliance on causation by omission is better understood as a disguised form of responsibility attribution. The audience will be primarily legal philosophers and tort law scholars whose work depends on omissions counting as causes for liability. A few metaphysicians of causation will also be present, some sympathetic to my view and some not. I need a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I am likely to receive, ordered by how damaging they are, phrased as a legal theorist or metaphysician would phrase them, with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
