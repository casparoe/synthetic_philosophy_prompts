So in my epistemology seminar we spent two weeks on Nozick's tracking theory and the closure debate, and two of my coursemates got into this argument after class that I've been turning over ever since. They were going back and forth about whether Nozick's denial of epistemic closure is actually a problem for his view or not. I was kind of taking notes because it seemed like neither of them was obviously wrong at any point. Here's roughly how it went:

James started by saying that epistemic closure is non-negotiable — if you know that P, and you know that P entails Q, then you're in a position to know Q just by deducing it. If a theory of knowledge says otherwise, so much the worse for the theory. He said Nozick basically admits his tracking conditions force you to give up closure, and that alone should disqualify the theory.

Mira pushed back and said that's question begging. She said the whole interest of Nozick's view is that it gives us a principled explanation of why closure fails in exactly the cases where it seems most questionable — like the brain-in-a-vat case. You know you have hands because your belief tracks that fact. You know that having hands entails you're not a handless brain in a vat. But you dont know you're not a handless brain in a vat, because if you were one, you'd still believe you weren't. The tracking condition fails for the BIV proposition, and that's exactly what we should want a theory to say.

James replied that this is just dressing up a bullet to look like a feature. He said it gets worse: on Nozick's view, you could know P, know P entails Q, competently deduce Q from P, and yet still fail to know Q. He said that's not just counterintuitive — it makes a mockery of deduction as a way of extending knowledge.

Mira said that's only a problem if you think deduction always transmits knowledge, and we should be open to the possibility that it doesn't when the relevant tracking conditions diverge. She brought up the fake barn case — you look at the one real barn in a field of fakes and form the belief "there's a barn." Your belief is true and you used a reliable method, but intuitively you don't know. She said this shows that knowledge is sensitive to features of the situation that go beyond what you can deduce from your evidence, and once you accept that, closure isn't so sacred.

James said fake barns are a problem for lots of theories, but you don't need to ditch closure to handle them. A safety condition — where your belief has to be such that it couldn't easily have been false — handles fake barns whilst keeping closure intact. So Nozick's sensitivity condition isn't the only game in town, and it's the one that pays the highest price.

Mira said safety has its own problems — it's too permissive in some cases and too demanding in others — and anyway, the point isn't that Nozick's theory is flawless, it's that denying closure isn't the automatic disqualifier James thinks it is. It might be the right price to pay.
