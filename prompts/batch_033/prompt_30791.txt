I've been in a reading group on indicative conditionals for a year and we're exactly where we started, and I want someone to diagnose the impasse rather than sell me a side. Everyone in the room accepts Adams's thesis, at least as a datum: the probability of "If it rained, the match was cancelled" tracks P(cancelled | rain), not the probability of the material conditional ¬rain ∨ cancelled. And everyone accepts Lewis's triviality results: no proposition can have, for all probability functions, a probability equal to the corresponding conditional probability, on pain of the probability function collapsing into something trivial. So the group splits.

The truth-conditional people say conditionals do express propositions — either the material conditional, with Adams's thesis demoted to a story about assertability or implicature, or a Stalnaker-style closest-world proposition, with Adams's thesis holding only under restrictions. The suppositional people say Adams's thesis is the one thing we shouldn't give up, so conditionals don't express propositions at all; "if" is a device for evaluating the consequent under a supposition. But then they have to say something about "It's not the case that if A then C" and "If the butler did it, then if the gardener is lying, the knife is in the shed," and their answers always sound hand-wavy to the other side. My working theory is that this isn't one disagreement: it's partly a verbal dispute about what "meaning" requires (truth conditions or use-rules), partly a disagreement about which data are load-bearing (bare probability judgements vs. embedded uses), and maybe partly a real empirical question about what speakers are tracking when they accept or reject conditionals. Is that diagnosis right? And given it, what would progress even look like — a formal loophole in the triviality results, a new class of linguistic data, or just dropping the question as ill-posed? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions.
