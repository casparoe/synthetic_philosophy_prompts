I just spent an evening arguing with a friend who insists that evolutionary psychology is little more than a collection of just-so stories. I pushed back, but I found I could not state the rule behind my own reactions. Here are my verdicts on several specific claims, and I would like to know what methodological principle, if any, captures them.

I find the claim that humans are biologically prepared to acquire fears of snakes and spiders convincing: people condition to them faster and extinguish more slowly than to objects that are far more dangerous in modern life, such as guns and electrical outlets. That looks to me like a real evolved bias.

I find the claim that people prefer savanna-like landscapes because our ancestors evolved in such environments close to a just-so story: the preference data are mixed, and "ancestral environment" is too vague to yield a definite prediction. Yet I cannot say why this differs from the snake case, since both infer an evolved disposition from present-day responses and then invoke an ancestral environment.

I accept the finding that stepparents harm children at higher average rates than genetic parents, and I am inclined to accept something like discriminative parental solicitude as the explanation, though I am unsure the evolutionary gloss adds much beyond the proximate account.

I am torn about male sexual jealousy as an evolved response to paternity uncertainty. The rationale is elegant, but the evidence feels thin: much of it is self-report, and the cross-cultural variation is what one would expect if the phenomenon were largely cultural.

I find the cheater-detection claim, that people solve Wason selection tasks much better when the rule concerns catching violators of a social contract, persuasive as evidence of a social-reasoning specialization, but not persuasive as evidence of a dedicated cheater-detection module as opposed to a broader sensitivity to deontic rules.

I find the claim that depression is an adaptation, for instance that it serves analytical rumination, nearly unfalsifiable, since it seems able to accommodate both the presence and the absence of any pattern.

Please state the principle precisely, show how it handles each case above, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether those verdicts should go. Then apply the principle to a case I have not listed: the claim that incest avoidance is an evolved mechanism rather than a byproduct of childhood co-residence. Also, please separate what is methodologically settled from what is genuinely contested among researchers in this area, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. I would like the response in American English.
