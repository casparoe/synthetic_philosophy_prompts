I'm a retired atmospheric physicist — did climate modeling for 35 years, now I potter around reading philosophy and somehow ended up on my regional water authority's long-range capital planning committee. We're supposed to recommend a standard discount rate for appraising infrastructure projects with 50-to-100-year horizons: reservoirs, flood barriers, big treatment plants, that sort of thing. The economists on the committee keep pushing for 4 or 5 percent, which is roughly the private-sector cost of capital and also roughly tracks how people actually trade off consumption now versus later in their own lives. Their line is basically: if we use a lower rate, we're blessing inefficient projects that tie up money that could've earned better returns elsewhere. But here's the snag. A 4 percent discount rate means a dollar of benefit in 2100 is worth about two cents today. So if a flood protection project costs $100 million now and prevents $5 billion in damages in 2100, it looks marginal or even lousy on net-present-value terms. That just seems... off? I've been reading around — Parfit, some of the climate-economics stuff like the Stern/Nordhaus debate — and it seems to me that the core philosophical question is whether pure time preference is defensible at all when you're talking about different people rather than one person's life. I get that people discount their own future utility because they might die, or because of impatience, but why should we discount the welfare of some kid in 2080 just because she's temporally downstream from us? And even if you set pure time preference to zero, you still have to deal with the expectation that future people will be richer (so a dollar means less to them), which points to a positive discount rate anyway — but then you're betting on growth rates over a century, which feels like hubris given what I spent my career modeling.

Anyway, the committee chair has tasked me with drafting a short decision memo — three or four pages — that recommends a specific discount rate (or a range, or a deliberate process for selecting one) with the reasoning laid out, so the full committee can debate and vote on it next month. The memo has to be something the water authority's board could actually adopt as policy. And it needs to engage honestly with the economists' side, not just dismiss it. I've got my own leanings but I want the strongest version of the other case on the page too. Could you draft it?
