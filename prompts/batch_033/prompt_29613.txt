I'm a staff engineer on a team that maintains a small library of formally verified components — sorting and search routines, parsers, a scheduler — that sits underneath a safety-critical product, so the team lead has to sign off on every proof we merge. Part of my job is reviewing pull requests where a collegue claims 'this proof shows the function is total' or 'we've proved a witness exists.' We've been burnt a few times by proofs that a thing exists but don't actually hand us any way to find it: the proof goes through by assuming the thing doesn't exist and deriving a contradiction, and when we go to extract the algorithm there's just nothing there. I dimly remember this being the difference between classical and constructive (intuitionistic) logic, but I don't have the background to turn that into a review checklist.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for reviewing such proofs that a non-logician engineer can actually run in about twenty minutes per proof, once or twice a week, grounded in what intuitionistic logic and constructivism actually say about when a proof yields a witness. It should tell me what to look for to decide whether a proof is constructive enough that we can extract an algorithm, or at least a bound; which classical moves — excluded middle, double negation elimination, proof by contradiction, noncomputable choice, that sort of thing — are red flags and which are actually harmless in context (negative results, finite or decidable domains, etc.); which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped; where the procedure should stop and hand the proof to someone with real logic background; and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three worked cases: (1) a proof that any list can be sorted, done by assuming a minimal permutation exists and deriving a contradiction otherwise; (2) a proof that a particular parser never loops on malformed input; and (3) a termination proof for the scheduler that relies on a well-ordering with no computable description. I need at least one of these to come out with an answer the author won't like, and I want you to say plainly what the procedure can't settle.
