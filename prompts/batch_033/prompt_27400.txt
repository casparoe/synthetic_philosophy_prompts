I've been reading Pascal's Pensées, specifically the fragments on justice and force, and a friend reconstructed the argument he thinks underlies them. The reconstruction runs as follows.

(1) A rule of justice can actually govern a community only if it can be enforced; a justice that has no power behind it does not bind anyone in practice.

(2) Human beings cannot agree about what justice requires. What one party regards as just, another regards as arbitrary, and there is no shared criterion by which their dispute could be settled.

(3) Force, unlike justice, is recognized by everyone and settles disputes without needing to be interpreted: its verdict is plain, and no one argues with it for long.

(4) Therefore the rules that actually govern any society are the rules backed by force, not the rules recommended by justice. Since we cannot make what is just prevail by force, we make what prevails by force count as just.

(5) So the only sense in which an enforced rule is "just" is that it is enforced by the stronger party. Justice has no content independently of what force happens to uphold.

(6) But this conclusion seems to prove too much. If it were right, calling a law unjust could only mean "the law is not backed by my side," and a sufficiently stable tyranny would be no less just than a legitimate order, which is precisely the thing the argument was supposed to explain away.

I can't accept (6), and yet each step seemed forced on me by the one before. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I'd like to know whether the argument goes wrong in its logic, or whether it is valid and the real choice is which of the plausible premises to give up.
