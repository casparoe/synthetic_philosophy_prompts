You are a professional philosopher working in the philosophy of language, with a background in Wittgenstein and metasemantics. Please think carefully, and before you settle on a final structure, spend some time brainstorming distinct kinds of considerations that could bear on the question.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend about Kripkenstein's rule-following problem, and I found myself defending the skeptical position more thoroughly than I had expected to. The view I ended up defending was this: there is no fact of the matter — not in my past behavior, my mental images, my dispositions, or my occurrent states — that determines whether by the word plus I meant addition rather than a deviant function, call it quus, that agrees with addition on every case I have so far considered and diverges on some case I have not yet considered. And the right response is not to keep looking for the fact that constitutes meaning, but to adopt a skeptical solution: meaning ascriptions do not state truth conditions but rather mark when a speaker is entitled, by the standards of a linguistic community, to use an expression as they do.

I do not want you to elaborate or defend that view. I want you to tell me what would count against it. What would have to be true for my friend's side — that there is a fact that makes it true that I meant plus and not quus — to be correct after all? What observations, arguments, formal results, or developments in adjacent areas of philosophy or cognitive science would show that the paradox rests on a bad assumption, or that the skeptical solution is false or collapses into something weaker? I can already see a few directions: a sufficiently sophisticated dispositionalist, causal, or teleosemantic account that claims to single out addition; the charge that the paradox overgeneralizes and would dissolve facts of every kind, not just semantic ones; the objection that the skeptical solution cannot explain first-person authority or the difference between being correct and merely being in step with one's community; and the worry that the view ends up as behaviorism or as communitarian relativism about meaning. I want you to assess those and to identify the ones I have missed.

For each defeater, say whether it would be fatal, seriously damaging, or merely awkward, and rank them from fatal to merely uncomfortable. Be clear about which of these a committed Kripkensteinian would themselves concede as counting against the view, and which they would dismiss as not really engaging it. Then give an honest overall assessment: is the view well insulated, or is it protected only by qualifications that drain it of content — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind? Finally, I would find it useful if you separated the parts of the view that are empirical or psychological from the parts that are metaphysical and the parts that are quietly normative.
