I am a retired natural scientist—a chemist by training—who has recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest, and I keep returning to a question I once would have answered without hesitation: do the sciences carve nature at its joints, or do our classifications partly impose the structure they claim to find? I would like to commission a particular kind of document from you.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two positions in the natural kinds debate. Name them as you think most accurate, but the two parties I have in mind are, on one side, a natural-kind realist, who holds that at least some scientific categories track mind-independent divisions in nature and that among the many possible classifications of a domain some are objectively closer to those divisions than others; and, on the other side, a promiscuous realist or pluralist, who holds that a domain typically admits many equally legitimate, cross-cutting classifications and that which kinds we recognize is ineliminably shaped by our interests and purposes. The statement must be one that a thoughtful, good-faith advocate of each position could actually sign: not a lowest-common-denominator compromise, not a document in which one side's view is silently reworded into the other's, and not a premature verdict on who is right.

The document should have two parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than vacuous, and they should cover at least the following: the difference between a merely convenient classification and a natural kind; the status of the chemical elements, where classification has been unusually stable; the fact that biological taxonomy cross-cuts in ways chemistry mostly does not; what the metaphor of carving at the joints can and cannot be asked to do; and which strands of the dispute, if any, are merely verbal. Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state the precise point at issue, then give the realist's formulation of the realist's own view and the pluralist's formulation of the pluralist's own view, each worded as its own side would accept, and a note on whether the disagreement survives that reformulation.

Then, in a separate section, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer to them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case will show when examined closely. I expect the cases to include at least the following, though you may add others: the classification of the chemical elements by atomic number; the species problem in biology, with its competing species concepts; and one cross-cutting classification from a domain such as psychiatry, soil science, or ecology where multiple schemes coexist. For each case, do not adjudicate it; the point is to make the residual disagreement exact and to specify what would count as evidence against each side's view.

Throughout, avoid relying on citations or bibliography; the document should stand on the arguments themselves. Where you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal—for example, that the two sides mean different things by mind-independent or objective—say exactly which part and propose the reformulation under which it dissolves. And if some proposed crux turns out not to bear on the disagreement after all, say so and drop it.
