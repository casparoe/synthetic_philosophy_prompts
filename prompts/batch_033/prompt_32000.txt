I'm a retired neuroethologist — spent most of my career recording from invertebrate ganglia, looking at how sensory inputs map onto motor outputs in things like crickets, mantises, the occasional cockroach. Since retiring I've been working through the free will literature, and I keep getting tangled up on this notion of "sphexishness" that Dennett and Hofstadter made a whole thing about back in the day. You probably know the example: the Sphex wasp drags a cricket to the entrance of its burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect the tunnel, comes back out, and pulls the cricket in. But if some meddling ethologist moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is down in the burrow inspecting — the wasp comes out, dutifully drags the cricket back to the entrance, and then goes inside to inspect *again*. And you can loop this indefinitely. It never occurs to the wasp to skip the inspection step; the program just resets.

Here's the thing that bugs me. The standard compatibilist response to sphexishness goes something like: sure, but humans aren't rigid like that — we have metacognition, we notice when routines stop paying off, we can step back and revise our policies. We're flexible in ways the wasp simply isn't, and that flexibility is what matters for free will. But after forty years of poking around in neural circuits, I just don't see how "more complex mechanism" gets you to "not a mechanism." The behaviors I studied that looked most flexible — prey discrimination, courtship decisions — turned out to be implemented by circuits that were, at bottom, every bit as determinsitic as the Sphex's. More modulatory inputs, more recurrent loops, richer internal state, absolutely. But still a physical system doing what physical systems do given their structure and inputs.

So here's the argument I want to see made properly: the sphexishness challenge to free will isn't really about whether an organism can detect that its routine has been derailed and switch strategies. That's just a higher-order version of the same underlying problem. The real challenge is whether *anything* can act for reasons that aren't themselves the product of blind causal processes — and saying "yes, because some causal processes involve reasons-responsive mechanisms" just relabels the problem without actually dissolving it. I want the case that compatibilism is doing a kind of bait-and-switch here, and that the sphexishness worry survives the "but humans are flexible" response more or less intact.
