I'm a philosophy professor putting together a syllabus on religion as a natural phenomenon — the kind of course where you treat religious belief and practice as something to be explained rather than evaluated — and I keep tripping over a pattern I don't have a tidy name for.

Here's what I mean. When you survey the religious concepts that actually catch on and travel — the ones ordinary people remember and retell, not the stuff that lives only in commentary traditions — they almost all have the same structure: they take something totally mundane and flip exactly one or two of your intuitive expectations about it, leaving the rest alone. A ghost is a person who lacks a physical body but still has desires, memories, and a location. A talking snake is a snake that happens to have language. A mountain that's also a god is a big rock with agency and intentions. Water that cures disease is water plus a causal power it shouldn't have. These are all ordinary ontological categories with a single property swapped out. Meanwhile the really baroque representations — the deities with eleven dimensions and no location who are somehow also three persons and a dove — either stay bottled up in theology departments or don't make it past the second generation of oral transmission. The pattern seems real, and I know the cognitive science of religion people must have noticed it, but I'm not sure what they call it or whether it's considered a distinct phenomenon at all.

So my question: is there an established term for this "minimal violation" structure of successful religious concepts? Is it just a special case of something broader about memory — like the von Restorff effect, where the one odd item in a list pops out — or does it have its own name and its own proposed mechanism? And if there is a name, what are the edge cases: how many violations are too many before a concept stops being transmissable, and does it matter which domain of intuitive knowledge you're messing with (folk physics versus folk biology versus folk psychology)? I'd appreciate the answer in American English since most of my students are from the States and I want the terminology to match what they'd find in the literature.
