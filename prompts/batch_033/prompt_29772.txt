I've become convinced that self-deception in the strict sense is impossible. Not that people don't do things we casually call self-deception — obviously they do — but that the thing itself, literally deceiving yourself into believing something you know isn't true, can't happen. The paradox is old: if you know P is false, you can't just decide to believe P, because belief isn't voluntary like that. And if you succeed in believing P, you no longer know it's false, so there's no deception — you're just wrong. What we call self-deception is better described as motivated reasoning, biased evidence-gathering, wishful thinking, or plain old akrasia about following through on what you know. None of these require the person to simultanously hold P and not-P in the way genuine deception would.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not just the obvious objection that it flies in the face of common sense — I already know that one. I want the best cases, the best arguments, the best experimental results that a defender of genuine self-deception would throw at me. Which of these would a committed impossibilist concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as misdescribed? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. And I want an honest answer to whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — if every apparent case of self-deception gets redescribed as something else by definition, is the view saying anything at all, or is it just a linguistic preference?
