I'm running a reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and we're about to spend three sessions on the Everettian many-worlds interpretation. The sticking point, as always, is the Born rule problem: if the universal wavefunction just unitarily evolves and every possible outcome is realized in some branch, where do the probabilities come from? Why should we expect to observe frequencies that match |ψ|² rather than, say, just counting branches equally? The group has a mix of physics graduate students and a couple of philosophers, and I want to give them something they can work through together that doesn't presuppose they already know the landscape.

I'd like you to construct a decision tree or flowchart that takes someone from the starting commitment — "I accept that the universal wavefunction evolves deterministically via the Schrödinger equation, and decoherence produces an emergent branching structure" — all the way to a named position on the Born rule problem. Please approach this as a professional philosopher of physics would: build the tree from the logical space of possible moves rather than from a list of names. First map out the key decision points where views genuinely come apart, then locate the actual defended positions within that space. Flag any logically possible combinations that nobody (or almost nobody) defends, with a brief note on why — is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

The leaves should include at least: the decision-theoretic approach (Deutsch, Wallace, Saunders), the self-locating uncertainty approach (Vaidman, Greaves' caring measure, and any important variants), the primitivist view that treats the Born rule as an additional postulate not derived from the MWI framework, Zurek's envariance-based derivation, the typicality or frequentist approach, and the skeptical view that MWI cannot recover probability and therefore fails as an interpretation. Where there are important sub-variants within any of these — for instance, within the decision-theoretic camp, or within self-locating uncertainty — the tree should separate them if it can and note where it cannot.

The tree should also explicitly address the branch-counting challenge — why not assign equal probability to every branch? — since different positions answer this differently, and I want the tree to make those differences visible. Please think through the logical structure carefully before laying out the tree: start by identifying the fundamental choice points, then build the branching structure, and finally populate the leaves.
