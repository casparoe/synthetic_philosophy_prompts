"Explain Williamson's anti-luminosity argument. Then discuss the relationship between the anti-luminosity argument and the rejection of the KK principle: does the former entail the latter, or are they independent theses? Be as precise as possible."

Here is the student's answer:

"Williamson defines a condition C as luminous just in case: whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. His anti-luminosity argument is meant to show that no non-trivial condition is luminous. The argument proceeds by considering a subject who undergoes a very gradual change — for instance, slowly transitioning from feeling cold to feeling warm as the temperature rises in imperceptible increments. Suppose, for reductio, that feeling cold were luminous. Then at each moment when the subject feels cold, she is in a position to know she feels cold. But knowledge requires a margin for error: if one knows that p, then p must hold not only in one's actual situation but also in all sufficiently similar situations. As the subject's thermal sensations change in tiny, subjectively indiscriminable steps, there will come a point where she no longer feels cold. Because the change is gradual, there must be a last moment at which she feels cold and a first moment at which she does not. At that last moment, by the luminosity assumption, she is in a position to know she feels cold. By the margin-for-error principle, this knowledge would require that she still feels cold at the next moment too — but by hypothesis she does not. Contradiction. So feeling cold cannot be luminous, and the same reasoning generalizes to any condition that can vary along a continuum.

Now, regarding the KK principle: the KK principle states that if a subject knows that p, then she is in a position to know that she knows that p. Williamson's anti-luminosity argument directly entails the falsity of the KK principle. This is because knowledge itself is a condition, and the anti-luminosity argument shows that no condition is luminous. Since knowledge is not luminous, it is not the case that whenever someone knows p, she is in a position to know that she knows p — which is exactly what the KK principle asserts. Thus, the anti-luminosity argument and the rejection of KK are not independent: the former entails the latter. In fact, one could say that the KK principle is just a special case of the luminosity claim, with 'knowing that p' plugged in for the condition C. The anti-luminosity argument therefore refutes KK automatically."
