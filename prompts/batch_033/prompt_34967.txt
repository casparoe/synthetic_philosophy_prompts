Two friends of mine got into an argument about nuclear deterrence at a bar last weekend, and I'm still not sure who was right. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Mike said the whole concept of nuclear deterrence is logically broken. The threat to launch a retaliatory strike after you've already been attacked is a threat you'd never rationally carry out — you'd be destroying the planet for no reason except revenge, since the initial attack already happened. And if the other side knows you're rational, they know you're bluffing. So the threat isn't credible, and deterrence shouldn't work.

(2) Sarah pushed back. She said that's exactly why we build commitment devices — launch-on-warning protocols, submarines that can't be reached, chain of command arrangements that make it hard to back down. The whole point of deterrence theory, she said, is to make the threat credible by tying your own hands in advance, like Odysseus and the sirens.

(3) Mike said that's just theater. The president can always override launch-on-warning; sub commanders can choose not to fire; the system is full of human judgment. And if you actually did remove the human override — built a literal doomsday machine that automatically retaliates — that would be insane, because a false alarm would end the world. So you're stuck: either the commitment is fake, or it's real but suicidal.

(4) Sarah said he was treating credibility as all-or-nothing, which is a mistake. She brought up Schelling's idea that you don't need to be certain to retaliate; you just need the other side to think there's a non-negligible chance you might, whether through accident, loss of control, or a leader who goes off-script. A 10% chance of annihilation is still a deterrent, so perfect credibility isn't required.

(5) Mike replied that this just moves the problem — now you're relying on the other side fearing you might be irrational or lose control, which means deterrence works only insofar as you're not a perfectly rational actor. But then the whole edifice of strategic rationality that deterrence theorists built is just a fancy rationalization of the fact that we're all scared of each other's unpredictable craziness.

(6) Sarah said that's not a bug, it's a feature. The point of brinkmanship is precisely to manipulate risk — to deliberately create situations where things could slip out of control, so that the adversary is forced to back down rather than gamble. That's rational strategy, not irrationality.

At this point they ordered another round and I changed the subject because my head was spinning. Can you walk through each of their moves (1 through 6) and tell me who's got the better argument at each step, and who's more right overall? Are there important points neither of them made?
