**What the Cogito Actually Proves**

The *cogito* is routinely summarised as the claim that because I am thinking, I must exist. Descartes himself presents it as a first certainty, an Archimedean point from which the rest of knowledge can be reconstructed. But the interpretive consensus that has built up around the *cogito* over the past several decades suggests that the argument is far less straightforward than the slogan makes it sound, and that what Descartes takes himself to have proved and what the argument actually licenses may come apart in philosophically instructive ways.

The standard presentation of the argument runs something like this: even if an evil demon is deceiving me about everything else, it cannot deceive me about the fact that I am thinking, because the very act of being deceived is itself a form of thinking; and if I am thinking, then I must exist as the thing that does the thinking. But this formulation already smuggles in more than the reasoning can bear. The move from "there is thinking occurring" to "I exist as a thinking thing" is not a single step but several, and each of them deserves scrutiny.

First, there is the question of the "I". What exactly is the referent of that pronoun when Descartes concludes *sum*? The argument, as usually reconstructed, shows at most that there is thinking going on. It does not by itself show that this thinking belongs to a persisting subject, nor that the subject is a substance in anything like the scholastic or Cartesian sense. Lichtenberg's famous objection—that Descartes was entitled only to "there is thinking" rather than "I think"—presses precisely this point. The *cogito* establishes the existence of an episode of thinking, but the inference from an episode of thinking to a thinker is substantive and not obviously licensed by the indubitability of the premise.

Second, there is the temporal scope of the conclusion. Descartes needs the *cogito* to deliver a persisting self, because he goes on to construct arguments for God's existence that take time to run through, and he needs a self that lasts long enough to do the reasoning. But the *cogito* as stated in the Second Meditation seems to deliver only a momentary certainty: "I am, I exist, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind." The phrase "whenever" is doing considerable work here. It suggests that the *cogito* proves the existence of a self only at the instant of thinking, and that the proof must be renewed moment by moment. This is a peculiar kind of foundation for knowledge—one that is constantly lapsing and must be constantly re-established. Some interpreters have argued that this is why Descartes needs the proofs of God's existence so urgently: only a non-deceiving God can underwrite the reliability of memory and thereby bridge the gaps between successive *cogito* moments.

Third, there is the question of what kind of thing the *cogito* actually is. Descartes sometimes treats it as an inference—a syllogism whose suppressed major premise is "whatever thinks exists"—but at other times he insists that it is a direct intuition, something grasped in a single mental act rather than arrived at through reasoning. The tension matters because if the *cogito* is an inference, then it depends on the suppressed premise, and that premise is something the evil demon could in principle be deceiving me about. If it is an intuition, then it is less clear how it can serve as a model for the rest of the Meditations, which proceeds by explicit argumentation. The *cogito* may be less a foundation for a system of knowledge than a special case that cannot be generalised.

What, then, does the *cogito* actually prove? It proves that the act of doubting one's own existence is self-undermining: the very attempt to doubt that one is thinking confirms that there is thinking. But it does not prove that I am a thinking substance, that I persist through time, or that I am the unified subject of my thoughts. Those are further claims that require further arguments, and the fact that Descartes treats them as if they follow directly from the *cogito* may be the most consequential philosophical mistake in the Meditations—or, alternatively, the most productive ambiguity, since it is precisely the gap between what the *cogito* proves and what Descartes needs it to prove that generates the rest of the Cartesian project.
