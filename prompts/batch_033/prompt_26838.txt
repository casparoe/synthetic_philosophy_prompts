I am a novelist, and I am researching a novel about a fatal collision caused by a Level 4 autonomous delivery van. In the scene I am drafting, the van's perception and control stack is trained end-to-end; after it strikes and kills a cyclist, the company's engineers can say which inputs the network received and which outputs it produced, but they cannot offer a satisfying explanation of why it classified the scene as it did or why it selected the maneuver that killed her. While I was describing the scene to two friends, they began arguing about where the moral responsibility lies, and I could not referee them. The first, an engineer who works on learning-based control systems, and the second, a former prosecutor, were not arguing about what any existing law requires; they were arguing about what is true, morally, of the case. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move, as it actually unfolded.

(1) The engineer said that this is a textbook case of a responsibility gap. The programmers did not intend the death, could not have foreseen this particular failure, and did not control the system at the moment of action; the van is not a moral agent; and the remote supervisor had neither the information nor the time to intervene. So there is a death, and an avoidable one, but no individual who satisfies the conditions of blame. That, she said, should disturb anyone who takes blame seriously.

(2) The prosecutor replied that this assumes responsibility must be located at the moment of impact and in an individual. The blameworthy event, he said, is earlier: the company chose to deploy a system whose failures it knew to be partly opaque and un-reconstructable, in exchange for scale and profit. That decision was made by identifiable people with the relevant knowledge. We do not need to explain this particular error to find fault with that decision.

(3) The engineer answered that this merely relocates the problem. A corporation does not have intentions or a conscience, so saying that the company is to blame either is a figure of speech for blaming some unnamed individual or it is vicarious liability dressed up as moral blame. And if the negligent act is supposed to be the deployment itself, then the standard is self-defeating: the system's overall accident rate was, on the evidence available before the crash, lower than the human-driver baseline, so refusing to deploy would have meant accepting more predictable deaths to avoid this one.

(4) The prosecutor said that this treats foreseeability far too finely. Negligence has never required foreseeing the exact failure mode; it requires that some class of serious failures was foreseeable and that reasonable precautions were not taken. The company knew the system would sometimes err in ways it could not later explain, and it knew its testing could not enumerate those ways in advance. Deploying on those terms was a choice, and choices made under uncertainty can be negligent. The engineers who set the validation thresholds and the executives who signed off made that choice, and they cannot escape blame by pointing out that the eventual failure was novel.

(5) The engineer granted that this works for the sign-off, but said it collapses in the residual case. Suppose the company had done everything a reasonable firm would do: disengagement testing, shadow driving, incident reviews, metrics better than the human baseline. A residual error still kills a cyclist. Now the negligent-deployment story has nothing to grip, and the gap reappears: an avoidable death with no individual at fault. She was not saying the family should go uncompensated; she was saying that compensation is not blame, and that a theory that cannot produce blame in this case has conceded her central point.

(6) The prosecutor replied that perhaps it has, and perhaps that is not the disaster she thinks. The insistence that someone must be blameworthy for every bad outcome is a retributive instinct, not a necessary feature of responsibility. Strict liability already handles injury without requiring blame. The serious question is forward-looking: who should change what, so that the next failure is less likely. Under that description, responsibility is distributed across the company, the regulator, and the industry, and there is no gap — only a gap in our appetite for punishment.

I cannot tell how much of this is a real disagreement and how much is two people talking past each other. Please go through the exchange move by move. For each move, say what is right and what is overstated, conceded, or confused; identify which claims depend on contested theories of blame or responsibility; and then say whether there is a genuine responsibility gap in the residual-error case, or only a gap relative to a particular — perhaps dispensable — conception of blame. I am less interested in which friend wins overall than in a precise account of where they actually disagree.
