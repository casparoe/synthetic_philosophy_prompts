Question: Friedman (1953) defends what Samuelson later called the F-twist — the idea that the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions. Explain Friedman's argument for why the realism of assumptions doesn't matter, using at least one of his examples. Then evaluate the objection that Friedman runs together different kinds of unrealistic assumptions, and say whether Friedman's position is best read as instrumentalism or realism.

Student answer: The F-twist is Friedman's claim that theories should be judged by their predictive success, not by whether their assumptions are descriptively accurate. His billiard-player example makes this vivid: an expert player behaves as if they solve the relevant physics equations, but nobody thinks the player actually does the math. Likewise, leaves on a tree are spaced as if each one were jostling for as much light as possible. Since we accept these as-if hypotheses without checking whether the as-if claim is literally true, Friedman thinks we should do the same in economics: assumptions like profit maximization are fine so long as the theory predicts well.

The standard objection is that this ignores the different jobs assumptions do. Nagel distinguishes three kinds of unrealistic assumptions: negligibility assumptions, where a factor is left out because it makes no real difference; domain assumptions, which specify when a theory applies; and heuristic assumptions, which are false but help us reach the right result. Friedman's maximization assumption is a negligibility assumption — the agent's internal calculation is a small detail we can safely omit, like air resistance in a falling-body law. So the objection is that Friedman treats all false assumptions as if they were that kind.

On the last question, Friedman is clearly an instrumentalist. Since he says the only test of a theory is its predictive success, he must hold that theories aren't true or false but merely instruments for generating predictions. That's what instrumentalism says, so the F-twist commits him to it.
