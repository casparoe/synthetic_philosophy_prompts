I am a software engineer at a mid-sized company, with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been asked to make the case to our internal responsible-AI review group for adopting a precautionary principle to govern the deployment of a new customer-facing machine-learning system that makes consequential decisions about people, such as eligibility determinations that feed into downstream systems. The version I want to defend is deliberately weak rather than the familiar strong form: it does not require proof of safety or zero risk, and it does not apply to every conceivable downside. It says only that, for a specified class of harms — severe, difficult to reverse, and reached by a mechanism we can spell out rather than merely imagine — the absence of solid evidence of safety counts as a reason against deployment, and the burden falls on the deployment team to show that the plausible pathway is adequately controlled. My argument is that our organization already reasons this way about severe security vulnerabilities and privacy breaches, where we do not ship while a known severe flaw has an uncontained blast radius; the principle extends that existing practice to a wider class of harms and makes the implicit default explicit. The review group is not philosophical: it includes engineering leads who think in terms of failure modes, rollback plans, and measurable risk; product managers accountable for launch timelines; a compliance officer concerned with auditability; and two members who follow the AI-safety literature and will press for stronger restrictions than I am proposing. I expect them to object in the vocabulary of shipping software, not in the vocabulary of the academic debate.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet: the eight or so objections I am likely to receive, ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they can do, each phrased the way this particular audience would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Since the strong and weak versions of the precautionary principle pull apart in practice, the sheet should also flag, for each objection, whether it is pushing me toward the strong version, toward abandoning the principle entirely, or toward a genuine refinement, and how I should respond to that pressure.
