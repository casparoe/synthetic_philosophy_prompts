I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist is a philosopher of logic, and I need to map the conceptual terrain of the problem Lewis Carroll identified in "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (1895): the apparent infinite regress that arises when we try to justify a deductive inference by adding the inference rule as a further premise. Please construct a decision tree or taxonomy of positions on the justification of deduction that the tortoise problem raises. The tree should be built from diagnostic questions — concrete claims about what kind of thing a logical rule is, whether the regress is vicious or benign, whether deductive inference requires justification at all, whether there is a principled distinction between rules and premises — phrased so that someone could answer them without knowing the labels of the positions. Each leaf should name the position (or note that it is unnamed), give a one-sentence summary, and identify its most awkward commitment. The following positions must appear as leaves: the view that the regress is vicious and deduction cannot be justified; the view that the regress is stopped by a non-propositional grasp of inference rules (a knowing-how rather than knowing-that); the view that logical rules are inference licenses, not premises, so the regress never gets started; and the view that the regress is benign because we are entitled to default trust in our basic inferential dispositions. Please flag any pairs of positions the tree cannot cleanly separate, and note any logically possible positions that no one defends, with a brief diagnosis of why. The tree should be usable by someone with an undergraduate background in philosophy but no specialized knowledge of the tortoise literature. Aim for roughly six to ten leaves. Write in American English.
