"Explain Berkeley's master argument and assess whether it succeeds in establishing that mind-independent objects cannot exist."

Here's the student's answer (I've tidied up the prose a bit but kept the substance intact):

---

Berkeley's master argument is his central argument for idealism, aiming to prove that physical objects are nothing but collections of ideas in our minds. The argument appears in both the Principles and the Three Dialogues, though it's given its most famous statement in the Principles. It runs like this: try to conceive of something that exists entirely unconceived — that is, without being perceived or thought about by any mind whatsoever. For instance, picture a tree deep in a forest where no one is around to see it. Berkeley points out that in the very act of trying to conceive this tree, you are yourself conceiving it. The tree you're imagining is therefore in your mind. You cannot step outside your own mind to conceive of something as genuinely mind-independent, because the act of conception itself brings the object into a mind. The upshot is that the phrase "mind-independent object" is strictly incoherent — we cannot even form the idea, let alone verify that such things exist.

What makes the master argument distinctive is that it doesn't rely on any empirical premises about how perception works. It's meant to be a purely conceptual demonstration: the very notion of mind-independent existence is shown to be contradictory from the inside, as it were. If Berkeley is right, the materialist isn't just advancing a false theory but literally doesn't know what she's talking about.

I think the argument fails, however. The standard objection — and I find it convincing — is that Berkeley conflates the act of conceiving with the content of what is conceived. When I conceive of a tree existing unconceived, the content of my thought is "a tree that exists without anyone thinking about it." The fact that I am doing the thinking doesn't contradict this content; I am thinking of it precisely as not being thought about. Consider an analogy: I can think about the Jurassic period, long before any minds existed on earth. My act of thinking occurs now, in my mind, but what I'm thinking about — a mindless prehistoric world — doesn't thereby become mind-dependent. Berkeley's argument only goes through if we already grant that the object of thought must inherit properties from the act of thinking, which is exactly what's in dispute. So the master argument begs the question and fails to establish idealism.
