I work as a software engineer, and I have been reading about free will and moral responsibility in my spare time. I recently came across Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and I want to check whether I have understood it. As I read it, the argument runs roughly as follows. Your actions flow from your mental makeup — your beliefs, desires, values, and dispositions — and you did not choose that makeup, because any choice you made about it would itself have to flow from a makeup you already had. So responsibility for your actions would require an infinite regress of self-creation, and therefore no one is ultimately responsible for what they do.

What I cannot see is how this differs, except in presentation, from the standard argument that determinism undermines responsibility because the causal chain ultimately runs back to factors outside the agent. On my reading, Strawson is simply saying that the initial state of the system and its transition rules were set by something other than the agent, so the outputs are not the agent's fault. If that is right, then the obvious fix from a systems perspective would be to break the causal chain by introducing genuine randomness into decision-making, perhaps through quantum-level indeterminacy, so that not every output is fully determined by the initial state and the rules; a process with a true random component is not a function of its seed alone. So my question is this: does the Basic Argument collapse into the familiar determinism-based challenge to responsibility, and would a libertarian account built on quantum indeterminacy be enough to escape it? If the argument is not simply about determinism, what exactly does it rule out that determinism alone would not already rule out?
