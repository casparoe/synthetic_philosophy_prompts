I recently heard a seminar instructor give a compact summary of Jeffrey conditionalization, and I want to know how much of it I can safely repeat. From my notes, the gloss ran something like this: "Jeffrey conditionalization is the Bayesian rule for updating when your evidence is not the certain learning of a proposition. Suppose your observation makes it 70% likely that a bird was a warbler and 30% likely that it was a goldfinch, while you remain certain it was one or the other. The rule says: set the new probabilities of those two possibilities to 70 and 30, hold fixed all your old conditional probabilities on each possibility, and assign every other proposition the probability-weighted average of those old conditional probabilities. Because the rule merely re-weights your old conditional probabilities by the new probabilities you feed in, it makes no assumptions beyond the new probabilities over the partition; in particular, the partition you use is irrelevant, and any rational agent who adopts those new probabilities must end up with the same final credences. And since Jeffrey conditionalization generalizes ordinary Bayesian conditionalization, it inherits the latter's formal virtues — repeated updates commute, so the order of successive uncertain observations never matters." I gather the "hold fixed" condition is called rigidity, and I suspect it is doing more work than the gloss admits, but I cannot say exactly how. Could you go through this summary and tell me which parts are accurate, which are overstatements, and what would need to be true for the claims about partition-independence and commutativity to follow? I would also like to know what a careful defender of Jeffrey's framework would accept in this gloss and what they would push back on.
