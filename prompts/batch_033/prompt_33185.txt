During a panel on end-of-life policy I attended last month, one of the speakers repeatedly invoked the phrase "death with dignity" as though it sufficed to settle the case for legalizing assisted dying. The claim seemed to be that once we grant that people are entitled to a dignified death, it follows straightforwardly that the law must permit physicians to help them end their lives. I found myself uncertain about what, exactly, the phrase was being used to assert — and whether the asserted claim actually carries the argumentative weight it was given. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan "death with dignity" might be meant to express? I am thinking of claims about the nature of dignity itself, about what specifically makes a death dignified or undignified, about the relationship between dignity and autonomy or between dignity and the avoidance of suffering, and about the respective obligations of the state, the medical profession, and the family. For each candidate thesis, please indicate whether it is plausible (and whether it is contested or largely uncontroversial), and whether, if true, it genuinely supports a legal right to assisted dying. Finally, could you say which reading or readings appear to be doing the rhetorical work when the slogan is deployed to close debate rather than open it?
