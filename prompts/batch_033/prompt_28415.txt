Two members of my cohort—one working on well-being, the other on moral psychology—got into an extended argument at our reading group about Nozick’s experience machine, and both later asked me to adjudicate. I reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can.

(1) Maya: The experience machine refutes hedonism. If well-being consisted solely in felt experience, a life inside the machine would be just as good as the corresponding real life, and everyone would plug in. Yet people refuse, even when we stipulate that they will forget they plugged in and that no one they leave behind will suffer. So we value something beyond the experiences themselves: actually doing things, actually standing in relationships, actually being in contact with reality.

(2) Daniel: That is too quick. The machine is described as a one-way, total replacement of a life one already understands, and the refusal is made from inside one’s current, non-machine values. That looks like status quo bias. A hedonist can explain the refusal entirely in hedonic terms: people anticipate that a life of simulated relationships and achievements would feel hollow, or they recoil at the thought of being deceived, and anticipated distress is just another felt state.

(3) Maya: But you can stipulate the hollowness away—the machine gives you the felt conviction that your relationships are real and your achievements are your own. Once that is stipulated, the residual objection is not a predicted bad experience; it is that the conviction would be false. That is precisely the point at which the content of the life, rather than its felt quality, is doing the normative work.

(4) Daniel: Now you are running two different arguments. First you said refusal shows people care about reality as such. Then you said the intuition survives even a machine that is indistinguishable from reality from the inside—but that just restates the original intuition, it does not vindicate it. A hedonist may reasonably distrust an intuition produced by a creature who has never faced the machine’s options and is, by hypothesis, adapted to the real world. Why should a pre-theoretical aversion to a stipulated life settle a theory of well-being?

They each believe the other conceded more than they did. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: identify who is right about which specific point, where each move overreaches, and whether the experience machine, run this way, actually refutes hedonism, merely forces a revision of it, or leaves it standing.
