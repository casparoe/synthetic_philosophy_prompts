I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about deontological constraints—the idea that some actions are wrong regardless of the consequences they prevent—by examining my intuitive reactions to cases. My hope is that there's a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures the pattern. Here are my verdicts:

1. **Push.** A runaway trolley will kill five people unless I push a bystander off a footbridge onto the track, which will stop the trolley but kill him. Verdict: impermissible.

2. **Switch.** The same trolley can be diverted onto a side track where it will kill one person instead of five. Verdict: permissible, though I feel the moral weight of it.

3. **Transplant.** A surgeon can kill a healthy patient and distribute his organs to five people who need them, saving those five. Verdict: impermissible.

4. **Murderer at the door.** A would-be killer asks where my friend is. If I tell the truth, he will kill my friend and four others. If I lie, he finds no one. Verdict: lying is permissible.

5. **Preventing lies.** If I tell the truth in a certain situation, five other people will each be induced to tell a serious, harmful lie. If I lie, they will all tell the truth. Verdict: lying is permissible—in fact, I feel it is nearly required.

6. **Preventing broken promises.** If I keep my promise to attend a colleague's lunch, five other people will each break a weightier promise (to visit a dying parent, say). If I break my promise, they will all keep theirs. Verdict: breaking my promise is not permissible, or at least very difficult to justify. I sense a difference from case 5 but cannot articulate it.

7. **Torture to prevent torture.** I can prevent five innocent people from being tortured to death only by torturing one person for information. Verdict: impermissible.

8. **Stealing to prevent theft.** I can prevent five bicycles from being stolen by stealing one bicycle myself (as a decoy, say). Verdict: permissible, though it feels odd to say so.

9. **Killing to prevent killings (aggressor).** Five attackers are about to kill an innocent person. I can stop them only by killing one of them. Verdict: permissible.

10. **Killing to prevent killings (innocent shield).** The same five attackers are using an innocent person as a human shield. I can stop them only by shooting through the innocent person, killing them, and then stopping the attackers. Verdict: impermissible.

I find myself pulled in two directions. The thought "never violate a constraint even to prevent more violations of it" fits cases 1, 3, 7, and 10, but fails on 4, 5, 8, and 9. The opposite thought—"violate the constraint whenever it minimizes total violations of that constraint"—fails on 1, 3, 6, 7, and 10. And the pattern does not seem to track which constraint is at stake: killing is sometimes overrideable (9) and sometimes not (1, 3, 10); lying is overrideable (4, 5); but promise-breaking is not (6), and torture is not (7). The doing-allowing distinction captures some of this (2 versus 1) but cannot explain why 5 and 6 feel different, or why 8 and 9 feel permissible when 1 and 7 do not.

Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all or most of these verdicts? If some verdicts must be overturned, which ones should go, and what is the argument for overturning them? And what does the resulting view say about a case I have not considered: I can prevent five people from being tortured over the next year by framing one innocent person for a crime, knowing that person will be tortured as a result?
