So in my writing workshops people always say the self is like a narrator — you know, we're all telling ourselves the story of our lives, the "I" is the narrative center that stitches everything together into one coherent thing. I've heard it so many times I basically absorbed it as true. But then I read about these split-brain patients, where you sever the corpus callosum and suddenly the left hand is buttoning a shirt while the right hand is unbuttoning it, and when you flash a command to the right hemisphere the left hand obeys but the speaking left hemisphere confabulates a reason — "oh I just felt like standing up." And some philosophers take this to mean the unified self is a kind of confabulation too, that we're not really one narrator but more like two (or many) and the sense of unity is just the left hemisphere's press secretary doing its job.

I want to know how far the narrator analogy actually holds up under this. Give me the mapping: what in the split-brain findings corresponds to what in the novelist's toolkit — point of view, free indirect discourse, unreliable narrator, the editorial "we," whatever else you need. Then tell me where it breaks. And most importantly: does the conclusion that the unified self is an illusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the narrator analogy smuggle in assumptions about what a "real" narrator would have to be like that don't actually apply to selves?

Because honestly as a novelist I've written characters with fractured consciousness and I know a narrator can be multiple things at once — I'm not sure the people using this analogy have actually read enough novels.
