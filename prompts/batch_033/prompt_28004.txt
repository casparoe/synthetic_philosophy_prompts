I've been turning over an argument about what my own position in the sequence of human lives tells me about how many more of us there will be, and I can't find the step to reject. Here it is as cleanly as I can put it.

Let SHORT be the hypothesis that the total number of humans who will ever live is about 2 × 10^11: roughly a hundred billion have already lived, and roughly a hundred billion more are still to come. Let LONG be the hypothesis that the total will be about 2 × 10^14: a future measured in hundreds of thousands or millions of years rather than generations. I find myself around birth rank 10^11, in the sense that about a hundred billion humans preceded me.

(1) Absent more specific information, I should reason as if my birth rank is drawn at random from a uniform distribution over all humans who will ever live.
(2) Conditional on SHORT, my birth rank is entirely typical: I am near the middle of the sequence.
(3) Conditional on LONG, my birth rank is extraordinarily early: I am in the first 0.05% of all humans who will ever live.
(4) By Bayes' rule, my observed birth rank therefore favors SHORT over LONG by the ratio of the two total populations — a factor of about a thousand — and this likelihood ratio does not depend on the prior.
(5) A likelihood ratio of a thousand is enough to swamp any reasonable prior confidence in LONG; so I should now be very confident in SHORT.
(6) But SHORT says humanity is very likely to end within a few generations, and I refuse to accept that my birth rank, of all things, licenses that conclusion.

So either one of the premises is false, or the move from (4) to (5) is illicit, or the inference to (6) is invalid. I can't see which. If I had to choose, I would most like to keep the Bayesian apparatus in (4) and the thought that my temporal position is at least some evidence about the future. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Whatever step you reject, say what principle or distinction you would put in its place, and whether that replacement handles ordinary cases — finding yourself in a city of a certain size, or joining a long-running institution in a given year — without quietly reproducing the same conclusion.

In your answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the step you reject and, in one clause, what you would put in its place. Then give the full reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
