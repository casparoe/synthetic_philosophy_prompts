Over the semester I've been keeping a running list in my notes app of how my advisor, who works on paternalism and nudging, comes down on cases, because she refuses to state her principle outright and just gives verdicts. So far: she approved of calorie counts on chain restaurant menus; approved of graphic warning labels on cigarette packs, even while admitting they work partly through disgust; approved of a cafeteria that put fruit at eye level and moved the candy to a low shelf (still available, just less convenient); approved of automatic enrollment in a retirement plan at 6 percent with a clear opt-out form and annual reminders; rejected subliminal messages aimed at getting people to eat less, because they bypass awareness; rejected a city ordinance capping soda servings at 16 ounces, because it just deletes an option; and approved of making people answer a yes/no organ-donation question before they can renew a license, which she called the least manipulative item on the list because it forces a choice rather than nudging one.

Now there's a new case. The university is thinking about defaulting new employees into a high-deductible health plan with an HSA, with the option to switch plans at any time. The plan is probably better and cheaper for most younger employees, but the default would not be announced at orientation: it would only appear as a line in the 60-page benefits handbook and on the first pay stub. So it's easy to reverse but not salient.

If she's being consistent with herself, what should I expect her to say about this case? I want you to lay out the two or more principles that fit her past verdicts, say what each predicts here, identify which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and give your best guess about her verdict with the reasons for it. If the past verdicts genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what principle she'd be committing to with each answer, and what further case would most cleanly seperate the candidate principles.
