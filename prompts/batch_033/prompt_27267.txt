Two friends of mine got into it at a party over Hume's argument against miracles. Neither is a philosophy major, but they'd both read the relevant bit of the Enquiry. The argument went like this:

(1) Dan said Hume's point is basically airtight: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature, and the evidence for a law of nature is all of our uniform experience, so the evidence against any miracle is always going to be stronger than the evidence for it, which is just some testimony. (2) Maya said that can't be right, because if it were, we'd have to dismiss any singular event that overturns a scientific consensus — she gave the example of the first report of ball lightning, which was dismissed for decades because it didn't fit known physics. (3) Dan replied that ball lightning isn't a violation of a law, it's just an unexpected phenomenon, and that's totally different from a resurrection or something. The laws don't say "ball lightning is impossible"; they just didn't predict it. (4) Maya said okay, but then Hume's criterion just collapses into "miracles are events that are impossible," which begs the question. If a credible person reports a resurrection, you can't just say it violates a law so it didn't happen — that's assuming the law is exceptionless, which is exactly what's at stake. (5) Dan said you're misreading him: Hume doesn't say miracles are impossible by definition, he says the rational person weighs the probability that the witness is mistaken or lying against the probability that the event occured, and the former always wins. (6) Maya came back with: so if twenty people I know to be honest and sober tell me they saw a man rise from the dead, I should still believe they're all lying or hallucinating, because the alternative is "more miraculous"? That's not a principle, that's just stubborness. At that point they looked at me and I said I needed another drink.
