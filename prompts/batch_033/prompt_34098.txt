I've been teaching a seminar on personal identity and practical ethics, and I keep returning to the problem of advance directives without being able to settle what I think. I've collected my verdicts on a series of cases, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures the pattern — and whether some of my verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases. In each, the person wrote an advance directive while competent, later lost competence, and now faces a medical decision where the directive conflicts with what the person's current self appears to want, or where the directive's meaning is disputed.

Case A. A woman writes an advance directive at fifty, in excellent health, after watching her mother decline with Alzheimer's. The directive says: if I ever have severe dementia and cannot recognize my family, I refuse all life-sustaining treatment, including antibiotics for pneumonia. At seventy-two she has advanced dementia, does not recognize her children, but seems content — she laughs at old songs, enjoys chocolate pudding, and smiles when someone holds her hand. She develops pneumonia. Without antibiotics she will die within days. Her children are divided. I think the directive should be honored. The contentment of the current self does not undo the authority of the earlier self's refusal.

Case B. Same facts as Case A, except the woman wrote the directive at twenty-five, when she was healthy and had no experience with dementia in her family. She never revisited it. At seventy-two, the same dementia, the same pneumonia. I am less confident, but I still think the directive should be honored. The fact that it was written long ago and never reconsidered bothers me, but I cannot point to a principled reason why it should lose its force.

Case C. A man joins the Jehovah's Witnesses in his thirties and signs an advance directive refusing blood transfusions. At fifty-five he leaves the faith, formally revokes the directive, and tells his family and doctor he wants all standard medical care. At sixty he develops a brain tumor that causes personality changes; he begins expressing Jehovah's Witness beliefs again and tells his doctors he does not want a transfusion. He now needs one. The earlier directive was revoked, but his current self voices the same preference the original directive expressed. I think the revocation stands: the transfusion should be given. The tumor-induced reversion does not restore the authority of a directive he deliberately renounced.

Case D. A woman with bipolar disorder writes a psychiatric advance directive during a period of stability, after extensive consultation with her psychiatrist. It says: if I become manic, I consent to hospitalization and mood stabilizers even if I refuse them at the time. Six months later she becomes manic, insists she is fine, and refuses treatment. I think the directive should be honored. Her earlier, stable self understood the condition and made provision for it, and the manic self's refusal is exactly the scenario the directive was designed for.

Case E. A man writes an advance directive stating only that he wants "no heroic measures" if he is terminally ill. He later suffers a severe stroke, cannot communicate, and is not terminally ill but cannot eat on his own. Doctors disagree about whether a feeding tube counts as a heroic measure. I think the directive is too vague to settle the question, and the decision should default to what is in his current best interest — which, in this case, I take to be placing the feeding tube.

Case F. Same as Case E, except the man wrote a detailed directive specifically addressing artificial nutrition and hydration, stating that he refuses a feeding tube under any circumstances where he cannot eat on his own, regardless of whether his condition is terminal. I think this directive should be honored, even if his current self shows no signs of distress and might live for years with the tube.

Case G. A woman in her twenties has a traumatic experience with antipsychotic medication during a brief psychotic episode. The side effects are severe, and she resolves never to take such medication again. She writes an advance directive refusing all antipsychotic medication if she ever develops a condition involving psychosis. At forty she develops schizophrenia. She is floridly psychotic, terrified, and suffering, but when the topic of medication arises, she repeats that she does not want it, consistent with her directive. I find myself wanting to override the directive. The suffering of the current self seems to me to outweigh the authority of a directive written in response to a single bad experience, by someone who had never experienced schizophrenia.

Case H. Same as Case G, except the woman wrote the directive at thirty-five, after her sister developed schizophrenia. She saw what the untreated illness was like, discussed it with psychiatrists, and still concluded that for her, the risk of side effects was worse than the risk of psychosis. At forty she develops schizophrenia, with the same terrified suffering, and refuses medication consistent with her directive. I still lean toward overriding the directive, but I am much less comfortable doing so than in Case G. Something about the quality of the earlier self's deliberation matters, but I am not sure it matters enough to change the verdict.

I am left with the sense that I am tracking several factors at once: the specificity of the directive, the quality of the earlier self's information and deliberation, how recently the directive was made or reaffirmed, whether the current self is suffering, and whether the current self's preferences are stable or the product of the very condition the directive contemplated. But I cannot state a principle that weighs these factors against one another, and I suspect that some of my verdicts are in tension — perhaps Case B with Case C, or Case D with Case G.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures my verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon, and why? And what does the principle say about a case I have not listed: a person who writes an advance directive refusing treatment for dementia, develops dementia, and then, in a lucid interval, says she wants to live — but the lucid interval passes and she returns to her demented state, which is content?

Please write your answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
