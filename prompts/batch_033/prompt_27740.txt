I'm a science journalist working on a longform piece about the neuroscience of time and the debates over when conscious experience happens relative to the neural events that produce it. I'd like to close the piece with a short poem — specifically, a Shakespearean sonnet — built around the color phi phenomenon and the distinction Daniel Dennett drew between Orwellian and Stalinesque accounts of conscious experience.

For context: in the color phi experiment, a subject sees a red dot flash briefly on the left, followed by a green dot flashing briefly on the right. Under the right timing conditions, subjects report seeing a single dot moving from left to right and changing color from red to green roughly midway. The puzzle is: how can the brain know the second dot will be green before the moving dot arrives? Two families of explanation emerged. The Orwellian account says the brain initially represents a red dot moving right, but when the green dot appears, it revises the memory — retroactively editing the conscious record, as in the Ministry of Truth. The Stalinesque account says the brain delays conscious experience until after the second dot appears, then presents a single coherent narrative — the dot was always moving and changing color, a show trial of perception. Dennett's point was that no experiment can distinguish these two accounts, because any evidence we could gather would itself be subject to the same ambiguity.

The sonnet should use the form's structure to do philosophical work. I'd like the octave to set up the phenomenon — the two dots, the apparent motion, the color change mid-trajectory — and the volta at line 9 to introduce the Orwellian–Stalinesque fork. The closing sestet should capture the vertigo of realizing the two accounts are empirically indistinguishable: that the question "when did I actually experience the color change?" may have no determinate answer. The final couplet should land on the unsettling implication — not that we don't know the answer, but that the question itself may be ill-posed.

The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind would recognize the argument, but it should also work as a poem for a general science readership — no jargon, no proper names in the verse itself.

Please follow the poem with a section titled "Commentary" (roughly 300 words) explaining the formal and philosophical choices: why a sonnet, how the volta maps onto the argument, what each section of the poem is doing, and how the imagery was chosen to carry the conceptual weight without technical language.
