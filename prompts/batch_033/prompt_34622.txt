I have come to hold the following view: that for any consequential forecasting or planning decision, the outside view — anchoring on the distribution of outcomes in a reference class of sufficiently similar past cases — should be one's default epistemic stance, and departures from it toward the inside view (with its case-specific narratives, plans, and details) require a positive justification that the present case is a relevant exception to the base rate. This is not merely the claim that the outside view is a useful corrective to be consulted alongside the inside view; it is the claim that the outside view gets presumptive weight, and that the inside view's track record of systematic overoptimism — from the planning fallacy to megaproject cost overruns to the forecasting literature — means the burden of proof falls on the inside view. I take this to be the central upshot of Kahneman and Tversky, of Bent Flyvbjerg's reference-class forecasting, and of Tetlock's finding that superforecasters are distinguished largely by their use of outside-view reasoning.

I want to know what would count against this position. I am not asking for the standard objections I already know about (e.g., that reference classes are hard to select, that every case is unique in some respect). I want a map of what would actually have to be true for the position to be wrong, or at least importantly limited. Specifically:

1. What empirical findings would undermine it? If a well-designed study found that inside-view forecasts outperformed reference-class forecasts in some identifiable domain, what features of that domain would matter — and are there any domains where this has arguably already been shown?

2. What does the position commit me to in cases where no informative reference class exists — genuinely novel situations, one-off institutional decisions, the first attempt at something? Does the position have anything to say, or does it fall silent exactly where it is most needed?

3. Is the position itself justified by inside-view reasoning? That is, when I cite the track record of the outside view, am I not making a case-specific argument from the details of that track record — which looks like an inside-view justification for the outside view? If so, is this a regress problem, a consistency problem, or neither?

4. Has the position been qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification? For instance, if an outside-view forecast fails, one can always say the wrong reference class was chosen, or that the inside-view adjustment was mishandled. Is this a genuine unfalsifiability worry, or is it the ordinary imprecision of a heuristic that we accept because the alternative is worse?

5. Are there decision-theoretic or ethical contexts where the outside view is the wrong stance even if it is more accurate — for instance, because adopting it undermines the motivation needed to attempt difficult things, or because it treats cases as mere instances of a class in a way that fails to respect what is distinctive about them?

I want a committed response: take a definite position on each of these, and tell me which challenges you think should move a reasonable defender of the view and which should not. Keep it to no more than 1,000 words.
