Question: "Describe Ellsberg's three-color urn and the two-urn version of the paradox. State the usual choice pattern in each, and explain exactly what it shows about subjective expected utility (SEU) theory. Is ambiguity aversion just risk aversion? Finally, describe one formal model that can accommodate the usual Ellsberg choices, and show briefly how it handles the three-color case."

Student's answer: "In the three-color problem there are 90 balls: 30 red, and 60 black or yellow in unknown proportions. People typically prefer a bet paying $100 on red to a bet paying $100 on black, and they also prefer a bet on black-or-yellow to a bet on red-or-yellow. These can't both be represented by any single subjective probability over the states, because the first preference requires P(red) > P(black) while the second requires P(black) > P(red), with the yellow state cancelling out. So the choices violate the independence axiom of expected utility theory. The two-urn version is the same idea: people prefer to bet on red from a known 50/50 urn rather than red from an urn with unknown composition, and likewise for black, which would make the probability of red in the unknown urn both less than and greater than 1/2. I think this shows ambiguity aversion is really just risk aversion about not knowing the probabilities, and a sufficiently concave utility function over money can capture the choices, so you don't need a new theory. The model I'd use is maxmin expected utility: the agent has a set of priors and evaluates each act by the minimum expected utility over that set. In the three-color case the set of priors is all those consistent with 30 red and 60 black/yellow, so the red bet gets worst-case expected utility 1/3 and the black bet gets 0, which gives red > black; black-or-yellow has worst-case 2/3 while red-or-yellow has 1/3, giving the second preference. So maxmin EU rationalizes the Ellsberg choices."
