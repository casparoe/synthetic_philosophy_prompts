I've been going down a rabbithole on Newcomb's problem and I'm pretty sold on one-boxing. The way I see it, if the Predictor is like 99% accurate, you just take box B and walk away with a million bucks — two-boxing gets you a measly thousand and that's just worse, end of story. I get that there's this dominance principle thing where whatever the Predictor already did, you're better off taking both boxes, but that feels like it's ignoring the whole point about what your decision says about you, or about the kind of agent you are. So yeah, I'm a one-boxer.

What I actually want to know is: what would count against this? Not just the standard two-boxing arguments I've already seen — I want to know what evidence, thought experiments, weird edge cases, or results from other parts of philosophy would make a committed one-boxer genuinely sweat. Like, are there variants of the problem where even diehard one-boxers go "okay yeah in this version I two-box"? And has one-boxing been qualified so much that it's basically unfalsifiable — like no matter what happens you can just say the Predictor wasn't accurate enough or whatever? I want an honest map of where this view is actually vulnerable, sorted from "this would basically kill it" to "this is just kinda awkward."
