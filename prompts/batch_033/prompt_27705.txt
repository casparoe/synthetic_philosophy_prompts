I am on my school's debate team, and we are preparing for a tournament in which the resolution asks whether it is morally permissible for a physician to administer life-ending medication to a consenting, terminally ill patient. The opposition repeatedly invokes the slogan "There is a moral difference between killing and letting die" as though it settles the matter: actively ending a life, they argue, falls into an entirely different moral category from withdrawing life-sustaining treatment and allowing the underlying disease to take its course. I can sense that the slogan is doing genuine argumentative work, but I lack the conceptual vocabulary to articulate what exactly it is asserting. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this slogan might be making — specifying, for each, what kind of difference is being posited (intrinsic or merely instrumental? agent-relative or agent-neutral?), what the difference is supposed to be grounded in (causal structure? the presence or absence of a bodily movement? the distinction between initiating and sustaining? intention? the agent's relationship to the harm?), and what scope the claim is meant to have (does it apply across all cases, or only when other factors are held equal, or only in medical contexts?) — and then, for each reading, assess whether it is independently plausible and whether it actually supports the opposition's conclusion that active euthanasia is impermissible while the withdrawal of treatment is permissible?
