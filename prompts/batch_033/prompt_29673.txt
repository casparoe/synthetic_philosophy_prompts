Stroud's influential objection to transcendental arguments is that, even when they succeed, they establish at most how we must think or experience the world, not how the world is; moving to an ontological conclusion seems to require an added verificationist or idealist premise. With that objection in view, please rank the following four transcendental arguments into tiers according to how much of their intended anti-skeptical conclusion survives it. The arguments are: (i) Kant's Refutation of Idealism — that the determinate temporal ordering of one's inner states presupposes a permanent in outer experience; (ii) Strawson's argument in Individuals — that self-consciousness presupposes the ability to distinguish oneself from an objective world of re-identifiable particulars; (iii) Davidson's argument from radical interpretation — that having beliefs requires communication with another speaker, and hence a shared external world about which one is mostly right; and (iv) Putnam's brain-in-a-vat argument — that the supposition that we are envatted is self-refuting given the causal constraints on reference. For each argument, state its intended conclusion precisely, then say what survives Stroud's objection, what does not, and what additional premise would be needed to close any gap. The deliverable should be the tier ranking with a sentence or two of justification for each placement, not a general survey of transcendental arguments.
