Write a sonnet from the perspective of an actor whom Rousseau, in the Letter to d'Alembert, has denounced as a professional counterfeit. The sonnet should be a reply to the charge: the actor defends their art by demonstrating it. The first part of the sonnet — roughly the opening eight or nine lines — should present the actor's case with such conspicuous formal polish, with rhymes that call attention to themselves as rhymes and a volta that feels almost too well-timed, that the reader feels the artifice as artifice. In the remainder, the actor should attempt to break free of the form and speak plainly, only for the rhyme scheme to reassert itself, so that the attempted escape from artifice is itself revealed as another layer of performance. The closing lines should leave the reader uncertain whether the actor has vindicated Rousseau's critique or refuted it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the Letter would recognize the specific charges Rousseau levels against the actor: the cold-blooded simulation of passion, the erosion of the self through the habit of impersonation, and the moral danger of being paid to be other than oneself.
