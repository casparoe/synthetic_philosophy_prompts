I keep noticing a pattern in moral arguments that I don't have a word for. Someone makes a true moral accusation against another person — a manager who padded his own expense reports denouncing a colleague for doing the same, or a politician who dodged taxes condemning a rival for tax evasion — and the accusation is accurate, and the target really is at fault. Yet everyone says the accuser is in no position to talk, and I share the feeling that something is wrong, not with what is said, but with that person saying it. It isn't the familiar move of dismissing a charge because the accuser did the same thing and therefore the charge is false; I don't think the charge is false. And it doesn't seem to be only that hypocrisy is an unpleasant character trait, because the defect attaches specifically to the act of accusing. I'm looking for the concept, if one exists, for whatever a person must have in order to be entitled to make a true moral accusation — the thing a hypocrite lacks even while speaking the truth. Does this have an established name? How exactly does it differ from the you-did-it-too fallacy and from hypocrisy as a mismatch between word and deed? And what removes it: must the accuser have done the very same deed, or do other kinds of involvement — having benefited from the wrong, having failed to stop it, having once done something similar and since reformed — remove it too, or leave it intact?
