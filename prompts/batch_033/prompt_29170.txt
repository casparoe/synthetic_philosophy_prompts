In debates about consciousness, the global workspace theory is frequently introduced through the theater metaphor: consciousness is a stage, attention is a spotlight, and the vast array of unconscious specialized processors are the audience in the dark. Whatever enters the illuminated region of the stage is what we are conscious of; whatever remains in the wings or the dark is not. Proponents of the theory use this picture to argue that explaining consciousness is a matter of explaining how information wins the competition for global access — that once we have characterized the functional architecture that selects, broadcasts, and sustains contents in the workspace, we have explained consciousness itself.

Ned Block and others have objected that this conflates access consciousness — information being poised for use in reasoning, report, and the rational control of action — with phenomenal consciousness, the felt, qualitative what-it-is-likeness of experience. The theater metaphor appears to capture access consciousness neatly: what is on stage under the spotlight is precisely what is globally accessible to the system. Whether it captures phenomenal consciousness is far less clear.

I want to audit this analogy systematically. What exactly maps onto what in the theater picture — the stage, the spotlight, the audience, the wings, the illumination itself? Where does the mapping break when we press on the access/phenomenal distinction? Does the analogy quietly import assumptions that the global workspace theory has not earned — for instance, that there is a single unified stage where everything comes together for a single audience, that what it is like to be the system just is the fact of being globally broadcast, or that the distinction between what is lit and what is dark corresponds to a sharp boundary in nature rather than a graded one? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that the global workspace theory explains consciousness simpliciter — and not merely access consciousness — survive, or was the theater metaphor doing more argumentative work than its proponents acknowledge? If the analogy is ultimately misleading, is there a better one that captures what the theory can and cannot account for?
