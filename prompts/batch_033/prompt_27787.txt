I run a small sanctuary for animals taken out of industrial farming, and for two years I have been making allocation decisions that used to strike me as someone else's thought experiments: whether to fund one expensive surgery or palliative care for a dozen animals, whether to take in one more animal when that strains care for the others, whether an animal may be used in ways that benefit many provided its suffering is counted honestly. Those decisions have pressed a definite position out of me, and I have since learned it is associated with Nozick. I treat human beings as protected by side constraints: a person may not be used merely as a means, and I would not sacrifice one person even to save several, no matter how the numbers came out. With animals, I am a utilitarian in structure rather than merely in degree: an animal is a locus of welfare that matters, but its welfare enters an aggregate, so I am willing to trade one animal's serious harm for a sufficiently larger total benefit, and I do not think an animal has the kind of inviolability a person has. I am not asking whether this reading of Nozick is faithful; I am asking what would actually count against the position.

I want a map of the view's exposure, not the standard list of animal-rights objections. Tell me which possible findings — about animal cognition, about the grounds of moral status, about what side constraints are supposed to protect — would do the most damage; which cases it handles badly (humans at the margins, unusually self-aware nonhumans, my own reactions to particular trade-offs); and which of these a committed defender of the split view would themselves concede counts against it rather than dismiss. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each whether the view can absorb it by qualification and at what cost. I suspect the cases I will find hardest to ignore concern humans with severe cognitive disabilities and animals with something like narrative self-awareness; if those are not the deepest problems, say so and tell me what is. I also want an honest verdict on whether the position has insulated itself — whether, for example, the line between person and animal could always be redrawn to keep the view safe — and whether that insulation is a vice or just what a normative position looks like.

Please structure the response as follows: "The view, sharpened" (one short paragraph, flagging ambiguities in my statement); "Where it is exposed" (two paragraphs); "The defeaters, ranked" (as many entries as needed, each with a one-sentence rating of how decisive it is); "What a defender concedes" (one paragraph); "Insulation check" (one paragraph); and "Bottom line" (one paragraph). In the bottom line, say how confident you are in the overall assessment and single out the one point you are least sure of.
