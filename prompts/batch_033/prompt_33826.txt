I'm presenting a paper at a metaphysics workshop next month. The paper defends a version of endurantism about persistence through time — the view that objects are wholly present at each moment they exist, rather than being composed of temporal parts — but it does so without relying on presentism. Instead, I argue that the problem of temporary intrinsics can be adequately handled by an adverbialist strategy: properties are not instantiated simpliciter but in a time-indexed way, and this is not an ad hoc fix but reflects something deep about the nature of instantiation. The real burden of the paper is the claim that perdurantism has been getting a free ride on the supposed intuitiveness of its treatment of change, and that once we properly distinguish the epistemic question — how we reidentify objects through time — from the metaphysical question of what persistence consists in, the main motivations for adopting temporal parts collapse.

The audience will be about twenty philosophers: roughly half are metaphysicians who work on persistence and are mostly perdurantists, two are philosophers of physics who care about special relativity and think endurance is in tension with it, three are historians of philosophy who know the medieval origins of the debate, and the rest are generalists with no settled view. The perdurantists in the room include at least one who favours the stage view over the worm view, and I am told he will press me on whether adverbialism about temporary intrinsics simply is a notational variant of the temporal parts view.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I need the six or seven objections I am most likely to get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased roughly as this audience would phrase it. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Also include the question I should be praying nobody asks — the one that would expose the deepest unresolved tension in the paper — and suggest how I might handle it if it does come up.
