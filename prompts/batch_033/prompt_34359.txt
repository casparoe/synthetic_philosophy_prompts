I've been working on a paper about the moral status of insects, and I keep coming back to a puzzle that I find difficult to resolve. The puzzle concerns what some people in animal ethics call the "scale argument." The idea is this: even if you think the probability that any given insect is sentient is exceedingly low — say one in a thousand, or one in ten thousand — the sheer number of insects on Earth is so vast (on the order of a quintillion at any given time, with the turnover of generations adding further orders of magnitude) that the expected number of sentient insects is still staggeringly large. If you do the multiplication, even a one-in-ten-thousand credence in insect sentience yields an expected population of sentient insects that dwarfs the human population thousands of times over. And if you accept any form of expected value reasoning in ethics, this seems to imply that insect welfare should loom very large in our moral deliberations — perhaps larger than the welfare of humans or other mammals, just because of the numbers.

I am not entirely comfortable with this conclusion, and I have encountered several objections in the literature and in conversation. One objection is that when probabilities fall below some threshold, we should simply treat them as zero for practical purposes — that there is something pathological about letting extremely low-probability possibilities dictate our moral priorities. Another objection is that the argument proves too much: if you can run the same reasoning for plants, or for simple artificial systems, or for borderline cases of consciousness generally, then the expected-value framework seems to demand moral concern for almost everything, which is paralyzing. A third objection is that expected value reasoning might break down when we are dealing with moral uncertainty about the very existence of a morally relevant property like sentience, rather than with uncertainty about empirical facts given a fixed moral framework. The thought is that sentience is not the kind of thing you can assign a credence to in the way the argument requires.

I want to defend the scale argument against these objections. I think it is actually more robust than its critics allow, and that the discomfort it generates is something we should sit with rather than dismiss. Please argue for the position that the scale argument gives us strong, non-negotiable reason to take insect welfare seriously, even granting very low credences in insect sentience. Address the objections I have mentioned and try to make the case that the argument is not a reductio of expected value reasoning but a genuine moral insight we should reckon with.
