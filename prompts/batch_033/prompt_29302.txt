So I overheard this argument in the philosophy department lounge that's been bugging me ever since. Two grad students I know — Maya works on experimental philosophy, Leo is more of a traditional conceptual analyst — got into it about the cross-cultural intuition studies, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the upper hand at any given point.

Maya kicked it off by bringing up those studies on the Gödel/Schmidt case, where you tell people the story of someone who's only ever heard that a man named Schmidt proved the incompleteness theorems which were then stolen by Gödel, and then you ask who "Gödel" refers to. She pointed out that Western undergrads overwhelmingly give the causal-historical answer (the name refers to Gödel himself), while East Asian participants split much more evenly or tilt toward the descriptivist answer (it refers to the guy who actually did the proving, i.e. Schmidt). She also mentioned the Gettier case replications where East Asian participants seem less inclined to withhold knowledge attributions than Westerners do. Her claim: this kind of systematic cross-cultural variation means philosophers can't legitimately treat their own intuitive reactions as evidence about the nature of reference, knowledge, or whatever — because those reactions might just be local cultural products rather than insights into the concepts themselves.

Leo said that was way too fast. First, he pointed out that the effect sizes in these studies are often modest and the results haven't always replicated cleanly — but Maya granted that and said the overall pattern across dozens of studies is pretty robust. So Leo moved to his real objection: even if the variation is real, he said, all it shows is that some people are confused about reference. Physics is hard too, and if you polled people across cultures about quantum mechanics you'd get variation — but nobody thinks that undermines physics. The fact that some cultures get the wrong answer doesn't mean there's no fact of the matter.

Maya came back with what I thought was her strongest point. She said the physics analogy gets things backwards: in philosophy, the intuitions are supposed to be the data, not the thing explained. If physicists' instruments gave systematically different readings depending on which country they were in, that would be a crisis for physics. The intuitions are philosophy's instruments, and cross-cultural variation is exactly the kind of calibration failure that should make us doubt the instrument.

Leo then made a move I hadn't expected. He said: okay, suppose the variation is real and it's not just error. That doesn't mean we should throw out intuitions — it means we should study the variation itself. Maybe "reference" or "knowledge" aren't univocal concepts; maybe they're context-sensitive or culturally shaped in ways that are philosophically interesting. Mapping that variation could be part of the positive project, not just a debunking move.

At that point Maya said if Leo is conceding that the concepts shift around depending on cultural context, then he's already abandoned the project of traditional conceptual analysis, which aims at universal necessary and sufficient conditions. If "knowledge" means one thing in Hong Kong and another in New Brunswick, then the armchair project of analyzing knowledge simpliciter is dead.

I walked away feeling like they'd both landed some hits but also maybe were talking past each other by the end. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — rate each argument, say who has the better of it at each stage, and then tell me whether Maya's final move (that Leo's pluralism just is abandoning the traditional project) and Leo's final move (that mapping variation is a constructive response) are as incompatible as they seemed, or whether there's a way both could be right about that last exchange?
