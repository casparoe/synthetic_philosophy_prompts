I'm a biostatistics PhD student, and last week I ended up listening to a postdoc in our group argue with a philosopher of science who was visiting the department. They were going back and forth about a cardiovascular risk model we use, and I genuinely couldn't figure out who was right, so I'd like a careful read from someone who knows the philosophy of probability.

The postdoc started by saying that a 10-year risk score for an individual patient is basically meaningless, because the patient belongs to infinitely many reference classes — all 55-year-old men, 55-year-old men with LDL above 160, 55-year-old men with LDL above 160 and a family history, and so on — and each class has a different event rate. Since you could report any of those numbers, he said, the 23% the model outputs is just an artifact of which class someone happened to pick. The philosopher replied that this was too quick: the model's output is a conditional probability given a specified set of covariates, and conditional probabilities are perfectly well-defined; the 'problem' only shows up if you insist on treating the number as an unconditional frequency, which nobody does. The postdoc came back: sure, but conditional on which covariates? Adding variables changes the number, and most people can be placed in multiple classes that give different risks. Without a principled rule for when to stop, 'conditional on my model' is just a polite way of saying 'conditional on my arbitrary choice.' The philosopher then said the target isn't a class frequency at all — it's a single-case chance, a propensity of this particular patient, and the model is only an estimate of that; if that's the target, the reference class issue is an estimation problem, not a conceptual one. The postdoc said propensities don't escape it, because you can't observe a single-case chance directly; the moment you estimate it you're back to putting the person in a class, so the problem returns at the estimation stage. And anyway, he added, a propensity is compatible with many different observed frequencies, so what would '23%' even mean for this one patient? It trailed off after that, and I've been turning it over ever since.

I'd like you to act as an expert on the philosophy of probability and go through this exchange about the reference class problem move by move. Please definately start by brainstorming the strongest versions of each side's position, then rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who is right about which specific point, and end with what you think both of them are missing.
