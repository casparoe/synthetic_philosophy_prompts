Here's the thing. I teach a final-year philosophy of mathematics course that spends most of its time on constructive mathematics, and every year I write the same comments on student work: 'that's a proof by contradiction, not a constructive proof of an existnce claim', 'you can't get an existential from a negated universal', 'this disjunction never says which side holds'. I'd like to hand them something better than my red pen. Turn the BHK interpretation into a checklist a student (or a referee) can actually run on a proof to decide whether it's constructive. The students have had a term of first-order logic, so symbols are fine, but they can't be assumed to know proof theory, and the checklist has to be runnable in about ten minutes on a proof of ordinary length. I want: a clause for each connective and quantifer; the order to check them in; what counts as a sufficient witness or method; where the checklist stops with a no; and where it has to hand the decision to me rather than the student because the constructive content is genuinely unclear. Then run it on three proofs: the usual classical proof that there are two irrational numbers a and b with a^b rational, an argument that derives ∃x ¬P(x) from ¬∀x P(x), and the standard proof that every bounded monotone sequence of real numbers has a limit. I expect at least one of those runs to come back with an answer I won't like — say so plainly. Be explicit about which parts of the checklist are just BHK and which parts are your own strictness added on top. And be honest about the classic way a non-constructive proof sneaks past the checklist because the offending step is hidden in an ordinary phrase like 'choose' or 'one of these must be the case', or an appeal to a classical theorem the author doesn't spell out. I don't need a bibliography or a survey of constructive schools. British English, please.
