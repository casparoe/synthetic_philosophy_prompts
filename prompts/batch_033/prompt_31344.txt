Working title: The Heterogeneity Problem as a Dilemma for Hedonism

The heterogeneity problem for hedonism is usually presented as a demand for an account of what all pleasures have in common. The initial appeal of hedonism lies in the thought that pleasure is a distinctive kind of felt condition, something that all pleasant episodes share and that explains why they are good for the person who experiences them. The difficulty is that the candidate common element is hard to locate. The pleasure of a hot bath, the pleasure of finishing a proof, the pleasure of hearing that a cruel rival has failed, and the pleasure of relief after a narrow escape seem to differ not merely in intensity and duration but in felt character. On the face of it, they do not feel like instances of the same kind of thing at all.

Two broad replies are available. The first insists that there is nonetheless a common phenomenal quality, a hedonic tone or felt goodness, present in every pleasant episode. The trouble is that this quality must be specified independently of the things it accompanies; otherwise the view is empty. Yet introspectively, the felt difference between, say, the pleasure of scratching an itch and the pleasure of understanding a proof does not present itself as a difference in a separable accompanying tone. The difference seems to pervade the whole experience. The residual candidate quality is thin to the point of being uninformative.

The second reply gives up the search for a homogeneous sensation and treats pleasure as a functional or attitudinal kind: to be pleased is to like, enjoy, or take a pro-attitude towards an experience or state of affairs. This handles heterogeneity easily, because the attitude is one thing and its objects are many. But it invites a different problem. If what makes an episode pleasant is that the subject likes it or wants it to continue, hedonism starts to look like a notational variant of desire-satisfaction or preference-based theories of well-being. The value that hedonism assigns to pleasure then depends not on how an experience feels but on the subject's stance towards it. That may be a coherent theory of well-being, but it has surrendered the central intuitive datum that recommended hedonism in the first place: that what is good for a person is something about the felt character of her life from the inside.

I am inclined to press this as a dilemma rather than a mere request for clarification. If hedonism identifies pleasure with a homogeneous felt quality, it cannot cover the full range of paradigmatic pleasures without stretching that quality beyond recognition. If it identifies pleasure with a heterogeneous functional state such as liking, it preserves coverage but abandons the felt-quality motivation, and the view becomes a preference theory wearing hedonist vocabulary. My worry is that the second horn is often adopted quietly, as if it were a technical refinement, when in fact it changes the content of the theory.
