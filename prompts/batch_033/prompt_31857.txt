I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar on the epistemology of skill, and we have just spent two weeks on the distinction between knowing how and knowing that: Ryle's objection to the idea that intelligent action requires a prior piece of theoretical knowledge, the regress that seems to follow if applying a rule always requires a further rule for its application, and the more recent dispute over whether know-how can be analyzed as a species of propositional knowledge after all. I would like to close the unit with a poem rather than a handout, something the students can read aloud and then argue about.

Please write a strict villanelle from the point of view of someone who has just been asked to explain in words how to do something they can do effortlessly, such as riding a bicycle, tying a bowline, or another skill of your choosing. The two refrains should be the poem's philosophical engine. One refrain should state a piece of knowing-that about the skill, in propositional, statable form; the other should enact or invoke knowing-how, in a way that resists being converted into the first. As the refrains alternate, retreat, and finally meet in the closing couplet, the form should do the work of showing why the attempt to turn the skill into a set of propositions keeps circling without quite capturing the skill. Please leave the question of whether knowing-how reduces to knowing-that unresolved rather than resolving it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Ryle's regress would recognize what is going on, and good enough that someone who has never encountered the distinction would still find it a satisfying poem.
