I am a novelist writing a political novel in which a populist movement justifies nearly every commitment—constitutional reform, trade, immigration, criminal justice—by invoking "the will of the people." I want the movement's chief theorist to be a serious intellectual rather than a caricature, so before I put words in his mouth I need to know what a defensible account of that phrase would have to get right. Write me the desiderata for a theory of the will of the people: what any adequate account of what the will of the people is, and of when a policy, vote, or institution expresses it, must deliver.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. The list should at least address: the ways the same electorate appears to answer the same underlying question differently depending on how the question is framed or aggregated—issue-by-issue majorities that conflict with the majority of their elected representatives, majority cycles, referendums that contradict legislative outcomes; the instability, ignorance, and manipulability of expressed public opinion; the boundary problem of who counts as the people, including nonvoters, noncitizen residents, minors, future generations, regional minorities, and the diaspora; the temporal problem of which moment's public is authoritative; and the distinction between what people presently prefer, what they would prefer after adequate deliberation, and what is in their interests or done in their name.

Also mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudices of particular camps—for example, that the will must be expressible in a single binary choice, that it must exist prior to and independently of institutions, or that it is something discovered rather than constructed. Flag any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied—I suspect at least one impossibility result is hiding in the list, and identifying it is the part I most want to see—and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the familiar accounts: the plebiscitarian, the deliberative, the aggregative, and the identitarian. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that motivate them; a brief scorecard of those four accounts is welcome but secondary. The purpose is fixed by the novel: the account must be strong enough to function as the governing idea of a movement an intelligent person could defend, without quietly collapsing into the view that whatever the movement's leader wants is, by definition, the will of the people.
