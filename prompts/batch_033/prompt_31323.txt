I've been following the metaethics literature on and off since grad school, and the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism has started to feel like watching two grandmasters play out a draw they both saw coming on move twelve. You know the shape of it: expressivists hold that moral judgments are conative attitudes — disapproval, acceptance of norms, plans — rather than beliefs with representational content that can be true or false. Geach pointed out that this makes the compositional semantics of moral language deeply puzzling, because moral terms appear unasserted in conditionals, disjunctions, and the antecedents of arguments, where the speaker plainly isn't expressing the attitude in question. Take a simple modus ponens: if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore, getting your brother to lie is wrong. The argument is plainly valid, but in the conditional's antecedent, "lying is wrong" isn't being used to express disapproval of lying — so what does it contribute to the sentence, and why does the inference go through? Blackburn answered with higher-order attitudes: to accept the conditional is to approve of a combination of attitudes, so that you approve of disapproving of getting-your-brother-to-lie given disapproving of lying. Gibbard shifted the framework to norm-governed planning states. Schroeder gave us being-for. Each wave addresses some subset of the logical problems — negation, mixed sentences, capturing the right inference patterns — but critics keep identifying gaps, and the reply is always a more baroque attitudinal structure that begins to look, to the critics, suspiciously like a representational belief in disguise.

I'm not asking who wins. What I want is a diagnosis of why this debate has the shape it does — why it has run for sixty years without anything that looks like a decisive shift. Is it a straightforward engineering problem: expressivism needs a compositional semantics for non-representational mental states, we haven't found the right formal tool yet, but the back-and-forth is healthy refinement and we're narrowing in? Or is it a deeper clash about what a semantic theory is supposed to do — expressivists trying to explain what speakers are up to when they use moral language, critics demanding a truth-conditional compositional theory that assigns propositions to sentences, and neither side acknowledging they're answering different questions under the same heading? Or is the Frege–Geach problem actually a reductio of expressivism that its defenders have treated as a research program, so that the whole sequence is degenerative and the impasse is a refusal to take the hint? I want the diagnosis to engage the actual moves in the literature — not a hand-wavy "they're talking past each other" — and to say what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal impossibility result, a new distinction that reframes the problem, or the honest conclusion that the impasse is permanent and we should stop expecting it to resolve.
