I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about the Allais paradox, and I'm left with a knot I can't untie. He laid out an argument that seems to prove that anyone with the standard Allais preferences is straightforwardly irrational — and yet I have those preferences, he has those preferences, and as far as I can tell almost everyone does. Something has to give, but I can't figure out what.

Here's his argument as cleanly as I can reconstruct it. (1) The independence axiom of expected utility theory says: if you prefer lottery X to lottery Y, then for any lottery Z and any probability p > 0, you should prefer the compound lottery (p chance of X, 1−p chance of Z) to (p chance of Y, 1−p chance of Z). (2) This axiom seems undeniable — if X is better than Y, and you're going to mix each with exactly the same thing Z in exactly the same proportions, how could the mixture containing Y end up the better choice? It would be like preferring apples to oranges, but then preferring a basket that's 90% bananas and 10% oranges to one that's 90% bananas and 10% apples. (3) Now consider the two choices that make up the Allais paradox. In the first, you choose between A (a sure $1 million) and B (an 89% chance of $1M, a 10% chance of $5M, and a 1% chance of nothing). In the second, you choose between C (an 11% chance of $1M, 89% chance of nothing) and D (a 10% chance of $5M, 90% chance of nothing). (4) Most people — including me, and including my friend before he started thinking hard about this — prefer A over B and D over C. (5) But we can redescribe these lotteries. Both A and B can be thought of as giving you an 89% chance of $1M plus something else: A gives you an additional 11% chance of $1M, while B gives you a 10% chance of $5M and a 1% chance of nothing. The 89% common part should, by independence, be irrelevant to the comparison. (6) When you strip that away, preferring A to B amounts to preferring a sure $1M to a lottery that gives you a 10/11 chance of $5M and a 1/11 chance of nothing. (7) Now look at C and D. They share an 89% chance of nothing, and the remaining 11% is: for C, a sure $1M; for D, that same 10/11 chance of $5M and 1/11 chance of nothing. (8) So by independence, if you prefer A to B, you must prefer C to D — they reduce to the same underlying comparison. (9) But almost everyone prefers D to C. (10) Therefore almost everyone has preferences that violate the independence axiom. (11) The independence axiom is a requirement of rationality. (12) Therefore almost everyone is irrational — including people who understand the argument perfectly and still feel the pull of the Allais preferences.

My friend's conclusion is that the Allais preferences are simply a mistake and we should train ourselves out of them. But I can't shake the feeling that an argument whose conclusion is that virtually every human being is irrational about a simple choice between lotteries has to be missing something important. The certainty of $1M in option A feels genuinely different from the 11% chance of $1M in C, even though the independence argument says they're structurally the same choice once you factor out the common parts. I want to say that something about certainty, or about the shift from the domain of gains to the domain of near-certainty, matters to rational choice in a way that independence doesn't capture. But I can't articulate it, and my friend just says that's an emotional reaction, not an argument.

Where does the argument go wrong? Which premise should I give up, and what do I lose by giving it up? Please keep your response to no more than 600 words.
