Write a dialogue of about 900 to 1,200 words between two classical Indian epistemologists who have just left a formal debate and continue the argument while walking back to their lodgings. One is a Naiyāyika, who defends four distinct instruments of knowledge (pramāṇas): perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda). The other is a Buddhist epistemologist in the tradition of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, who recognizes only perception and inference as genuine pramāṇas and treats the remaining two as reducible to them.

The dialogue should center on two disputes. First, whether testimony reduces to inference. The Naiyāyika should maintain that understanding a sentence does not proceed through an inference about the speaker's reliability, and should press the point that a hearer can understand a false or insincere utterance, which sits awkwardly with treating the utterance as a sign from which its content is inferred. The Buddhist should reply that what is inferred is the speaker's intended meaning rather than the truth of the content, and should ask the Naiyāyika where the word–meaning relation comes from if not from observed linguistic convention. Second, whether comparison is a distinct instrument. The Naiyāyika should deploy the standard case of the gavaya: a person who has been told that a gavaya resembles a cow, and who later, on seeing such an animal in the forest, comes to know that this animal is what the word gavaya denotes. The Naiyāyika should explain why this resulting cognition is neither perception, nor inference, nor testimony alone. The Buddhist should argue that the case is a composite of perception, memory, and testimony, or alternatively an inference, and the Naiyāyika should say what each reduction leaves out.

Throughout, gloss the technical vocabulary on first use (for example, vyāpti as the invariable concomitance that licenses an inference). Both characters should be equally sharp and should respond to the actual arguments the other has just made, conceding a local point where it is forced. The dialogue should end in a genuine standoff: neither side's core thesis should collapse, and the disagreement about what qualifies as a distinct pramāṇa should remain unresolved but sharper than when the walk began.
