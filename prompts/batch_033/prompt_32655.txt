I'm taking a seminar on Burke and the conservative tradition, and last week a classmate used a slogan to end a debate I thought I was winning. I had argued in discussion that the university should end its legacy admissions preference for children of alumni. He replied: "That's just Chesterton's fence — don't ever take a fence down until you know the reason it was put up." His point was that my inability to produce a good reason for the policy was not an argument against it; it only showed that I had not yet done the work, and until I had, the policy deserved to stand. The instructor nodded and moved on.

I looked up the source afterward. In "The Drift from Domesticity," a chapter of his 1929 book The Thing, Chesterton writes: "In the matter of reforming things, as distinct from deforming them, there is one plain and simple principle; a principle which will probably be called a paradox. There exists in such a case a certain institution or law; let us say, for the sake of simplicity, a fence or gate erected across a road. The more modern type of reformer goes gaily up to it and says, 'I don't see the use of this; let us clear it away.' To which the more intelligent type of reformer will do well to answer: 'If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it.'" What struck me is how much more moderate the original sounds than my classmate's use of it. Chesterton's intelligent reformer tells the other reformer to go away and think, and explicitly holds out the possibility of destruction once the use is understood; my classmate treated my failure to state the reason as if it settled the matter in the policy's favor.

I'd like to get clear about what the slogan actually commits someone to. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses "Chesterton's fence" could be expressing, arranged from the almost trivial to the radical — for instance, "don't act on the basis of your own ignorance," "the reformer bears the burden of proof," "enduring institutions should be presumed to encode the wisdom of generations" — and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or false? Then tell me which of those readings the original passage actually supports, which one my classmate needed for his use of the slogan to be legitimate, and whether those two are the same. Our instructor has also been pressing the Burkean idea that institutions store the accumulated wisdom of many generations, which feels like a further step beyond the passage; I'd like to know where that claim sits in the menu, and whether the fence slogan needs it in order to do the work my classmate made it do.
