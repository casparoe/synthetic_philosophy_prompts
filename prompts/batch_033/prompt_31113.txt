I have been trying to pin down what looks to me like an inconsistent pair of commitments in the way the scientific community thinks about extraterrestrial life, and I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can.

The practice, as I understand it. Under COSPAR's planetary-protection rules, Mars landers are assembled in cleanrooms and their bioburden — the number of viable Earth microbes they carry — is driven down to very low levels before launch; mission architectures are altered, sometimes at real scientific cost, to keep spacecraft away from regions where terrestrial microbes might survive and reproduce, regions the planetary-protection literature calls special regions. The stated rationale is that we must not contaminate or confound a possible Martian biosphere before we have had a chance to study it. Alongside this, SETI programmes spend modest but real resources listening for radio and optical technosignatures from other civilisations, while treating any candidate signal as a false alarm unless it survives an extremely demanding confirmation protocol; and there is a long-running, unresolved argument about whether humanity should actively transmit, with many practitioners treating deliberate transmission as a step that would require international consultation, if not a de facto moratorium.

What I want is not a policy recommendation but an account of what these practices presuppose. In particular: what theory of the value of extraterrestrial life does planetary protection implicitly commit us to — is it a claim about the moral standing of hypothetical Martian microbes, or only about their scientific value as a second sample of life, or something else again? What prior probability does each practice implicitly assign to the existence of extraterrestrial life, and are those priors consistent with one another and with the high evidentiary bar applied to candidate signals? Does the asymmetry between our caution about contaminating a possible Martian biosphere and our comparatively relaxed stance toward passive listening — and our unresolved caution about transmitting — presuppose a particular position in Great Filter or Fermi-paradox reasoning, or is it compatible with several? Finally, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several theories of value or evidence would license exactly the same behaviour, and what would have to change in mission planning or SETI practice if we replaced the implicit theory with a different one — for example, a view that only presently existing moral patients matter, or a very low prior on complex life?

Please write the answer in British English, and throughout say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
