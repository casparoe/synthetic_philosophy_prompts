I am a novelist. For the past year I have been researching a novel about a couple who decide to have a child in the face of accelerating climate disruption, and the book's central question is whether that decision can be justified. In an uncomfortable coincidence, my partner and I now face a smaller version of the same question ourselves. We want a child, but we have good reason to expect that the child's life would be harder than ours in concrete ways — persistent economic insecurity, a strained healthcare system, a degraded local environment — though not so hard that the life would not, on balance, be worth living. A friend who enjoys argument has told me that we are confusing ourselves needlessly. His claim is that so long as the child's life is worth living, we do not harm the child by bringing them into those conditions, because the only alternative for this particular child is never existing at all. We would not be choosing between a flourishing child and a struggling child; we would be choosing between a struggling child and no child. I feel the pull of this reasoning, but it also feels too convenient, and I cannot locate the error. Is my friend right that we would not be harming our child? If he is right, is there nonetheless a coherent way to say that the decision troubles me for good reason — or should I treat the unease as mere sentiment and set it aside? I am asking as a novelist, because the same fault line runs through the book I am writing, and I want an answer that shows me the structure of the problem rather than telling me what to do.
