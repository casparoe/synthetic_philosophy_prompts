I am about to work through the recent proposals for how Scanlonian contractualism should handle aggregation, and I want the specification of the problem fixed before I read any of them. Take as fixed the core of the view: an act is wrong just in case it would be disallowed by every principle for the general regulation of behaviour that no person could reasonably reject, where the grounds for rejection are always reasons that particular individuals have, never the value of a set of persons taken as such. Against that background, write the desiderata for an adequate contractualist account of aggregation: the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it must respect, the structural constraints it must satisfy, and the fixed points it must preserve, with the consideration behind each.

The list should engage at least the following, though it need not stop with them:

- the simple rescue, in which one can save either one stranger or five strangers whose claims are all equally strong;
- the unequal trade-off, in which one person faces a very serious harm and a large number of people each face a much smaller harm;
- the distinction between adding up burdens within a single life and adding them up across different lives;
- the distinction between ex ante justification, in terms of each person's prospects before risk is resolved, and ex post justification, in terms of what actually happens to each person;
- the role of complaints and pairwise comparison: whether one person's stronger complaint must always defeat many people's weaker complaints, and if not, what breaks the deadlock;
- the individualist restriction: the requirement that any principle permitting one person to be sacrificed for the sake of others be justifiable to that person by reasons she herself has;
- the separateness of persons: why the distribution of harms across distinct individuals should block the straightforward summing that is unproblematic within one life.

For each desideratum, mark it as non-negotiable for any contractualist or as a preference of a particular camp that a contractualist could coherently reject. Then do two further things. First, identify any pair or subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied as stated, and say whether the right response is to weaken one of them or to reject the demand. Second, say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive proposals. I am less interested in a scorecard of named positions than in the criteria themselves and the tensions among them.
