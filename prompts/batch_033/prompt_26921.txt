So I've been chewing on this argument about moral compromise and I'm stuck. The rationalist-sphere has this line about how moral disagreements are just preference disagreements in disguise, and the same logic of gains-from-trade that makes markets work should let us resolve value conflicts peacefully. I think the idea traces back through the econ-and-ethics crossover stuff — like if Alice cares way more about abortion policy than school funding and Bob is the reverse, there's a deal to be made. So far so good.

But then I tried to take the logic seriously all the way down and now I have an argument whose conclusion I really don't like:

(1) For any two agents with different fundamental values (or different weightings on shared values), there exist possible trades that make both better off by their own lights — just like in economics. If I weight suffering-reduction at 0.8 and liberty at 0.2, and you weight them at 0.2 and 0.8, I should be willing to trade some liberty for some suffering-reduction, and you the reverse.

(2) The scope of this principle can't be restricted by the *content* of the values, only by their formal structure. If it works for suffering and liberty, it works for purity and loyalty, or for aesthetic preferences, or for anything else an agent might fundamentally value.

(3) Therefore the principle applies to value systems we find abhorrent. If someone has a deeply held terminal value for, say, ethnic homogeneity, then by (1) and (2) there exists some trade — some concession to their ethnic homogeneity in exchange for concessions on something we care about — that makes us both better off by our own lights.

(4) If such a trade exists, we have a pro tanto reason to take it. Refusing is leaving value on the table — value as measured by your own standards.

(5) But we clearly don't have even a pro tanto reason to trade away civil rights for, I dunno, highway funding, even if the racist party would give us a great deal on highways. Something has gone wrong.

(6) So either the gains-from-trade framing doesn't apply to moral values at all (but then why does it seem to work for the easy cases?), or there's a constraint that blocks (3) without also blocking the benign applications.

I can feel the shape of an answer — something about how moral values aren't just "weights in a utility function" because they make claims on *everyone*, not just on the holder, so you can't legitimately bargain them away. But I'm not sure that move doesn't also rule out the abortion/school-funding trade. Where does this argument actually break, and what do I have to give up to break it?
