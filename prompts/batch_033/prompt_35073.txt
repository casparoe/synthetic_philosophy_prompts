Here's the argument:

(1) The probability of an indicative conditional "If A, then B" just IS the conditional probability of B given A. So P(A → B) = P(B | A), for any A, B where P(A) > 0. This is sometimes called "Adams's Thesis" or just "the Equation." It sounds right — like, how confident are you that if it rains the picnic will be cancelled? That's exactly how confident you are that the picnic is cancelled, given that it rains.

(2) By the law of total probability, we can expand any probability over a partition. Take the partition {B, ¬B}:
P(A → B) = P(A → B | B) · P(B) + P(A → B | ¬B) · P(¬B)

(3) Now apply the Equation again, this time to the conditional probabilities inside the expansion. The Equation was supposed to hold for any probability function, including the ones we get after conditionalizing on B or on ¬B (provided the conditioning event has positive probability). So:
P(A → B | B) = P(B | A ∧ B) = 1
P(A → B | ¬B) = P(B | A ∧ ¬B) = 0

(4) Plug those in:
P(A → B) = 1 · P(B) + 0 · P(¬B) = P(B)

(5) But we also have from (1) that P(A → B) = P(B | A). So P(B | A) = P(B) for any A, B with P(A) > 0.

(6) That means every pair of propositions is probabilistically independent. But that's absurd — P(rain | dark clouds) should be higher than P(rain), P(you have the disease | you tested positive) should be higher than P(you have the disease).
