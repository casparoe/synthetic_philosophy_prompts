**Exam Question:**

Does the distinction between lying and merely misleading carry genuine moral weight? Or is it a distinction without a moral difference? Defend your answer with reference to at least one philosopher who argues that the distinction matters morally and at least one who argues that it does not.

---

**Student's Answer:**

The distinction between lying and misleading is usually drawn in terms of what the speaker asserts. A lie is an assertion the speaker believes to be false, made with the intention that the hearer come to believe that falsehood. Misleading, by contrast, involves causing the hearer to form a false belief without asserting anything the speaker believes to be false — for instance, by making a technically true statement that the speaker knows will be misinterpreted. The classic example is the unfaithful spouse who, when asked "Were you with someone else tonight?", truthfully replies "I was at the gym" — which they were, earlier in the evening — knowing the partner will infer that no infidelity occurred.

Kant is the most prominent defender of the view that the distinction matters morally, because he holds that lying is uniquely impermissible. In his essay "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy," Kant argues that a lie always wrongs the hearer and, more broadly, wrongs humanity by undermining the trustworthiness of all speech. For Kant, the wrongness of lying is absolute and does not depend on consequences; it is grounded in the categorical imperative. Kant would therefore say that while misleading may sometimes be imprudent or uncharitable, it is not a violation of duty in the way lying is, because only lying involves making an assertion that, if universalized, would destroy the practice of assertion itself.

On the other side, Jennifer Saul has argued that the lying/misleading distinction cannot carry genuine moral weight. Her central claim is that misleading is morally equivalent to lying. If a speaker intends to produce a false belief, and deliberately shapes their communication to produce that belief, the fact that their words happen to be technically true does nothing to reduce their culpability. The outcome is the same, the intention is the same, and the hearer's trust is violated in the same way. Saul concludes that any moral framework that treats lying as seriously wrong must treat misleading as equally wrong.

I find Saul's argument more convincing. If we focus on what actually matters — the harm done, the intention to deceive, the betrayal of trust — the distinction between lying and misleading simply tracks a superficial linguistic feature rather than anything morally deep. In fact, one could argue that misleading is worse than lying, because the misleader has taken extra care to construct a statement that is technically true, displaying a calculating attitude that compounds the deception rather than mitigating it. Either way, the distinction does no real moral work. Since lying and misleading are morally equivalent, and lying is generally impermissible when it aims at deception, misleading must be equally impermissible whenever it aims at the same end. There is no moral difference between them.
