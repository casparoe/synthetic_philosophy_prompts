I'm an undergraduate currently enrolled in a philosophy of language seminar, and we've been working through Frege's puzzle about identity statements — roughly, the problem of how a true identity claim like "a = b" can be informative while "a = a" is trivial, even though both assert the same relation. I recently had an experience that struck me as a lived instance of this puzzle, and I'm trying to work out whether my interpretation of it is correct and what it implies.

Last week I attended a departmental reception where I fell into conversation with a man who introduced himself as Tom. We talked at length about his recent trip to Patagonia, his love of early music, and some of his favorite hiking trails in the area. He was warm, unpretentious, and genuinely curious about my undergraduate research. I left feeling I'd made a nice connection with someone in the department.

The following day, a friend asked how the reception went, and I mentioned Tom. She looked at me oddly and said, "You mean Professor Ashford? The one who teaches the metaphysics proseminar?" Professor Ashford is, by reputation, one of the most intellectually intimidating figures in the department — the kind of instructor whose written comments, I've been told, are incisive to the point of devastating, and who is known for a certain coldness in seminar. I had never met him before, but I had certainly formed a picture of him from what others had said.

What's puzzling me is this: I now feel that I have learned something — that my knowledge has somehow expanded — yet I can't identify any proposition about the world that I now believe and previously didn't. I already knew that Professor Ashford existed, and I already knew that the man I spoke to at the reception existed. The proposition expressed by "Tom is Professor Ashford" was true all along, and if I had been asked before the reception whether Tom and Professor Ashford were the same person, I would have had no view either way. But discovering the identity feels like a genuine cognitive gain, not merely a linguistic one.

I'm trying to figure out whether this is really the phenomenon Frege was pointing to, and if so, whether his solution — distinguishing sense from reference — actually captures what's going on here. I'm also wondering whether this experience suggests anything about how we ought to think about the relationship between the way an object is presented to us in thought and the object itself. Is there a way of understanding this that doesn't require positing senses as abstract objects, or is Frege right that we need something like that to make sense of cases like this?
