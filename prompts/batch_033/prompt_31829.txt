I am working on a position paper about the normative foundations of AI alignment, and I have noticed that the debate over whose values an aligned system should serve tends to run along a single axis: either aggregate everyone's preferences into some kind of social welfare function, or remain scrupulously neutral between conceptions of the good and impose as little as possible. I want to step back and ask what any adequate theory in this area would have to deliver before I evaluate either pole. Please write the desiderata: the full specification a theory of whose values an aligned AI should serve must meet in order to count as adequate for a system that has to act under partial information, with limited authority to ask people what they want, and with consequences that fall on people who never consented. For each requirement, give the motivating case, distinction, or formal result behind it; mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (aggregationist, neutralist, contractualist, and so on); and flag any subsets of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied given Arrow-style impossibility results or the liberal paradox. The part I most want is the pressure test of the desiderata themselves: if some standard demand is really a partisan commitment dressed up as a fixed point, say so, and say what work that demand was doing.

The phenomena the list should cover, though you should not treat my list as exhaustive, include: conflicts within a single person across time or across contexts; conflicts across persons with very different conceptions of the good; future people and merely possible people; nonhuman animals and possible digital minds, if and insofar as they have interests; adaptive preferences and preferences formed under oppression; and the gap between a person's stated preferences and their interests or values. The theory must also respect at least these distinctions: between interests, preferences, and values; between being a patient whose interests count and being an author whose voice gets a say; and between the question of what is good for someone and the question of what it is permissible to do to them or in their name. Finally, in a short closing paragraph, score the two poles of the debate—preference aggregation and strict liberal neutrality—against your list, and identify the single requirement that most excludes each.
