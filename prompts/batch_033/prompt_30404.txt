Two data scientists on my team spent most of an afternoon arguing about how to update a model after a noisy test, and neither would concede. I sat in on the discussion and could not referee it. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Elena: Suppose the monitoring test fires, but it has a 10% false-positive rate. We did not learn that the bug exists; we only learned that the test fired. So we cannot condition on "the bug exists." The right rule is Jeffrey conditionalization: shift the probability of the bug to whatever the test evidence supports while holding fixed our conditional probabilities given the bug exists and given it does not.

Marcus: That is an unnecessary detour. We did learn something with certainty — that the test fired. Condition on that. The prior already encodes the test's error rates, so Bayes does the rest. Jeffrey's rule just takes the answer as an input.

Elena: Conditioning on "the test fired" works only because someone put that event into the model ahead of time. For a blurry glance or a colleague's vague report, there is no clean observation sentence. Jeffrey's rule covers exactly that case: the evidence shifts probabilities over a partition without making any cell certain. Your approach is partition-dependent too — how finely you describe "the observation" changes the posterior, so it is not the neutral rule you present it as.

Marcus: If there is no observation sentence, then you have not said what was learned, and the update is unconstrained. Jeffrey's rule does not fix that; it asks for new probabilities over a partition but gives no rule for where those numbers or the partition come from. Conditioning at least derives the posterior from the prior.

Elena: But Jeffrey updates on different partitions generally do not commute. Apply them in a different order and you get a different final credence. If conditioning on observations were the full story, two certain observations would commute. The order-dependence shows noisy evidence is not just conditioning in disguise.

Marcus: I would call that a bug, not a feature. When you genuinely learn two facts, you condition on both and the order is irrelevant. If Jeffrey's rule makes the answer depend on the order or the partition, then it is too permissive to count as a rule of inference.
