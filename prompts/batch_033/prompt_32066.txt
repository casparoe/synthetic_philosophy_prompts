I run a monthly rationality workshop for about a dozen adults, mostly engineers and graduate students, with two hours available. Design a structured in-class game in which participants make a series of small-stakes choices over delayed rewards — using points or tokens, not real money — and in doing so run face-first into a money pump: the setup should be arranged so that many participants reverse their preference as a payoff approaches, and a designated trader (or the rules themselves) can assemble a cycle of trades that each participant accepts at the time and that leaves them strictly worse off by their own lights. They should not hear the name "money pump" until the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the formal structure of the argument — the apparent intransitivity or instability in their choices, present bias versus genuinely changing one's mind, why the obvious defenses ("I'll just refuse the final trade," "the trades feel different at each step") either work or fail, and what commitment devices would have protected them. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, participants becoming too risk-averse or colluding once they sense the setup — and how to handle it, and be explicit about which part of the money-pump argument the game cannot reproduce and the facilitator will have to supply.
