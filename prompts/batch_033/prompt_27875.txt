So I'm a software engineer at a mid-size robotics company — we make autonomous delivery drones for last-mile logistics, nothing too fancy. I got pulled into this "ethics working group" (basically me, two other engineers, and a product manager who read half of a Peter Singer book once) because our drones are starting to operate in denser neighborhoods and we need to actually spec out the behavior for edge-case situations where something's going to go wrong and the drone has to pick between bad options.

Here's where I'm stuck. I've been reading around — mostly blog posts and the odd SEP article when I can't sleep — and the whole debate seems to come down to: should the system reason like a utilitarian (minimize expected harm, do the calculation, pick the lowest number) or like a deontologist (follow hard rules, don't do certain things even if the numbers say you should)? And the thing is, I can see cases where each one feels right. If the drone's sensor says the sidewalk is empty, maybe you swerve to avoid the car. But if the rule is "never deliberately steer toward a person," then you don't, period. I don't have a principled way to decide which lens to use when, and neither does anyone else on the team — we just go with our gut and then argue about it for an hour.

What I want — and what I think would actually unblock us — is a decision proceedure. Not a philosophy paper. Something I can turn into a flowchart or a set of guard conditions in code. The procedure should take a description of a drone's decision situation and tell us: is this a utilitarian situation or a deontological one? Or some hybrid? What features of the situation flip the switch? Walk me through the questions in order, tell me what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and mark the points where we're going to have to make a judgment call that can't be reduced to code (so we know what needs to go to a human reviewer or a policy doc).

Then apply it to three cases so I can see it in action:
1. The drone's motor fails over a residential street. It can either drop onto a parked car (no one inside, our sensors confirm) or veer onto a sidewalk that our sensors say is empty — but it's a sunny Saturday and there could be kids out.
2. The drone is carrying a defibrillator to a cardiac arrest call and a separate EpiPen to an anaphylaxis call in the opposite direction. It can only make one delivery in time. The dispatch system didn't prioritize them; it just assigned both to the same drone because of bad routing logic.
3. The drone detects a software fault that's about to cause a flyaway. It can intentionally crash itself into a dumpster (destroying the drone and the package, which is someone's insulin) to guarantee it doesn't hit anyone, or it can try to limp to a landing zone with a 15% chance of hitting a person on the way.

I want to see at least one case where the procedure spits out an answer my team is going to hate — because if it never does, I'll assume it's just telling us what we already wanted to hear. And be straight with me about what the procedure can't handle: the situations where the whole framework just breaks, or where it gives an answer but you wouldn't want to stake anything on it.
