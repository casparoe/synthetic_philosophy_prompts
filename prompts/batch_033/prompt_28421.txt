Okay, I've been turning this over for a week and its making me a little crazy. A friend in my reading group built this argument out of the old my-one-flight-burger-vote-doesn't-matter line, and now none of us can find the exit:

(1) An act is wrong only if it makes a difference to whether the harm happens. If the harm would happen just the same without you, you haven't done anything wrong.

(2) In the big collective cases, your individual act makes no difference to whether the harm happens. The plane burns the same fuel whether you buy the seat or not, the cow is already dead by the time you order the burger, and the candidate wins or loses by thousands of votes whether you vote or not.

(3) So you have no moral reason to skip the flight, skip the burger, or vote — each of those would change nothing.

(4) But the same principle applies to cases we all think are wrong. A member of a firing squad can say that if he doesn't fire, the prisoner still dies. A second arsonist can say the house is already burning down, so his match changes nothing. A factory can say the river is already poisoned, so its one extra barrel makes no detectable difference.

(5) Those acts are wrong, and the people doing them have reasons not to do them.

(6) So the argument has to fail somewhere in (1) through (3). But where?

I'd most like to keep the firing-squad verdict and also the thought that there's something right about the inefficacy objection — that my one burger isn't on a par with shooting a prisoner. But I can't see how to have both. If I reject (1), I need a story about why some contributions that don't change whether the harm occurs are still wrong, and I worry that story will catch my burger too. If I reject (2), then the inefficacy objection just collapses as an empirical matter. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
