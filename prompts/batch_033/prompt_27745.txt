I write about consciousness science for a general but scientifically literate audience, and I keep running into a move that strikes me as both intuitive and possibly circular. In discussions of Daniel Dennett's essay Quining Qualia, people defending the existence of qualia often describe the Chase and Sanborn example as if it were a calibration problem involving two private instruments. Two coffee tasters both say they no longer enjoy a coffee they once liked. Chase says the coffee tastes the same to him but his standards have gotten stricter; Sanborn says the coffee tastes different to him now. The qualia-friendly gloss is that each man has a private qualitative reading of the coffee's taste, and one man's instrument has drifted while the other's sample has changed. The analogy is used to support the conclusion that there is a fact of the matter about how the coffee tastes to each taster, independent of all downstream judgments and behavior.

I want an audit of this instrument-calibration analogy. Lay out the correspondences: sample, instrument, baseline, calibration, reading, and units. Then show where the mapping breaks. Does introspection really provide independent access to the two hypothesized variables—the pure taste-quality and the evaluative standard—or does the analogy quietly import the distinction it is supposed to secure? Finally, tell me what follows for the larger debate. If the instrument analogy fails, is that merely a warning against one bad picture, or does it strengthen Dennett's claim that the notion of a private qualitative content is not just unhelpful but incoherent? If qualia are not like instrument readings, what better analogy, if any, would capture what defenders of qualia are trying to preserve?
