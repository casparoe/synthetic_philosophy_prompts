In the semantics of quantified modal logic, the treatment of de re modality has been contested for over fifty years between two approaches that both possess well-developed formal machinery and extensive philosophical motivation. On one side, counterpart theory, introduced by David Lewis in 1968 and elaborated in his 1986 *On the Plurality of Worlds*, holds that no individual exists in more than one possible world. When we say that Humphrey could have won the election, the truth of this claim is not underwritten by Humphrey himself appearing in another world with the property of winning; it is underwritten by the existence of a counterpart of Humphrey — an individual in another world sufficiently similar to Humphrey in the relevant respects — who wins. Counterparts are not identical to the individual they represent; the counterpart relation is a context-dependent similarity relation. On the other side, the transworld identity view, advanced by Saul Kripke in *Naming and Necessity* and developed with different metaphysical commitments by Alvin Plantinga, holds that de re modal claims are literally about the same individual across possible worlds. When we say Humphrey could have won, we are talking about Humphrey himself, the very same individual, as he exists in another possible world.

The debate has been remarkably stable. Lewis's original arguments — that transworld identity faces the problem of accidental intrinsics, that it requires a primitive and mysterious cross-world identification relation, and that counterpart theory handles these difficulties with greater ontological economy — were met with replies from Kripke, Plantinga, and others, who argued that counterpart theory gets the content of modal claims wrong (the Humphrey objection), that the problem of accidental intrinsics can be solved with world-indexed properties, and that possible worlds are stipulated rather than discovered, so no criterion of transworld identification is needed. Both sides have refined their positions, but neither has conceded ground on the core dispute. The arguments on both sides are now thoroughly familiar, and the literature has largely moved to other topics in modal metaphysics rather than resolving the original standoff.

My hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about the truth conditions of modal sentences — at the level of which sentences come out true, the two theories can be made to agree on any particular case by adjusting the counterpart relation or the accessibility relation. Rather, it is a disagreement about what makes modal sentences true: about whether the semantics of modal discourse should be interpreted realistically (as describing a structure of worlds and individuals that exists independently of our descriptive practices) or as a formal device whose value lies in its theoretical virtues rather than its correspondence to modal reality. If that is right, the debate is a clash of metaphilosophical commitments that neither side has articulated as such, and the impasse reflects a deeper disagreement about the aims of semantics. But I am not certain this diagnosis is correct, and I want to know whether the debate admits of a different, perhaps more tractable, analysis.
