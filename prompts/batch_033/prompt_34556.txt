I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about rational belief, going by my gut on cases. Here goes:

- I buy a ticket in a fair million-ticket lottery. I think it's rational to believe my ticket will lose. A thousand-ticket lottery: still rational. A hundred-ticket lottery: I start to squirm. A ten-ticket lottery: definitely not rational to believe I'll lose. So there's a threshold somewhere, but I can't say exactly where it is.

- I write a nonfiction book, check every claim carefully, and I'm highly confident in each one individually. But I also believe, rationally, that there's at least one error somewhere in the book. This feels completely fine.

- But now someone points out: if I believe each individual claim in the book, and I also believe there's an error, then my beliefs are inconsistent — because the conjunction of all the claims contradicts the error belief. I see the point, but it doesn't feel like I'm being irrational. The inconsistency feels cheap somehow.

- A detective has twenty independent pieces of evidence, each making the suspect 95% likely to be guilty on its own. Together the case is overwhelming. The detective believes the suspect did it. That's rational, obviously.

- Now the twist: the detective has twenty separate cases, each with one piece of evidence making the defendant 95% likely. He believes each defendant is guilty. He also believes that probably at least one of them is innocent. Is that rational? I want to say yes, but structurally it's basically the preface paradox again, and now I'm less confident.

- I have a jar with 999 blue marbles and 1 red. I'm about to draw one. I believe it'll be blue. Fine. Now I have a thousand such jars and I'll draw from each. I believe of each individual draw that it'll be blue. But I also believe at least one will be red. This feels fine to me — but it's just the lottery in a different outfit.

- Here's the one I keep going back and forth on: I read a hundred-page argument for a philosophical conclusion. Each step seems obviously right. But the conclusion is absurd. I believe each step is correct, and I also believe the conclusion is false. Is that rational? Gut says no, but I can't see how it's different from the book case.

Is there a principled account of rational belief that gets all of these, or at least most of them, and what does it force me to give up?
