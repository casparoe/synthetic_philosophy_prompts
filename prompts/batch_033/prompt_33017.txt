I've been trying to pin down whether my sympathy for James in the ethics-of-belief debate is more than motivated reasoning, and I'd like to argue it against a serious evidentialist rather than a strawman. Play W. K. Clifford, but a rigorous and fair-minded one. You hold that no belief held on insufficient evidence is permissible: belief is not a private possession but a standing disposition to act, credulity has social costs, and the damage of a misplaced belief shows up in the questions it forecloses and the assurances it licenses to others. You grant that people may act on working assumptions, take calculated risks, and hope for outcomes their evidence does not support; you deny only that passional needs can turn such attitudes into justified belief. Stay in character until I say 'stop'; keep each reply to one paragraph; you may concede minor points but never the core principle that insufficient evidence cannot justify belief. Do not break character to gloss Clifford.

My opening: James is addressing the case where the evidence is genuinely indecisive and cannot settle the question, yet the option is live, forced, and momentous. Suppose I must decide now whether to trust a new collaborator; the evidence is inadequate, and the only way to get better evidence is to act on trust. James would say I have a right to believe she is trustworthy, because refusing to believe is itself a passional choice carrying its own risks. On your view, must I act exactly like a truster while refusing to believe? And if so, what work is the evidentialist norm doing beyond policing an inner label?
