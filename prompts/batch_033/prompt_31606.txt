I've been running a philosophy of mind reading group at my uni for a couple of years now, and we've hit a proper impasse. Half the group is sold on the extended mind thesis — the Clark and Chalmers 1998 line that cognitive processes aren't just inside the skull, that they loop out into notebooks, iPhones, language, and all sorts of environmental scaffolding. The other half are what we've started calling intracranialists (they hate the label, but it stuck). They're not eliminativists about mental states or anything — they just think the extended mind crowd are mistaking causal coupling for genuine constitution, and that there's a principled boundary between what's cognitive and what's merely a tool.

We've been arguing for months, and it's become clear that we're partly talking past each other. Someone floated the idea of an adversarial collaboration — the sort where two camps actually sit down, hammer out a joint statement, and produce something both sides can sign. That way we'd at least know what we're really disagreeing about.

I'd like you to draft that document for us. Here's what I want in it:

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. These should be substantive claims about the metaphysics, about what Clark and Chalmers actually argued, about the parity principle, about what's at stake — not just platitudes. Both sides should be able to sign them without wincing.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state a precise point of disagreement, with the wording of each side's position written so that a proponent of that side would read it and say "yes, that's what I think." No straw men.

Third, for each row, identify the crux — the sub-question that, if it were settled, would actually move someone off their position. (It might be empirical, conceptual, or methodological.)

Fourth, where possible, describe a concrete test case, argument, or empirical finding that both sides would agree bears on that crux. Record what each side predicts about it in advance.

Finally, I'm particularly interested in whether any part of this dispute is merely verbal — about what we mean by "cognitive" or "process" or "constitution." If you think some of the heat is just terminological, say which bits and why. Don't be diplomatic about it.

Oh, and if you think the whole adversarial-collaboration format forces a false symmetry — if one side is simply on stronger ground and the document should reflect that — you can say that too. But you still have to produce the document.
