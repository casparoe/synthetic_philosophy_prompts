Two people in my philosophy of probability reading group got into it about Dutch book arguments last week, and I left genuinly unsure who was right.

(1) Marcus said Dutch book arguments are basically circular: they assume that your fair betting price for a gamble equals your credence, but that's exactly the link between belief and action that a non-probabilist can reject. If I have incoherent credences, I can just refuse to bet, or set my betting prices by some other rule — so the fact that I'd lose money if I bet a certain way doesn't show my credences are irrational.

(2) Elena said no, the Dutch book isn't a practical warning about getting tricked by bookies. It's a representation argument: if your credences violate the probability axioms, there's a set of bets you'd find individually fair that together guarantee a loss. That reveals a kind of inconsistency in your credal state itself, not just in your betting behavior.

(3) Marcus said that's the same point dressed up. The "inconsistency" only arises because you're interpreting credences as fair betting prices. A credence of 0.6 in P and 0.5 in not-P is only inconsistent if you also accept the norm that credences fix betting odds. The contradiction is in the package — credences plus the decision rule — not in the credences alone.

(4) Elena said that's why the modern versions of the argument don't use betting at all. They use accuracy: if your credences violate the probability axioms, then there's some other set of credences that's more accurate than yours at every possible world, by any reasonable measure of accuracy. The Dutch book is just a vivid way to make the same point. The real argument is that incoherent credences are accuracy-dominated.

(5) Marcus said he's seen that move and doesn't think it escapes the circle. The accuracy argument assumes a proper scoring rule, and a scoring rule is "proper" by definition if it gives the highest expected score to someone who follows the probability axioms. So you're assuming probabilism to justify probabilism.

(6) Elena said that's not right — proper scoring rules are characterized independently as those where your expected score is maximized by reporting your true credences, whatever they are. The fact that this characterization ends up favoring probabilistic credences is a result, not an assumption. You can define proper scoring rules without mentioning the probability axioms at all.

They went back and forth like this for twenty minutes and I couldn't tell who was gaining ground. Can you adjudicate each of these six moves — who has the better of it at each step — and then say who's more right overall? Keep it to about 500 words.
