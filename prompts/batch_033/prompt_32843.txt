Since retiring from the chemistry department I have joined a philosophy discussion group, and at our last meeting two members got into a protracted argument about Nomic — the game, invented by Peter Suber, in which players vote on rule changes and the rules themselves govern how rules may be altered. I have since read some of the literature, but I still cannot settle their dispute to my own satisfaction.

The scenario they debated was this. Suppose the rules include a provision stating: "Any rule may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the players." A player then moves to amend this very provision to read: "Any rule may be amended by a simple majority vote." The motion receives sixty percent support — more than a majority but less than two-thirds. Has the amendment passed?

Aldrich argued that it has not passed. The amendment procedure in force at the time of the vote was the two-thirds rule, and the vote fell short of that threshold. That the amendment would have changed the threshold is irrelevant; one cannot invoke a rule that is not yet in effect to govern the vote on whether to adopt it.

Moran replied that this makes the amendment procedure forever entrenched. If the existing procedure always governs votes to change it, then no amendment procedure can ever be altered, which contradicts the whole premise of Nomic as a game of fully mutable rules. Moran argued that the very act of voting on the amendment must be understood as governed by the rule that would result from a successful vote, because otherwise the rule governing its own amendment occupies an illicitly privileged position — it alone among the rules is effectively unchangeable.

Aldrich responded that this is not a special problem for Nomic; it is simply the logic of self-reference. In constitutional law, one follows the existing amendment procedure to amend the amendment procedure itself. The fact that a rule governs its own alteration does not create a paradox; it merely means that altering it requires meeting its current standard.

Moran countered that the constitutional analogy fails because a constitution typically distinguishes between fundamental and ordinary provisions, whereas Nomic treats all rules as formally equal. To insist that the amendment rule can only be changed under its own terms is to treat it as de facto fundamental without any textual basis for doing so. Moreover, the sixty percent vote reveals that a majority of players want the new rule, and if the players are sovereign, their will should prevail over a procedural catch-22.

They went back and forth for some time before noticing I had gone quiet and asking me to adjudicate. I found I could not. Please go through their exchange move by move and assess who has the better of each argument, and then give your overall judgment about which position is more defensible.
