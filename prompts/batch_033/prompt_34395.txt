I work in public health policy, and recently, during a strategy meeting about sugar tax legislation, a colleague shut down a line of argument I was developing by saying, "You can't derive an ought from an is." I had just presented longitudinal data showing that sugar taxes reduce consumption in low-income communities and are associated with improved health outcomes. I was about to argue that these findings give us reason to adopt the tax, but he cut me off with the slogan, as if the empirical evidence were categorically irrelevant to the normative question of what we should do. Several people around the table nodded. I have been turning the exchange over in my mind ever since.

I understand the basic idea: you cannot read a normative conclusion straight off a set of descriptive premises. But I am increasingly convinced that the slogan, as it is actually deployed in conversation, papers over a number of importantly different claims. It seems to me that "you can't derive an ought from an is" could be expressing anything from a narrow logical point about deductive entailment to a sweeping metaphysical thesis that would, if taken seriously, make evidence-based policy incoherent. The problem is that I cannot yet articulate the distinctions clearly enough to push back.

I want to go into the next meeting better equipped. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this slogan could be making, ranging from the most modest and uncontroversial to the most ambitious and contentious? For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible on its own terms, and — this is the part that matters to me — whether it actually licenses using the slogan to dismiss empirical evidence as irrelevant to a policy recommendation. Which readings, if any, would justify my colleague's move? Which readings are overreach that I could reasonably contest? The deliverable I am looking for is a kind of menu: here are the things someone might mean when they say "you can't derive an ought from an is," and here is what each would imply for the sugar-tax argument.
