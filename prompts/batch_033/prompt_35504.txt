Ok so I help run a small theater in Geneva and we're opening our first season in a building that was explicitly built as a playhouse, which is a loaded thing to do here because Rousseau — the Genevan, the one everyone claims — wrote a whole Letter to d'Alembert arguing that Geneva shouldn't have a theater at all. I've been asked to give the opening-night remarks, and I dont want to just wave the objection off. The audience is a mix: our board and donors, city officials, actors and stage crew, plus a couple of Rousseau scholars who will know the Letter inside out. My understanding of his worry is roughly this: theater doesn't purge or correct our passions, it flatters and inflames the very ones it puts on stage; and it lets us feel warmly about fictional suffering while doing nothing about real suffering — it replaces active citizenship with a comfortable kind of spectatorship, which for a small republic like Geneva he thought was genuinely corrosive. I want the speech to be warm and celebratory and land for the actors and donors, but it has to take that objection seriously and let it shape what the speech can and cannot say. Like, you can't resolve it by saying "and tonight we prove him wrong by being good citizens in our seats," because that's exactly the move he'd predict. No direct quotes from him either — the scholars would wince if I misquote. Maybe eight minutes. And write it in my voice: I'm not a philosopher, I'm a theater person who has actually lost sleep over this.
