I've been teaching an introductory ethics course this semester, and we just finished a unit on Kant's Groundwork. The universalizability test has been the hardest thing to teach, not because the students don't get the mechanics, but because I've realized I don't have a stable grip on it myself. The idea, roughly, is that an action is permissible only if the maxim on which you act could be willed as a universal law without contradiction — with the contradiction being either in the very conception of the maxim as universal law, or in the agent's ability to rationally will such a world. But as I've worked through cases with my students, my own intuitive verdicts about which maxims pass and which fail have come apart from any single formulation of the test I can state cleanly. I'm hoping you can help me extract whatever principle is actually driving my judgments.

Here are my verdicts on a set of maxims, each stated as the agent would formulate it, including the relevant circumstances:

1. "When I need money and a lender requires a promise of repayment, I will promise to repay even though I know I cannot." — Fails. The standard case: universalizing this undermines the institution of promising, so the maxim cannot even be conceived as universal law.

2. "When an innocent person is hiding in my house and a would-be murderer asks where they are, I will lie and say I do not know." — Passes. A world where everyone lies to murderers to protect the innocent is perfectly coherent and, if anything, better than the alternative.

3. "I will never help anyone in distress, because I prefer to devote all my resources to my own comfort." — Fails. I can conceive of this world, but I cannot rationally will it, since I would be willing away the assistance I might someday need myself.

4. "I will spend my Sunday mornings playing tennis at the public courts." — Passes. No issue of either kind.

5. "When I can cheat on my taxes with no risk of being caught, I will do so." — Fails. If everyone did this, the tax system would collapse; the practice of taxation would become incoherent.

6. "I will break a promise to meet a friend whenever keeping it would be mildly inconvenient." — Fails. Like case 1, universalizing this destroys the practice of promising.

7. "I will never lie, under any circumstances, no matter the consequences." — Passes. A world of universal truth-telling is perfectly coherent and rationally willable.

8. "I will refuse to cultivate any of my talents, preferring a life devoted entirely to idle amusement." — Fails. I can conceive of it, but I cannot rationally will a world in which no one develops their capacities.

9. "When I see someone drowning in a shallow pond and saving them would ruin my expensive shoes, I will walk past." — Fails. This feels like it should fail the contradiction-in-conception test: if no one ever performed easy rescues, the very concept of a rescue — and perhaps the expectations that make social life possible — would erode.

10. "I will give to charity only when my donation is publicly recognized and enhances my social standing." — Passes. The maxim does not generate a contradiction in conception (charitable giving still occurs), and while the motive is not admirable, I can rationally will a world where all charitable giving is reputationally motivated, since the giving still gets done.

Here is what bothers me. Cases 2 and 7 both pass, yet they seem to be in tension: if I universalize "never lie," I cannot lie to the murderer; if I universalize "lie to murderers to save the innocent," I cannot also will "never lie." So passing the test apparently does not require consistency across the set of maxims one adopts — but then what normative work is the test doing? More acutely, I cannot tell what principle distinguishes case 10 (passes) from case 5 (fails). Both involve free-riding on a cooperative scheme while hoping others comply. Is there a principled difference, or am I simply inconsistent? And case 9 versus case 3: both are failures of beneficence, but case 9 feels like it should fail the contradiction-in-conception test, while case 3 feels like it fails only the contradiction-in-willing test — yet I cannot articulate what feature of a maxim determines which kind of contradiction it generates.

What I would like from you: first, state as precisely as you can the principle or small set of principles that best captures my verdicts across these ten cases, making explicit what triggers each kind of failure and what suffices for passing. Second, tell me which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to revise, and why. Third, apply the principle to a case I have not considered: "When a medical researcher can make faster progress toward a cure by running an experiment that involves a small amount of deception of the participants, with no lasting harm and full debriefing afterward, she will do so." What does your principle say about this maxim, and does that verdict seem right? Please write your answer in American English.
