I'm running an undergraduate metaphysics seminar and I keep noticing that the ontological argument produces a weird sorting effect: people who agree about whether God exists still disagree about which step of the argument fails, and people who disagree about God turn out to share the same modal instincts. I want to lean into that. Could you build me a decision tree for the seminar that sorts readers by what they actually accept about existence, conceivability, and modality, not by which label they like or whether they're theists. The tree should start from the question whether the description a being than which no greater can be thought is a coherent description at all, and then branch on claims the positions genuinely come apart on: whether existence or necessary existence is the kind of thing that can make a being greater, whether conceivability or coherence is enough for possibility, whether the move from possibly necessary to actually necessary is legitimate, and whether God's nonexistence is itself possible. Nodes must be phrased so a student can answer them without knowing which leaf they're heading toward, and the question do you believe in God should not appear anywhere.

I'd like around ten to twelve leaves. The leaves should include at least: Anselm's own argument, a Gaunilo-style parody, Kant's objection that existence is not a real predicate, the empiricist objection that no a priori argument can establish existence, Leibniz's repair (the argument only works after God's possibility is proven), Malcolm's and Hartshorne's S5 versions, Plantinga's rational-acceptability version, Gödel's positive-properties version, the reverse modal argument (possibly no God, therefore actually no God), and an S5-skeptic leaf. For each leaf give the name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where the logical space has a combination of answers nobody occupies, say so and give a one-line diagnosis: is it incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. And please flag the pairs or positions the tree can't seperate — I suspect Anselm's and Descartes' versions will collapse on this tree, and maybe Malcolm and Hartshorne too — rather than pretending the branches distinguish them.

I don't want citations or a reading list; the instrument should stand on its own for teaching. But I do want you to be self-critical about the tree itself: tell me where the placement of a branch or leaf is most contestable (for instance whether Kant's objection is a root objection or only bites a specific Cartesian version, and whether Gödel's version belongs under the S5 branch at all), and what consideration or argument would make you move a leaf to a different part of the tree or add a branch you'd left out.
