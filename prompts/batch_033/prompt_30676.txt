I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences, and two of my students have spent the term arguing past one another about methodological individualism and holism. One defends the view that satisfactory explanations of social phenomena must terminate in facts about individual agents — their beliefs, desires, opportunities, and actions — and that appeals to structures, institutions, or norms are legitimate only as shorthand for complex constellations of individual-level facts. The other defends the view that social structures, institutional roles, and relational properties have explanatory autonomy, and that some genuine social-scientific explanations cannot be decomposed into individual-level narratives without losing what does the explaining. I have asked them to stop debating and instead draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to write the document they should produce.

The document should open with a numbered list of claims both parties can sign without reservation, stated precisely enough that they are neither vacuous nor slanted; these should include claims about supervenience (for example, whether all social facts are fixed by facts about individuals), about the difference between ontological and explanatory theses, and about what it would mean for a social explanation to be "reducible" in the relevant sense. It should then present a disagreements table in which each row states a residual disagreement, phrased so that its own side would accept the wording and the other side would recognize it as the genuine target. Where a disagreement is merely verbal — for example, if the two parties mean different things by "reduction" or "microfoundations" — say so explicitly and restate the substantive issue, if any, that survives the clarification. The document should then set out the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or kind of evidence that both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case or evidence will show. Candidate cruxes you might take up, if both parties would recognize them as load-bearing, include: whether multiple realizability of social kinds by individual-level configurations blocks explanatory reduction or only identity reduction; whether there are explanatory successes at the structural level that would be impossible, rather than merely inconvenient, to reproduce at the individual level; whether the demand for microfoundations is a constitutive requirement on social explanation or a defeasible methodological preference; and whether the "individuals" to which the individualist appeals are themselves constituted by social relations in a way that undercuts the proposed explanatory base. Do not pad the document with bookkeeping, and do not include any bibliography, citations, or appeals to authority; the interest of the exercise is entirely in the formulations, the diagnosis of verbal disputes, and the pre-registration of what would change each side's mind.
