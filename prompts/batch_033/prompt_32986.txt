I have been working through Dennett's critique of Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality, and I have reconstructed what I take to be the core argument against original intentionality. But I am troubled by the suspicion that, if the argument succeeds, it succeeds too well — it would also undermine claims about functional and teleological properties throughout the special sciences. I cannot locate the mistake, and I would appreciate a diagnosis.

(1) If a mental state possesses original intentionality — if it is intrinsically about something, as Searle maintains — then there must be a determinate fact about what it is about, a fact that does not depend on any observer's adopting an interpretive stance toward it.

(2) Dennett's two-bitser thought experiment is meant to show that no physical state, considered in itself, fixes its own content. A vending machine's coin-detector is physically identical whether we describe it as a quarter-detector or a quarter-balboa-detector; the physical facts underdetermine the content. So content depends on an interpretive stance.

(3) The natural Searlean reply is that the detector's causal and evolutionary history breaks the tie: the mechanism was designed, or selected for, detecting quarters rather than quarter-balboas. This historical fact, not the intrinsic physical state, makes it determinately about quarters.

(4) Dennett can push further: the property of having been selected for detecting quarters is itself stance-dependent. Evolutionary history does not come labeled with descriptions like "selected for detecting quarters"; that description is something we theorists impose when we adopt the intentional stance toward the selection process. The very same selection history can be redescribed as selecting for detecting round metallic objects of a certain mass and composition, or for any number of gerrymandered disjunctive properties. The appeal to history merely pushes the regress back one step.

(5) Since every attempt to anchor original intentionality in some further fact — causal, historical, teleological, or otherwise — invites the same regress, there is no non-arbitrary stopping point. Therefore, no mental state possesses original intentionality; all intentionality is derived.

(6) Now consider the property of being a heart. The same physical object can be interpreted as a blood pump, a noise-maker, or a lump of muscle tissue. We want to say that evolutionary history settles the matter: it was selected for pumping blood. But by the reasoning in (4), the description "selected for pumping blood" is itself stance-dependent; the same selection history can be redescribed as selecting for something else. By parity of argument, then, there is no determinate fact about whether something is a heart.

(7) The argument generalizes: if it succeeds against original intentionality, it succeeds against all functional and teleological properties — that is, against most of the properties invoked by biology, psychology, and the social sciences. There would be no fact of the matter about whether something is a heart, a belief, or a recession.

(8) That conclusion is absurd. So the argument must go wrong somewhere. But where?

I am inclined to think that the regress is not vicious — that at some point the interpretive chain grounds out in non-intentional, non-teleological facts. But I am unsure how to say this without begging the question against Dennett, since he can always ask what makes those particular facts the right ones to ground the chain. I would like to retain (1) if possible, because Searle's intuition that there is a real difference between what my thoughts are about and what the marks on this page are about seems difficult to surrender. But I want to know what rejecting any given premise would cost.
