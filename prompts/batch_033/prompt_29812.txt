In my environmental ethics class, a friend and I have been stuck on a case our teacher gave us. A vegetable farm near us treats its fields each spring with a broad-spectrum insecticide, mainly to control aphids and flea beetles. The treatment prevents a modest but real loss of yield and keeps produce affordable, but it also kills enormous numbers of insects across the field, most of which are not pests, and it kills some bees and other pollinators that visit the crop. My friend thinks the practice is plainly permissible: insects, he says, are not the kind of beings that can be wronged, and the human benefit is concrete. I am less certain, but I cannot tell whether my unease comes from thinking insects may feel something like pain, from concern about pollinators and the wider ecosystem, or from the suspicion that the farmer could use more targeted methods instead. I would put my credence that this practice is morally impermissible, all things considered, at about 0.6; my friend puts his at about 0.2.

I would like your own number, but more importantly I would like to see the reasoning decomposed. Please assign a rough probability to each sub-question your final answer depends on — for instance: whether at least some of the affected insects are capable of something like pain or suffering; whether insect suffering, if real, has genuine moral weight; whether the aggregate harm to insects and other non-target organisms outweighs the human benefits in a case like this; and whether the availability of more targeted alternatives makes the broad-spectrum choice harder to justify. Give a one-line justification for each number, show how you combine them into your overall credence, and then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, which term you are least confident in, and what evidence would most change your mind. If you can, also say where the gap between my 0.6 and my friend's 0.2 most likely lives — which sub-question we probably disagree about.
