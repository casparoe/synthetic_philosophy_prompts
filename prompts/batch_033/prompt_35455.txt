Two members of my research group have been going back and forth about how to forecast the cost of a rental-subsidy program we're designing, and I've been asked to summarize their disagreement for the group so we can decide how to proceed. The trouble is, I can't tell who has the better of the argument, and I'm hoping you can adjudicate it move by move.

Here's the dispute. We're developing a proposal for a new city-level rental subsidy. The program has a novel feature: a gradual tapering formula that phases out the subsidy smoothly as income rises, rather than the sharp eligibility cliff most existing programs use. We need a five-year cost estimate.

Mara has been building a detailed forecasting model. She's incorporating the local rental market, projected population growth, our specific eligibility thresholds, the anticipated take-up rate given the tapering design, and the administrative costs of a phase-out system. She thinks that once the model is complete, it will give us a reasonable estimate — and that the tapering feature is different enough from what existing programs do that it genuinely needs to be modeled from the ground up.

David told her she was wasting her time. He said we should simply look at the actual costs of comparable rental-subsidy programs in other mid-sized cities, take the distribution, adjust for city size and cost of living, and use that as our estimate. He said her model would almost certainly be too optimistic, because it's built from the inside and the track record of inside-view forecasts in policy is terrible.

Mara pushed back: she said David's "outside view" is just a fancy name for refusing to engage with the specifics of our case. The tapering formula is a genuinely novel design feature, and no reference class of past programs will capture it. His approach, she said, amounts to assuming our program will be average, which is exactly what we're trying not to be.

David replied that of course every program has novel features — that's the whole problem. Everyone thinks their case is special, and that's why inside-view forecasts are systematically overconfident. The outside view doesn't assume the program is average, he said; it starts from the base rate and adjusts only when you have good reason to think you'll beat it, and the burden of proof should be on the person who thinks their case is the exception.

Mara came back with: but how do you even pick the right reference class? She said she could put our program in a class with well-run programs that came in under budget and get one estimate, or with programs that had rocky rollouts and get another. The outside view is smuggling in judgment calls while pretending to be objective, and the reference-class problem means it's just as vulnerable to bias as the inside view — it's just hidden better.

David's final move was to say that even if reference-class selection involves judgment, the outside view still outperforms the inside view in practice because it at least anchors you to real outcomes rather than letting you build a model you can tweak until it says what you want. Epistemic modesty, he said, means admitting you're not special and your case probably isn't either.

They went around a few more times after this, but these were the main moves. I need to write up something for the group about how to proceed, and I honestly can't tell who has the better of the argument. Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the four main arguments (Mara's two, David's two) and say who, if anyone, is more right overall. And if the whole framing of the debate is missing something important — if the question itself rests on a confusion, or if the task of picking a winner can't be done well on these terms — I'd rather hear that plainly than get a tidy verdict that papers over the real difficulty.
