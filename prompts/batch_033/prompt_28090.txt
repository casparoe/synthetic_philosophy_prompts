I have been working through the problem of verisimilitude and have constructed an argument that ends in a conclusion I cannot accept. I would like to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

(1) The content account of verisimilitude says that a false theory A is closer to the truth than a false theory B just in case A has all of B's true consequences and B has all of A's false consequences, with at least one of those inclusions strict. Equivalently: A has at least as much truth content as B and less falsity content, or A has more truth content and no more falsity content. A theory's truth content is the set of true sentences it entails; its falsity content is the set of false sentences it entails.

(2) Consider a miniature world whose complete truth is P ∧ Q ∧ R. Theory A asserts P ∧ Q ∧ ¬R; theory B asserts ¬P ∧ Q ∧ ¬R. Both are false, but A is plainly closer to the truth: it errs only about R, whereas B errs about both P and R.

(3) Neither theory has all of the other's true consequences. A entails P, which B does not entail; B entails ¬P ∨ R, which is true because R is true, but A does not entail it. So their truth contents are not nested.

(4) Nor does either theory have all of the other's false consequences. A entails P ∧ ¬R, which is false and not entailed by B; B entails ¬P ∧ ¬R, which is false and not entailed by A. So their falsity contents are not nested either.

(5) Therefore, by (1), neither theory is closer to the truth than the other—contradicting the plain verdict in (2).

(6) The problem is not a quirk of the example. In general, if a false theory A has all of B's true consequences and also some true consequence B lacks, then A has a false consequence B lacks: take a true consequence a of A not entailed by B and a false consequence f of A; the conjunction a ∧ f is false, entailed by A, and cannot be entailed by B, since that would give B a as well. Symmetrically, if B has a false consequence that A lacks, then B has a true consequence that A lacks. The two inclusions required by (1) can therefore never be jointly satisfied by false theories, and the content account makes no false theory closer to the truth than any other.

(7) I have been assuming that the content account is the only serious way to make the notion of closeness to truth precise.

(8) If the only serious explication of verisimilitude makes no false theory closer to the truth than any other, then the idea that some false theories are closer to the truth than others is empty.

(9) But that idea is not empty. Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than Aristotelian physics, and Copernican astronomy is closer than Ptolemaic astronomy, even though each pair consists of false theories.

(10) So either the comparisons in (9) are illusory, or verisimilitude cannot be coherently explicated.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I am confident that (5) follows from (1)–(4), and I am not willing to give up the comparisons in (9). The suspect steps seem to me to be the content account itself, the generalization in (6), or the assumption in (7) that no other explication is available. Which premise should I reject, and what would a replacement account have to look like—and what would it cost?
