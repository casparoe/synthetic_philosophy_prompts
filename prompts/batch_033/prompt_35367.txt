Was at the pub with a couple of mates who both did game theory in grad school, and they got into this proper row about the centipede game that I've been turning over ever since. Quick setup in case you're not familiar: it's a two-player sequential game with a known finite number of rounds — say a hundred. On each turn, the player whose go it is can either take (grabbing the larger pile and ending the game) or pass (which grows the total pot but flips who gets the larger share next round). The standard backward induction argument says you should take on move one: at round 100, player 2 would take rather than pass and get nothing; knowing that, at round 99 player 1 would take; and this unravels all the way back to the first move.

Dan was adamant that this is simply the correct answer for ideally rational players with common knowledge of rationality, and anyone who balks is letting psychology cloud their judgement. Maya wasn't having it. Her first move was that the backward induction argument only holds if, after player 1 passes on move one (contrary to the BI prescription), player 2 still believes player 1 is fully rational in the BI sense. But player 2 has just observed player 1 do something that, by the BI argument itself, a rational player would never do. So player 2 has to update — maybe player 1 is irrational, or maybe player 1 is rational but thinks player 2 is irrational, or there's some higher-order uncertainty. And once player 2 updates away from certainty about player 1's future play, the unraveling doesn't go through anymore. Dan shot back that this is just the trembling-hand move in different clothes: you're introducing the possibility of mistakes and then acting surprised when the logic shifts. If we're talking about ideal rationality with literally zero probability of error, the BI reasoning is airtight. Maya said that's exactly the problem — the BI argument assumes rationality can survive a move that rationality itself says will never happen, and that's a contradiction. If rationality says player 1 will never pass on move one, then rationality cannot also specify what player 2 should believe if player 1 does pass on move one, because there is no consistent belief to have at that node. Dan said that's just confusion about counterfactuals: of course you can reason about what you'd do if something impossible happened — we do it all the time. Maya said it's not a counterfactual about the weather; it's a counterfactual about the very thing your model of the other player says they won't do, and that's a self-referential problem, not a standard one.

They went round and round and I honestly couldn't tell who had the upper hand. Can you go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate? Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who's right about which specific claim, and tell me whether there's a resolution to the backward induction paradox that neither of them quite landed on.
