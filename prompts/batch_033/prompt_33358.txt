I am preparing a statement of my considered position on the extended mind debate for our department's work-in-progress group, and before I circulate the statement I would like an audit of whether the position actually hangs together. The view I find myself defending has six parts. First, I accept the parity principle in its standard sense: if a process located outside the skull does work that we would unhesitatingly call cognitive if it were done inside the skull, then it should count as part of the cognitive process. Second, I think the familiar notebook case succeeds — an agent whose memory impairment leads him to keep new information in a notebook he always carries can, under the right conditions, be said to believe what is in the notebook, and the notebook can be part of his standing cognitive system rather than a mere external aid. Third, I take the right conditions to be roughly these: the resource must be available whenever the relevant question or task arises, in the way a notebook carried at all times would be; what it stores must be retrievable with little effort; the agent must accept its deliverances without re-checking them on each occasion; and that default acceptance must trace back to the agent's own earlier endorsement. Fourth, I hold that phenomenal consciousness does not extend, and I regard the extended mind thesis as a claim about cognitive processes and states rather than about conscious experience. Fifth, I am moved by the familiar complaint that the parity principle alone draws no principled line between cognitive coupling and merely causal coupling; I think the view needs some substantive criterion of the cognitive if it is not to slide into the claim that every piece of scaffolding is part of the mind. Sixth, I nevertheless also believe that an external resource can be part of someone's cognitive system even when it is not available at every moment, provided it is reliably available and woven into the person's standing cognitive routines; the mathematician who regularly consults a proof assistant is my test case.

I have tried and failed to satisfy myself that this set is jointly tenable. Please go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only look as though they are, and how severe each real tension is. I would like the tensions ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad, with an account of what each tension would require me to revise and what that revision would cost; if a pair that seems to conflict in fact dissolves on inspection, say why. Then tell me which single revision would leave the position most coherent overall. Finally, say how confident you are in this assessment, and single out the point on which you are least sure.
