You are a professional philosopher working on value theory, the ethics of markets, and the structure of moral obligations. Please think carefully and in a structured way: begin by brainstorming several candidate reconstructions of the practice's implicit theory, then argue for the one you find most defensible, and be explicit about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.

Consider the following existing practice. Airlines, tech firms, and fossil-fuel companies routinely advertise products or operations as carbon neutral or pledge net zero by purchasing carbon offsets: they pay for projects elsewhere — a reforestation scheme, a methane-capture project, a cookstove program — that are estimated to avoid or remove emissions equal to the emissions they continue to produce. A closely related practice, biodiversity offsetting, allows a developer to destroy a wetland on one site if it funds the creation of a comparable wetland elsewhere, sometimes in another country and years later.

What theory of value and moral obligation does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can. Does the practice presuppose that harms and benefits are commensurable and fungible across kinds, places, times, and affected parties — that a wrong done here can be canceled by a sufficiently large good done there? Does it presuppose that the moral ledger may be summed across persons, or does it respect something like the separateness of persons? Does it presuppose a consequentialist structure of moral reasons, or could a deontologist who accepts agent-relative constraints coherently endorse it? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with.

Then consider a tension. Many of the same firms and legal systems treat other wrongs as non-fungible: a company may not buy the right to violate worker-safety law, commit fraud, or discriminate, no matter how much compensation it pays. What would have to change in carbon and biodiversity offsetting if the implicit view were replaced by one on which certain environmental harms are constraints that may not be traded away? Is the current practice coherent, or does it quietly combine two incompatible theories of obligation? Where, if anywhere, is the practice underdetermined such that several rival theories would license it?
