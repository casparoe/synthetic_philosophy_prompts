I chair the fellowship selection committee in my department's philosophy PhD program. This year we interviewed finalists in a fixed order and, after each candidate, went around the table from most senior to most junior before voting. One candidate, M., had a genuinely split file: two senior members thought the writing sample was clever but derivative and the letters lukewarm, while our newest faculty member, who had read the sample closely, thought it was the most original thing in the pool. By the time discussion reached her she'd already heard four people speak against M.; she visibly softened her position, and we voted to reject almost unanimously. Last week she told me privately that she still thinks we got it wrong, but in the room she figured four respected colleagues couldn't all be mistaken, so she kept quiet and went along. Now a waitlist spot has opened and we need to decide by Thursday whether to revisit M. Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action on M. this cycle, and then propose a procedure for next year so the discussion doesn't just become the first two or three voices setting everyone else's priors. I don't want a lecture on rational herding; I want a document the comittee can act on, and it should take seriously the possibility that the senior members were right and the junior member really was updating on their expertise.
