I've been reading about a legal doctrine that seems to have philosophical implications I can't quite pin down. Trademark law grants a company the exclusive right to use a name it invents for its product — "Kleenex" for facial tissues, "Xerox" for photocopiers, "Velcro" for hook-and-loop fasteners. But courts also recognize a doctrine called genericide: if the public starts using the brand name as the ordinary term for the product category itself, the company loses the trademark. The name enters the public domain, and competitors can use it freely. This happened to "aspirin" (once a Bayer trademark for acetylsalicylic acid), "escalator" (once an Otis trademark for moving staircases), "thermos" (once a trademark for vacuum flasks), and "yo-yo." Companies actively resist this: they run advertisements reminding the public that "Velcro" is a brand, not a product, and that one should say "hook-and-loop fastener." But the law is clear — if the public's usage shifts far enough, the meaning that prevails is the public's, not the company's, no matter how vigorously the company insists otherwise.

I'm trying to figure out what theory of meaning this practice commits the legal system to, if it commits it to any at all. The initial grant of a trademark seems to presuppose that a speaker — or a corporate entity — can stipulate the meaning of a term: "Kleenex" means the product we make, and that's that. But the genericide doctrine seems to presuppose the opposite: that meaning is determined by the linguistic community's actual patterns of use, and that no amount of stipulation survives once the community has settled on a different use. This looks like a tension between something like an intentionalist theory of meaning and something like a use-based or social-externalist theory, coexisting inside a single body of law.

What I'm trying to work out is where this lands relative to the kind of semantic externalism associated with Putnam's Twin Earth arguments. Putnam's picture, as I understand it, is that the meaning of a natural kind term like "water" is partly determined by the actual nature of the stuff in the world — so if Twin Earth has XYZ where we have H2O, "water" means something different there even though the speakers' internal states are identical. Genericide is about a different kind of term — brand names, not natural kind terms — and the external factor is the community's usage patterns rather than the hidden structure of a natural kind. But both seem to share the idea that meaning is not fully under the control of the individual speaker or the original stipulator. I want to know: does the genericide doctrine actually presuppose a form of semantic externalism, or is it neutral between externalism and its rivals? Does it quietly rule out the view that meaning is fully determined by speaker intentions? And if it does presuppose something like externalism, is it the same kind of externalism Putnam was arguing for, or is it a different beast — one that makes meaning depend on social consensus rather than on the underlying nature of the world? If the latter, does that create a genuine inconsistency within trademark law, since the initial grant of a trademark seems to presuppose the very intentionalism that genericide later overrides? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined — such that several rival theories of meaning could license the same legal practice, and the law doesn't actually commit to any of them?
