I'm designing a module for an undergraduate course on moral philosophy and the ethics of war, and I want to pair G. E. M. Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree" with Christopher Nolan's film *Oppenheimer* (2023). The idea is to use the film not as a documentary of the historical events but as a cultural artifact that dramatizes a certain moral psychology around the bomb — and then to ask whether the film, read through Anscombe's critique, ends up reinforcing or evading her central charge.

Anscombe's argument, as you'll recall, is that Truman's decision to drop atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki was straightforwardly murder. Her key claim is that the civilian deaths were not a tragic side effect of a military operation aimed at a legitimate target; they were the very means chosen to produce surrender: "For men to choose to kill the innocent as a means to their ends is always murder." She also argues that the defense offered on Truman's behalf — that the bombs ended the war and saved lives — would not have been accepted at Nuremberg, where the tribunal treated the intentional slaughter of civilian populations as a crime regardless of the strategic payoff. For Anscombe, the fact that the Allies won the war does not change the moral calculus; there are absolutes, and the deliberate killing of noncombatants violates one of them.

Nolan's *Oppenheimer* is fascinating in this connection because it is almost entirely structured around Oppenheimer's own subjective experience of guilt, yet it is notably reticent about what exactly he is guilty of. The film lingers on the Trinity test as a Promethean triumph, cuts away from Hiroshima and Nagasaki themselves (we never see the cities), and instead gives us Oppenheimer's hallucinatory visions of the effects on his audience during the Los Alamos victory speech. The guilt the film dramatizes seems to attach more to the existence of the bomb as such — to the unleashing of a power that might end the world — than to the specific choice to incinerate two cities full of civilians. Meanwhile, Truman appears in one brief, devastating scene and dismisses Oppenheimer's moral anguish as self-indulgent.

I'd like you to read the film through Anscombe's lens. Where does the film inadvertently illustrate her argument — for instance, in the way it depicts the decision-making process that treats the cities as means to a demonstration of force? Where does the film's moral framework diverge from hers, perhaps by locating the wrong in the technology itself rather than in the intention to kill the innocent? And does the film, in its focus on Oppenheimer's tortured conscience, end up obscuring the question Anscombe thought was central — namely, whether Truman was a murderer, and whether the Allied publics who celebrated the bombings were complicit in something that, on any ordinary understanding of murder, was exactly that? I'm not looking for a verdict on whether Anscombe was right, but for a reading that takes both the film and her argument seriously and identifies the points of genuine friction.
