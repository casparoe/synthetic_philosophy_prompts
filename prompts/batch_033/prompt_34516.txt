You are a professional philosopher who specializes in ethics and moral psychology and has experience running adversarial collaborations between rival philosophical camps. Draft the joint statement that a committed defender of Susan Wolf's position in "Moral Saints" (Journal of Philosophy, 1982) and a committed defender of moral sainthood as a genuine human ideal could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Begin by brainstorming the ground the two sides share.

The positions are these. Wolf argues that a life aimed at maximal moral goodness is not a life we should want to live or admire: the moral saint, judged by our own standards, would be humorless and one-dimensional, lacking the non-moral excellences — wit, taste, artistry, playfulness — that make a person attractive and a life rich. The opposing camp replies that this reaction reveals our own moral weakness, not a defect in the ideal, and that moral goodness properly dominates a well-lived life.

Produce the document in three sections. First, a numbered list of claims both camps can jointly accept — about what the saint's life would look like, about the content of morality, and about the descriptive facts of how ordinary people react to saints. Second, a disagreements table whose rows state the precise residual points of disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or body of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, and record what each side predicts about that case in advance.

Think through each formulation slowly and resist the temptation to make one side sound better than the other. The measure of success is that each camp could sign the document without embarrassment while still recognizing exactly where they part ways.
