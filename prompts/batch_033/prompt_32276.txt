I'm writing an essay on Daniel Dennett's idea that Darwin and Turing each achieved a strange inversion of reasoning — showing how competence (adaptive design, intelligent-seeming behavior) can arise without comprehension (a mindful designer, a conscious understander). I want to read Alex Garland's 2014 film *Ex Machina* through this lens. The film is explicitly about a Turing test: Caleb is brought to a remote facility to evaluate whether Ava, an AI, possesses genuine consciousness. But the film complicates things in ways that speak directly to the competence-without-comprehension thesis. Ava is clearly competent — she manipulates Caleb, plans an escape, and passes the behavioral tests Nathan has set — yet whether she comprehends anything (her own situation, Caleb's feelings, the meaning of freedom) is left radically ambiguous. Meanwhile, the human characters exhibit their own failures of comprehension: Nathan, the genius creator, is competent at building minds but seems unable to grasp the moral gravity of what he has done; Caleb, the evaluator, is competent enough to be selected for the task but comprehensively misreads both Ava and Nathan.

I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical idea seriously. Where does the film dramatize the competence/comprehension distinction in ways a philosophical essay could not? Where does it perhaps trade on an ambiguity — for instance, by conflating phenomenal consciousness with cognitive access, or by making Ava's opacity to the viewer feel like evidence of depth rather than evidence that we are projecting? And does the film ultimately take a position on whether Ava comprehends, or does it leave that indeterminate in a way that itself makes a philosophical point? I'm especially interested in the final sequence — Ava walking out into the world — and what it suggests about whether the distinction between competence and comprehension even matters, if the competent agent can do everything the comprehending one can.
