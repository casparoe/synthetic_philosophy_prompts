I've been teaching "Freedom and Resentment" for years and I thought I had a handle on my own views about when blame and resentment are appropriate, but lately I've been jotting down my gut reactions to cases and they don't line up as neatly as I expected. I'm hoping you can help me extract the principle I seem to be operating with—or tell me which verdicts I need to abandon.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) A man develops a brain tumor that causes sudden aggressive outbursts; during one he strikes his wife. Verdict: resentment and blame are inappropriate—this is a Strawsonian exemption, not an excuse. (2) A woman severely abused throughout childhood is, as an adult, emotionally volatile and sometimes verbally cruel to her partner during arguments. Verdict: I want to say blame is also inappropriate here—her capacity for healthy relationships was damaged by forces entirely beyond her control—but I'm less comfortable calling this a full exemption than I am in the tumor case. (3) A normally kind colleague snaps at me viciously during a meeting; later I learn his mother was just diagnosed with terminal cancer that morning. Verdict: his behavior warrants resentment initially, but once I learn the context, resentment should be withdrawn—classic excuse. (4) A person with antisocial personality disorder lies to and manipulates their business partner, causing serious financial harm. They genuinely cannot grasp the moral reasons against doing so. Verdict: I'm torn. Part of me says blame is pointless—they can't access the moral demand. Another part says we still blame them; we just also quarantine them. (5) Someone raised in a uniformly racist community holds sincerely racist beliefs and denies someone a job on that basis. They've never been exposed to compelling counterarguments. Verdict: blame is appropriate, though perhaps mitigated. This feels different from the psychopath case even though both involve an inability of some sort to see the wrongness of their actions. (6) A sleep-deprived new parent forgets to pick up their friend from the airport as promised, leaving the friend stranded for two hours. Verdict: some mild resentment is appropriate, but substantially mitigated. (7) A teenager with severe OCD washes their hands compulsively for hours, making the family late to an important event. The teenager knows it's irrational and feels terrible. Verdict: resentment is inappropriate—this feels like an exemption of some kind, though the person isn't globally incapacitated. (8) A normally decent person, with no particular stressor or pathology, on one occasion tells a cruel and unnecessary lie about a coworker to make themselves look better. When confronted they're genuinely ashamed and can't explain why they did it. Verdict: blame is fully appropriate; the inexplicability of the action doesn't diminish responsiblity. (9) An elderly person with moderate dementia repeatedly says hurtful things to their caregiver, things they would never have said before. Verdict: exemption, like the tumor case.

The case that's really bothering me, and that I'd like the principle to adjudicate, is this: someone commits a serious crime while in the grip of a psychotic episode during which they believed they were acting on divine command. They are now stabilized on medication and horrified by what they did. Is blame appropriate? My gut says no, but I can't tell whether this is the tumor case, the abuse case, or something else entirely.

So: what principle or small set of principles best captures my verdicts? Which verdicts does it force me to revise? And what does it say about the psychosis case?

I'd like the response structured as a JSON object with the following fields: "principle" (a precise statement of the principle), "principle_type" (e.g., "Strawsonian", "reasons-responsive", "character-based", "hybrid"), "case_analysis" (an array of objects, each with "case_id", "original_verdict", "principle_verdict", and "match"), "verdicts_revised" (an array of case_ids where the principle forces a different verdict than my original, with a brief explanation for each), "further_case_verdict" (what the principle says about the psychosis case), and "deepest_tension" (the hardest-to-resolve tension in the overall view).
