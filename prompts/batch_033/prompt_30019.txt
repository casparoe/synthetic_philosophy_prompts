In a seminar on evolutionary approaches to mind, the instructor kept returning to one image: the mind is a Swiss Army knife. On this picture, evolution did not give us a general-purpose reasoning machine; it gave us a collection of specialized tools, each one shaped to handle a recurring problem in our ancestral environment, from recognizing faces and detecting cheats to choosing mates and avoiding snakes. The image was doing real argumentative work. It was being used to make a general-purpose mind sound like a folk myth, and to make the further claim that the mind is massively modular, built mostly out of innate, domain-specific mechanisms, seem almost unavoidable. I found the image compelling, but I have started to suspect it is doing more than an analogy can honestly do, and I cannot tell where the picture ends and the argument begins.

I would like you to audit the analogy the way you would audit an argument. Lay out the correspondence in prose: what is meant to correspond to the knife's handles, its blades, the factory that produced it, the act of selecting one tool rather than another, and the fixed number of tools it comes with. Then press where the mapping strains. A blade is rigid and already shaped for one job, whereas a person can repurpose capacities, acquire new skills, and face problems no ancestor encountered. A Swiss Army knife was designed by an engineer with foresight, while natural selection works without foresight. The knife is not the one choosing among its tools, but a mind seems, at least partly, to be the chooser. Tell me which of these disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy was meant to support and which are harmless. Then give me your honest bottom line: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the Swiss Army knife still support the claim that the mind is massively modular and that a general-purpose learning capacity is a myth, or was the analogy carrying more weight than it can bear? Throughout, separate what is settled among people who study this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write in continuous prose, as you would in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
