I'm a high school student in an AP Statistics course, and we recently covered Simpson's paradox — the phenomenon where a trend present in each of several subgroups reverses or vanishes when the subgroups are combined into a single aggregate. The textbook used the Berkeley graduate admissions case from the 1970s: in the aggregate data, men were admitted at a higher rate than women, which looked like evidence of sex discrimination; but when admissions were broken down by department, women were admitted at higher rates than men in most departments, and the aggregate reversal was driven by the fact that women applied disproportionately to the more competitive departments with lower overall admission rates. The numbers in both the aggregate and the disaggregated tables were perfectly accurate, yet they pointed in opposite directions.

Ever since that lesson, I have been unable to stop thinking about a phrase I hear constantly: "The numbers don't lie." People say it to end arguments about data interpretation — as if the data speak for themselves and any attempt to look behind the aggregate is just special pleading or motivated reasoning. But Simpson's paradox seems to show that the numbers can be entirely truthful and yet support opposite conclusions depending on how you group them. The numbers do not lie, but neither do they tell you which grouping is the right one to use.

I want to write a short paper for my class that uses Simpson's paradox to examine what anyone who says "the numbers don't lie" could actually be claiming. I would like you to lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing, arranged from the most trivial to the most substantive. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is false, and whether Simpson's paradox poses a specific problem for it. I am especially interested in which reading is being invoked when someone uses the slogan to dismiss the possibility that a single dataset can legitimately support two contradictory conclusions — since that move seems to be the one that Simpson's paradox most directly threatens. I want to walk into the paper knowing exactly which version of the claim I am arguing against and which versions I can grant without giving up my point.
