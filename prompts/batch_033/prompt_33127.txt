Two people in my philosophy of science reading group got into an argument last week that I keep replaying, and I'd like a referee. We were supposed to be discussing Bayesian confirmation theory and the problem of old evidence. Marcus opened with what he called the textbook point: if you already know some fact E, then your credence in E is 1, so P(H | E) = P(H), and conditioning on something you're certain of cant move your credence in a hypothesis H. So, he said, old evidence never confirms anything in the strict Bayesian sense; the feeling that it does is just a leftover from the fact that were imagining not already knowing E.

Elena pushed back that this is a reductio of the Bayesian account, not a discovery about confirmation. She used the way South America and Africa look like they fit together: people had noticed that for ages before plate tectonics, and when the theory arrived it clearly got a boost from that known fact. If a formal theory of confirmation says that boost is impossible, she said, then the formal theory is the thing in trouble, not the intuition. Marcus replied that there are standard fixes. You keep E's probability at 1 in your actual state, but you ask what your credence would have been if you didn't know E, and then you check whether H makes E likelier relative to that counterfactual prior; if P(E | H) > P(E) in that imagined state, then the old evidence confirms H after all. Elena came back that this is too easy, because there is no unique way to subtract your knowledge of E, and different subtractions give different amounts of confirmation, so the fix only works by smuggling in an arbitrary choice. Marcus then said maybe we should not conditionalize on E at all: what scientists actually learn when a new theory is proposed is that H entails E (or at least that H explains E), and that is a new fact with probability less than 1, so it can do genuine Bayesian work. Elena said that just relocates the puzzle: now you need a prior over propositions like the proposition that H entails E, and as soon as you learn that proposition it becomes old evidence too, so the same problem reappears one level up.
