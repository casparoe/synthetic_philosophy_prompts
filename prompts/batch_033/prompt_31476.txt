"In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, Kuhn argues that paradigms are incommensurable, meaning there is no neutral, shared standard by which two paradigms can be measured against each other to determine which is objectively better. First, semantic incommensurability: the central terms of a paradigm — like 'mass' in Newtonian vs. relativistic physics — change their meaning, so that proponents of different paradigms literally talk past each other when they use the same words. Second, methodological incommensurability: the two paradigms disagree about what counts as a good scientific explanation or what problems are worth solving. Kuhn describes this as a shift in the standards of what counts as a legitimate scientific problem. Third, perceptual incommensurability: after a paradigm shift, scientists inhabit a different world — they see different things when they look at the same phenomena. Kuhn's famous example is the pendulum: where Aristotelians saw a heavy body seeking its natural place through constrained fall, Galileans saw a regular oscillatory motion.

Because there is no shared standard, Kuhn argues that paradigm choice is not governed by a logical algorithm or decision procedure. Scientists must rely on persuasion, rhetorical appeals, and eventually generational turnover to settle disputes between paradigms. This means that paradigm choice is ultimately irrational — or at least arational — since there is no way to demonstrate that one paradigm is objectively better than another.
