I sat in on a fight between two people in my department last week and I've been turning it over since, and I still can't sort out who was right about which part.

The background: they were arguing about whether a particular mutual fund would beat the S&P 500 over the next five years. Amara had read a pile of analyses and was fairly confident it would. Ben had read the same pile and was fairly confident it wouldn't. They both agreed they were peers on this — similiar background, same evidence, neither one obviously better at this kind of judgment. Then the argument stopped being about the fund and became about what they should do given that they disagree.

(1) Amara said: when you find out that someone just as smart and informed as you looked at the same evidence and came out on the other side, the only non-arrogant thing is to move your confidence toward theirs. If I just stay put, I'm treating my own judgment as special.

(2) Ben said: but I have my reasons. I can see the considerations that support my side, and your disagreeing doesn't add any new fact about the fund — it just tells me something about you. My evidence for my conclusion hasn't changed.

(3) Amara said: the disagreement is itself evidence. It's higher-order evidence that at least one of us mishandled the first-order evidence. Ignoring it is like two people adding the same column of numbers, getting different answers, and each just saying "well, I did my arithmetic carefully." You can't both be rational staying put.

(4) Ben said: but if that rule is right, it applies to itself. There are people just as smart as you who reject conciliationism — which is the very view you're using on me right now. So by your own rule you should be a lot less confident in the rule, which means you should be a lot less confident that I have to move toward you. Your view saws off the branch it's sitting on.

(5) Amara said: that's not a sawing-off, it's just what it looks like to apply the rule consistently. When peers disagree about the rule, the rule says to move toward them, and if that leaves you unsure, that's the honest answer. You don't get to exempt your own favorite principle from its own application.

(6) Ben said: no, it's worse than that. If applying the rule to itself leaves you unsure of the rule, then you can't go back and use the rule to justify having moved in our original argument. And if you don't move in the original case, you're back to trusting your own first-order reasoning — which is exactly what I was doing. So your position collapses into mine.

(7) Amara said: maybe I was overstating it. I don't need equal weight — I only said move toward, not split the difference. How far you move could depend on how independent your assessments were, how much you've checked the reasoning, that kind of thing. The basic point is just that peer disagreement should shake you some.

(8) Ben said: but now you owe me a number, or at least a principle for the number. Once you admit it isn't equal weight, any particular amount looks arbitrary — it's just how much confidence you're willing to hand over before you start feeling stubborn.

At that point they turned to me and I couldnt referee it. So: go through this exchange and say who's right about what at each step, and what both of them are missing. Don't just pick a winner — rate each move and explain what a defensible position would actually look like after all eight moves. And write the whole thing in American English.
