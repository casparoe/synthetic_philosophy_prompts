I'm in my high school's philosophy club, and we've spent the last few meetings on L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience. I'm supposed to present my own position next week, and I want to make sure it's internally consistent before I stand up in front of everyone. Here's what I've landed on:

(1) Paul is right that some experiences are genuinely transformative in both senses: you can't know what they're like from the outside (epistemically transformative), and undergoing them changes what you value (personally transformative).

(2) Because of (1), standard expected utility theory can't handle these decisions — you can't assign utilities to outcomes whose value you can't assess from your current perspective, and you can't know your future preferences well enough to weigh them.

(3) But testimony from people who've undergone the experience is still a legitimate input to deliberation: it tells you something about the range of possible outcomes and what people on the other side actually care about, even if it doesn't tell you which outcome you'd personally get.

(4) The fact that most people who make a certain transformative choice report being glad they did is a genuine reason to make that choice — it's not just a comforting story or a rationalization after the fact.

(5) At the same time, someone who declines a transformative experience that most people are glad they had isn't making a mistake — their choice can be fully rational, and they don't owe anyone an apology for it.

I can feel that some of these are pulling against each other, but I'm not sure which tensions are real and which just look like tensions. (2) says EU theory can't handle these decisions, but (4) seems to be offering a reason of some kind — if it's not an expected-utility reason, what kind of reason is it, and does that conflict with (2)? And (3) says testimony is useful for deliberation, but (5) says you can rationally disregard what most people tell you — does that mean the testimony wasn't actually doing normative work? Go through the commitments pair by pair, distinguish the genuine tensions from the merely apparent ones, and rank the real problems by severity. Then tell me what I'd need to revise to make the whole set consistent, and what the cheapest fix would be.
