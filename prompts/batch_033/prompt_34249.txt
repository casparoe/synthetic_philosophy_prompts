I've been thinking about reasons a lot lately, ever since my brother hit a rough patch. He's depressed—genuinely doesn't want to get better, says he doesn't see the point, won't see a therapist, won't try medication. And I keep telling him he has a reason to get help. But then I catch myself: what does "has a reason" even mean when he's got no motivation pointing that way? So I started going through cases, trying to figure out what my own gut says about when someone has a reason to do something, and here's what I've got:

My brother: has a reason to seek help, even though he has no desire to get better and no desire for anything that getting better would lead to. I'm sure about this one, but it's the hardest to defend.

A friend who wants to be a good parent but doesn't know that reading to her kid at night helps with language development: she has a reason to read to her kid. Easy—the motivation is already there, she just needs the facts.

Someone who wants to go to a concert but forgot where it is: has a reason to check the ticket. Same deal as the parent case.

A guy with zero sympathy, genuinely doesn't care about anyone's suffering, and is considering torturing someone for fun: I think he has a reason not to. Even though nothing in his motivational set connects to not torturing.

Someone who grew up in an isolated cult, has no desire for autonomy, and is content: I think she has a reason to leave. But I'm less confident about this one than the torture case.

My uncle, who wants to quit smoking and knows it's bad for him, but just can't be bothered: he has a reason to quit. Straightforward internal case.

A talented musician who genuinely wants to be a musician but has been convinced by a jealous rival that she has no talent, so she gives up: I think she has a reason to keep going. But is this internal or external? She has the desire, she just has a false belief. So maybe internal? But then what about false beliefs that aren't just factual errors—like someone who "knows" deep down they're worthless?

And then the one that really trips me up: someone who has a single, self-destructive desire—say, to drink themselves to death—and no countervailing desires at all. Do they have a reason not to? I want to say yes, but I can't see how it's internal when the only relevant desire points the other way, and if it's external I'm not sure what makes it a reason for *them* as opposed to just a reason period.

I guess what I'm asking is: is there a principle that captures all or most of my verdicts here, and if not, which ones do I have to give up? What does the principle say about the self-destructive desire case? And am I basically running something like Williams' internal reasons view with a bunch of ad hoc exceptions, or is there a cleaner way to carve it?
