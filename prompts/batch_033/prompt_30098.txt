Over the course of writing fiction I have repeatedly run up against the same difficulty, and I have begun to suspect that it is not merely a novelist's problem. When I try to describe a character's sensory life, I find that first-person claims about an experience's changing or staying the same have no anchor. Take two instances. A character of mine returns every September to the same garden; she reports that the smell of wet box hedge has not changed, that her memory of it is as vivid as ever, and that she has not altered her standards for pleasantness, and yet the smell is somehow different to her now. A second instance, which I later learned had been discussed in philosophy, concerns two coffee tasters. One finds a familiar coffee's taste altered even though his gustatory abilities test as unchanged; the other finds the taste unaltered but no longer likes it; and both insist that only they can judge the taste as it is for them. In both cases, every possible explanation—the object changed, the person's memory changed, the person's standards changed, or the experience itself changed—is consistent with everything the person can say or do. The bare assertion that the experience itself has changed seems to do no independent work.

What I want is not a reading list but the precise name, if one exists, for this configuration: the use of such trans-temporal or interpersonal comparisons of sensory experience to argue that there is no fact of the matter about the experience's qualitative character, because the introspective report cannot be checked against anything beyond itself. I know the broad term qualia; I do not know whether the configuration I have described has a name of its own, how it differs from ordinary scepticism about memory on one side and from the claim that qualia are ineffable on the other, and whether the coffee-taster case is an instance of it or something narrower. If no established name exists, propose one and define its boundaries.
