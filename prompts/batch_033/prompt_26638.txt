I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Rationality and Strategy in the Nuclear Age" — about 16 students, mostly philosophy and political science majors, one 90-minute session. We have read Schelling's *The Strategy of Conflict* and *Arms and Influence*, and we have discussed the credibility problem in deterrence theory at length: a threat to retaliate with nuclear weapons is not credible if carrying it out would be suicidal, but if the threat is not credible, it will not deter. Schelling's claim that the power to bind oneself can be a source of strength, and that appearing irrational can be rational, has come up repeatedly, but I can tell from discussion that students find it clever rather than true — they can state the argument but they do not feel its force.

Design an in-class game that makes them experience the credibility paradox directly: they should discover, through their own choices and outcomes, that the player who can credibly commit to an apparently self-destructive course of action systematically does better than the player who cannot. The game should also give them a feel for brinkmanship — the idea that escalating a crisis means creating a shared risk of catastrophe that neither side fully controls, and that this shared risk, not the certainty of retaliation, is what makes threats credible in practice.

The game should work with 16 students (8 pairs), require no special materials beyond paper, something to write with, and perhaps a coin or a phone for randomisation, and fit within about 50 minutes of activity followed by a 30-minute debrief (the remaining 10 minutes are for settling in and transitions). No real money or stakes beyond the game's internal scoring. The debrief should map specific moments in the game to specific concepts from the reading — the credibility problem, the rationality of irrationality, the threat that leaves something to chance, the distinction between deterrence and compellence — and should surface the backward-induction logic that drives the base game so that students see why commitment changes the strategic situation. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I want the idea to show up in what happens during play, not in a lecture afterwards: by the time I name the concepts in the debrief, students should already have felt them.
