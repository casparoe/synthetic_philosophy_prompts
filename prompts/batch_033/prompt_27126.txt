I'm a journalist working on a piece about how humans (and machines) induce concepts from just a handful of examples—the kind of thing Bongard problems were designed to probe. I've been making up little puzzles for myself, trying to notice what my own mind does, and I've ended up with a set of verdicts that I can't quite unify. I'm hoping you can help me find the principle behind them, or tell me which verdicts I need to give up.

Here are the cases. In each one I give you a few positive examples (things that fit the concept) and a few negative ones (things that don't), and my intuitive verdict on what the concept is.

**Case A.** Positives: {2, 4, 8, 16, 32}. Negatives: {3, 5, 7, 9, 10}. My verdict: the concept is "powers of two." Even though "even numbers" fits all the positives and excludes all the negatives, I don't think that's what's going on. "Powers of two" feels like the right induction.

**Case B.** Positives: {abb, aabbb, aaabbb}. Negatives: {ab, aabb, abbb, baa}. My verdict: the concept is "n copies of 'a' followed by n+1 copies of 'b', for some n ≥ 1." Not "more b's than a's," not "starts with a and ends with b," not "contains exactly one a-to-b transition." All those simpler rules fit the data too, but I reject them.

**Case C.** Positives: a red circle, a blue square, a green triangle. Negatives: a red square, a blue triangle, a green circle. My verdict: the concept is something like "shape and color are correlated in a specific mapping"—but I'm not even sure what mapping I mean, and I notice that "the shape–color pair is not one of the negative examples" is a perfectly good rule that fits the data. That feels like cheating, though.

**Case D.** Positives: a single photo of my friend Emma smiling, a single photo of my friend Emma frowning. That's it—two positives, no negatives given. My verdict: the concept is "Emma," not "Emma making an expression" and not "human face." I feel like I've learned the concept "Emma" from just two images, and the concept would generalize to Emma in any pose, lighting, or expression.

**Case E.** Positives: {1, 3, 6, 10, 15}. Negatives: {2, 4, 7, 11, 16}. My verdict: "triangular numbers." I don't even check "numbers that are 1 mod something" or whatever. The triangular numbers just pop out.

**Case F.** Positives: a chihuahua, a Great Dane, a poodle. Negatives: a wolf, a coyote, a fox. My verdict: "dog." But I'd never say the concept is "domesticated canines weighing under 150 lbs with floppy ears or curly tails in some breeds"—even though that's more extentionaly accurate. The concept "dog" is what I reach for.

**Case G.** Positives: {2, 3, 5, 7, 11}. Negatives: {1, 4, 6, 8, 9}. My verdict: "primes." Fine. But now suppose I add one more positive: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, and also 13. My verdict is still "primes." Nothing changes. Now suppose instead I add 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, and then 15 as a positive and 13 as a negative. Now my verdict flips: suddenly I think the concept might be "odd numbers except 9 and 13" or some gerrymandered thing. But the amount of data is the same—six positives, five negatives—and yet my willingness to stay with "primes" is totally different.

**Case H.** Positives: a sparrow, a robin, a blue jay. Negatives: a bat, a butterfly, a dragonfly. My verdict: "bird." But if you told me the negatives were a penguin, an ostrich, and an emu, I'd still say the positives are birds—but now I'd be confused about what the negatives are doing, since they're also birds. I'd probably reinterpret: maybe the concept is "flying birds" or "perching birds." That means I'm using the negatives to shape the concept, not just the positives.

So here's my problem. I seem to be doing something like: among all concepts consistent with the data, pick the simplest one. But "simplest" is doing a huge amount of work. In Case A, "powers of two" is simpler than "even numbers" in what sense? In Case D, "Emma" is a specific person—that's not simple in any Kolmogorov-complexity sense; it's a massive bundle of visual features. In Case F, "dog" is a natural kind but not the shortest description of the data. In Case G, my concept is stable under additional positives that fit it, but collapses when the data violate it—which looks like I'm not just picking the simplest consistent concept, because "primes" is still simpler than "odd numbers except 9 and 13" even after the new data; I'm doing something else.

What principle of concept induction makes sense of all these verdicts? Is there a single principle, or do I need different ones for different domains? Which of my verdicts can't be saved and why? And I'd be curious what your principle says about a case I haven't mentioned: positives are {a, b, c, ab, bc, abc} and negatives are {ba, ca, cb, cba}—what's the concept there?

If you could put your bottom-line answer in \answer{...} at the very end—just a short label for the principle you end up endorsing, or a one-sentence summary.
