I've spent the last several months working through William James's "The Will to Believe" and W.K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" on my own, and I've also read some of the discussion these essays have generated over the years. What I can't figure out is why the debate hasn't settled into anything resembling a consensus.

The disagreement looks simple on its face. Clifford argues that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence, because our beliefs inevitably affect other people and we bear moral responsibility for the downstream consequences of beliefs we form carelessly. James replies that Clifford's principle only makes sense if we ignore a whole class of cases: genuine options where the hypotheses are live for the believer, the choice is forced because refusing to decide is itself a decision with consequences, and the stakes are momentous. In those cases, James says, our passional and volitional nature may legitimately decide, because waiting for sufficient evidence is not a neutral act — it amounts to choosing to forgo the possible benefits of belief.

Both of these arguments were on the table by 1896. And yet, over a century later, people still divide along roughly the same lines. I've noticed something when I try to apply the debate to concrete situations: I keep bumping into the question of whether we can voluntarily control our beliefs at all. If doxastic voluntarism is false — if we simply find ourselves believing things and cannot choose to believe otherwise — then Clifford's duty and James's right both seem to be demanding something psychologically impossible. If voluntarism is true, or at least partly true, then the debate seems to turn on facts about human psychology that neither James nor Clifford investigated empirically.

So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a normative dispute about epistemic duties that could be settled by better philosophical argument, or is it secretly an empirical question about belief formation that we just haven't gathered the right data for? Or is there some other diagnosis — maybe the two sides mean different things by "belief," or they're answering different questions under a single label? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate forward? Please write in American English, and end with a one-sentence summary as the last line.
