I've been following the long-running debate between advocates of the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption in anthropic reasoning, and it strikes me that the two sides have spent decades talking past each other. The literature is full of each camp restating its own analysis of the same thought experiments—the Doomsday Argument, the Presumptuous Philosopher, Sleeping Beauty, the God's Coin Toss, the Adam and Eve cases—without ever really engaging the other side's core intuition. What the field needs, I think, is a formal adversarial collaboration: a document that a committed SSA theorist and a committed SIA theorist could both sign, laying out exactly what they agree on, where they genuinely diverge, and—crucially—under what conditions each side would change its mind.

Please draft the joint statement that would result from such a collaboration. The document should have the following structure. First, a numbered list of agreed-upon claims. These must be substantive, not truisms: they should cover the shared formal machinery (e.g., the role of reference classes, how Bayesian updating interacts with indexical information, what it means to treat oneself as a random sample), the thought experiments whose validity both camps accept, and any constraints both sides agree an admissible theory of anthropic reasoning must respect. Second, a disagreements table with columns for (i) the issue, (ii) the SSA position phrased so an SSA advocate would sign it, (iii) the SIA position phrased so an SIA advocate would sign it, and (iv) whether both sides regard this as a crux—where settling it would move at least one side. For each crux, add an auxiliary row specifying a concrete consideration, test case, or line of argument that both sides agree bears on that crux, along with each side's prediction about what it would show if pursued fully. Third, a separate section identifying any disagreements that, on closer inspection, turn out to be verbal rather than substantive, with a proposed reformulation that dissolves them. The value of the document depends on avoiding formulations so abstract that neither side feels the weight of what they are conceding: the agreed claims should be precise enough that signing them costs each side something.
