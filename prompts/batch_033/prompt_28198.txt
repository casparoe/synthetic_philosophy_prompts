I'm a PhD student working on diachronic rationality, and I've accumulated a set of commitments that I'd like stress-tested before I build a dissertation chapter around them. Here they are:

(1) Money pump arguments (diachronic Dutch books) do genuine normative work: they establish that certain patterns of preference are irrational, not merely that they lead to bad outcomes or that the agent would regret them.

(2) There is no standing requirement of temporal consistency across a life. It can be perfectly rational to prefer X at 30 and not-X at 50; preferences evolve, and that fact alone is not a rational failing.

(3) If I know now that my preferences will shift in a way that makes me vulnerable to a money pump, I have a reason now to bind my future self. The rationality of Ulysses contracts does not depend on thinking my future self will be irrational — it depends only on my current preferences and my knowledge of future shifts.

(4) Synchronic coherence requirements (transitivity, completeness) and diachronic ones (not being money-pumpable across time slices) are different in kind, and diachronic requirements are less weighty: violating them is a milder rational failing than violating synchronic ones.

(5) Money pump arguments succeed against myopic agents who take each choice in isolation, but a sophisticated agent who sees the full sequence can avoid being pumped by refusing the first trade. This undercuts the claim that the underlying preference pattern itself has been shown irrational.

I am not sure these all hang together, and I am not signalling which I would give up. Please go through them and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Finally, I would like to know: what argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about the ranking you give, and where in your reasoning is failure most likely?
