I'm taking a graduate seminar on self-deception, and our professor has been giving us case-by-case verdicts all semester without telling us what theory she holds. So far, here are her judgments:

1. A man's brother is killed in a climbing accident. The body is recovered and identified, a funeral is held, and the man attends. Yet he continues to speak of his brother in the present tense, leaves voicemails on his brother's phone, and tells friends his brother is traveling. When asked directly, he says he believes his brother is alive. → Self-deception.

2. A woman feels a lump but avoids seeing a doctor for months. She reads articles about how most lumps are benign, reminds herself she is young and healthy, and convinces herself it is nothing. When she finally goes and learns it is malignant, she is genuinely shocked. → Self-deception.

3. A student submits a paper copied almost verbatim from an online source, having convinced himself that changing a few words and rearranging some sentences made it original. When confronted, he is sincerely outraged and insists the work is his own. → Self-deception.

4. A patient in a clinical trial is told explicitly that she is receiving a sugar pill with no active ingredient. She understands this. She reports significant pain relief and says she believes the pill helped her. → Not self-deception.

5. A hiring manager sincerely believes he evaluates candidates purely on merit. Data show he disproportionately hires men. When shown the data, he is genuinely surprised and insists it must reflect real qualification differences he cannot articulate. → Not self-deception.

6. A terminally ill patient accepts his diagnosis, writes a will, and says goodbye to his family. Yet he also tells people he is going to beat the cancer, and when asked, says he genuinely believes he will be the exception. → Self-deception.

Next week we are discussing a new case, and I am supposed to predict her verdict:

A young couple is told by a fertility specialist that their test results indicate they cannot conceive children naturally. The news is delivered clearly, with detailed lab results, and the couple asks intelligent questions that show they understand the findings. They had no prior beliefs about their fertility one way or the other — this is the first information they have received on the topic. Over the following weeks, they focus on the fact that the specialist mentioned a very small error rate for the test, that they have heard stories of couples who beat the odds, and that they are young and healthy otherwise. They convince themselves the diagnosis is probably wrong and decline further treatment. They genuinely believe they will conceive.

I can see at least two theories of self-deception that fit her existing verdicts, and they seem to point in different directions on this new case. I would like you to: (1) identify the two or three most plausible theories that are consistent with her verdicts so far, (2) say what verdict each theory would give for the fertility case and why, (3) give your best guess about her actual verdict with your reasoning for preferring one theory over the others, and (4) propose a further case — not among the ones we have already seen — whose verdict would most cleanly discriminate between the candidate theories. Please also tell me how confident you are in your prediction and which part of your analysis you are least sure about.
