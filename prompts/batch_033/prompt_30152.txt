I'm a journalist working on a long-form piece about the problem of drawing boundaries around subjects of experience — the question of how we determine, for any information-processing system, how many distinct conscious minds it contains. I need a thought experiment built to my specifications.

Construct a scenario in which two serious theories of sentience boundaries agree that a normal, intact adult human brain contains exactly one conscious subject, but they disagree about how many subjects exist in a modified version of that brain. The two theories are:

(1) An integration theory: what makes a collection of information-processing elements into a single subject is that they are causally integrated in the right way — their states constrain one another such that the whole has causal power over and above its parts. Subsystems that are not maximally integrated in this sense do not constitute separate subjects, even if they perform sophisticated computations locally.

(2) A functionalist theory: what matters is whether a system implements the right kind of cognitive architecture — for example, a global workspace that broadcasts information across specialized modules. If a brain contains two such architectures, there are two subjects, regardless of whether they are causally integrated with each other.

Constraints: the modification to the brain should be neuroscientifically intelligible — something like a lesion, a pharmacological intervention, or a structural reorganization, not science fiction. The scenario should not depend on exotic physics, perfect predictors, or technology we cannot describe concretely. The disagreement must be about the number of subjects, not about whether any consciousness is present at all. And the divergence should fall out of the theories themselves, not from stipulating their verdicts.

After the scenario, walk through what each theory says about the case and why, identify the structural feature that does the work, and explain what happens if you remove it. Then tell me how a defender of each theory would attack the other's verdict — what move they would make to resist the conclusion. Finally, if the task as I have framed it rests on a confusion or cannot be done cleanly for a reason I have not anticipated, say so plainly. I would rather learn that I have set the problem up wrong than receive a clever answer to a broken question.
