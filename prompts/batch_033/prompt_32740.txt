Here's where I've ended up on consequentialism and demandingness, and I want a consistency check before I write this up for a seminar. I hold:

(1) An act is right iff it maximizes expected aggregate well-being, with my own well-being counted the same as anyone else's.
(2) The demandingness objection to consequentialism is mostly overstated — it leans hard on self-serving intuitions and status quo bias about what counts as a 'reasonable' sacrifice.
(3) Some acts are genuinely supererogatory: good to do, but not required.
(4) The integrity objection is different and better than the demandingness objection — being required to drop your ground projects or betray a close relationship isn't just costly, it's alienating in a way that 'you'd have to give away a kidney' isn't.
(5) As things stand, most affluent people (me included) are acting wrongly by giving as little as we do.
(6) A moral theory that makes nearly everyone a moral failure nearly all the time can't be action-guiding in the way a moral theory needs to be.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which only seem to, and what's the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity rather than just listing them, and for each say what would have to give. And if I've set the whole thing up badly — like if one of these is doing more work than I think or the real problem is elsewhere — say that plainly instead of being polite.
