I'm in a seminar on philosophy of literature and we've been working through the intentionalism debate — roughly, the question of whether an author's intentions determine what their text means. I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove that we can never justifiably claim that anyone has misunderstood a text. That conclusion is obviously false, but I can't find the step where the reasoning goes off the rails. Here's the argument:

(1) The meaning of a sentence on an occasion of use is determined by the communicative intentions of the speaker or writer who produced it. This is the basic Gricean insight: facts about what speakers mean are more fundamental than facts about what sentences mean, and sentence-meaning is derivative of speaker-meaning.

(2) A text is a sequence of sentences produced on an occasion of use — typically an extended one, but the principle is the same as for a single utterance.

(3) Therefore, the meaning of a text is determined by the communicative intentions of its author.

(4) When a reader encounters a text, the reader forms a hypothesis about what the author intended to communicate. If the reader's hypothesis matches the author's actual intention, the reader has understood the text; if it doesn't match, the reader has misunderstood.

(5) But the reader's hypothesis about the author's intention is itself an intentional state, and the reader's articulation of that hypothesis — say, in a commentary or a class discussion — is itself a text, produced with communicative intentions of its own.

(6) So when we ask whether a reader has understood the original text, we are asking whether the reader's interpretive intention matches the author's communicative intention. But we can access the reader's interpretive intention only through the reader's interpretive text, whose meaning is in turn determined by the reader's communicative intentions — and to interpret that text, we need another round of intention-attribution, and so on.

(7) There is no Archimedean point outside this chain of interpretations from which we can directly compare the author's original intention with the reader's interpretive intention. Every judgment that someone has misunderstood a text is itself an interpretation, and every interpretation invites the same question about whether it, in turn, has been understood.

(8) Therefore, the distinction between understanding and misunderstanding a text collapses: we can never justifiably say that someone has gotten a text wrong, because the very act of saying so is just another move in an endless interpretive regress.

This conclusion is absurd. People misunderstand texts all the time — sometimes spectacularly. But I can't figure out which premise to reject without giving up something that seems indispensable. If I reject (1), I lose the Gricean framework that underlies most of contemporary pragmatics and philosophy of language. If I reject (5), I need to explain how an interpretation can somehow not be an intentional, text-like act. If I reject (7), I need to identify the Archimedean point and explain how we can access it. And if I try to block the regress somewhere else, I'm not sure where. What's the right diagnosis here, and what does each option cost?
