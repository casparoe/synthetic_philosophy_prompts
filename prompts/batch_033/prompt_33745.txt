I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on precommitment and democratic theory, and I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think by working through cases. I'd like you to look at my verdicts and tell me what principle, if any, captures the pattern — or whether some of my verdicts need to go.

Here are the cases:

1. **Ulysses.** Ulysses instructs his crew to tie him to the mast and to ignore any contrary orders until they have sailed past the sirens. When they pass the sirens, he begs to be released. The crew refuses. I think the crew acts rightly, and Ulysses' earlier instruction is binding on his later self.

2. **Advance directive.** A woman in her fifties, in excellent mental health, signs an advance directive stating that if she develops severe dementia, she refuses antibiotics for life-threatening pneumonia. At seventy, she has moderate dementia. She seems content, enjoys visits from her grandchildren, and when asked, says she wants to live. She contracts pneumonia. Her family wants antibiotics administered. I think the advance directive should be honored — the treatment should be withheld.

3. **Legislative entrenchment.** A legislature passes a statute requiring a two-thirds supermajority to repeal certain environmental protections. The statute explicitly says these provisions are binding on future legislatures. The next legislature, controlled by a different party, has a simple majority and votes to repeal the protections without meeting the supermajority threshold. I think the repeal is invalid — the supermajority requirement binds the later legislature.

4. **Constitutional eternity clause.** A constitution contains an eternity clause stating that certain principles — say, the federal structure of the state — can never be amended, not even by the ordinary amendment procedure. Three generations later, a broad democratic movement, supported by a strong majority, seeks to amend those principles. I think the eternity clause is not binding on the later generation, however noble its intentions were.

5. **No-divorce contract.** A couple, both competent adults, signs a legally binding agreement not to divorce for twenty years, waiving their right to petition for divorce during that period. After ten years, one spouse becomes emotionally abusive — not physically violent, but controlling and demeaning. The other spouse wants to divorce. I think the contract is not binding under these circumstances, and the divorce should be granted.

6. **Ulysses with a release valve.** Suppose Ulysses had instead instructed his crew: "If I beg to be untied, keep me tied for one hour, and then untie me." He begs, and after one hour the crew unties him. I think this precommitment is binding, and the crew acts rightly — and moreover, this is a better precommitment than the original, precisely because it includes a built-in release condition.

7. **Amendment of the amendment procedure.** A constitution specifies that it can be amended by a three-fourths majority. A later generation, with a simple majority but not three-fourths, wants to amend the amendment procedure itself to require only a simple majority. I think the amendment is invalid — they must follow the existing procedure even to change it.

I'm fairly confident about cases 1, 5, 6, and 7. I'm less confident about 3. And I keep going back and forth on whether 2 and 4 can be reconciled — I feel the pull of treating them alike, since both involve a past agent trying to bind a future agent on a matter of deep importance, but my verdicts go in opposite directions.

What I'm hoping you can do: state a principle (or a small set of candidate principles) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, explain which cases motivate which clauses of the principle, and tell me honestly which verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. If your principle forces me to overturn one of my verdicts, say which one and why. Also, please say what your principle predicts about a case I haven't listed: a person who, while sober, hands their car keys to a friend and says, "Don't give these back to me tonight no matter what I say." Later, drunk, they demand the keys back, insisting they are fine to drive.

Please write your answer in American English.
