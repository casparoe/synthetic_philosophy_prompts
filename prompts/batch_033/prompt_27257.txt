I serve on the clinical ethics committee at a small hospital. We review maybe a dozen cases a year — treatment refusals, family members demanding we hide a diagnosis, triage decisions in a bad flu season — and our recommendations have to be defensible to the medical board if someone complains. We meet for an hour, and most of us are clinicians, not philosophers. Our current method is a one-page list of principles, and every meeting devolves into the same argument: one member says the principle settles it, and another says the principle is just a rule of thumb and this case is one of the exceptions. I gather philosophers have actually fought about this under the names generalism and particularism, and the particularists say something like: a feature that counts against an act in one case can count in favor of it in another, so there are no exceptionless moral principles to fall back on. I want you to turn that debate into a procedure the committee can actually run in an hour. What questions do we ask, in what order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each? Where should the procedure stop with a no, and where does it have to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than fact? Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure cannot do — in particular whether writing a procedure at all is already a concession to generalism. Then run the procedure on two cases: a patient with capacity refusing a blood transfusion on religious grounds, and a nurse who looked at a patient's chart in order to warn the patient's partner about an HIV diagnosis. Make sure at least one of those comes out the way the committee would not like. And please, no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables — continuous prose only, the way you'd write an email.
