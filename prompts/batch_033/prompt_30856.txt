I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in formal epistemology and social choice, and I am writing the take-home essay for the unit on the Condorcet jury theorem. I want a question that cannot be answered by restating the theorem or the usual slogans about wise crowds; it should force students to work with the model and say what the theorem does and does not establish about majority rule. Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 900 words, and a marking scheme for 20 marks, with the question calibrated for about 90 minutes of writing. The question should have three parts and should be built around this crux: the inference from 'large groups of independent, better-than-chance voters are almost certainly right' to a defense of majority rule depends on assumptions that the slogan hides. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain, and it should reward answers that do all of the following: (1) state the classical theorem precisely, including the binary-choice setting, the homogeneous-competence assumption, and the independence condition; (2) explain why the independence the theorem needs is conditional on the correct alternative, and why 'the voters do not influence one another' is not sufficient for it — common causes such as a shared news source or common training can correlate votes without any voter influencing another, and this can defeat the convergence result; (3) distinguish the probability that the majority is correct under the model from the probability that a particular observed majority is correct, and say what extra information would be needed to obtain the latter; and (4) say why the theorem, even where its assumptions hold, does not by itself settle the case for democratic majority rule — it assumes a determinate correct answer, takes the competence assumption as given rather than shown, and its homogeneous-competence assumption rules out cases in which a single known voter is far more reliable and majority rule need not be optimal. The model answer should be accurate enough that a colleague in formal epistemology would not object, and the marking scheme should also identify the specific errors that should cost marks, such as treating independence as merely the absence of strategic interaction or reading the theorem as a claim about the correctness of a given electoral outcome. After the marking scheme, add a brief note saying how confident you are in the model answer and singling out the one point you are least sure of.
