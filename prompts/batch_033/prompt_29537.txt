Two members of my reading group recently spent an evening arguing about Elizabeth Anscombe's protest against Oxford's proposal to award Harry Truman an honorary degree, and neither could convince the other. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange.

Daniel opened with what he called the core of Anscombe's charge: deliberately killing the innocent as a means to one's end is murder, whatever the end is, and the atomic bombings were an instance of exactly that — cities were destroyed in order to kill their civilian populations and thereby force surrender. If one accepts that principle, Truman's order was murder even inside a just war.

Ruth replied that the principle looks compelling only because it brackets the alternatives. An invasion of the home islands was expected to kill far more people, including civilians, on both sides. If the rule forbids killing innocents even to prevent a much larger slaughter, then the rule is not morality but a recipe for paralysis, and Truman chose the lesser evil.

Daniel answered that this is the defense Anscombe anticipated and rejected. The claim that the bombings saved lives cannot be a sufficient justification, because every massacre can be defended by someone who judges that it will save lives in the long run. Besides, the invasion was not the only alternative: the Allies could have modified the demand for unconditional surrender, clarified the Emperor's position, or found another way to demonstrate the weapon. The dilemma was partly self-imposed, and one cannot create a dilemma and then plead necessity.

Ruth said those alternatives were armchair hindsight — a failed demonstration could have hardened resistance, and unconditional surrender had real coalition and postwar reasons behind it. She then pressed on the internal structure of Anscombe's view: Anscombe is not a pacifist and allows that a just war will foreseeably kill some civilians. So the difference between Truman's bomb and a legitimate attack with heavy collateral damage comes down to a claim about what the agent intended, which is too fragile a distinction to support calling one man a murderer and the other a soldier — let alone opposing a degree for a head of state who ended the war.

Daniel replied that this is exactly the distinction the doctrine of double effect exists to draw, and the test is not private psychology but the structure of the act: had the civilians been absent, the bombing would have had no point, so their deaths were the means and not a side effect. That, he said, is what Anscombe means by murder; she was not demanding pacifism, only the admission that the bombings fell outside the limits that even a non-pacifist morality must keep. And the degree was the occasion for her protest precisely because Oxford was set to honor the bombings without debating them.

At that point they asked me to referee, and I found I could not do it fairly. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, where each argument overreaches, and what both of them are missing. I would also like the verdict to be self-critical: tell me which further argument or consideration would most likely change your overall judgment, and where your own reasoning is least secure.
