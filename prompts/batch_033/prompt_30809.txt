I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the last few months working on a piece about fakes, forgeries, replicas, and why originals have the pull they do. Instead of a tidy feature, I've ended up with a pile of my own gut verdicts, and I can't tell whether there's a principle underneath them or whether I'm just carrying around the usual art-world snobberies with better PR. So here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases, reported as honestly as I can. Tell me what principle, if any, captures them — and be straight about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.

1. The molecule-for-molecule copy. A scanner reproduces an old master down to the paint chemistry and the craquelure, and prints a canvas that no instrument can distinguish from the original. The original is insured for tens of millions; the copy would sell for a few thousand. My gut says that gap isn't just a market fact — the copy really is worth less, and anyone who treated the two as interchangeable would be missing something.

2. Van Meegeren's fake Vermeer. Before the reveal, critics called it a major Vermeer; after the reveal, the painted surface hadn't changed, but nobody would call it a great Vermeer again. My gut says the reveal changed something real about the artwork — that it is now a less good painting than it was taken to be — even though I can't point to a single visible property that got worse.

3. The inaudible violin. A modern violin with a fake Stradivarius label, set up so well that professional players in a blind test can't tell it from the real thing. I still think the Strad is worth it's price and the modern one is worth a fraction, and I don't think the players' inability to hear a difference shows they're "really the same instrument." But I can't name any audible property that justifies the gap.

4. The Sol LeWitt-style wall drawing. You buy a certificate and a set of instructions; any trained assistant executes the wall. If the wall gets painted over and redrawn from the instructions, it's still fully the artwork. Here my gut says originals don't matter at all — the work just is the instructions, and the physical marks are replaceable.

5. The posthumous cast. A bronze cast from the sculptor's own mold while she was alive, and an identical cast from the same mold made after her death. I'd pay more for the lifetime cast and I'd feel a difference, but I can't say what the lifetime cast has that the other lacks, besides having been made while she was around.

6. The high-res scan. For actually looking at a painting, a good scan on a good screen gets me most of what I want, and for studying brushwork it might beat standing behind glass. I don't have the "it's really worth less" reaction to the scan that I have to the molecule-for-molecule copy — I just treat the scan as an excellent reproduction. And I can't say why the physical copy in case 1 isn't also just an excellent reproduction.

7. The reattributed portrait. A portrait that hung for a century as a Rembrandt, studied and loved as a Rembrandt, gets reattributed to a competent pupil. The value collapses and the museum moves it to storage. My gut says the museum is right, and that the painting really was less valuable all along — not just that people now know a sad fact. But then the value was never in the painted surface, which didn't change.

8. The better fake. A forger paints a "newly discovered" work in the style of a minor 17th-century Dutch painter, and critics agree it's better than anything that painter actually made. When it's exposed as modern, my gut says it becomes less valuable as an artwork, even though it's still, brushstroke for brushstroke, the best thing ever painted in that style. That strikes me as slightly monstrous: punishing the painting for being too good too late.

9. The forged diary. A forged diary of a Holocaust victim, textually accurate and genuinely moving, used in a museum exhibit. When it's exposed, the museum pulls it. My gut says that's obviously right, and that the artifact is worse — not just less useful as evidence, but worse as a thing. But the text is the same text, and the suffering it describes is real.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? If the principle forces me to revise some of my verdicts, tell me which and why. And tell me what the principle says about a case I genuinely haven't made up my mind on: a lost symphony by a great composer, completed from sketches by a brilliant living composer who writes the missing thirty percent so seamlessly that listeners can't tell where the sketches end. Is the completed symphony a work by the dead composer, by the living one, or something else — and is it great in the way an all-authentic work would be?

One more thing. If the whole request is misframed — if "the value of an original" isn't one thing but several, or I've rigged the cases by how I described them — tell me that flat out. I'd rather be told my list is incoherent than be handed a principle with nine sub-clauses and told it's elegant.
