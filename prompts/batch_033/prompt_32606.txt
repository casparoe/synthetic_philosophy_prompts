I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy and I've been roped onto my university's staff recognition committee. We give out an annual award for going above and beyond — it's public, so getting the category wrong is embarrassing — and every year we have the same fight: one camp wants to give it to someone who just did their job very well under awful conditions, and the other camp wants to give it to someone who did something genuinely optional and costly that nobody could have demanded. We need a reusable proceedure we can run on any nomination — we meet once for ninety minutes to get through a dozen of them, so it can't be a seminar — to decide whether the act is actually supererogatory: good, optional, praiseworthy to do, not blameworthy to omit. We need to distinguish that from merely dutiful, merely permissible, and praiseworthy-but-required. The commitee are not philosophers, so it has to be plain language, but it also has to force the hard distinctions rather than blur them. Give us: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops and rules a nomination out; where it hands off to a judgment call (I'm especially worried about the cost threshold — how much sacrifice before we say nobody could demand it); and say honestly what the procedure cannot do. Then run it on three nominations: a custodian who kept working through a flood; an employee who donated a kidney to a coworker's spouse; and a manager who quietly took a pay cut so her team wouldn't be laid off. I suspect one of these comes out as not supererogatory even though it's the one the committee loves — tell me which and why. End with the three verdicts on one line, in the form \answer{...}, each a short label like supererogatory, dutiful-but-praiseworthy, or judgment-call.
