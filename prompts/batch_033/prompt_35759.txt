I am a novelist at work on a book in which two characters, both sincere egalitarians, argue over whether a third character is owed compensation for a serious disadvantage. One character thinks in the manner of Ronald Dworkin's equality of resources, with its distinction between option luck and brute luck and its hypothetical insurance test. The other thinks in the manner of G. A. Cohen's critique of Dworkin, holding that the only defensible cut is between what a person chose and what merely befell them. I would like a thought experiment I can adapt into their argument.

Please construct a case to the following specifications. The scenario should be realistic, set in an ordinary workplace, family, or artistic milieu, and describable in one paragraph; no science fiction, no contrived gambles, no eccentric stipulations. The disadvantaged character's predicament should trace to a taste, ambition, or commitment acquired through unchosen circumstances, such as an upbringing or formative environment they never selected, which the character now wholeheartedly endorses as central to their identity. The two positions should come apart as follows: the Dworkinian, applying the hypothetical insurance test and the option luck/brute luck distinction, judges that no compensation is owed; the Cohen-style luck egalitarian judges that the disadvantage is bad brute luck and that compensation is owed. The divergence must not arise because either character is relying on a crude or strawman version of the theory; each should be applying a defensible interpretation of it.

After presenting the scenario, walk through what each theory says about it and why it reaches that verdict. Then identify which feature of the case is doing the work, explain what would happen to the disagreement if that feature were removed, and say how a defender of the Dworkinian verdict would attack the case.
