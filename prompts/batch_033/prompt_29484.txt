I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I want to test whether an argument I keep encountering actually survives being restated in the terms I trust. This is not a metaphor exercise: the vocabulary below is how I actually reason about formal systems.

The argument, associated with J. R. Lucas and Roger Penrose, runs roughly as follows. Any machine or formal system that could match human mathematical competence would have to be consistent and sound. Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that for any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic, there is a sentence the system cannot prove but which a human can see to be true. Therefore the human mind cannot be equivalent to any such machine or formal system.

Restate this argument in my working vocabulary: programming languages and their type systems, type checking, formal verification, soundness and completeness of a checker, the halting problem, Rice's theorem, diagonalization, quines and self-reference, self-hosting compilers, and the distinction between a program, its specification, and the checker that verifies one against the other. I want a systematic translation, not an analogy. For each step of the argument, give the nearest computational statement you can make, and mark which parts map cleanly, which you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression in the vocabulary at all. Translate it as charitably as the vocabulary allows; do not rig the translation so the argument conveniently falls apart.

Then give me the residue report. The step I most distrust is the claim that the mind can directly see the truth of the Gödel sentence. In my world, seeing that a program is correct means running a checker I trust against a spec, and the checker's own soundness is exactly the kind of thing that cannot be established from inside and usually just gets pushed to another checker or taken on faith. Translate that worry into the terms of the argument and tell me whether it lands. When the whole anti-mechanist argument is translated, does it survive as the claim that some concrete property of minds defeats any program, or does it collapse into the true but unremarkable statement that for any sound verifier for a sufficiently expressive language, there are true statements about that language it cannot verify — a statement equally true of any program written in that language? Take a definite position on which side of that line the argument falls. Identify the point in your translation where you are least confident, and say what would change your verdict.
