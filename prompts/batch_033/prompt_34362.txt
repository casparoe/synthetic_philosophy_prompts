I've been teaching Berkeley's Three Dialogues in my modern philosophy survey for, god, going on fifteen years now, and every spring semester when we hit the master argument I get the same thing. You know the passage — Philonous challenges Hylas to conceive of a tree existing entirely unconceived by any mind, and when Hylas tries, Philonous pounces: "But you're conceiving of it right now, so it's not unconceived!" The students eat it up, and then they all confidently reproduce the standard objection, which I've dutifully taught them: Berkeley (or Philonous, whatever) is confusing the act of conceiving with the thing conceived. The idea-of-the-tree isn't the tree, and the fact that you need an idea to think about the tree doesn't mean the tree itself depends on being thought about. Russell says something like this somewhere, and it's basically the party line.

But here's the thing. I've been grading these papers for years and I'm starting to wonder whether the standard objection is actually as decisive as everyone thinks. I've got this nagging sense that the objection only works if you buy into a particular picture of how mental representation works — a picture where the act of conceiving and the content conceived are cleanly separable, like a container and the thing contained. And I'm not sure that picture is mandatory, or even especially well-motivated, once you press on it. If you're willing to be a bit more, I don't know, phenomenological about it — if you think that the act of conceiving is partly constitutive of the content, rather than merely a vehicle for it — then the master argument starts looking a lot more interesting.

So here's what I'd like from you. Please argue, as forcefully and carefully as you can, that the standard objection to Berkeley's master argument fails. Not that Berkeley's idealism is true — I'm not asking you to defend the whole system — just that the master argument specifically is more resilient than the textbooks say it is. I want you to map out exactly what assumptions the standard objection relies on, and then show why those assumptions are optional or question-begging in the relevant sense. If you need to draw on stuff about intentionality or mental content from outside the early modern period, that's fine — I'm not fussy about that. Just don't give me a bunch of citations; I'll track down the secondary literature myslef if I decide to write something on this.
