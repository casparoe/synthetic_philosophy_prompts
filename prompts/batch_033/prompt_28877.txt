A book I'm reading on self-reference and meaning offers the following summary of a distinction from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach:

"Every message, Hofstadter argues, has three levels. The inner message is the explicit content — the proposition expressed, the data transmitted. The outer message is a set of implicit signals about how to interpret the inner message: what language it's in, whether it's ironic, what genre conventions govern it. The frame message is simpler still: it's the brute physical fact that a message exists at all — sound waves, marks on paper — which tells you that someone is attempting to communicate. Hofstadter's insight is that meaning depends on all three, and that focusing only on the inner message — as information theory does — misses most of what matters."

Something about this bothers me. I've read parts of GEB and I recall Hofstadter making a sharper point — something about the frame message itself being a kind of message, not just a physical fact, and the outer message being more than metadata. But I'm not confident I'm right, and the book's author is a professional.
