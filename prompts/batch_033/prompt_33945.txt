Question: Describe the color phi phenomenon and explain Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of experience. Does the distinction, together with the phenomenon, show that there is no determinate fact about exactly when a subject's experience occurred? Answer with reference to the multiple drafts model.

Student's answer:

The color phi phenomenon is a classic perceptual illusion. A red dot is flashed at one location, and then a green dot is flashed at a nearby location shortly after. Subjects don't report two separate dots; they report a single dot that moves smoothly from the first location to the second and changes from red to green about halfway along its path. The odd thing is that the halfway point comes before the green dot is physically present, so the brain seems to have retroactively filled in the motion and the color change and presented them as one continuous experience.

Dennett uses this to set up two possible accounts. On the Orwellian account, the subject first has a conscious experience of a stationary red dot, and then after the green dot arrives the brain rewrites the memory of that experience, so the subject recalls having seen a moving dot that changed color. It's Orwellian because the record is altered after the fact, like the Ministry of Truth. On the Stalinesque account, the brain delays the red dot's entry into consciousness until after the green dot has arrived, so the subject's conscious experience was always of a moving, color-changing dot. It's Stalinesque because the show is staged before the audience sees it, like a show trial.

Dennett says the two accounts are empirically equivalent: no introspective report, reaction-time measurement, or brain scan could tell them apart. But I think even if no experiment can separate them, we can still ask which is the better explanation, and Stalinesque is more plausible. The Orwellian account requires a special memory-rewriting mechanism after the fact, for which we have no independent evidence, whereas the Stalinesque account only requires a brief delay in processing, which is a known kind of thing. So Stalinesque wins on parsimony.

On the multiple drafts model, there is no single place or moment where an experience becomes conscious, so the question of exactly when the subject experienced the moving dot is a bad question and should be dropped. So the lesson isn't that there's a determinate timing we can't find out; it's that the demand for a determinate timing rests on the Cartesian Theater picture.
