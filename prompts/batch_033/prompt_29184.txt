I retired a few years ago from a career in physical oceanography, and I have since been spending part of my week on decision theory and formal epistemology with a reading circle of former scientists and engineers. We have been working through the two-envelope problem, and I have been struck by how different it feels when one is actually holding an envelope: the argument that one should always switch, whatever one finds inside, is extraordinarily seductive, and yet acting on it seems obviously unwise. I would like to run a session that lets the group feel that seduction directly, before any labels or diagnoses are put on the table, and then lets them locate the exact point where the reasoning goes wrong.

Please design a 90-minute structured activity for about fourteen people, meeting in person, using plain envelopes and small real stakes—no more than a few dollars per person, with the money returned or redistributed at the end. The activity should be arranged so that participants themselves generate the always-switch argument, perhaps by playing several rounds under a fixed but only partially revealed rule for filling the envelopes and then comparing their decisions, before the problem is named. The debrief should map each moment in the activity onto a premise of the argument: the claim that the other envelope is equally likely to contain half or double what one sees; the expected-value calculation that yields a gain from switching; the apparent symmetry between the two envelopes; and the background assumption that there is a probability distribution over possible amounts under which, whatever one finds, half-or-double remains equally likely. Please make the design distinguish resolutions that reject the conditional-probability claim from those that reject the unconditional expectation, and say what each repair costs. You should also say explicitly which feature of the real paradox a finite-stakes version cannot reproduce—for example, anything turning on unbounded or improper distributions—and how the facilitator should supply that missing feature without lecturing.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—participants refusing to switch out of caution, the filling rule being inferred too quickly, the discussion collapsing into the Monty Hall problem, and so on—and how I should handle each. I would rather the group leave with a precise sense of where the paradox lives than with a slogan about it.
