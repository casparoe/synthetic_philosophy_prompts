I was talking with a friend the other day about planning for future states where you know you’ll be forgetful, and we invented a little scenario that seems to generate a genuine puzzle, and I’m wondering if this thing already has a name in decision theory or game theory. Picture a driver on a straight road with two identical‑looking intersections in a row. He’s absent‑minded in a very specific way: he can’t remember whether he’s already passed an intersection, so whenever he arrives at one, he has no idea if it’s the first or the second. His goal is to turn off at the second intersection, where he gets a big reward (say payoff 4). Turning off at the first intersection gives him nothing (payoff 0), and driving straight through both intersections gives him a small consolation payoff of 1. Before he starts the trip, he can commit to a randomized strategy—the only thing he can control is the probability that he’ll turn off whenever he sees an intersection. Working it out, the optimal strategy from this ex ante (planning) perspective is to turn off with probability 1/3 each time he sees an intersection, and continue with probability 2/3. That yields a nice expected payoff. But here’s where it gets weird: once he’s actually sitting at an intersection, not knowing which one it is, he can reflect on his own strategy. If he tries to recompute what he should do right now, taking into account that he might be at the first intersection or the second, the reasoning seems to pull toward a different probability of turning off—something like 1/2 instead of 1/3—because his belief about which intersection he’s at depends on his own strategy in a circular way. So there’s this rift between the plan that’s optimal beforehand and the action that feels rational in the moment, even though his preferences and information haven’t changed; it’s just the lack of memory that creates a kind of entanglement between his current self and his past possible selves.

I’m sure this isn’t just garden‑variety time inconsistency (like hyperbolic discounting or changing tastes), because here the agent’s preferences are perfectly stable, and the conflict arises purely from imperfect recall and the loopy way that belief and strategy interact. It’s also not the sort of thing you see in standard forgetfulness puzzles where you just have to write a note to yourself; the very act of recomputing what’s rational seems to generate two different answers depending on when you ask the question. Is there an established name for this specific decision‑theoretic configuration, and if so, what’s the core insight that reconciles the planning‑optimal strategy with the in‑the‑moment reasoning? If it doesn’t have a canonical name, I’d love to know what the nearest concepts are and how this puzzle carves out its own niche—I’m less interested in a bare label than in understanding exactly what kind of phenomenon this is.
