I serve on the research ethics committee at a large university. Our committee reviews protocols involving animal subjects, but we are increasingly asked to rule on cases that fall outside the standard vertebrate framework: experiments on cephalopods, honeybee colonies, neural organoids, and even complex AI systems that some researchers argue may warrant moral consideration. Our existing guidelines assume a binary — either a creature is a protected subject or it is not — and that binary is breaking down. We need a decision procedure we can apply case by case to determine what level of moral consideration an entity deserves, where the answer might be "none," "full protection," or some intermediate status with specific constraints (e.g., "you may use them but must minimize suffering" or "you may use them but must obtain consent from a surrogate decision-maker").

Please write a reusable decision procedure grounded in the philosophical literature on sentience as a matter of degree — the idea that moral status is not binary but comes in gradations, and that different capacities (nociception, affective experience, self-awareness, diachronic identity, etc.) contribute differently to an entity's moral claim. The procedure should take the form of a sequence of questions a committee member can ask about a proposed research subject, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a determination, and where it explicitly flags that the question cannot be settled by the procedure and must be handed to a human judgment call. The procedure must be usable by committee members who are not philosophers — biologists, veterinarians, legal counsel — in the course of a one-hour meeting. Then apply the procedure to three cases: an experiment on adult octopuses involving temporary confinement and tissue sampling; an experiment that trains a large language model using reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the model displays behavior that some lab members describe as "distress signals"; and an experiment on a slime mold that involves exposing it to aversive chemical stimuli. For each case, walk through the procedure step by step and state what outcome it yields. Finally, identify at least two kinds of cases where the procedure will systematically misfire or give an answer that the committee should not trust, and explain why.

Please write the answer in continuous prose, the way one would write a report or a long memo — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Use American English throughout.
