I teach a grad seminar on disagreement, and I've been reading the more formal end of things. There's a move in those models I keep wanting a name for, and if it doesn't have one, I'd like a label I can put on the syllabus.

The setup is always like this. Two agents begin with the same probability distribution over possible states of the world before either of them has seen any private evidence. Then each observes a different private signal, each updates by Bayes's rule, and they announce their posterior probabilities. There's a well-known theorem that, once those announcements become common knowledge and the agents are rational and know each other to be rational, their posteriors have to coincide. I'm not asking about that result. What I want named is the initial modeling decision: the requirement that everyone start from the same prior, so that any difference in final belief must trace entirely to a difference in the information they got, never to a difference in how they were inclined to weigh possibilities before any evidence came in. It's often treated as though there is simply no question to ask at the starting point — as if two rational people could not even in principle start from different priors, and any difference in final belief that isn't traceable to information is a sign somebody messed up.

Concretely: two clinicians could disagree about whether a patient has a disease because one saw the blood test and the other didn't, and the framwork is completely comfortable with that. But if they both saw the same test and still disagree because one started out with a much higher baseline for how common the disease is in this population, the framework seems to want to call that a mistake — or at least to have nothing to say about it except that at least one of them must have been irrational somewhere.

So: does this starting-point assumption have an established name, and is it a claim about what rationality requires or just a convenience for keeping the models tractable? How exactly does it relate to the theorem that rational agents can't agree to disagree — is it a premise of the theorem, or something stronger than the theorem needs? And how is it different from the principle of indifference, which also sounds like it's dictating a unique rational starting point? If there's no standard name, what should I call it so my students dont confuse it with common knowledge of rationality, which is a different piece of the setup?
