I teach a decision theory unit in an undergrad philosophy of economics course — about 24 students, one 75-minute session — and I want to spend it on the St. Petersburg paradox. Last time I taught it I just walked through the arithmetic on the board (infinite expected value, nobody would actually pay more than twenty bucks, etc.) and the students nodded along but the point clearly didn't land: it stayed a curiosity about a coin-flip game instead of a live problem about how expected value relates to what you should actually pay or choose. So I'd like a runnable in-class game or auction that makes them feel the gap themsleves before I name the paradox. Design the activity with actual rules, materials (coins, slips, a cap on rounds so it fits the time), some way of paying out that doesn't require real money — classroom scrip or points toward participation is fine — and a simple way for them to record what they'd pay to play. The doubling payoff can't go unbounded in a real classroom, so include a truncation rule and then use the debrief to show how the infinite version is the limit of what they just experienced. I want the debrief to map what actually happened — the bids, the variance, the role of the bankers deep pockets, the difference between the average payout and what you'd actually stake — onto the distinction between expected value and what people are willing to pay. Also tell me what tends to go wrong when people run this kind of thing: I'm imagining either someone will want to play forever because 'the math says so', or the class will just refuse to bid above a dollar and the auction will be boring. How do I run it so neither of those kills the point, and is there anything about the paradox itself the activity can't reproduce no matter how well it goes?
