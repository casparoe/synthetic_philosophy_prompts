I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a chapter about Dennett's account of the self as a center of narrative gravity — the idea that the self is not a substantial entity but an abstraction, a theorist's fiction posited to make sense of the ongoing stream of behavior and self-narration, much as a center of gravity is a useful fiction in physics rather than a thing one could point to. I've been trying to think through whether this view can do justice to the phenomenology of self-deception and retrospective self-revision, and it occurred to me that Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* might serve as a kind of test case. The novel is narrated by Stevens, an aging butler who has spent his life subordinating his own desires, emotions, and moral judgments to a narrative of professional dignity and unwavering loyalty to his employer, Lord Darlington. As he drives across the English countryside, he revisits his memories and gradually, incompletely, comes to see that the narrative he has lived by was a form of evasion — not only of Darlington's political entanglements but of his own emotional life, particularly his relationship with the housekeeper Miss Kenton.

What I'm trying to work out is whether the novel, read closely, supports or challenges Dennett's view. On one hand, Stevens seems to be a paradigmatic case of a self constituted entirely through narrative: there is no "real" Stevens beneath the performance, only the story he tells himself and others, and when that story begins to crack under the pressure of memory, what emerges is not a hidden authentic self but something more like grief at the story's insufficiency. On the other hand, the novel's devastating power seems to depend on the reader's sense that there is a person behind the narrative — a self that was damaged by the story it told itself, and whose suffering at the end is real in a way that Dennett's deflationary account might struggle to capture. I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical view seriously, that doesn't reduce Ishiguro to a mere illustration of Dennett but also doesn't dismiss Dennett as obviously inadequate to the literary experience. What does the novel show about narrative selfhood that Dennett's account gets right, and where does it resist the account in ways that might point to a genuine philosophical difficulty? I'd especially appreciate attention to specific scenes — the conference at Darlington Hall, the encounter with the village doctor, the final meeting with Miss Kenton — where the relationship between Stevens's narrative and whatever lies beneath it comes into sharpest focus.
