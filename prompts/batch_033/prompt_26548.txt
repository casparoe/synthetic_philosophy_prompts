I want your credence that the repugnant conclusion is true. By "the repugnant conclusion" I mean the claim that for any possible population of lives all worth living, there exists some much larger possible population whose existence would be better in impartial terms, even if every life in that larger population is barely worth living—lives that contain only the smallest net positive balance of well-being over suffering. I am not asking whether you find the conclusion repugnant; I am asking whether you think it correctly describes the structure of impartial value.

Please decompose your credence into at least the following sub-claims:

(1) The impartial betterness relation is transitive and complete across populations of different sizes, so that there is a fact of the matter about which of two populations is better.
(2) Conditional on (1), the correct structure of that relation is total-utilitarian rather than average-utilitarian, critical-level, variable-value, or person-affecting.
(3) There is no non-arbitrary well-being threshold below which lives, though worth living, contribute zero or negative marginal value to a population's overall goodness.
(4) The intuition that the conclusion is repugnant is better explained by scope insensitivity, status-quo bias, or some other heuristic than by tracking a genuine axiological fact.

For each sub-claim, give a probability and a single sentence of justification. Then show how you combine them to reach your overall credence. Finally, identify the sub-claim your overall credence is most sensitive to—the one where a decisive argument would shift your number the most—and say what form that argument would need to take.
