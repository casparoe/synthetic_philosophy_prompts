I keep seeing accuracy-first epistemology recommended in rationalist circles as one of the few places philosophy produces actual theorems instead of vibes, and I recently read a piece that summarized it in a way that set off my "too good to be true" alarm. The article said something like this: "The accuracy-first program shows that the probability axioms aren't just rules of thumb, they're theorems. The only thing a degree of belief has to do is be accurate — that is, get as close to the truth as possible. If you score accuracy with the Brier score, the average squared distance between your credence and the truth-value of the proposition, then any set of credences that violates the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated: there's another set of credences guaranteed to be at least as close to the truth in every possible world and strictly closer in some. So an agent who cares only about being right is forced, by her own lights, to be probabilistically coherent. And because the Brier score is strictly proper — meaning it's the unique way of measuring how far a credence is from the truth — the result doesn't depend on which scoring rule you happen to pick. Probabilism is therefore proved from the single uncontroversial premise that beliefs aim at truth."

I don't know enough formal epistemology to tell where this is compressed-for-a-general-audience and where its actually wrong. The "unique way of measuring" bit especially smells off to me, but I can't articulate why, and I also suspect "proved from a single uncontroversial premise" is doing more work than the author lets on. Could you go through this summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a defender of the accuracy-first program would sign off on, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and whether the last claim actually follows from anything true in the summary? I'm not looking for a reading list — I want to know what this summary gets right and wrong so I can correct my own mental model.
