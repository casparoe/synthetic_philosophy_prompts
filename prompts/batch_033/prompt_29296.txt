For an advanced undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, write a decision tree that sorts positions in the units-of-selection debate. Build the tree from the logical space of possible commitments rather than from a list of named positions. Each branch node should be a claim or question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — for example: whether natural selection requires replicators at all; whether a unit of selection must be a causal interactor in a selective process or may simply be a level at which we keep the books; whether higher-level selection requires heritable variance in fitness at the higher level; whether group fitness is merely an aggregate of individual fitnesses or something context-dependent that cannot be read off individual fitnesses; and whether species selection is selection on species or merely differential persistence (sorting). The leaves must include at least: the gene's-eye/replicator view, organism-centered interactor views, multi-level selection theory (distinguishing MLS1, where group fitness is an aggregate of individual fitnesses, from MLS2, where group fitness is a property of the group itself), species selection, and hierarchical pluralism. For each leaf, give one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Note any combinations of branch answers that are unoccupied, and say for each whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate rather than papering over the difference. End with a short note saying which split in the tree is doing the most argumentative work and is most likely to be contested, and what argument or empirical finding would make you merge or redraw two of the leaves.
