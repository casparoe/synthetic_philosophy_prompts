I cover science and technology policy for a general-interest magazine, and over the past few years I've come to treat the singleton hypothesis as my default frame for thinking about long-run technological risk. By that I mean the view that Earth-originating civilization will at some point come under the control of a single decision-making agency — a unified government, a superintelligent system, or some functionally equivalent global authority — with the capacity to prevent rival power centers from arising, and that this is both more likely than any stable multipolar alternative and, on balance, desirable, because a durable patchwork of competing powers would leave us permanently vulnerable to coordination failures, arms races, and existential catastrophe.

I want to know how exposed that view really is. What would count against it, and how much? Please rank the considerations, from those that would be fatal to those that are merely awkward. I'm particularly interested in: historical episodes that cut against any long-run tendency toward consolidation; game-theoretic or mechanism-design results suggesting decentralized coordination can be robust under realistic conditions; technological developments — in cryptography, open-source AI, surveillance, or elsewhere — that make durable fragmentation more plausible than the hypothesis allows; and internal tensions in the idea of a stable singleton itself, such as the problem of succession, value drift, or the agency's dependence on the very infrastructure that could be used to contest it. I'd like you to separate the descriptive claim, that a singleton will emerge or is likely to, from the normative claim, that we should aim at one, since defeaters may hit them differently. Which parts of the hypothesis carry the predictive weight, and which parts have been qualified to the point of unfalsifiability — and if the latter, is that a serious defect for a view of this kind? Where would a committed defender of the hypothesis concede ground, and where would they argue that an apparent counterexample is actually consistent with it?

Finally, turn the assessment on itself: which single argument or consideration would be most likely to change your mind about the singleton hypothesis, and where in your own reasoning about it is a mistake most likely to be hiding?
