I'm taking a philosophy of religion course and we've been doing Hume on miracles, and I keep seeing this one argumentative pattern in the readings and in online debates that I don't have a word for. Someone says that no amount of testimony could ever rationally establish a miracle, because a miracle would be a violation of a law of nature, and the evidence for that law comes from the whole uniform run of past experience, so it will always be more likely that the witness is lying, confused, or deceived than that the law actually failed. The other person says this move quietly builds the conclusion into the setup: by assigning the miracle an extremely low probability before any witness is even considered, you make the testimony incapable of moving you, and then you turn around and say the testimony was too weak. It isn't just a disagreement about how credible the witness is, and it isn't quite a burden-of-proof fight either, because both sides can accuse the other of the same circularity. I saw it happen in a seminar about a modern healing claim and again in a thread about a historical miracle.

I know this is somewhere near Humes argument, but I'm after the specific structure of the standoff, not a summary of Hume. Does this pattern have an established name in epistemology or philosophy of religion, and if it does, how is it different from a general problem about choosing priors in Bayesian reasoning? If there's no name, what would you call it, and what would a solution have to look like?
