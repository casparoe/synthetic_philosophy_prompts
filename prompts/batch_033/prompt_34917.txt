I've gotten into the modern Stoicism stuff over the past year — reading Holiday and Irvine, listening to some podcasts, that kind of thing. And there's a move I keep seeing that I can't figure out if it has a name.

Here's what I mean. Someone's anxious about a job interview, and the Stoic advice is: you can't control the hiring decision, only your preparation and your conduct. Right, classic Epictetus. But then the same person will, in the same breath, say something like "so the outcome doesn't matter" or "a Sage wouldn't care either way." And what's weird to me is that nobody seems to notice they've switched tracks. They started with a claim about what's causally up to you — the interviewers' decision isn't in your muscles, as it were. And they ended with a claim about what's worth caring about. But those are different things! I can't control whether my kid gets sick, but I sure as hell care about it.

I started noticing the same pattern in other contexts. Someone talks about climate change: "you can't control global emissions, only your own actions." Fine. Then: "so you shouldn't feel guilty about your footprint." Then, a bit later: "so there's no reason to prefer a world where emissions drop." Each step feels like it's just applying the same principle, but we've gone from causal limitation to responsibility to preferences about states of the world. 

The word "control" or "up to us" seems to be doing a lot of work here, and I think it's ambiguous between at least three things: what you can cause, what you're answerable for, and what you ought to value. The rhetorical trick — if it is a trick — is that the three are presented as a single distinction, so you get to move from one to the next without anyone asking for an argument.

Is there a name for this specific kind of slide? Not just equivocation in general, which is when a word has two meanings and you switch between them. This feels more like a conceptual distinction that's being asked to do three jobs at once, and the moral conclusions are smuggled in under the cover of a factual-sounding claim. If there's no established term, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from plain old equivocation or from the fact-value gap? I keep wanting to describe it to people but I end up having to recapitulate the whole thing each time.
