I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been reading about dispositions in my spare time — the idea that salt is soluble, glass is fragile, a person can be irascible, and so on. I keep running into a pair of patterns that seem importantly different but I don't have words for either of them, and I'm hoping you can tell me whether philosophers have already named and analyzed these.

Here's the first pattern. You have something with a disposition — say, a glass vase is fragile, meaning it would break if struck. But suppose the vase is wired to a device that, the instant the vase is struck, heats it to a temperature that makes it elastic rather than brittle. The vase doesn't break, but not because it wasn't fragile before the strike — it was fragile right up until the moment it would have manifested that fragility, and then the disposition itself was removed by the very event that was supposed to trigger it. The interference destroys the disposition at the moment of manifestation.

Here's the second pattern. Same fragile vase, but this time it's packed in a custom-molded foam casing. Strike it and it doesn't break. The fragility is still there the whole time — the glass hasn't changed — but something external is blocking the disposition from producing its characteristic manifestation. The disposition is intact; it just can't express itself.

Once I started noticing these, I saw them everywhere. In software: a server has a disposition to crash under heavy load, but a load-balancer masks it (second pattern); or a monitoring script that would send an alert on failure gets killed by the same failure it's supposed to detect, so the disposition to alert is removed at the moment of manifestation (first pattern). In people: someone is irascible but takes a beta-blocker before a stressful meeting — that feels like the second pattern, the irascibility is still there but blocked. Whereas someone who would become angry but the stress of the meeting triggers a dissociative state that makes them eerily calm — that feels like the first pattern, the disposition is temporarily gone. In law: a company has a disposition to monopolize a market, but a consent decree blocks the conduct (second pattern); versus a company that would monopolize but gets acquired by a larger firm at exactly the moment it was about to make its move, so the entity with the disposition no longer exists in the relevant form (first pattern, or something near it).

My questions: (1) Do philosophers have established names for these two kinds of interference? (2) Are they treated as importantly different from each other, or are they just two ways of saying "the disposition didn't manifest"? (3) What's the philosophical upshot — if dispositions can be interfered with in these ways, does that cause trouble for the simple idea that to have a disposition just is for a certain conditional to be true (e.g., "if struck, it breaks")? I'd like the answer to separate what's settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and not to present a contested view as if it's the consensus. Please write in American English.
