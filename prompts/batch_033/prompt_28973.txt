I'm leading a discussion section on "The Grand Inquisitor" from Brothers Karamazov next week and I've been trying to get my own views straight before I have to stand in front of twenty undergrads. I wrote down the five things I think I believe about the chapter and now I'm worried they don't all hang together. Here they are:

(1) The Inquisitor is straightforwardly wrong: you can't justify removing people's freedom for the sake of their happiness, even if the people in question would end up grateful.
(2) But I think his empirical diagnosis is basically correct — most people really do find freedom exhausting and would happily trade it for security, bread, and someone else making the hard calls.
(3) I also buy his institutional critique: the Church (and by extention any large institution) reliably betrays the radical ideals of its founder, and this isn't just a bug, it's structural.
(4) Yet I don't think submitting to authority is always a betrayal of freedom. If someone reflectively chooses to enter a monastic order with a vow of obedience, that looks to me like a legitimate exercise of autonomy, not its abdication.
(5) Finally, I hold that a decent political system should be built around what people would choose under good conditions (full information, no coercion, not desperate), not what they in fact choose when they're exhausted, scared, or hungry.

I can feel these pulling against each other but I can't quite map the fault lines. Can you go through and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only seem to be, and rank the real tensions by severity? For each real tension, tell me what the cheapest revision would be — which commitment has to give and what it costs. Keep it to 500 words.
