You are a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics and the philosophy of well-being, with practical experience in how abstract normative theories do and do not survive contact with institutional decision-making. Think carefully about what follows, and where your own reasoning is uncertain, flag that uncertainty rather than papering over it.

I direct the grantmaking program at a mid-sized philanthropic foundation focused on global health and development. Over the last two years our board has become persuaded by something in the neighborhood of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — roughly, the view that there is a threshold of suffering so severe that no amount of pleasure, flourishing, or other positive goods can compensate for it, and that preventing or relieving suffering above that threshold has lexical priority over promoting happiness or any other positive value. The board has not settled on a precise formulation, and they have no appetite for doing so. They have, however, started asking why our grant portfolio still looks the way it did before they adopted this view.

I need a decision procedure my team can actually run on each grant proposal we evaluate — something that operationalizes the lexical threshold idea without requiring my program officers to become philosophers or to resolve intramural debates the board itself has avoided. The procedure should be a sequence of questions we ask about a proposal, in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is irreducibly a matter of judgment or values.

The constraints are these. We allocate roughly $40 million per year. We evaluate about 120 proposals per cycle, four cycles per year. My team has about 30 minutes of structured deliberation time per proposal before they have to write a recommendation. Most of them have backgrounds in public health, development economics, or program evaluation — not philosophy. The procedure has to be something they can learn in a half-day training and apply consistently. It also has to be transparent enough that I can explain to a rejected applicant why they were rejected, in language that does not require quoting the board's philosophical commitments.

Here are some specific challenges the procedure must handle, and I want you to address each explicitly in the design:

- What counts as crossing the threshold? We need an operational criterion that a program officer can apply to a proposal's description of the suffering it addresses — not a criterion that just pushes the philosophical problem one step downstream.
- How do we handle proposals that address suffering whose severity relative to the threshold is genuinely uncertain or contested?
- How do we handle proposals that prevent future suffering — where the suffering has not yet occurred and its severity and probability are both estimates?
- How do we compare a proposal that prevents moderate suffering for a very large number of people against one that prevents extreme suffering for a small number? The lexical threshold view seems to imply that if the small-number suffering crosses the threshold and the large-number suffering does not, the former wins regardless of the numbers. Is that really what the procedure should say, and if so, how do we make that administrable and defensible?
- What do we do with proposals that primarily promote positive goods — joy, flourishing, achievement, community — rather than relieve suffering? Do they get funded only when no suffering-relief proposals are on the table? Or is there some trade-off below the threshold, and if so, how is that trade-off structured?

Please also build in a step that explicitly checks whether the procedure is about to produce a recommendation that the board or our stakeholders will find unacceptable on grounds that the procedure itself cannot recognize as legitimate — and instructs the program officer to escalate rather than override.

Once you have designed the procedure, apply it to three hypothetical proposals:

1. A program to provide palliative pain relief to terminal cancer patients in low-income countries. The suffering is clearly extreme, the intervention is evidence-backed, but the number of beneficiaries is modest relative to the per-patient cost, and the program cannot be scaled easily.

2. A program to treat depression in adolescents through school-based cognitive behavioral therapy in a middle-income country. The suffering is real and widespread, the intervention is cost-effective by conventional metrics, but it is unclear whether most cases of adolescent depression cross the threshold as the procedure defines it.

3. A program to fund community festivals, music, and cultural events in refugee camps. The proposal primarily promotes positive goods — joy, social connection, cultural continuity, moments of meaning — rather than relieving suffering, though the target population is undoubtedly suffering in other ways that the proposal does not address.

For each, walk through the procedure step by step and show what it recommends. The procedure should give a clear answer on proposal 1 and a clear answer on proposal 3. It should honestly flag what it cannot settle about proposal 2 and show exactly where the program officer must exercise judgment.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do — what kinds of proposals it will systematically mishandle, what philosophical assumptions it bakes in that even a sympathetic board might reasonably reject, and where it is likely to drift in practice as program officers find workarounds for its more demanding requirements.
