So I was at this conference on formal epistemology last fall—I cover the science-and-ideas beat for a magazine you've heard of, and honestly I was there to sit in on a panel about Bayesian networks in medical diagnosis, not to get sucked into a bar argument. But here we are.

Two guys I'd seen on a panel earlier, both probability philosophers, got into it at the hotel bar about something called van Fraassen's Reflection principle. I'd never heard of it. The rough idea, as I pieced together from their argument: your current credence in a proposition should equal what you expect your future credence in it will be. If you're now 40% sure it'll rain, but you know that by tonight, after you check the radar, you'll be 80% sure—then you should already be 80% sure. Otherwise you're treating your future self as though they'll be systematically wrong, which seems incoherent if you think that future self will be rational.

Here's how the argument went, reconstructed from the napkin I scribbled on.

Miles (younger guy, postdoc energy) defended the principle. David (older, tenured, the kind of guy who says "look" before every point) said the principle is either false or empty.

David opened: Suppose I know I'm going to forget evidence. Tonight I read a proof that some mathematical conjecture is true—I follow every step—so tomorrow morning I'll be at credence 0.95. But I also know that by next month I'll have forgotten the proof entirely. I'll be back at 0.5. Should my credence tonight equal my expected credence next month? Obviously not. Tonight I should be at 0.95, and I should expect to be at 0.5 next month. Those aren't equal. Reflection fails.

Miles: That's not a counterexample. Reflection only applies when you're certain your future self will be rational. Your future self who forgot the proof isn't fully rational—he's lost evidence.

David: Forgetting isn't irrational, it's just a fact about brains. And van Fraassen didn't say "only when your future self is rational." He stated it for any future time. You're adding a restriction to save the principle from a case it clearly can't handle.

Miles: The restriction is implicit in the motivation. The whole point is that you should defer to a future self who's epistemically better-positioned than you. If your future self has less evidence, they're not better-positioned. No mystery.

David: Fine, so let's try a case where my future self has more evidence but I have reason to distrust the path they took to get it. Suppose I'm on a jury. The prosecution puts on an expert this afternoon who's extremely persuasive—I know that by dinner I'll be at 0.8 that the defendant is guilty. But I also know, right now, two things: first, this expert has a documented history of overstating forensic certainty, and second, the judge will instruct us tomorrow morning to discount his testimony. So tonight I'll be at 0.8, tomorrow morning I'll be back at 0.4. Which future self should my lunchtime credence defer to? Tonight's self is rational and has more evidence than I do now. Tomorrow's self is also rational and has even more evidence—the judge's instruction. Reflection says my current credence should equal my expected future credence, but it can't equal both 0.8 and 0.4. So which one?

Miles: Tomorrow's. You should defer to the most informed future rational self on the horizon. Tonight's self is rational but on a trajectory toward a still-better-informed state. The principle picks out the limiting case.

David: So now Reflection says: defer to the maximally informed future self you expect to have. But that's just the claim that you should conditionalize on all future evidence you expect to receive. Which is true, but it's not the interesting claim van Fraassen made. The interesting claim was that any future rational credence exerts normative pressure on you now. That claim generates paradoxes you can't escape by adding epicycles. You've retreated to a trivial version.

Miles: I don't think it's trivial. The nontrivial part is that there's a normative connection between your present self and your future rational self that isn't just about evidence—it's about recognizing that future self as you, as the same epistemic agent extended in time. The forgetting and jury cases just show that the connection can be disrupted by specific kinds of noise. That doesn't make the core principle empty.

David: Look, if the principle only holds when nothing disrupts it, and you can't say in advance what counts as a disruption except by checking whether the principle holds, then the principle isn't doing any work. It's a restatement of the cases where it works, not a constraint on them.

And then someone ordered another round and I had to go file a piece about the medical AI panel. But I've been turning this over for months. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of each point? And is there a version of Reflection that survives David's cases without collapsing into the triviality he accuses Miles of retreating to?
