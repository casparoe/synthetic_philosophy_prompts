I'm a PhD student in political theory working on nuclear deterrence, and I keep running into two patterns in the literature that feel related but I cant find clean names for — when I ask people, they just wave at slogans. The first: once both sides have a secure retaliatory force, so that a direct war is close to unwinnable, each side seems to become *more* willing to fight small wars, run proxy conflicts, and push on the other's red lines, as if the big threat of escalation protects them from things getting out of hand. The second: a threat to use nuclear weapons is only believable if the threatener gives up some control — automatic launch procedures, delegated authority, public commitments that tie their hands — but each of those moves also makes it more likely that the weapons go off by accident, or through a local decision nobody at the top intended. Are these two recognized paradoxes, and do they have established names? More than the names, I want the carving: is the second just the general problem of making a commitment credible, or do nuclear weapons add something structurally different? And is the first pattern downstream of the second, or does it have its own cause?
