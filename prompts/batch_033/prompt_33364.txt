hey so in my philosophy club we got onto antinatalism and someone brought up Benatar's asymmetry. The way he laid it out, it seems to prove that being born is always a net harm, and that sounds so extreme I figure there has to be a slip somewhere, but every time I try to find it I end up either biting the bullet or begging the question. Can you tell me where this goes wrong, or if it doesnt, what I would have to give up to escape it? Here's the version we've been arguing about.

1. If a possible person never comes into existence, all the pains they would have had are absent, and that absence is good.
2. If a possible person never comes into existence, all the pleasures they would have had are absent, but that absence is not bad, because there is no actual person who is deprived of them.
3. If a possible person does come into existence, their pains are bad and their pleasures are good.
4. So compare the two options. Nonexistence has something good going for it, namely the absence of pain, and nothing bad going against it, while existence has something bad going against it, namely pain, and something good going for it, namely pleasure.
5. So nonexistence is always better than existence, for any possible person.
6. So for any person, it would have been better if they had never existed, and by the same logic it would be better if no future people were ever born.
7. But that conclusion is nuts. It would mean every parent who has a child is making that child worse off than if the child had never existed, and that we should maybe stop having kids altogether.

I dont know whether the weak point is step 1, step 2, or the comparison in step 4, or whether the argument is actually fine and I just hate the conclusion. Some part of me wants to say the word "good" in step 1 is doing something shifty, because if nobody exists then there is no one for whom the absence is good, but I cant tell if that's a real objection or just me refusing to think about possible people. So which step is doing the real work, and what does it cost to reject it? If the argument is valid, I want to know which premise is the least bad to give up and what I'd be left with after giving it up. I'd rather not end up committed to "having kids is always wrong" unless the argument actually forces me there.

oh and please no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables in your answer. Just write it out in continuous prose like an email, even when you're going through the steps.
