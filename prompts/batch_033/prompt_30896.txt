I'm a senior systems architect at a large infrastructure company. Years ago I took a handful of philosophy courses, and one problem that's stuck with me is something I think was called the paradox of deontology. As I remember it, the puzzle runs roughly like this: if you're committed to an agent-relative prohibition on a certain kind of act — say, killing an innocent person — then you're required to refuse to kill one innocent person even when that refusal predictably results in five other innocent people being killed by someone else. But the prohibition was supposed to be grounded in the value of the person, or the badness of the act-type, and that grounding seems to care about numbers. So the constraint appears to forbid the very thing — minimizing violations of the constraint — that would make sense of why we have it. I recall there being a literature of responses: something about agent-centered prerogatives, something about constraints being fundamentally non-aggregative, something about the separateness of persons.

I now spend my days reasoning about distributed systems, and I've come to suspect that this problem has a natural expression in the vocabulary I actually use. I think constantly about the difference between a local invariant enforced inside a module and a global property I want the whole system to satisfy; about cases where rigidly enforcing a constraint at every node produces worse system-wide outcomes than allowing controlled, logged violations under a well-specified fault model; about defense in depth, graceful degradation, and the design question of where a check should live in the stack. The deontologist who refuses to kill one to save five looks, from where I sit, like a service that refuses to drop a malformed packet even when dropping it would prevent a cascade failure that corrupts the entire message bus — principled at the local level, catastrophic at the system level. But I don't trust that analogy yet, and I want to know whether it holds up under a serious translation.

Please restate the paradox of deontology — including the main philosophical responses to it — in the vocabulary of software and systems architecture. Use the concepts I work with: invariants, local versus global properties, fault models, graceful degradation, defense in depth, idempotency, circuit breakers, the end-to-end principle, or whatever else from the engineering toolkit genuinely fits. Be precise and keep the translation honest: don't smuggle architectural assumptions into the philosophy and call it a translation. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto engineering concepts? Which parts required distortion to fit, and what got warped in the process? Which parts have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? And, crucially: does the residue point to something the philosophers are confused or imprecise about, or does it point to something my engineering vocabulary is structurally incapable of expressing? I want to know whether the paradox of deontology is a real problem that my toolkit can clarify, or whether my intuition that it reduces to a system-design tradeoff is missing the point.
