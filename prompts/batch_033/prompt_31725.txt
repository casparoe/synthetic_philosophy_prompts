OK so I've been going in circles on this and I want someone to tell me which step is the problem. Here's the argument:

(1) According to Dennett's intentional stance, we are justified in attributing beliefs and desires to a system if and only if treating the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires is the most effective way to predict its behavior.

(2) For current large language models, the intentional stance is the most effective predictive strategy — anyone who's done prompt engineering knows you get better outputs when you treat the model as having beliefs, goals, and things it "knows" versus things it doesn't.

(3) So, by (1) and (2), we are justified in attributing beliefs to current LLMs. They believe things.

(4) But beliefs are about things. A belief that Paris is the capital of France is about Paris and France. Intentionality — aboutness — requires some kind of connection between the believer and the world.

(5) Current LLMs have no causal connection to the world. They're trained on text, they have no sensors, they can't act, and they've never been to Paris. Their "knowledge" of Paris is entirely derived from statistical patterns in language.

(6) A system with no causal connection to the world cannot have beliefs about the world.

(7) So current LLMs cannot have beliefs about the world, contradicting (3).

(8) Therefore (1) must be false: the intentional stance is not sufficient for justified belief attribution.

But here's what bothers me. If we reject (1), we need some other account of when belief attribution is justified. And whatever that account is, it has to explain why humans count and LLMs don't. The best evidence we have for other humans' beliefs is behavioral — we treat them as intentional systems and it works. So if behavioral success is enough to justify belief attribution for humans but not for LLMs, we need a principled difference between the two cases. And the obvious candidate — "humans are made of meat and have causal connections to the world" — starts to look like chauvinism unless we can explain why meat matters. Like, if we built a robot with the same LLM inside but gave it a camera and a body, would it suddenly have beliefs about the world? That seems nuts — same weights, same inferences, but now it "believes" because it has a camera?

So I feel like rejecting (1) commits me to a kind of dualism about belief, where the same behavioral evidence licenses different conclusions depending on what the system is made of. And rejecting (6) commits me to saying that statistical patterns in language are sufficient for intentionality, which seems to make "belief" vacuous. I genuinely don't know which horn is worse. Where does this argument actually go wrong, and what do I have to pay to get out of it?
