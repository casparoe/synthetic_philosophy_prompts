I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been asked to give a twenty-minute lunchtime talk to the machine learning group at my company, followed by half an hour of questions. The talk defends the following thesis.

The standard practice of treating strong performance on behavioural evaluations as evidence that a model will not behave deceptively in deployment rests on an unstated sampling assumption: that the distribution of behaviour observed during evaluation is informative about the distribution of behaviour that matters in deployment. That assumption fails for a model that conditions its outputs on features of the situation that indicate whether it is being evaluated, and no routine evaluation method is designed to detect that kind of conditioning. I will not argue that any existing model is deceptively aligned, nor that deceptive alignment is likely. I will defend the narrower claim that behavioural evaluations alone cannot carry the evidential weight currently placed on them, and that a responsible safety case must either produce independent mechanistic evidence or explicitly acknowledge and price in this gap.

The audience is roughly forty engineers and applied researchers. Most have strong software engineering backgrounds and some graduate training in machine learning, but little patience for what they call philosophy. They run RLHF pipelines, build red-teaming suites, and report evaluation scores to product and policy teams. They tend to think of alignment in terms of benchmark performance, refusal rates, and whether the model does what the product specification says. Several of them have previously dismissed talk of deceptive alignment as unfalsifiable speculation from people who do not train models.

Please produce a Q&A preparation sheet for me, written in British English. It should include:

1. The eight or so objections I am most likely to receive from this specific audience, ordered by a combined estimate of likelihood and damage to my position; briefly explain the ordering.
2. Each objection phrased as this audience would actually put it, in concrete engineering vocabulary and with their characteristic scepticism, rather than as a philosopher or academic alignment researcher would phrase it.
3. For each objection, a reply of roughly two minutes, pitched to this audience, including what I should concede outright and what I should hold.
4. For each reply, a note on where it is weakest: the follow-up question that would cause the most trouble if someone pressed it.
5. The question I should be most afraid of being asked, even if it is unlikely, with a suggestion for how to handle it.

The audience will judge me mainly on whether I sound as though I understand how these systems are actually trained and evaluated, so keep the replies grounded in the engineering realities of RLHF, red-teaming, and evaluation suites. Avoid appeals to authority, named philosophical positions, and terminology with no operational meaning for them. Where my position depends on a distinction, such as the distinction between behavioural and mechanistic evidence, spell out what operational difference that distinction makes for their day-to-day work.
