I'm a science journalist and I've been chewing on something for a while. I cover a lot of fields where people use thought experiments — physics, philosophy of mind, ethics, sometimes biology. And I've got these gut reactions about which ones actually teach us something and which ones are just... stories, I guess. But I can't articulate the principle behind my reactions. I'm hoping you can help me find it, or tell me if my verdicts are just inconsistent.

Here's where I land on a bunch of cases:

- Galileo dropping balls from the tower of Pisa (or rolling them down inclines, whatever). Even though he probably never actually did it, and the real world has air resistance, this seems to me like it genuinely yielded knowledge — it got us to separate the concept of weight from the concept of falling speed in a way that real messy experiments at the time couldn't.

- Maxwell's demon. A tiny creature sorting molecules to violate the second law of thermodynamics. This one feels like it absolutely yielded knowledge — it forced physicists to figure out the connection between thermodynamics and information, which is a real thing we now understand.

- Einstein chasing a light beam at age 16. Imagining what you'd see if you caught up to a light wave — that seems to have been genuinely productive, it led to special relativity.

- Schrödinger's cat. I'm torn on this one. On the one hand it made people take the measurement problem seriously, which is real physics. On the other hand it's been so abused in popular science that I feel like it's done more to confuse people than to clarify anything. I guess I'd say it yielded knowledge for the physics community but not for the broader public — but that feels like a cop-out. Let me say it did yield knowledge, grudgingly.

- The Chinese room (Searle). I cover AI and this one makes me crazy. It feels like it didn't yield any knowledge at all — it just gave people a vivid image to hang their pre-existing intuitions on. Nobody who wasn't already skeptical of AI was convinced by it, and nobody who was skeptical needed it. But I worry I'm just saying that because I don't like the conclusion.

- Mary the color scientist, who knows all the physical facts about color but has never seen red. I actually think this one did yield knowledge — it forced people to get precise about what they mean by "physical facts," and it spawned a whole literature on the knowledge argument that clarified things even if the argument itself is disputed.

- The trolley problem. Ugh. I know philosophers love it, but I can't shake the feeling that it hasn't yielded knowledge so much as it's revealed patterns in people's gut reactions — and I'm not sure that's the same thing. But then I think about how it's actually influenced the design of autonomous vehicles, and I waver. Let me say: hasn't yielded knowledge about ethics, but has yielded knowledge about human moral psychology. Is that a legitimate distinction? I don't know.

- Newcomb's problem. The one with the perfect predictor and the two boxes. I genuinely don't know whether this has yielded knowledge. It seems to reveal a genuine tension in decision theory, but after decades of debate nobody seems to have resolved anything. The debate just is the thing now. I'm going to say: hasn't yielded knowledge, or at least hasn't yet.

- Thomson's violinist. The one where you wake up hooked to a famous violinist who needs your kidneys. I think this one did yield knowledge — it changed the terms of the abortion debate by isolating a key intuition, and even people who disagree with Thomson's conclusion have to engage with the structure of the argument.

- The experience machine (Nozick). Plugging into a simulation that gives you all the pleasures you want. I think this one yielded knowledge — it showed that people care about more than just the quality of their experiences, which is a genuine discovery about human values.

Okay, so that's where I am. I can see at least one tension already: I'm inclined to say the Chinese room didn't yield knowledge because it just gave people an image for their pre-existing views, but I could say the same thing about the violinist — and I think the violinist did yield knowledge. So I'm probably being inconsistent, or I'm applying some hidden criterion I haven't articulated.
