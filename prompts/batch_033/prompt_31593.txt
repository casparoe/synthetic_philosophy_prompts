I work in underwriting at a mid-size life and long-term care insurer, and I've been turning something over since a long argument with a friend who does biostatistics. We were talking about how I set individual premiums. My rate manual is full of cells like "female, 60–64, non-smoker" or "male, 50–59, type 2 diabetes, BMI 30–35," and my friend kept insisting the right answer is to condition on everything I know and use the narrowest cell with enough data, because that's what a probability for this particular person means. I kept pushing back that the narrowest cell is often tiny and its rate is mostly noise, or it's a cell of one, or there's no cell at all for a combination of conditions. By the end he admitted there's no mechanical rule, but he said the choice is just a model choice and I should stop pretending it's anything else.

I need something my team can actually run. Please write a decision procedure for choosing a reference class when pricing an individual policy: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the choice is one of values rather than facts. It should be grounded in whatever philosophy of probability and formal epistemology actually know about single-case probabilities and reference classes, but translated into steps an underwriter without that background can follow. It has to be runnable in about twenty minutes using our rate manual and whatever historical claims data we can pull, and it has to be defensible to the cheif underwriter and to state regulators. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on these three applicants: (1) a 47-year-old woman with well-controlled type 2 diabetes and a BMI of 31, where the broad age/sex cell says standard rates but the narrow diabetic-plus-BMI cell has 80 policies and says 2.4 times standard, driven mostly by a few large claims; (2) a 58-year-old man with sleep apnea and a past transient ischemic attack, a combination that appears in no cell in the manual; and (3) a 62-year-old woman whose narrowest well-populated cell carries a big loading because of a cancer she was treated for twenty years ago and has had no recurrence since, while the broader cell would put her at standard. Include one case where the procedure gives an answer we won't like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
