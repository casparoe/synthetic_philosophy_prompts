I am teaching a graduate seminar on the ethics of artificial intelligence, and next week we are discussing responsibility gaps for autonomous systems — the idea, associated most prominently with Andreas Matthias, that as systems become more autonomous and their behaviour less predictable, we may reach a point where no human agent (designer, programmer, trainer, deployer, or user) can fairly be held morally or legally responsible for a harmful outcome the system produces. I want to give my students a decision tree that sorts them into a position on this problem based on what they actually believe about a sequence of nested issues, rather than by which label they recognise.

Please construct the decision tree as follows. Each branching node should be a yes/no question phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing the names of the positions in the literature. The questions should carve the logical space at the joints: start from the most fundamental disagreement and work outward, so that the tree is built from the space of possible answers rather than from a list of named views. Each leaf should carry the name of the position it corresponds to, or "unnamed" if no one in the literature defends that combination, together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and flag any unoccupied leaf with a brief note on whether the combination is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because it seems underexplored.

The tree should include at least the following positions as leaves: the strict liability / gap-denial view (there is always a human who can be held responsible), the precautionary view (gaps exist and we should restrict autonomy to avoid them), the revisionist view (gaps exist and we should revise our concepts of responsibility to close them), and the distributed-responsibility view (the appearance of a gap arises from an overly individualistic picture of responsibility). You may add further leaves if the logical space demands them.

After laying out the tree, please add a brief methodological note: identify which branching question in the tree is, in your assessment, the most contestable — the one where a student's answer is most likely to shift under pressure from further argument — and say what kind of argument or consideration would most plausibly change someone's answer at that node, thereby restructuring which leaf they land on.
