I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rationality and action — about sixteen students, one seventy-five-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of Kavka's toxin puzzle before I name it. For context, the puzzle is this: a reliable benefactor will pay you tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a harmless but unpleasant toxin tomorrow afternoon; the payment depends only on your intention at midnight, not on whether you later drink it. You have a clear reason to intend, no reason to drink, and yet the intention seems unavailable. The activity should let students run into that structure themselves. Give me concrete rules and materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money (or only trivial stakes), a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction between reasons to intend and reasons to act, and onto the difference between deciding, predicting, and intending. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect some students will game the verification of intention, and others will simply decide to perform the aversive act — and how to handle those responses inside the activity. Be explicit about which feature of the puzzle a classroom cannot reproduce, especially the fact that the toxin payoff depends on a private mental state at a single moment rather than on any observable behavior. The answer should be in British English.
