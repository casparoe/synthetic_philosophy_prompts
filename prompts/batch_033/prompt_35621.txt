You are a professor of philosophy with a joint appointment in computer science, specializing in the foundations of algorithmic fairness. You are known for your ability to identify subtle tensions in others' positions without imposing your own views. I am preparing a position paper for a FAccT workshop, and before I commit my views to print, I would like you to audit the coherence of the position I have arrived at over several years of working on these issues.

Please think through each commitment carefully. Before you begin diagnosing tensions, take a moment to map out what each commitment entails and what it rules out — I want to see the logical landscape before you apply it to my position. Then identify all tensions among my commitments, rank them by severity, and for each, explain what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. Distinguish genuine tensions from ones that only appear to be in tension. Do not simply list every possible friction — I want the tensions ordered by how much trouble they cause, with clear explanations of what drives each one.

Here are my commitments, stated as precisely as I can make them:

(1) I accept the core impossibility results as mathematical facts. Calibration, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class cannot all hold simultaneously across groups, except when base rates are equal or prediction is perfect. This is not up for debate.

(2) I hold that the fundamental moral intuition behind algorithmic fairness is treating like cases alike. If two individuals are relevantly similar with respect to the features that bear on the decision, they should receive the same decision. This is the principle that any fairness metric must ultimately answer to.

(3) I believe calibration is the fairness constraint you should not give up. At minimum, a risk score of 0.7 must mean a 70% chance of the outcome regardless of which group the individual belongs to. To abandon calibration is to abandon the meaning of the score itself — you would be saying that the score means different things for different people.

(4) I also find equalized odds deeply compelling. If an algorithm makes more false-positive errors for one group than another, that is a fairness violation. I cannot see how a system that systematically mistakes innocent members of one group for high-risk while doing better for another group is fair, even if the risk scores are well-calibrated within each group.

(5) I reject demographic parity. Setting different decision thresholds for different groups to equalize overall acceptance rates — even when the individuals being treated differently have the same risk score — strikes me as a violation of the "treat like cases alike" principle. It makes group membership, rather than individual risk, the basis for decisions.

(6) I hold that the choice among fairness metrics is not merely a political decision or a matter of which stakeholder group has more power. There is a fact of the matter about what fairness requires in algorithmic decision-making, and the impossibility results do not show otherwise — they show only that we cannot simultaneously satisfy every constraint that someone might plausibly propose.

Please go through these commitments systematically. I want to know which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and whether the whole set is salvageable. If my position requires revision, tell me what the least costly revision is and what it costs.
