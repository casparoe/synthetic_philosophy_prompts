I've been working through Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" and I think I've built an argument that eats his whole position from the inside. I'm a retired biochemist, so I'm new to all this, but this one's been keeping me up. Here it is:

(1) Strawson says the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation — belong to the "participant stance," the basic posture we take toward each other in ordinary human relationships.

(2) He contrasts this with the "objective stance," where we see someone as a thing to be managed or treated rather than a participant — the stance we take toward the severely mentally ill, young children, etc.

(3) The objective stance suspends the reactive attitudes: you don't resent a schizophrenic person for their outbursts; you seek to treat or manage them.

(4) Strawson's key move against the determinist is that the truth of determinism wouldn't give us a reason to abandon the participant stance for the objective stance, because we simply cannot live in the objective stance toward everyone — it's psychologically impossible.

(5) But if determinism is true, then every action anyone performs is the causal product of factors ultimately outside their control (genes, upbringing, neural wiring, etc.).

(6) Now, why do we take the objective stance toward the severely mentally ill? Presumably because their actions are caused by factors outside their control — that's what exempts them from normal responsibility.

(7) If determinism is true, then everyone's actions are caused by factors outside their control in just the same way. The difference between the schizophrenic and the ordinary person is one of degree, not kind — different causal chains, but both equally outside the agent's control.

(8) So if determinism is true, the objective stance is the rationally appropriate stance toward everyone.

(9) The fact that we find it psychologically impossible to take this stance (as Strawson insists) is just a fact about our cognitive limitations, not a justification for the participant stance. "We can't help it" is a psychological fact about us, not a normative reason.

(10) But here's the kicker: "we can't help it" is precisely the kind of excuse Strawson's own framework recognizes as undermining responsibility! So his defense of moral responsibility rests on an excuse that, by his own lights, would excuse us from being responsible for holding people responsible.

(11) So Strawson's account either (a) fails to answer the determinist challenge on its own terms — it just changes the subject from "are we justified?" to "can we help it?" — or (b) shows that our practice of holding each other responsible is itself something we can't be held responsible for, which is a skeptical conclusion dressed up as a vindication.
