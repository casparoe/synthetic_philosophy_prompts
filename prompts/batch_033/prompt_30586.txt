You are a philosopher of language and epistemology who reads rationalist blogs and forums closely and is comfortable moving between credences, assertion, and self-knowledge. Before drafting the quiz, brainstorm at least six candidate diagnostic questions, then check each against two tests: a reader should be able to answer it from their reaction to a concrete case without already knowing the names of the positions, and it should genuinely separate at least two of the positions you plan to include.

Write a "which account of Moore's paradox do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for readers of a rationalist-adjacent blog. The quiz should sort readers by how they respond to cases, not by which label they prefer.

The phenomenon at stake: a speaker asserts that p while also asserting that they do not believe that p, or asserts that p while asserting that they believe the negation of p. The assertion strikes most people as absurd even though what is asserted can be true, and even though the same content asserted about another person or about one's past self is unremarkable.

Write 8 to 10 questions. Each question should present a concrete case with two or three answer options. Cases should include, at minimum: someone saying the Moorean sentence aloud; someone silently judging the same conjunction; a third-person version; a past-tense version; a version in which the speaker says they assign high credence to p while also saying they do not believe p; a version in which the speaker is reading lines on stage or otherwise not speaking in their own voice; and a version in which the second conjunct is replaced by a claim not to know p. Make sure at least two questions probe whether the reader locates the absurdity in the act of assertion rather than in the state of belief, at least two probe whether the absurdity survives when the second conjunct concerns partial belief, and at least one probes whether the first-person present tense is essential rather than merely a vivid instance.

Build the scoring key from the logical space of answer combinations first, then locate named positions in that space. The leaves should include at least: (a) the view that the absurdity is a contradiction between what the assertion expresses and what the second conjunct states; (b) a pragmatic view on which the absurdity arises from what the assertion implicates or conventionally signals rather than from the content of what is said; (c) the view that assertion is governed by an epistemic norm such as knowledge or belief and that Moorean assertions violate it; (d) the view that the paradox traces to a structural fact about first-person belief and self-knowledge, so that the silent judgment is already defective; and (e) a deflationary view on which Moorean absurdity is a family of related infelicities with no single underlying explanation. For each leaf, give its standard name if it has one, a one-sentence sketch, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Note any answer combinations in the scoring space that no live position occupies, and say briefly whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

If any two leaves cannot be separated by the questions, do not pretend they can: flag the failure and state which further question would be needed to separate them.

Throughout the piece, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested. It is settled that such assertions are widely felt to be absurd while the content asserted is consistent; it is contested whether the primary explanation is semantic, pragmatic, epistemic, or psychological, and contested whether a special norm of assertion is doing the explanatory work. Do not present any contested account as the consensus. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not make any question depend on recognizing a particular paper or author.
