In American contract law, there is a rule called the parol evidence rule. When parties have reduced their agreement to a written contract that appears complete and unambiguous on its face, courts will refuse to hear evidence of what the parties actually intended or discussed during negotiations — drafts, emails, oral promises, side conversations. The meaning of the contract is taken to be what a reasonable reader would find in the four corners of the document, not what the parties privately had in mind. But the same legal system, when a will is contested, routinely admits evidence of the testator's intentions, even when the will's text is clear on its face; courts want to know what the testator actually meant. And in statutory interpretation, some judges will consult legislative history — committee reports, floor debates, sponsor statements — to determine what Congress intended, while others, the textualists, refuse on principle, insisting that the enacted text is the law and that what individual legislators thought they were voting for is irrelevant. So we have a legal system that in one domain heavily restricts evidence of intention, in another freely admits it, and in a third is deeply divided on the question.

What theory of textual meaning does each of these practices presuppose? Does the parol evidence rule commit the law to the view that meaning resides in the public text rather than in private authorial intentions — or can the rule be justified on grounds that are neutral between theories of meaning, such as administrability, the need for third parties to rely on contracts, and the incentives of sophisticated parties to draft carefully? If so, why do those practical considerations cut differently for wills, where third-party reliance is also a live concern? And the textualist's refusal to consult legislative history: does that practice commit a judge to a particular philosophical theory of meaning — for instance, that the meaning of a text is its public meaning rather than its authors' intended meaning — or is it also justifiable on purely institutional grounds, like the unreliability of legislative history? I am asking you to reconstruct the implicit theories of meaning that these practices presuppose, distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of interpretation would license the same practice. Please write the answer in American English.
