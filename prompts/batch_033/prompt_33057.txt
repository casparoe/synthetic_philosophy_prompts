In a seminar on higher-order evidence, a colleague pressed an argument he took to be decisive. When someone judges that she ought to get up and exercise but stays on the couch, we all recognize a familiar kind of irrationality: she acts against her own better judgment, and we call that weakness of will. Now consider a reasoner who has just gone over the evidence and concluded that it supports the defendant's guilt, yet continues to believe the defendant is innocent; or a reasoner who judges that her assessment of a job candidate is unreliable because she is sleep-deprived and politically biased, yet finds her confidence in the candidate unmoved. My colleague argued that these are simply weakness of will transposed to belief: epistemic akrasia. Since no one defends practical akrasia, he concluded, no one should defend epistemic akrasia, and any epistemology that permits believing against one's own assessment of the evidence is missing something fundamental.

A third participant pushed back, saying the analogy is doing all the work. Belief, unlike action, is not under direct voluntary control, she said, so weakness of will may not even make sense for belief; and in the practical case the agent endorses the better judgment while being pulled away by appetite or habit, whereas in the epistemic case the pull is just more evidence, and the better judgment may not be the kind of thing one can fail to obey. I am now unsure how much of my colleague's argument survives this objection and the other disanalogies.

Please audit the analogy between practical akrasia and epistemic akrasia. Give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the better judgment, the akratic action, the will, the temptation, and the failure. Then identify the disanalogies, separating the ones that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, say whether the conclusion that epistemically akratic combinations are always irrational survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy, if any, would be.
