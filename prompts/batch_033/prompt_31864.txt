I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, trying to get my views straight on individual responsibility in collective harm cases before I write my prospectus. I've been going back and forth on this stuff for months and I'd like someone to stress-test the whole package before I bring it to my advisor. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think the inefficacy objection is basically sound for individual consumer choices. When I buy a chicken at the grocery store, my purchase doesnt cause any additional chicken to be raised or killed — the supply chain doesn't respond to single purchases. Same for driving to campus: my individual trip doesn't cause any climate damage that wouldn't have happened anyway.

(2) But I think you should vote, even though your individual vote is also inefficacious. The reason — and I think this is the standard move — is that voting is a joint cooperative practice governed by fairness norms: everyone doing their part is what makes the outcome fair, and you have a reason to do your part regardless of marginal impact.

(3) I hold that Parfit's imperceptible-harm argument is ultimately correct: the fact that no single act makes a perceptible difference doesn't let you off the hook when many such acts add up to a serious harm. But I can't square this with (1), and I know it.

(4) I think that if a social practice is seriously unjust, you have an integrity-based reason to withdraw from it — a reason grounded in not wanting to be the kind of person complicit in that injustice — even when your withdrawal makes no practical difference.

(5) I'm drawn to rule-consequentialism as the right framework for collective action cases: act according to the rule whose general adoption would have the best consequences. This is what I reach for when I want to say you should reduce your emissions even though no single car trip matters.

(6) I think carbon offsets are morally legitimate — buying them actually cancels the wrongness of your flight — but I'm suspicious of humane-washing in animal agriculture and think buying "free-range" meat doesn't cancel the wrongness in the same way, and I need this to be principled rather than just a vibe.

I'm presenting an early version of this to my advisor next week and I want to know: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest revision? Don't just list everything as equally problematic — rank the tensions by severity, tell me which are real vs. apparent, and for each real tension say what would have to give and what it costs. I'd like a committed answer: tell me what you think the right resolution is, not just a menu of options.
