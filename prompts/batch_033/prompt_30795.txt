In a seminar on the applicability of mathematics, I offered a deflationary account of why mathematics works in physics: roughly, that we select, refine, and discard mathematical tools on the basis of empirical success, and that the failures simply do not survive. A philosopher of physics replied that this misses "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" — as if the phrase were itself an objection. I want to know exactly what that sentence could be claiming. Separate the distinct theses it might express, from near-trivial to radical: what is being described as effective, what would make that effectiveness reasonable or unreasonable, and relative to what baseline. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a false one, and state which readings would actually undercut a selectionist or deflationary account of applied mathematics and which merely gesture at the phenomenon. I would like the readings laid out as a menu I can bring back to the seminar, with the readings that do real argumentative work flagged as such.
