In the epistemic logic seminar I am taking, we have been working through the possible-worlds semantics for knowledge, and I have assembled an argument that now troubles me: the conclusion is plainly false, yet I cannot locate the failing step, and I am unsure whether the fault lies in the closure principle itself or in the semantics that licenses it.

(1) Knowledge is closed under logical consequence: if an agent knows P, and P logically entails Q, then the agent knows Q.
(2) A competent number theorist knows the axioms of Peano arithmetic — the usual finite list together with the induction schema, understood as licensing all of its instances.
(3) For any theorem T of Peano arithmetic, the axioms of PA logically entail T; that is just what it is for T to be a theorem of PA.
(4) From (1)–(3), the number theorist knows every theorem of PA: the familiar ones, the ones nobody has yet proved, and the ones whose shortest proofs are longer than any human could survey.
(5) But that is false. There are theorems of PA that nobody has proved, and no working number theorist knows them; if (5) were false, proving a theorem would never produce new knowledge, and mathematical research would be a kind of theater.
(6) Therefore at least one of (1), (2), and (5) must be rejected.

I would most like to keep (2) and (5), which pushes the trouble onto (1); but (1) seemed the least controversial premise, since it only says that one knows what follows from what one knows. I can see moves one might make — weaken (1) to closure under known entailment, or replace 'knows' with 'is in a position to know,' or distinguish implicit from explicit knowledge — but I want to know exactly which step you reject and what rejecting it costs, including whether such a weakening genuinely solves the problem or merely relocates it inside the semantics.
