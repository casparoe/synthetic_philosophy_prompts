In the criminal law as I understand it, the same physical act can be punished very differently depending on what was going on in the defendant's mind. A death is murder if the person meant to bring it about, manslaughter if the person was reckless, and often no crime at all if the person was merely careless. Prosecutors must prove this mental element beyond a reasonable doubt, and juries are expected to reconstruct, sometimes years after the fact, whether someone acted with a particular intention, belief, or degree of awareness. The system seems to treat these inner states as real, definite things that exist before the act, cause the act, and can be inferred reliably from behavior and circumstances.

Yet the same system also allows the brain to be treated as a window into the mind. Courts hear expert testimony about neurological damage, brain scans, and mental disorders, and they sometimes conclude that a defendant could not form the required intention because of a brain condition. That sits uneasily with the idea that a jury can establish a person's intentions beyond a reasonable doubt from the outside.

What picture of the mind does this practice commit the law to? Does it strictly presuppose that beliefs, desires, and intentions are real and causally effective, or could the practice survive as a merely practical way of organizing social responses to harmful behavior? If it turned out that beliefs and desires, as ordinary people talk about them, do not exist — that our mental vocabulary is a convenient but ultimately false description of brain processes — what would have to change in the practice, if anything? And are the two halves of the practice consistent with each other? I am less interested in what the law officially declares than in what philosophical commitments the practice could not function without.
