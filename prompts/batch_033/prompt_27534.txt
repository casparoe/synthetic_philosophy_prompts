I've been thinking about Hofstadter's idea that creativity is mostly knob-twiddling — you take an existing pattern, vary some parameters, and if you turn the right knobs the right amount you get something new. As a novelist this is basically my job description, but my gut reactions on particular cases dont seem to add up to any one principle. Here's where I land:

- A jazz player who improvises a whole new solo over "Autumn Leaves": creative, obviously. The changes are the same but the line is new.
- A wedding band that plays "Autumn Leaves" exactly like the record: not creative. Nothing got twiddled.
- Monet painting the same haystack in different light, over and over: deeply creative. The repetition is the whole point.
- A thriller writer who has written the same book eight times — same twist, same two character types, different city each time: not creative. One knob, and it's the setting knob.
- Borges's Pierre Menard rewriting Don Quixote word for word: creative. But this one bothers me, becuase at the level of the text no knob got turned at all. All the variation is in the context.
- A translator who turns a Russian novel into English and bends the idioms hard to capture the feel: creative.
- A student essay that swaps synonyms into a Wikipedia paragraph: not creative.
- A DJ who loops two seconds of a forgotten soul record and builds a five-minute track around it: creative.
- A painter who copies the Mona Lisa exactly: not creative. But a painter who copies it exactly and titles the thing "Mona Lisa (After the Original, 2024)": I want to call that creative in a conceptual-art way, and I can't see how that squares with the wedding band.

The Menard case and the conceptual Mona Lisa are the ones that keep me up, because they're zero-variation at the level of the object and yet I want to say creative. My working theory is something like: creativity is the amount of surprise the variation produces, given the theme, plus how much the variation changes how you experience the original afterwards. But that theory flatters the conceptual Mona Lisa too much and the jazz solo too little.

Is there a cleaner principle that gets all of these, or close? And what does it say about the thing I'm actually writing right now — a King Lear retelling set in a nursing home, with the same beats, just transposed? Am I twiddling one knob or doing something real? And if the principle has to drop one of my verdicts, which one goes?
