so two people in my philosophy of mind class got into it after the lecture on hedonism and i've been turning their argument over for like a week. i wasn't part of it, just listening, and i honestly can't tell who has the better of it.

the prof had been going through bentham and mill, and afterwards these two — call them dana and raf — started arguing about whether pleasures actually have anything in common.

dana said the whole hedonist project depends on there being a single sensation, pleasantness, that all pleasures share. like, bentham says pushpin is as good as poetry if it gives you the same quantity of pleasure, and that only makes sense if the pleasure from pushpin and the pleasure from poetry are the same kind of thing, differing only in amount.

raf said that's exactly the problem — they aren't the same kind of thing. the pleasure of solving a math problem and the pleasure of a back rub feel nothing alike. if you introspect honestly you won't find any common sensation running through both. what they share is that you want them to continue, or your satisfied by them, but that's an attitude, not a feeling.

dana came back with: so what? the fact that pleasures feel different doesn't mean they don't share a common core. red and blue feel different but they're both colors. maybe pleasantness is like that — a dimension that varies across experiences but is still a single thing you can have more or less of.

raf said that's a terrible analogy because colors share a physical basis — wavelength — and the analogy would require pleasures to share something similar, which is exactly what's in question. plus, he said, the "dimension" talk is just relabeling the problem: what is this dimension a dimension *of*, if not a common sensation?

dana said if there's no common sensation then hedonism collapses and we need a whole new theory of wellbeing. raf said maybe that's just the right conclusion — maybe hedonism is false and the heterogeneity of pleasure is one of the main reasons why.

then dana said raf was basically giving up on the project of explaining what makes a life go well in unified terms, and raf said that's not giving up, it's just not pretending there's a unity where there isn't one.

at that point they had to go to their next class so it ended unresolved. i've been trying to figure out who's right at each step. can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move? rate each move and say who gets the better of which point, and whether the overall dialectic leans one way or the other. also, is there some obvious move neither of them made that would break the stalemate? please write in american english.
