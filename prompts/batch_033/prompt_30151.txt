I run a sci-fi book club and next month we're doing Asimov's Foundation. I want to frame the discussion around Friedman's old 'F-twist' idea — the claim from his essay on positive economics that the realism of a theory's assumptions doesn't matter, only whether its predictions pan out, and that the best theories actually tend to have wildly unrealistic assumptions. I know Samuelson coined the 'F-twist' label as a dig. Here's the thing I keep going back and forth on: psychohistory looks like it was built to be the F-twist's dream case. Hari Seldon's whole model treats quintillions of people like particles in a gas, assumes individual choices wash out, and assumes the population doesn't know the prediction, because if they did they'd change their behavior and wreck it. And the Plan does keep predicting correctly through crisis after crisis. But then the Mule shows up — one unpredictable individual the model can't handle — and the Second Foundation has to secretly intervene to keep things on track, which muddies wether the predictions are even predictions anymore. So read Foundation through the F-twist for us: does the novel vindicate Friedman's line that unrealistic assumptions are fine as long as the predictions work, or does it dramatize exactly what his critics said could go wrong? Is the Mule the F-twist's nightmare, or just the usual ceteris paribus clause showing up? And what should the book club make of the fact that the Plan's success partly depends on hidden Second Foundation meddling — is that an 'as if' assumption in Friedman's sense or something shadier? Give me exactly five points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
