I sit on my university's academic integrity panel. We've got a case that's split us down the middle and I need to draft the panel's decision memo before next week.

A second-year philosophy student submitted an essay on free will. The essay cites four scholarly sources — "As Dennett (1984) establishes...", "Van Inwagen's (1983) consequence argument shows...", that sort of thing — and engages with them critically. The prose is entirely the student's own. The problem: the student never read any of those works. They got summaries from Wikipedia and SparkNotes, reconstructed what they figured the arguments must be, and wrote the essay as if they'd engaged with the primary texts. The attributions are factually accurate — the arguments really are in those books, more or less as described. But the student admits they deliberately created the impression of having read the sources, because they knew the marker expected it.

Half the panel says this is straightforward academic dishonesty: the student intended to deceive the marker about their research process, and that's what matters. The other half says there's no false statement anywhere in the essay — the claims are correct, the attributions are correct, the prose is original — so we're punishing someone for what the marker inferred, not for what the student actually did. One collegue keeps saying "you can't be done for misleading, only for lying."

I need a decision memo — misconduct or not, with reasoning — that takes both sides seriously and gives a recommendation we can actually act on. We're a British university, so our regs define misconduct as "any attempt to gain unfair academic advantage through deception." The question is whether what this student did counts as deception under that definition, and if it does, whether we ought to find against them given that nothing they wrote was false.
