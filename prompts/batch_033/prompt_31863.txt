I've been reading around in the public reason literature for a term paper, and I'm struck by how frozen the whole debate feels. On one side, Rawls and his followers say that when the state coerces people through law, the justification for that coercion has to be one that all reasonable citizens could accept despite their different religions and worldviews. So a legislator shouldn't vote for a policy just because her church teaches it; she needs a reason that could make sense to a reasonable atheist or Muslim or secular liberal too. On the other side, critics say this is rigged against religious believers. It tells them their deepest commitments can only enter politics after being translated into a supposedly neutral secular idiom, which either distorts those commitments or treats secular reasoning as the default and religion as the thing that needs a special permit. And the whole test depends on who counts as reasonable, and the defender's account of reasonable just builds in the liberal answer. Both sides have been making roughly these moves for decades, and I can't tell whether anyone has actually moved.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a first-order argument about whether religious reasons are good reasons. I suspect it's a disagreement about what political justification is for: one side thinks justification is about showing each citizen, given what she already believes, that she isn't being treated as a mere subject; the other thinks it's about the law being based on reasons that are right from some standpoint outside any particular citizen's head. Or maybe it's just a verbal fight about the word reasonable. Diagnose the disagreement, using what each side actually says, and tell me what kind of disagreement it is: substantive moral, hidden factual, two different questions under one heading, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for. Then say what would actually count as progress given that diagnosis, or whether I should expect none. And please commit: give me your actual diagnosis and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to pick.
