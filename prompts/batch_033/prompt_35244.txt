I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size fintech company, and I've stumbled into a situation that's been keeping me up at night. About three months ago, while debugging a race condition in our payment processing pipeline, I discovered that our internal audit logging system has a subtle but systematic blind spot: certain balance adjustments made through a legacy admin endpoint are executed correctly in the database layer but never written to the audit trail. The endpoint was built years ago by an engineer who left the company, and based on the commit history, documentation, and the fact that nobody has touched it since, I'm reasonably confident I'm the only person who knows about this gap. I could, in principle, make small adjustments to accounts I control—or accounts tied to shell entities I could set up—and those adjustments would be functionally invisible. There's no monitoring alert that would fire, no periodic reconciliation process that would flag them, and the database backups would simply reflect the new balances as though they had always been that way. Conservatively, the amount I could extract annually without triggering any downstream anomalies works out to roughly three or four months of my salary.

I haven't acted on this, and I'm genuinely not sure I would. But I've found myself unsettled by how difficult it's been to articulate why I shouldn't. The reasons people typically reach for in these situations—"you'll get caught," "it'll destroy your reputation," "it's against company policy"—all bottom out in consequences, and in this case the consequences are effectively zero. I've tried reasoning from broader principles: if everyone acted this way the system would collapse, but that's a generalization argument, and my individual choice doesn't actually cause anyone else to act similarly. I've considered whether I'd be violating some kind of implicit agreement with my employer or my coworkers, but it's not obvious to me that an agreement I never explicitly made—and that nobody would ever know I broke—carries real moral weight. I've also wondered whether the act itself is simply wrong regardless of consequences, but I can't ground that intuition in anything I find persuasive on reflection.

I'm not looking for legal advice or practical risk assessment—I've already modeled both thoroughly. What I'm after is a more philosophical take: can you help me think through whether there's a genuine moral reason not to do this, even granting a guarantee of never being caught? I'd like your response as a numbered list of exactly five distinct considerations or arguments, each expressed in a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. They don't need to all point in the same direction, but they should help me see the structure of the problem more clearly than I've been able to on my own.
