I've been collecting my own verdicts about when an assertion is okay, and I can't find a single principle that fits them. (1) A friend asks whether their ticket in a fair million-ticket lottery is a loser; I tell them "it's a loser" purely on the strength of the odds. I think that's not okay, even though it almost certainly is a loser and will turn out true. (2) Someone says "it's raining, but I don't know that it's raining." I think that's never okay, even when both conjuncts are true and the speaker believes them. (3) A biology teacher who privately rejects evolution but is following the state-mandated curriculum tells her class "humans evolved from earlier hominids." I think her assertion is fine, even though she doesn't believe it and so doesn't know it. (4) Someone with no grounds whatever announces "Desert Wind will win" before a race, and Desert Wind wins. I think the announcement was not okay, despite being true. (5) I tell a visitor "the train leaves at 9:15" because I checked the official schedule this morning, but the schedule was changed an hour ago and the train leaves at 9:00. I think my assertion was okay, even though it was false. Cases (1) and (5) especially bother me: in both I have strong but not conclusive grounds, yet I want to call one improper and the other proper. Is there one norm of assertion that captures all of these, or close to it? If some verdicts have to go, which should go, and what does the resulting principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: an advisor tells a client "this fund is safe" on the basis of a careful analysis that misses a hidden risk, and the fund collapses — is that like the train case, like the lottery case, or neither? Please commit: state the principle you think is best, say exactly which of my verdicts it rejects and why, and defend that trade-off.
