At a conference last month, two philosophers I know got into an argument about pure time preference that I've been turning over ever since. Neither convinced the other, and I couldn't sort out who had the better of it. Here's roughly how it went, reconstructed from memory and a few notes I took.

**Elena** started by saying that pure time preference — discounting the value of a good solely because it occurs in the future rather than the present — is straightforwardly irrational. She said it's the temporal analogue of spatial discounting: nobody thinks a child's suffering matters less because it happens in another country, so why should it matter less because it happens in another century? In both cases, she said, the mere fact of distance is morally and rationally irrelevant. She allowed that we might discount for uncertainty (the future is less certain) and for growth (future people may be richer), but those are different from discounting wellbeing itself just because of when it occurs.

**David** pushed back. He said the analogy to space is misleading, because the future, unlike another country, is not yet real. When we choose between a benefit now and a benefit later, we are choosing between something that will actually be enjoyed and something that may never come to be, for reasons that have nothing to do with epistemic uncertainty. He said there's a metaphysical asymmetry: the past is fixed, the present is actual, the future is open. That asymmetry, he argued, gives us a rational basis for preferring nearer goods over more distant ones that isn't reducible to a bias.

**Elena** replied that this confuses uncertainty about whether the future will arrive with the normative question of how to value it if it does. She said: when we make a decision under uncertainty, we already multiply outcomes by their probabilities. If the worry is that I might die before the future benefit arrives, that's already captured by the probability I assign to being alive. Pure time preference is the claim that even after accounting for probability, I should still discount the benefit just because it's later. And that, she said, is what's indefensible.

**David** said that still misses something. He pointed to the personal case: if I have to choose between a mild pleasure now and the same mild pleasure for my eighty-year-old self, he said, it doesn't seem irrational to prefer the nearer one — not because I might die, but because my eighty-year-old self is psychologically very different from me, with different values and concerns. He said this is the Parfit-style point about personal identity: what matters is psychological connectedness, and that connectedness attenuates over time. If what makes a future person's welfare matter to me is our degree of psychological overlap, then discounting the far future isn't a bias; it reflects the genuine structure of what we care about.

**Elena** responded that this argument proves too much. If psychological connectedness justifies discounting, then it should also justify discounting the welfare of other people to the extent they're psychologically different from me — which would license a kind of partiality that goes well beyond anything most of us find acceptable. And she added that even if the argument works at the individual level, it doesn't translate to the social or intergenerational case: a government setting climate policy shouldn't discount the welfare of future generations just because they'll be psychologically different from us.

**David** came back with two points. First, he said the social case is genuinely harder, but that's partly because the social case is where the pure-time-preference question matters most, and people on both sides of that debate sometimes smuggle in conclusions from the personal case without noticing. Second, he said that even in the personal case, Elena's position has a counterintuitive implication: if pure time preference is always irrational, then a person who prefers a moderate pleasure today over a slightly larger pleasure in fifty years is making a mistake — and that seems like a very strong claim about what rationality requires, one that most people would reject.

They went back and forth a bit more, but that's the core of it. I'd be interested in a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange. Specifically: at each stage, who has the better argument, and are there considerations that neither of them raised that would move the dispute forward?
