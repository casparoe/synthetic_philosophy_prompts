what does a theory of causation by omission need to get right? i want the full list of requirements, with the case or consideration that motivates each one, and a clear mark on which are non-negotiable and which are just things particular camps want.

here are the kinds of things that keep me up at night:

- the selection problem: the gardener fails to water the plant and it dies. the queen of england also failed to water it. we say the gardener's omission caused the death but the queen's didn't. a theory has to explain why, and it can't just say "the gardener had a duty" unless it's willing to make causation depend on norms, which is a whole thing.
- the preemption problem: suppose lifeguard A doesn't jump in to save the swimmer, but lifeguard B was also on duty and also didn't jump in, and B would have failed to jump in even if A had jumped in. did A's omission cause the death? the standard counterfactual test gives weird answers here because the death doesn't depend counterfactually on A's omission if B would have failed anyway.
- the baseline problem: when we say someone "omitted" to do X, we're comparing them to some baseline of what they should have done or normally would have done. but what sets the baseline? is it statistical, normative, contextual? and if it's normative, are we still doing metaphysics or have we switched to ethics?
- the metaphysics question: are omissions even the kind of thing that can stand in causal relations? some people say omissions are absences, and absences aren't real, so omission "causation" is just a façon de parler we use when we're assigning responsibility. if that's right, then a theory of omission causation is really a theory of responsibility attribution, not causation. but then what do we do with cases where omissions seem causal but there's no responsibility at stake (like a rock that fails to stop a rolling boulder)?
- the consistency constraint: should a theory of causation by omission be a special case of our best theory of causation simpliciter, or is it its own thing? if you're a counterfactual theorist about regular causation, you probably want omission causation to also be counterfactual. but if you're a process theorist (causation is about physical processes, mark-transmission, etc.), omissions look really awkward because there's no process connecting the omission to the outcome. so is it a desideratum that the theory unify both kinds, or is that optional?
- the moral entanglement problem: we use causal language about omissions all the time in moral and legal contexts—"her failure to disclose the risk caused the patient's harm." but if our theory of omission causation builds in normative elements (duties, expectations, reasons), then we can't use causal judgments about omissions to ground moral judgments without circularity. so is it a requirement that the theory keep causation and normativity separate, or is that asking too much?

i want to know which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just things people worry about that a theory can legitimately set aside. and crucially: are any of these in tension? like, if you solve the selection problem by appealing to norms, you might fail the moral entanglement requirement. if you solve the preemption problem by going counterfactual, you might fail the selection problem. i need a map of the tradeoffs.
