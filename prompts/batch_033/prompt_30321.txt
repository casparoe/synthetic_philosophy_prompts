Last week I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether a machine that produces fluent, well-formed responses could be doing so without understanding any of it. The argument went in circles, and I came to think the reason was that neither of us had put on the table the idea that would have organized the whole dispute: the possibility of competence without comprehension. That argument now has a sequel—I have agreed to lead a two-hour discussion on exactly this idea for a reading group I help run, and I want the session to be better than our argument was.

The group is about a dozen thoughtful non-specialists, mostly from humanities and social-science backgrounds, with no biology or computer science beyond school. I want the session to make clear the two strange inversions of reasoning associated with Darwin and Turing: Darwin's, in which design-like competence in living things is explained without any designer who understands, and Turing's, in which intelligent-looking competence is explained without any inner understander doing the thinking. Do not explain the idea to me; give me a plan for the explanation itself.

Lay out the order in which the components must be introduced and make the dependency structure explicit: what has to be in place before what, and why compressing or reordering any step would make the inversions seem either trivial or mystical. Choose one carrying example or case that can do the most work across both inversions, and say what it must have and what it must lack if it is to carry the idea rather than quietly reintroduce an understander. Give me the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form—for instance, that Darwin's inversion reduces adaptation to pure chance, that Turing's inversion licenses the conclusion that the machine genuinely understands, or that the whole line is a skeptical denial that understanding exists anywhere—and say at which point in the sequence each should be headed off, before it has a chance to settle. Tell me what to leave out at this level and to announce that I am leaving it out. End with one question or short exercise that would show whether they have grasped the inversion itself rather than merely nodded along with the examples.

Where you make claims about how the idea is best taught or about what the inversions imply, say how confident you are in each, and single out the point you are least sure of.
