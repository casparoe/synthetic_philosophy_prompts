I spent my career in geology—mapping formations, reconstructing past environments, that sort of thing. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, and I've gotten really interested in this literature on the "standing to blame." The basic idea, as I understand it, is that even if someone's done something wrong, not everyone is in a position to call them out for it. If you've done the same thing yourself, or something similar, you might lack the standing to blame—it comes off as hypocritical. (I've been reading Macalester Bell, Kyle Fritz and Daniel Miller, R. Jay Wallace, that crowd.)

Anyway, I was re-reading about the Nuremberg trials the other day, and it struck me as a really hard case for this framework. You've got the Allied powers—the US, UK, France, and the Soviet Union—prosecuting Nazi officials for crimes against humanity, aggressive war, and so on. But the Allies themselves weren't exactly clean: the British and Americans firebombed Dresden and Hamburg and Tokyo and dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the Soviets had the Katyn massacre and the gulag system, the French and British both had brutal colonial empires. So if standing to blame requires something like "you're not guilty of the same kind of thing," it looks like the Allies might of lacked standing, at least on some charges. But at the same time, it seems absurd to say the Nuremberg trials were illegitimate or that the Allies shouldn't have prosecuted. The crimes were different in scale and kind—the Holocaust was an industrialized genocide—and maybe that matters.

So here's what I want: analyze the Nuremberg trials through the lens of the standing-to-blame literature. Map the players, the charges, and the relevant similarities and dissimilarities between what the Nazis did and what the Allies had done. Where does the theory say the Allies had standing and where didn't they? Does the theory have the resources to explain why Nuremberg feels different from ordinary cases of hypocritical blame, or does Nuremberg expose a limitation in how philosophers have been thinking about standing? And if the theory can't handle Nuremberg well, is that a problem for the theory, or is there a way to refine it?
