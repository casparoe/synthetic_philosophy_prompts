I've been rereading Hofstadter's "Person Paper" lately — you know, the one where he swaps sexist language for racist language to make the default assumptions visible. The basic move is: take some invisible "neutral" that's actually a specific perspective, and flip the categories so you can't not see it.

I want you to write a short story that does something in that spirit, but for a different hidden default. Not gender/race again — pick some other axis where our language or practices encode an assumption so deep we barely notice it. Could be speciesism, could be assumptions around neurotypicality, could be how we treat the dead vs the living, whatever you think is rich.

The story should use some kind of inversion or substitution device to make the invisible assumption visable. Doesn't have to be exactly Hofstadter's technique, but it should have that same "oh wait, why do we think that's normal?" effect.

Important: it should work as actual fiction. Characters, a situation, something at stake. Not a philosophical dialogue where everyone's just arguing positions. Make it feel lived-in, even if the world is strange.
