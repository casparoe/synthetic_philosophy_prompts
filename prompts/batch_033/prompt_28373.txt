I convene a fortnightly reading group for philosophy postgraduates. The members have all completed a first course in formal logic — truth tables and natural deduction for propositional logic — but only a few have worked with modal operators, and none has previously encountered Fitch's paradox. Next week's session is mine to lead, and I have seventy-five minutes to take the group from the anti-realist principle that every truth is in principle knowable to the Fitch derivation and the choice it forces among the knowability principle, the factivity of knowledge, the closure of knowledge under conjunction elimination, and the apparently plain fact that some truths are not known. I am not asking you to give the explanation itself; I am asking for a plan for running the session.

The plan should specify the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what, so that the collapse from knowability to actual knowledge feels like a forced consequence rather than a conjuring trick; the one example proposition that should carry the derivation, and what it must have and must lack; the misconceptions this particular audience is likely to form — I suspect they will slide between knowability and actual knowledge, or write the result off as a self-referential curiosity — and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; what should be left out at this level and openly flagged as omitted; and one closing question or short exercise that would show whether someone has grasped the structure of the argument rather than merely watched it go through. The plan should be written in British English and should not depend on any outside reading.
