I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about Adam Smith and social media, and I'd like to hear what someone else makes of the question.

The disagreement was this. My friend argued that Twitter is basically the impartial spectator made concrete: millions of strangers can observe your conduct and react, and the fear of their judgment disciplines behavior in exactly the way Smith described. The mechanism is the same — you imagine how an impartial observer would view your action, and social media just gives that observer real eyes and a real voice. Smith, he claimed, would have seen this as a natural extension of his theory and would have been broadly in favor of the accountability it creates.

I took the opposite side. The impartial spectator, as I understand Smith, is not just any observer — it's a figure you construct through sympathy with people you actually know and whose judgments you respect. It's calibrated by repeated, face-to-face interaction with a limited community whose members you can read in full context: tone, expression, history, the whole person. Social media replaces that with an anonymous, deindividualized crowd whose reactions are performative, often uninformed, and disconnected from any real relationship to you. The result isn't the impartial spectator; it's a distorted mirror that makes people either anxiously conformist or performatively outrageous, neither of which has much to do with genuine virtue. Smith, I argued, would have been horrified.

We went back and forth on this for a while and got nowhere. So here's what I'd like from you: a careful reconstruction of what Smith would likely have thought about the dynamics of social media judgment — call it online shaming, call it cancel culture, call it the digital public square — given his account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in The Theory of Moral Sentiments. I'm not asking for a bibliography or a list of passages. I want you to reason through it: what features of the impartial spectator, as Smith conceived it, map onto social media dynamics, and which ones don't? Where would the fit be close enough that Smith would recognize the mechanism, and where would he see a departure serious enough to change his evaluation? And what would he make of the fact that on social media, the "spectators" are real rather than imagined — does that strengthen the mechanism or break it?

Please also tell me how confident you are in your overall answer and single out the specific point in your reasoning that you're least sure about. I'm not looking for a definitive pronouncement — I want to see where the real pressure points in the argument are.
