I'm a philosopher working in social epistemology, and I've been preparing a module on the epistemology of disagreement. In that context I recently came across the following passage in a popular book on rationality that several of my students have read:

"If two rational people disagree about the probability of some factual claim, Aumann proved they only need to keep comparing notes. As long as each person honestly announces their current credence and both update on what the other says, their estimates will eventually converge to the same number. The upshot is that persistent disagreement among intelligent, well-informed people — about climate sensitivity, the effect of minimum wages, or the risk of a recession — can only be the result of irrationality or someone hiding relevant evidence they're unwilling to disclose."

I find this summary usefully vivid, but I suspect it compresses the actual result in ways that mislead, particularly about what the theorem licenses us to conclude about real-world disputes. I'd appreciate a careful audit. Specifically: what exactly did Aumann prove, and what conditions does the theorem require? Go through the summary sentence by sentence and separate what it gets right, what it gets subtly wrong, and what it omits that matters. Finally: does the practical diagnosis of persistent disagreement — that it must be irrationality or hidden evidence — survive a more precise statement of the theorem? I'm looking for the kind of answer I could use to help a sharp undergraduate who has absorbed this popular treatment understand what the theorem actually says and doesn't say.
