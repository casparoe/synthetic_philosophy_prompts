I serve on a regional health authority's priority-setting committee. We regularly consider proposals that would narrow an inequality in health outcomes or service provision between two groups by making the better-off group worse off, with no clear benefit to the worse-off group: for instance, closing a service in an affluent area to match provision in a deprived one, or capping a treatment for everyone so that the better-off lose access while the worse-off are unchanged and the gap shrinks. I want a reusable decision procedure the committee can run on any such proposal, grounded in prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection. It should ask its questions in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; say where the procedure stops with a 'no' and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts; and mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. We have about ninety minutes per proposal, no member has formal training in moral philosophy, and the decision must be defensible to elected officials. Then run the procedure on these three proposals: (a) closing a diabetes clinic in an affluent district to match provision in a deprived district, with no redeployment of staff or budget, where the only claimed benefit is that the deprived district no longer sees a visible symbol of unequal provision; (b) closing the same clinic and redeploying its staff and budget to the deprived district, where the deprived district gains, but by less in raw health terms than the affluent district loses; and (c) capping physiotherapy sessions for everyone at the lower of the two current levels, which leaves the worse-off group unchanged and returns the savings to the treasury, so that the gap closes while no one gains. At least one of these should produce an answer the committee will find difficult to accept, and you should say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Please write the answer in British English.
