I've been covering consciousness science for a while now, and theres one argument I keep coming back to because it seems frozen in a way that other scientific disputes arent. The setup is the old color-phi experiment from Kolers and von Grünau: a red dot flashes on a screen, then a green dot flashes a short distance away, and subjects report seeing a single dot glide across and turn green roughly halfway along the path — which is odd, because the information about the green flash wasnt available until after the midpoint had come and gone. The fight is about what the brain did to produce that report. One side, the Stalinesque view, says consciousness was held up until both flashes were in, so the subject really did see the whole arc, color change included, after the fact. The other side, the Orwellian view, says the subject had some experience in real time and the brain quietly revised the memory afterwards, so the report of a mid-path color change is a sincere but retroactively edited story. Dennett and Kinsbourne framed it this way, and everyone seems to agree on the timing of the lights, on what subjects say, and on most of the neuroscience. And yet nobody has budged in decades.

My working theory is that this isnt an empirical disagreement at all — that the two accounts are the same data described from two different points of view, and the real problem is that "when did the experience happen" doesnt have a determinate answer for a process thats spread across time. But I'm not confident, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this actually — substantive, verbal, about standards of evidence, about what an explanation of consciousness has to do? And given that diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress, or is the right move to stop asking the question in that form?
