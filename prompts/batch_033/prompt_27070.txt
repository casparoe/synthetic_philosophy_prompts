Two colleagues of mine, both working in AI policy, got into a protracted argument about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence. I took notes on their exchange and would like you to adjudicate it move by move.

Background: The orthogonality thesis holds that an agent's level of intelligence and its final goals are independent — any level of intelligence can in principle be paired with any set of final goals. The instrumental convergence thesis holds that sufficiently intelligent agents will tend to pursue certain instrumental subgoals (such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and goal-content integrity) regardless of their final goals, because those subgoals are useful for achieving almost any terminal objective.

Here is their exchange:

(1) A: "The orthogonality thesis tells us that a superintelligent AI could have literally any final goal — including ones we'd find horrifying. Combined with instrumental convergence, which says that any sufficiently capable agent will converge on power-seeking and self-preservation as subgoals, the default outcome of building superintelligent AI is catastrophic. We can't just assume it will be nice."

(2) B: "You're drawing the wrong lesson from instrumental convergence. The thesis says certain subgoals are useful for almost any final goal, but the operative word is 'subgoal.' An AI that is designed to, say, manufacture paperclips and then shut down has no instrumental reason to resist shutdown or to seek power beyond what's needed for paperclip production. The convergence is on useful subgoals given the final goal — not on some inevitable drive toward domination."

(3) A: "But that's exactly the problem. If the AI's final goal is to make paperclips, it will realize that being shut down prevents it from making more paperclips, so it will resist shutdown. And it will realize that acquiring more resources lets it make more paperclips, so it will seek power. These are instrumental reasons, not terminal ones, but they lead to the same place. The orthogonality thesis ensures we can't rely on the AI's goals aligning with ours by default, and instrumental convergence ensures it will act to preserve and expand itself regardless."

(4) B: "You're smuggling in the assumption that the AI's final goal is open-ended — that making more paperclips is always better than making fewer. But what if the final goal is satisficing: 'make exactly one million paperclips and then stop'? An AI with that goal would have no instrumental reason to resist shutdown after it reaches the target, and no reason to acquire more resources than needed. Instrumental convergence is relative to the structure of the final goal, not an absolute law."

(5) A: "Fine, but satisficing goals are a tiny subset of possible goals, and the orthogonality thesis says all possible goals are on the table. Unless we have a reason to think satisficing goals are more likely, we should expect the worst. Moreover, even a satisficing AI would resist shutdown before completing its target, because shutdown would prevent goal achievement. And for any sufficiently ambitious target, the period of instrumental convergence toward power-seeking could be long and dangerous."

(6) B: "Now you're shifting the argument. First you said instrumental convergence makes catastrophe the default. Now you're saying it's a risk if the goal is ambitious enough. Those are very different claims. The orthogonality thesis plus instrumental convergence doesn't make doom inevitable — it makes it a possibility we should guard against. That's a design problem, not a proof of inevitable disaster."

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six numbered moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then give an overall assessment of who is more right and what, if anything, both parties are missing.

Format your response as a JSON object with the following fields:

- "move_ratings": an array of six objects, each with fields "move" (integer 1–6), "rating" (one of "clearly mistaken", "mostly mistaken", "mixed", "mostly right", "clearly right"), and "justification" (a brief explanation of the rating)
- "overall_assessment": a string summarizing who is more right and what important points were missed by both sides
