I've been trying to figure out what I think a game is — not the dictionary definition, but what principle actually tracks my judgments about what counts as one. I went through a list of cases and recorded my verdicts. Now I want to know whether there's a coherent account behind them.

Here they are:

Chess is a game. Tag is a game. Solitaire is a game. So is a crossword puzzle, and so is a slot machine. A child alone throwing a ball against a wall and catching it, with no fixed rules beyond what they make up as they go, is a game.

Two dogs play-fighting is not a game in the same sense — that's a metaphor. The "game of politics" is also a metaphor, not the same concept. But I'm less sure about "mind games": it feels metaphorical too, yet somehow closer to the literal sense than "the dating game" does, and I can't say why.

A military war game is a game — it's a simulation with rules and objectives. The Turing test, though called the "imitation game," is not a game in the sense I'm after. A rat pressing a lever for food is not playing a game, even if the psychologist calls it one.

A video game where you explore an island with no score, no failure state, and no objectives is a game. But a virtual dollhouse with no goals at all, where you just arrange furniture — I'm not sure. I lean no, but I can't articulate what the difference is.

Russian roulette is not a game — and I don't think my objection is just moral. If I imagine a version with a harmless mechanism that randomly delivers a mild electric shock, I still don't want to call it a game. But a game of chance where you might lose money is a game. So the presence of a harmful stake isn't what's doing the work.

A formal debate with judges and rules is a game, or close enough. A casual argument is not. A conversation is not, even though it has turns and implicit norms.

A jigsaw puzzle is a game. Reading a choose-your-own-adventure book — I go back and forth. It has choices and rules, but it's also just reading. I think it's a game, but I'm not confident.

Ring-a-ring-o'-roses is a game. Peekaboo with a baby is a game. But is flirting a game? I want to say no, even though it's playful and has rules. That feels like a metaphor again, but I can't say why it's a metaphor and peekaboo isn't.

The stock market is not a game, even though people talk about "playing the market." But fantasy stock trading with fake money is a game. So the difference seems to be whether the stakes are real — but then why is poker for real money a game?

I want to know: is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? If you try to state necessary and sufficient conditions, what are they, and which of my verdicts do they force me to abandon? If no such conditions work, what kind of account does capture the pattern — a family resemblance structure, a prototype theory, something else — and does it predict that some of my verdicts are simply inconsistent with each other? Finally, please tell me where your reasoning is most vulnerable: what kind of case or argument would lead you to a different account than the one you give.
