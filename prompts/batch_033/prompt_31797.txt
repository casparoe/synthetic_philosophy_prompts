I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a novel in which the narrator's intentions are deliberately opaque — the reader won't be able to tell, even by the end, whether certain cruelties in the story were intended by the narrator or are artifacts the narrator remains unaware of. As part of my thinking, I've been returning to Ian McEwan's *Atonement* and the way it embeds the problem of authorial intention at multiple levels of the narrative.

I'd like a reading of *Atonement* through the lens of the philosophical debate about authorial intention and the meaning of a text. That debate is often framed as a standoff: anti-intentionalists hold that a text's meaning is fixed by its publicly available linguistic and literary features, not by whatever the author happened to have in mind; intentionalists hold that authorial intention is constitutive of meaning, and that to interpret a text just is to recover what its author meant by it. *Atonement* seems to me to stage this question rather than merely illustrate it. There is Briony-the-character, whose intention to identify Robbie as Lola's assailant destroys two lives — an act whose meaning and consequences far outrun her thirteen-year-old intentions. There is Briony-the-author, who in the novel's final section reveals that the reunion she narrated between Robbie and Cecilia was a fabrication, and frames that fabrication as her final act of atonement. And there is McEwan, who has built all of this and who, one presumes, had his own intentions in doing so.

My question is whether the novel, taken as a whole, comes down on one side of the debate — and if so, which. Does Briony's final confession suggest that her authorial intention to atone succeeds at something genuine, or does the novel treat her rewriting as one more evasion, a further refusal to face what she actually did? Does the novel imply that what an author intends matters to the moral and literary standing of the resulting text, or does it demonstrate that a text's meaning and its effects are ultimately independent of intention, however much an author wishes otherwise? And if the novel resists both positions — if it complicates the debate in a way the philosophical arguments themselves fail to capture — what exactly does the novel add?

I'd like your reading to be candid about where its own reasoning is most vulnerable. If there is an alternative reading of a key scene, or a way the novel pushes back against your interpretation, that would most likely lead you to revise your view, I'd like you to name it.
