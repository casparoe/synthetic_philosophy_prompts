I'm a retired molecular biologist who has taken up philosophy in the last few years, and I've worked myself into a corner I can't get out of. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis.

The question that started it was whether DNA literally encodes information or whether "the genetic code" is merely a vivid metaphor. I constructed the following argument, which seemed to settle the matter — until I noticed where it leads.

(1) For a physical structure to literally encode something, the mapping from its states to what it putatively encodes must be fixed by something beyond the bare causal relationship between them: a convention, an intention, or at least a history of selection that makes the mapping arbitrary in the right sense. Mere causal correlation is not encoding. (A thermometer does not encode the temperature; it merely correlates with it.)

(2) The mapping from DNA codons to amino acids is fixed entirely by the causal properties of the molecules involved. Given the stereochemistry of a codon, the structure of the corresponding tRNA, the enzymatic activity of the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, and the mechanics of the ribosome, a particular codon simply causes a particular amino acid to be added to the growing polypeptide chain. There is no convention, no intention, no interpreter standing outside the causal chain and applying a rule.

(3) Therefore DNA does not literally encode anything. "The genetic code" is a metaphor — an extraordinarily useful one, but a metaphor nonetheless.

So far so good, I thought. But then I tried the same reasoning elsewhere.

(4) Consider a computer executing a program. The mapping from bit patterns in memory to processor operations is fixed entirely by the causal properties of the hardware. A particular voltage configuration at the input to the arithmetic logic unit simply causes a particular output. The fact that human engineers designed the instruction set does not alter the physics: once the chip is fabricated, the causal relations are what they are, convention or no.

(5) By parity with (1)–(3), a computer program does not literally encode anything either. The "code" in "source code" is a metaphor.

(6) Now consider a written sentence. Ink patterns on paper reflect photons into a reader's eyes, triggering a causal chain that terminates in neural activations. The mapping from ink pattern to whatever the reader understands is, at bottom, a causal relationship — as any materialist about mind must grant.

(7) By parity, written language does not literally encode meaning.

(8) Therefore nothing literally encodes anything. All talk of codes, information, and encoding across every domain — genetics, computation, language — is metaphor.

I cannot accept (8). It seems to obliterate a distinction we cannot do without. Yet I cannot find the misstep. If I reject (1), I worry I lose any way to distinguish encoding from mere causation, and then everything that reliably causes something else "encodes" it — clouds encode rain, which trivialises the concept. If I reject (2), I seem to be claiming there is an intention or convention operating inside the cell, which looks like vitalism or worse. If I reject (4) by appealing to human design, I need to explain why the intentions of an engineer, who is not present in the causal chain when the program runs, make a difference to whether the running program literally encodes anything. And if I reject the parity between the cases, I need a principled difference, and I have not found one.

Where does this argument go wrong, and what does it cost to say so?
