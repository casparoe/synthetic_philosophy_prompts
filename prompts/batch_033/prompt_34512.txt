Two people in my social choice seminar got into an argument after we worked through the doctrinal paradox, and none of us could figure out which moves in their exchange were sound, so I would like you to referee it. The setup we were given was a three-member hiring committee that must decide by majority vote whether to make an offer, where the stated rule is that an offer is warranted only if the candidate meets the research bar and fits the teaching needs. The votes came out this way: the first member votes yes on research and yes on teaching, hence yes on hiring; the second votes yes on research but no on teaching, hence no on hiring; the third votes no on research but yes on teaching, hence no on hiring. So a majority says yes on research, and a majority says yes on teaching, but a majority says no on hiring — even though the committee's own rule makes those two premises sufficient for a yes.

Sofia argued that this shows majority rule cannot be the whole story for a body that has to take a position: the committee would be affirming both premises while denying what follows from them, which is exactly the kind of inconsistency we would not tolerate in an individual reasoner. Marcus replied that this treats the committee as though it were a person with a single mind, but there is no group mind; the premises and the conclusion are supported by different majorities, so there is no single subject whose attitudes conflict. Sofia answered that the committee is nonetheless a corporate agent — it will issue an actual decision and give reasons for it — so if its stated grounds do not support its decision, its public position is self-contradictory, and the choice between voting premise-by-premise and voting on the bottom line is a substantive choice that determines what the group can be held to have said. Marcus responded that the contradiction appears only if you insist on assembling the votes into one set of group judgments; a conclusion-based procedure simply never commits the committee to the premises, and a premise-based procedure never commits it to a majority verdict on the bottom line, so each procedure is internally consistent, and the paradox is just what happens when you mix the two. Sofia came back that this is the dilemma rather than a way out of it: whichever procedure the committee adopts, it loses something — under premise-based voting it may be forced to hire someone a majority voted not to hire, and under conclusion-based voting it must withhold the reasons that majorities actually endorsed — so saying "pick a procedure and stay consistent" does not make the trade-off go away.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: identify which claims are correct, which are overstated or confused, and what both parties are missing. Be specific about each step of the argument rather than just declaring a winner, and say, if either side is more right overall, which one it is.
