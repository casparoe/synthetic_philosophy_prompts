I'm a PhD student working on the philosophy of economics, and theres a debate about revealed preference and the meaning of utility that seems to have been stuck for as long as I've been reading it. One side says utility is nothing but a numerical representation of choice: once you start treating it as a real quantity of satisfaction or a mental state, you're smuggling in psychology the formalism doesn't need. The other side says that if utility is only a summary of choices, it can't do the job we want it to do in welfare economics — saying someone is better off requires a substantive notion of well-being, and choices are at best noisy evidence for that. The weird thing is everyone agrees on the axioms and the representation theorems, so the fight isn't over the math. My hunch is that this isn't one debate but two — one about what 'utility' should mean inside a model, another about what choices can tell us about welfare — glued together by a shared word. Is that right? What kind of disagreement is it, actually, and what would count as progress?
