I'm taking a grad seminar on Bayesian epistemology and we just spent two weeks on the problem of old evidence, and honestly I left more confused than when I started. The basic puzzle is: if you already know some piece of evidence E, then P(E) = 1, so P(H|E) = P(H) and E can't budge your credence in H. But that's obviously wrong — Einstein used the known anomaly in Mercury's perihelion to support general relativity, and nobody thinks that was epistemically inert just because the anomaly was already documented. So the simple Bayesian picture has a hole.

We went through a bunch of proposed patches. The counterfactual approach says: don't use your actual P(E) = 1, use the probability you *would have* assigned E before you learned it. The "expand the algebra" move (I think it's associated with Garber?) says you add propositions about logical or mathematical entailment relations to your event space, so what you conditionalize on isn't E itself but something like "H entails E." Then there's the approach that reframes the whole thing around discovery: you're conditionalizing on the fact that a theory predicts E, not on E, so the evidence that's doing the work is really about the theory's content, not the observation. There's the eliminativist line that says fine, just ditch the simple ratio measure of confirmation and use a measure that doesn't break when P(E) = 1. And there's the view that confirmation is fundamentally a three-place relation — evidence, hypothesis, background — and the problem only looks like a problem because we've been sloppy about the third relatum.

I'm trying to sort out which of these actually solves the problem versus which just relocates the tension to some other part of the formalism. Could you rank these five approaches from most to least satisfying, and for each give a sentence or two on what you take to be its biggest strength or its fatal weakness? I'm not after citations or a bibliography — just your own philosophical assessment of how well each one handles the core puzzle.
