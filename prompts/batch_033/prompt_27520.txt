Here is the exam question:

"Present the surprise examination paradox in your own words. Then evaluate the following claim: the paradox reveals a genuine limitation on what can be known about the future, and no satisfactory resolution can preserve both the intuitive idea that the teacher's announcement is perfectly coherent and the idea that knowledge is closed under known logical implication. Defend your own view on the matter."

And here is the student's answer:

"The surprise examination paradox runs as follows. A teacher announces to her class that there will be exactly one exam next week, Monday through Friday, and that the students will not know the night before which day the exam will be. A clever student then reasons backward from Friday: if the exam hasn't occurred by Thursday night, then it must be on Friday — but then the students would know this on Thursday night, contradicting the surprise condition. So the exam cannot be on Friday. Given that Friday is eliminated, if the exam hasn't occurred by Wednesday night, it must be on Thursday — but then the students would know this, again contradicting surprise. By parallel reasoning, the student eliminates Thursday, Wednesday, Tuesday, and finally Monday, concluding that no surprise exam is possible. Yet the teacher then gives the exam on Wednesday, and the students are genuinely surprised — so something has gone wrong in the student's reasoning.

The core of the paradox, I argue, is that the student's backward induction tacitly assumes that the truth of the teacher's announcement is available as a premise at every stage of the reasoning. In particular, the move from 'the exam hasn't occurred by Thursday night' to 'the exam must be on Friday' requires the student to know, on Thursday night, that the teacher's announcement was true. But this is precisely what is in question. The announcement is what Frederic Fitch and others have called an epistemic blindspot: a statement that can be true but cannot be known to be true by the relevant agent at the relevant time. If the students cannot know the announcement to be true, then the backward induction never gets off the ground.

This suggests a resolution: the teacher's announcement is perfectly coherent — it can be true, and indeed it is true when the exam is given on Wednesday — but the students cannot know it in advance. The paradox arises only because we conflate the truth of the announcement with its knowability. Once we recognize that there are truths that cannot be known, the paradox dissolves. The student's reasoning is valid only given the premise that the announcement is known; since that premise is false, the reasoning is unsound.

One might object that the teacher is a reliable authority, and the students therefore do know the announcement. But this misses the point: the announcement's content itself precludes its being known, regardless of the teacher's reliability. The proposition 'there will be a surprise exam next week' has the logical property that if the students knew it, it would be false — because knowing it would trigger the backward induction, whose conclusion contradicts the announcement. This is structurally similar to Moore's paradox: just as 'it is raining but I don't believe it is raining' can be true but cannot be believed without being false, the surprise exam announcement can be true but cannot be known without being false.

Thus I reject the claim in the prompt that the paradox reveals a limitation on knowledge of the future in general. The limitation is specific to self-referential epistemic propositions. Knowledge of ordinary future events is unaffected. I also reject the claim that no resolution can preserve both the coherence of the announcement and the closure of knowledge under known logical implication. Closure is preserved because the student's deduction, while logically valid, relies on a premise — namely, that the announcement is known — that is not itself known. Since closure only requires that known premises transmit their epistemic status to known logical consequences, and the key premise here is not known, closure does not require the student to know the conclusion of the backward induction. The paradox therefore does not force us to abandon any core epistemic principle."
