I have repeatedly encountered a configuration in moral arguments and have not been able to find out whether it has an established name. The clearest case is this. A theory holds that deliberately killing an innocent person is simply not to be done — the prohibition is not derived from its consequences but from what the act is. Now suppose attackers credibly promise to kill five innocent people unless you kill one. The theory forbids your killing, so you refuse, and the five are killed. Each of those five killings is, by the theory's own lights, just as forbidden as the one you declined to perform; yet the theory appears to forbid the one act that would have kept the number of such killings as small as possible. I have seen the same structure with other absolute prohibitions — against lying, or against breaking a promise — where obeying the rule in a particular case guarantees that the rule is violated more often overall. It is not a formal contradiction, since the prohibition was never stated as a requirement to minimize violations; but it seems to put the theory at odds with the concern that motivates it.

I have also seen a structurally similar complaint made against rule-based consequentialism — the charge that one is worshiping the rule by following it when breaking it would better serve the rule's purpose — but that objection seems to presuppose that the rule is justified by its results, whereas the position I have in mind denies that from the outset. And I have noticed that some defenders of the position, when the stakes become extreme enough, allow the prohibited act after all while insisting that this is an exceptional threshold rather than a change of view; I cannot tell whether that is a solution to the configuration or a way of changing the subject. Does this configuration have a name? I am not just after a label; I want to know how the problem is usually set up, whether it is regarded as a genuine difficulty or a confusion, and how it is distinguished from nearby ideas such as emergency thresholds, the distinction between what one does and what one merely allows to happen, and the rule-worship charge.
