I'm preparing a unit for an upper-level seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, and I want an exercise that pushes past the familiar summary in which Mencius is the optimist about human nature and Xunzi the pessimist. Please write a dialogue between Mencius and Xunzi — or between two equally committed proponents of their positions — in which each tries to persuade the other on the question of whether human nature, xing, is good or bad, and on what follows for morality, ritual, and self-cultivation. The two should be evenly matched and genuinely responsive: neither should be a strawman, and each should have to answer the other's strongest formulation rather than restate a slogan.

The dialogue should work through the conceptual fault lines rather than decorate them. Mencius should press the child about to fall into a well and the downward tendency of water, and should push Xunzi on where ritual and rightness could possibly have come from if human nature is originally bad. Xunzi should press his distinction between xing, what is inborn and spontaneous, and wei, what is achieved through deliberate effort and artifice, together with his craft metaphors of the straightening board and the grindstone; he should also press the charge that if nature were already good, the sage kings and their institutions would be superfluous. At some point the two should confront how much of their disagreement is about where to draw the line between nature and cultivation, and how much is substantive — whether moral effort completes an inborn tendency or transforms a recalcitrant one. End not with a declared winner but with each speaker stating, as precisely as he can, the one objection from the other that he still cannot fully answer.
