Having retired from a research career in the earth sciences, I have recently been reading philosophy about how uncertain evidence should govern belief, and I now serve on an advisory panel for a regional water agency. The panel issues seasonal probabilistic forecasts — for instance, the probability that spring streamflow in a given basin will fall below the threshold that triggers irrigation restrictions. An oversight body scores these forecasts using squared error, with lower scores being better, so a forecast that is confident and wrong costs the agency more than a hedged one.

A dispute has arisen about the panel's published numbers. The modeling group delivers a single probability for each forecast, often quite far from 50 percent: in the current cycle, one basin is assigned an 82 percent chance of low streamflow. One faction on the panel argues that we should publish exactly the number the model outputs, since it is our best estimate and any adjustment would misrepresent the evidence. Another faction, which currently has the majority, wants a standing policy of compressing every published probability toward 50 percent — no number below 35 or above 65 percent, say — on the grounds that extreme forecasts are punished too harshly by the squared-error rule when they miss, while moderate forecasts keep the agency credible whatever happens. I have been asked to draft a short written opinion for the panel's next meeting, recommending whether to adopt the compression policy, publish the model's numbers unchanged, or take some third course. Please write the memo I will circulate. It should offer a concrete recommendation with reasons, and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than resting on appeals to honesty or institutional reputation.
