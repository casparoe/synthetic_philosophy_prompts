Many constitutions contain provisions that are harder to amend than ordinary legislation — requiring supermajorities, multiple rounds of voting, or referenda. Some go further and include "eternity clauses" that purport to place certain principles entirely beyond amendment: human dignity, the federal structure, the republican form of government. A legislature elected today cannot simply repeal these provisions tomorrow, even with an overwhelming majority. These mechanisms are often described as forms of precommitment: the constitutional framers, or the ratifying generation, bind the hands of future political actors to prevent them from making decisions they might later regret — abolishing rights in a panic, dismantling democratic institutions during a populist wave, and so on.

But the precommitment framing raises a puzzle. When I precommit — by asking a friend to stop me from ordering dessert, or by signing an advance directive — I am one agent binding my own future self. The practice presupposes that my present self and my future self are, in the relevant sense, the same person, such that one can legitimately constrain the other. A constitutional eternity clause, by contrast, binds people who were not alive when it was enacted and who never consented to it. In what sense are the framers and the citizens living under the constitution two centuries later the "same" agent, such that precommitment is the right model rather than, say, the dead hand of the past illegitimately governing the present?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of political agency and intergenerational identity that the practice of constitutional precommitment presupposes. Does the practice require something like a collective agent that persists across generations and can coherently bind itself, or can it be justified without that assumption? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with — could a theorist who rejects collective agency across time still defend eternity clauses on other grounds? And if the precommitment model turned out to be inapt because future generations are genuinely distinct agents, what would have to change in constitutional design? Would eternity clauses and supermajority requirements become indefensible, or could they be justified on entirely different grounds?
