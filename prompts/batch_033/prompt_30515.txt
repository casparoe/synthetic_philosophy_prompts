I am a retired natural scientist—most of my working life was spent on problems in physical chemistry—and in the last few years I have been reading moral philosophy with a small group. We have been working through the debate between egalitarians and prioritarians, and I have encountered a chain of reasoning that seems to me to prove too much. I cannot accept its conclusion, but I also cannot locate the step at which it goes wrong, and I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis.

Here is the argument as I understand it.

(1) Prioritarianism holds that a fixed increment of well-being has greater moral importance the worse off the person is to whom it accrues.
(2) It follows that, when some fixed loss must fall somewhere, it is less bad for the loss to fall on the better-off person than on the worse-off person.
(3) But if a loss to the better-off is less bad than a loss to the worse-off, then there is at least one respect in which it is better to lower the better-off person's level even when no loss was unavoidable and no one else gains: the resulting outcome is less unequal.
(4) So prioritarianism appears committed to the view that leveling down—making the better-off worse off while improving no one—can be better in some respect.
(5) Yet an outcome that is better for no one cannot be better in any respect.
(6) Therefore prioritarianism is false.

I should say where my sympathies lie. Premise (1) captures what I find most compelling in the priority view: it directs concern toward those who are badly off without treating equality itself as a good. Premise (5) also seems to me close to undeniable; I find it hard to accept that a change that benefits nobody can be called an improvement in any sense worth caring about. And yet the chain feels inexorable when I read it.

My question is this: which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is not valid, identify the precise step and the principle that step presupposes, and say whether a prioritarian can deny that principle without ceasing to give priority to the worse-off. If the argument is valid, tell me which of (1) or (5) a defender of the priority view ought to surrender, and what remains of the view afterward.
