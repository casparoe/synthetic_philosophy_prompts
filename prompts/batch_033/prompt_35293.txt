Consider the following argument, which was presented in a seminar on metaethics:

(1) If moral realism is true, there are mind-independent moral facts.
(2) If there are mind-independent facts about a domain, then rational inquirers who are well-informed and free of bias should eventually converge in their judgments about that domain.
(3) We observe widespread, persistent moral disagreement among rational, well-informed, apparently unbiased people — about abortion, distributive justice, animal ethics, and so on.
(4) The best explanation of this persistent disagreement is that there are no mind-independent moral facts to converge on; moral judgments instead express attitudes, cultural norms, or other response-dependent features.
(5) Therefore, moral realism is probably false.

Now here is the problem. The same structure of argument seems to threaten epistemic realism — the view that there are mind-independent facts about what we ought to believe, what counts as a justified inference, and what makes an explanation good. There is widespread, persistent disagreement among rational, well-informed, apparently unbiased people about these very things: foundational disputes between internalists and externalists about justification, debates about whether inference to the best explanation is truth-conducive, disagreements about the correct logic for counterfactuals, and so on. If premises (1)–(4) succeed against moral realism, a parallel argument succeeds against epistemic realism.

But if epistemic realism is false, the original argument against moral realism is self-defeating: it relies on claims about what we ought to infer from disagreement, what counts as the best explanation, and what makes a belief probably false — all of which presuppose epistemic facts.

So the argument from moral disagreement either fails (if we reject one of its premises) or proves too much (if we accept it and watch it consume epistemic realism along with moral realism).
