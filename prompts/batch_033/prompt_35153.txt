You are a professor of metaphysics specializing in the metaphysics of dispositions, powers, and counterfactuals. You are preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the problem of finks, masks, and antidotes for the conditional analysis of dispositions. The students have read the core papers: Martin's electro-fink, Bird's antidote cases, Lewis's reformed conditional analysis, and at least one statement each of categoricalism, dispositional primitivism, pandispositionalism, and the identity theory.

Now you want to give them a diagnostic decision tree—a flowchart—that takes them from their initial reactions to the fink/mask problem to the position in logical space that best fits their commitments. The tree should be built from the logical space of possible answers to the key questions, not from the list of named positions. Only after the tree is built should the positions be located in it.

Specific requirements:

1. **Leaves to include**: The simple conditional analysis (as a starting point, not a live option), the sophisticated conditional analysis (Lewis 1997, Choi, Manley and Wasserman), categoricalism/realizer functionalism (Armstrong, Prior, Pargetter, and Jackson), dispositional primitivism (Martin, Molnar), pandispositionalism/dispositional monism (Mumford, Bird), and the identity theory/dual-aspect view (Martin's later view, Heil, Jacobs). If there are other live positions in the logical space, include them as well.

2. **Branching questions**: Each branch node should be a question that a student can answer based on the cases they have read, without knowing which position the answer leads to. Phrase the questions as choices about concrete cases or claims. For example, the first question might ask whether a finkish case (like the electro-fink wire, which is live but wired to a safety device that instantly cuts the current if touched) shows that the conditional analysis is fundamentally on the wrong track, or merely that it needs a more sophisticated formulation. Each question should genuinely separate positions in the logical space.

3. **Build from the space, not the names**: First articulate the questions and the space of possible answer-combinations they generate. Then locate the named positions in that space. Identify any regions of the logical space that are logically possible but (as far as you know) undefended in the literature, and briefly diagnose why: is the position incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

4. **Cross-cutting issue**: Some philosophers (McKitrick 2003) argue that dispositions need not be intrinsic; there are extrinsic dispositions (e.g., being a key, being vulnerable, weight). This issue cross-cuts the positions in the tree. Add a note at the end indicating which positions can accommodate extrinsic dispositions, which cannot, and whether any positions are formally committed to intrinsicness. If this cross-cutting issue would change the tree structure if it were made a branching node, explain how.

5. **Flag what the tree cannot separate**: If two positions give the same answers to all the branching questions you have identified, note this explicitly. Explain what additional question or case would separate them, and whether that question is actually contested between their proponents or just a difference in emphasis or packaging.

6. **Audience sizing**: The tree should have between 4 and 7 branching questions, producing somewhere between 6 and 10 leaves. It should be usable as a single-page handout, so keep the questions concise and the leaf descriptions to one or two sentences each.

Before you write the final tree, please think through the logical space step by step: what are the fundamental fault lines in the debate, what are the possible combinations of answers, and which combinations are occupied? Then build the tree from the root down, and finally add the leaves and the cross-cutting note.
