For an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, write a final exam essay question on the debate over whether qualia are an incoherent posit, together with a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain.

Question:

Dennett's "Quining Qualia" uses the Chase and Sanborn cases to attack the claim that qualia are directly or immediately apprehensible. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly as it always did but he no longer likes the taste; Sanborn says the coffee now tastes different but his preferences are unchanged. Both agree the coffee's chemical profile is identical, and both are sincere. Dennett's challenge is: if qualia were genuinely directly apprehensible, how could Chase and Sanborn disagree about whether their taste-quale changed or their preference changed?

Your task: explain how this challenge is supposed to work, then evaluate whether it succeeds in advancing Dennett's eliminativist conclusion about qualia. In your evaluation, you must consider at least one serious reply on behalf of the qualia realist and explain why it does or does not rescue the directly-apprehensible thesis. Your answer should make clear whether you think the Chase/Sanborn cases target qualia themselves or merely a certain epistemological claim about them, and whether collapsing that distinction matters for Dennett's larger project.

Model Answer:

Dennett's Chase and Sanborn cases are designed to undermine one of the four supposedly essential features of qualia: that they are directly or immediately apprehensible. The other three features — ineffability, intrinsicality, and privacy — are also targeted elsewhere in "Quining Qualia," but the Chase/Sanborn cases do a specific kind of work. If qualia are directly apprehensible, then there should be no room for the kind of sincere, unresolvable disagreement that Chase and Sanborn exhibit. Each should simply know, immediately and incorrigibly, whether his taste-quale has changed or whether his evaluative attitude toward an unchanged quale has shifted. The fact that they disagree — and that there is no further fact of the matter that either could consult to settle the question — suggests that the distinction between a quale and a reaction to it is not given in experience itself. It is a theoretical overlay we impose after the fact.

Dennett's strategy is to show that the concept of a quale does no explanatory work here. There is no independent test for whether Chase's quale changed or his preferences changed; the two hypotheses are empirically equivalent with respect to everything Chase can report. The qualia realist, Dennett argues, is committed to there being a determinate fact of the matter, because qualia are supposed to be the very stuff of conscious experience, directly known. But if Chase himself cannot access the fact, the notion of direct apprehension collapses into vacuity. The eliminativist conclusion is that qualia are not real features of the mind but artifacts of a mistaken theory about consciousness — a theory that insists on a sharp boundary between the sensory and the evaluative where none can be drawn from the first-person perspective.

A serious realist reply concedes the epistemological point but denies that it reaches the metaphysical one. The realist can argue that Dennett has shown only that we lack infallible introspective access to the boundaries of our qualia, not that qualia themselves are incoherent. Compare: we can be uncertain about whether a pain is in our elbow or our shoulder, but that does not show there is no pain. Similarly, the fact that Chase and Sanborn cannot tell whether a change is in the quale or in the attitude does not show there is no quale — it shows only that our introspective access is coarse-grained, fallible, or conceptually mediated. The directly-apprehensible thesis, on this view, was never meant to entail infallibility or perfect discrimination; it was meant only to capture the idea that there is something it is like to have an experience, and that the subject has a distinctive kind of epistemic access to that something — an access that may be partial, corrigible, and still different in kind from third-person access.

The question then becomes whether this more modest epistemology of qualia can survive. Dennett's deeper point is that once you grant that introspection does not carve nature at its joints — that the subject cannot reliably distinguish the sensory from the evaluative — you have given up the core functional role that qualia were supposed to play. Qualia were posited as the raw feels, the given, the bedrock of conscious experience. If they are not given as such, then the posit loses its motivation. The realist might reply that the motivation was never that qualia are infallibly given; it was that without them, we cannot explain the subjective character of experience. But Dennett's eliminativism is precisely the claim that the subjective character of experience can be explained without positing qualia — that "subjective character" is itself a theoretical construct we can dissolve by redescribing the phenomena in terms of reactive dispositions, discriminative capacities, and the contents of heterophenomenological reports.

In my view, the Chase/Sanborn cases succeed in their limited aim: they show that the directly-apprehensible thesis, if interpreted strongly enough to do the work qualia realists need, cannot be sustained. Whether this advances the eliminativist conclusion depends on whether the realist can articulate a version of qualia that does not require that strong interpretation. The cases push the realist onto a slippery slope: each concession about what introspection cannot deliver makes qualia less distinct from the physical and functional facts they were supposed to explain. Whether the realist can stop sliding is the open question the cases leave us with.

Marking Scheme:

A-grade answer must contain:
1. A clear statement of what the Chase/Sanborn cases are supposed to show — specifically, that they target the directly-apprehensible criterion, not ineffability, intrinsicality, or privacy. (2 marks)
2. An explanation of why the disagreement between Chase and Sanborn is supposed to be unresolvable from the first-person perspective, and why this is a problem for the claim that qualia are directly given in experience. (3 marks)
3. An explicit articulation of how this feeds into Dennett's eliminativist conclusion: the idea that qualia do no explanatory work and are artifacts of a bad theory. (2 marks)
4. At least one serious realist reply, presented with genuine force — not a strawman. The reply must grapple with the distinction between fallible access and no access, and must not merely assert that qualia are private. (3 marks)
5. An evaluation that takes a position on whether the cases target qualia themselves or merely an epistemological claim about qualia, and explains why this distinction matters (or does not matter) for Dennett's eliminativism. (3 marks)
6. A conclusion that identifies what the cases actually achieve and what they leave unsettled. (2 marks)

Total: 15 marks.

What separates an A from a B:
- A B answer will correctly describe the cases and state Dennett's conclusion, but will treat the realist reply as obviously wrong or will offer only a weak version of it. A B answer typically asserts that the cases succeed because "we can't know for sure" without connecting this to the specific theoretical role qualia are supposed to play.
- An A answer will show that the student understands the dialectical burden: Dennett needs the cases to do more than show fallibility — he needs them to show that the concept of a quale is theory-driven rather than experience-driven. An A answer will also recognize that the realist can retreat to a modest epistemology without immediately losing the concept of qualia, and will evaluate whether that retreat is stable. The A answer makes the debate about the relationship between epistemology and metaphysics in the theory of consciousness, not just about who is right about the coffee.
