I'm presenting a colloquium paper at a science and technology studies department, defending a qualified version of the claim that artifacts can have politics. My thesis: some technologies materially encode political arrangements not by determining social outcomes, but by asymmetrically structuring the space of possible social configurations — making certain arrangements easy or natural while rendering others impractical or unthinkable, and doing so in ways that persist across different contexts of use. This positions me against two camps: hard social constructivists (especially SCOT) who hold that politics enters only through interpretive flexibility and the closure of controversy, and hard technological determinists who hold that artifacts directly dictate social orders. I develop the argument using the Moses low-bridge case, Winner's claim about nuclear power requiring authoritarian control structures, and two contemporary cases — one drawn from algorithmic sentencing and one from urban sensor networks.

The audience is roughly thirty people. The core includes three scholars from the SCOT tradition who will push back fiercely on any attribution of politics to an artifact prior to or independent of the social processes that stabilize its meaning; two researchers from the Actor-Network Theory tradition who will reject the whole "social versus technical" distinction I rest my middle-ground position on; a political theorist who works on deliberative democracy and will press me on whether my view implies that technologies can preempt democratic choice; and a senior philosopher of technology who knows the Winner literature and its reception history exhaustively and who will spot every place my argument departs from or fudges Winner's original claims. The rest are graduate students and faculty from across STS, anthropology, and informatics, mostly sympathetic to constructivism.
