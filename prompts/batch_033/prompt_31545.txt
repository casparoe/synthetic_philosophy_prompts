i'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind and i've been circling panpsychism for a while, mostly Russellian monism and the newer constitutive versions, and I keep hitting the same wall. Most of the literature treats the combination problem as the main objection, but "the combination problem" seems to me to be at least four different problems wearing one coat. There's the subject-summing problem (how do many subjects add up to one subject at all), the palette problem (how does a small set of micro-qualities generate the full range of experiential qualities we actually have), the structural mismatch or grain problem (the structure of a unified experience doesn't look anything like the structure of a bunch of discrete physical constituents), and the boundary problem (why do macro-subjects form at exactly the boundaries they do—why my brain and not my brain plus a few neurons, or the two hemispheres separately). Each has its own corner of the literature, and I can't tell which one is actually doing the most dialectical work.

So treat this as an agenda question, not a survey. Suppose you're advising someone like me: a philosophy PhD with maybe three more years, no real neuroscience background beyond the usual philosophy-of-mind minimum, who could spend that time developing one serious line of response to one of these problems. Which sub-problem should I actually work on, and how would you rank the rest? I'm less interested in the list than in the reasoning: for each, is it tractable with purely conceptual tools, how much of the case against constitutive panpsychism actually depends on it, and would solving (or dissolving) it also settle or reframe the others. If you think one of them is a pseudo-problem, or that the real action is somewhere I haven't listed, say so and defend that instead. For what it's worth, i'm more interested in finding out whether panpsychism is true than in defending it, so I'd be fine ending up arguing that the combination problem kills constitutive panpsychism if thats where the work leads.

And could you write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just the way you'd write an email or a letter.
