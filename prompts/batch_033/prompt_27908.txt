In my philosophy of science unit we spent two weeks on thought experiments, and I came away puzzled in a way the readings didn't resolve. One side — call them the rationalist camp — holds that thought experiments like Galileo's falling bodies, Newton's rotating bucket, or Einstein's chasing a light beam genuinely yielded new knowledge about the world, knowledge that wasn't already implicit in the empirical data available at the time. The other side — call them the empiricist camp — argues that thought experiments can only reorganize or draw out what we already knew from observation; they're heuristics or intuition pumps, not independent sources of evidence. What bothers me is that both sides seem to agree on the historical record, both sides agree on the physics, and yet the debate has been running for decades without either camp moving an inch. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't really about thought experiments at all — it's about what counts as a source of knowledge, and that's a bedrock commitment each side brings to the table rather than something they can argue for without begging the question. Is that diagnosis right? If so, is the debate permanently stalemated, or is there some move that could still count as progress? If I'm wrong, what kind of disagreement is it actually — a hidden empirical question, a verbal dispute about the word "knowledge," or something else — and what would settle it?
