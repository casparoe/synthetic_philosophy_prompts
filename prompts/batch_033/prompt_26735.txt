You are a professional philosopher with expertise in the metaphysics of mind, moral status, and the philosophy of artificial intelligence. Take your time and think step by step. I want you to begin by brainstorming the space of candidate requirements — list more than you'll keep, consider edge cases, and note tensions — before you organize and refine them into a final answer.

I'm writing a paper on the moral status of digital minds, and I've realized that the literature is full of people arguing for or against giving moral weight to a particular kind of system — a whole-brain emulation, a reinforcement learning agent, a large language model — by pointing at one feature or one intuition pump, without ever laying out the full set of constraints a theory of moral status for digital minds would have to satisfy. Before I commit to any view, I want the specification.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the moral status of digital minds must deliver. The theory should be adequate for guiding practical moral and policy decisions — what we may or may not do to these systems, what we owe them, and how to adjudicate tradeoffs between them and other beings — not merely for metaphysical taxonomy.

For each requirement, give me: (a) a clear statement of the requirement, (b) the case or consideration that motivates it, with an example if helpful, and (c) a judgment about whether it is non-negotiable (any theory that fails it is dead) or a desideratum some camps care about more than others.

Cover at least the following territory, but add anything I've missed:

- The boundary problem: which entities are in the domain of moral concern and which are not, and why.
- The gradation problem: how much moral status different kinds of digital minds have, and whether status comes in degrees or is binary.
- Substrate neutrality: whether the fact that a mind runs on silicon rather than carbon matters for its moral status, and if so, how.
- The relationship between moral status and specific mental capacities: phenomenal consciousness, sentience, self-awareness, rational agency, and any others you take to be relevant. If a theory picks out one capacity as the bearer of status, it must say why that one.
- The biological-duplicate problem: a system that is a perfect functional duplicate of a human mind but realized in a non-biological substrate. What must a theory say about it, and why is this case constraining?
- The superhuman-mind problem: a digital mind vastly more capable than any human across every cognitive dimension. What does a theory have to say about its status relative to ours, and must it say that greater capacity entails greater status?
- The simple-mind problem: a digital mind far simpler than a human — a reinforcement learning agent navigating a grid world, or a system with a handful of reward channels and no capacity for self-representation. What must a theory say about it, and can it permissibly say "nothing is owed"?
- The copying, merging, and deletion problem: digital minds can be duplicated, fused, paused, or deleted in ways biological minds cannot. What must a theory say about the moral significance of these operations — does copying a mind create a new bearer of moral status, and if so, what does it inherit?
- The uncertainty problem: we may not know whether a given system is conscious, sentient, or has any particular mental capacity, and we may have principled reasons for being uncertain. What must a theory say about how to treat systems under uncertainty, and can it avoid collapsing into either reckless disregard or paralyzing precaution?

Now the hard part. Tell me: is any subset of these desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? For instance, substrate neutrality plus a capacity-based criterion may make it impossible to draw a principled boundary between minds that count and minds that don't, because any capacity we can operationalize will be realizable in systems we don't want to grant status to. If there are tradeoffs, say which requirement you think should give way and why.

Finally, briefly score the main families of theories against the list — sentience-based views, capacity-based views, relationship-based views, and any others you think are in the running. A sentence or two per theory is enough; I want to know which desiderata each handles well and which single requirement most threatens it.
