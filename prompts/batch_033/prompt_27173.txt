I spent my career in planetary science, so the 2006 Pluto vote is the kind of thing I have strong feelings about. Lately I've been reading the social construction of kinds literature — the race and gender stuff, looping effects, all that — and I keep wondering whether the lens actually fits what happened with Pluto. I'd like you to run the analysis for me.

Here's the episode as I remember it. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union voted on a new definition of planet. The trigger was Eris, a Kuiper belt object that looked to be at least as big as Pluto. The definition they adopted has three parts: it orbits the Sun, it's round from self-gravity, and it has cleared its orbital neighborhood. Pluto fails the third part, so it got moved into a new category, dwarf planet. The vote itself was a bit messy — alot of the members had already gone home, and the final definition got hashed out at the last minute.

Here's my confusion. When I read the social construction people, I can't tell whether Pluto is a perfect case for them or a problem for them. On one hand, nothing about Pluto changed in 2006; what changed was a category, and it changed because a human institution voted. Planet had been doing non-scientific work for a long time — tradition, schoolroom mnemonics, the fact that people got sentimental about a small icy body. That sounds alot like social construction. On the other hand, there does seem to be a real dynamical difference between the eight planets and the swarm of Kuiper belt objects, and the vote was at least trying to track something physical, not just decide who gets to count.

So what I want is this. Walk me through the Pluto demotion the way a social constructionist about kinds would analyze it: what are the human practices and interests doing the constructing, what does the theory explain well about the episode, and where does it strain or misdescribe what was actually going on? Then tell me what the episode says about the theory — does Pluto make social construction of kinds look more plausible, or does it show the view overreaches when it moves from race and gender to natural-science categories? And one more thing I keep chewing on: Ceres was called a planet when it was discovered in 1801, then quietly demoted as more asteroids turned up. Does that earlier demotion, which happened without any big public vote, change the analysis?
