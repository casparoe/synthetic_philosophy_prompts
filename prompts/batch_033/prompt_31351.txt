I sit on the retirement benefits committee of a large public university system, and we vote on Thursday whether to adopt a new default for employee retirement contributions. Under the proposal, employees would be enrolled in a schedule that raises their contribution rate by one percentage point each year, up to a cap of ten percent, unless they opt out in writing at any time. The background we were given is this: when automatic enrollment at a three percent rate was introduced a decade ago, participation jumped and nearly everyone stayed in, yet very few employees have ever raised their rate above that floor, even though periodic surveys consistently show a large majority saying they intend to save more. One side of the committee argues that the escalation default merely operationalizes what employees already want for themselves in the long run, and that respecting a person's considered goals can require setting a default that their tired, rushed, or discouraged present self would not choose on any given day. The other side argues that this is paternalism wearing a neutral costume: a person's present choices are the only choices she actually makes, the institution has no business deciding that one of her selves is the real one, and an opt-out default works by exploiting inertia and inattention rather than by persuading anyone. The argument has turned partly on whether sharply discounting one's later self is a bias to be corrected or a legitimate preference to be respected.

I have been asked to draft the short written opinion the committee will circulate before the vote. Please write that memo for me. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should engage the strongest version of the losing side's position rather than dismissing it. Near the end, please say what consideration would most likely change your recommendation, or where you think your reasoning is most exposed to failure. And please write it in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter to colleagues.
