I'm giving a paper at a moral philosophy workshop next month and I've spent so long inside my own argument that I can't tell which parts are actually going to get attacked. The thesis is that a fairly orthodox act-utilitarianism — maximize total well-being, everyone counts for one and no one for more than one, where well-being is cashed out as something like hedonic state or satisfied preferences — survives the utility monster objection. My line is roughly this: the monster is just the limiting case of the same logic that makes utilitarians feed the starving person over the sated one, and the revulsion we feel is tracking the monster's weird biology, not a moral feature of the distribution. Also, in any realistic world with diminishing marginal utility the monster wouldn't actually get everything, so the objection only bites at the level of pure theory, and every theory has a bullet to bite at that level. I want to say the right response to the monster is "yes, and?" rather than retreating to prioritarianism or adding seperate side-constraints.

The audience is not the EA or LessWrong crowd. It's a workshop of mostly non-consequentialist moral philosophers: two contractualists (one very Scanlon-ish), a Kantian, a prioritarian who thinks the monster is the best ad for weighting the worse off, a welfare economist who works in social choice and keeps asking what the social welfare functional looks like, and a grad student doing population ethics who will probably worry about variable populations. I expect the objections to come in their vocabulary, not mine.
