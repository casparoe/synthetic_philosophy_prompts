A colleague and I have spent several months arguing about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and we have decided to write up the dispute as an adversarial collaboration rather than continuing to talk past one another. One of us holds that epistemic akrasia is always irrational: roughly, a fully rational agent cannot believe a proposition while at the same time believing that her total evidence does not support that proposition, or that her believing it is irrational; on this view, any adequate account of rationality must include an enkratic requirement tying first-order attitudes to the agent's own higher-order assessment of them.

The other holds that rationality can come apart across levels: there are cases in which an agent's first-order evidence genuinely supports p even though her higher-order evidence misleadingly indicates that it does not, and in such cases the rational agent may believe p while also rationally believing that her evidence does not support p. On this second view, epistemic akrasia is sometimes not merely excusable but permitted, and perhaps even required.

Please draft the document that both of us could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should open with a numbered list of claims we both accept, stated precisely enough to be non-vacuous — covering what higher-order evidence is, what a case of epistemic akrasia involves, and what any satisfactory treatment of such cases must explain. It should then set out our residual disagreement in a disagreements table, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would endorse without reservation; we would rather have two sharp formulations than one neutral sentence that blurs the difference. After that, identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that a settled answer to them would move at least one of us — and, for each crux, give the concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it (for example, a meticulous proof undermined by misleading testimony about one's own fatigue, a reason-distorting drug whose effects are fully understood, or a peer-disagreement case in which the higher-order evidence appears self-undermining). Record each side's prediction about how each case should be classified, and why. Finally, state explicitly whether any part of our dispute is merely verbal — for instance, a difference over how the word 'rational' is being used — and, if so, which part is verbal and why the remainder is not.
