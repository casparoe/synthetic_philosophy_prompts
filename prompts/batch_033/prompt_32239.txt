I'm a third-year undergraduate currently enrolled in a philosophy of probability course. We recently spent two weeks on the reference class problem, and I'm struggling to apply what we've learned to a situation I'm facing outside the classroom.

My mother was diagnosed with a serious illness last month. After reviewing her case, the oncologist told us that the five-year survival rate for someone with her profile is about 85%. The number was delivered with the intention of being reassuring, and my family took it that way. But I've been unable to stop thinking about what I learned in class. The oncologist's figure must come from situating my mother within some reference class — but which one? Is it women of her age with this cancer diagnosis? Women of her age, ethnicity, and stage at detection? Women treated at this specific hospital? Women whose tumors exhibit the particular genetic markers hers does? The survival rate could shift meaningfully depending on which class is chosen, and I have no way of evaluating whether the oncologist selected the right one. Worse, our seminar readings suggested that for a genuinely singular case, there may be no principled way to determine which reference class is correct — that the problem may not have a solution at all.

I want to be clear: I'm not asking for medical advice. My question is philosophical. Given the reference class problem, how should a person think about a single-case probability like this one? Is there a defensible way to treat the 85% as genuinely informative, or does the reference class problem undermine the very practice of offering individualized prognoses? I need to figure out how to relate to this number — whether to be reassured by it, or to regard it as too arbitrary to guide my emotions or decisions.
