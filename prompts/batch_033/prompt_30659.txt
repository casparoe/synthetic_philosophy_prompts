I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about wireheading, and I'm still not sure who had the better of it. Here's the exchange as I remember it — I've tried to be fair to both sides. I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

(1) My friend started us off: if neurotechnology advanced to the point where you could directly stimulate the brain's reward system and live in a state of uninterrupted, maximally intense bliss — no work, no struggle, no relationships — that life would be better than any ordinary life, because subjective experience is the only thing that ultimately makes a life go well.

(2) I pushed back: a life spent feeling proud of achievements you never achieved, loved by people who don't exist, and satisfied by projects you never undertook is a life of comprehensive delusion. Calling it "good" drains the word of meaning.

(3) She replied that this is just table-pounding. If the experience of pride in a real achievement and the experience of pride in a simulated one are subjectively identical, and experiences are what matter, then they're equally valuable. "Delusion" is a label, not an objection.

(4) I shifted ground: even if a single wireheaded life might be good for that person, a world where everyone wireheaded would be a disaster — no one would produce food, care for children, or sustain civilization. So we have reason to condemn the choice.

(5) She called this a scope error. Lots of individually fine choices — becoming a monk, retiring at thirty, having no children — would be catastrophic if universalized. That doesn't make them bad for the people who choose them. The question is whether one person's wireheaded life is good for them.

(6) I appealed to Nozick's experience machine: most people, offered the chance to plug in, wouldn't. That shows we value more than the felt quality of our experiences — we want to actually do things and be certain kinds of people.

(7) She said intuitions elicited by science-fiction vignettes prove nothing. If we can explain the reluctance to plug in as status quo bias, loss aversion, or an evolved preference for agency that carries no normative weight, the intuition loses its force. "Most people wouldn't" doesn't entail "most people shouldn't."

(8) I made one last attempt: pleasure that isn't about anything real — pleasure with no genuine intentional object — is fundamentally different from the pleasure of, say, finishing a marathon. The value of the marathoner's pleasure is partly constituted by its connection to the accomplishment. Wireheaded pleasure is empty pleasure.

(9) She answered that this is an empirical claim disguised as a conceptual one, and likely false: direct brain stimulation can produce experiences that feel richly meaningful and fully intentional to the subject. If the phenomenology is identical down to the apparent aboutness, the objection collapses.
