I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I recently overheard two colleagues arguing after a talk on predictive processing and the free energy principle (FEP). Neither of them works in neuroscience, but both follow the field closely. Their exchange went roughly as follows, and I'd like an expert adjudication of who is right about what.

**Arjun**: The free energy principle claims that organisms act to minimise free energy, which is an upper bound on surprisal—where surprisal is just the negative log probability of sensory states under the organism's own generative model. So the principle says: organisms act to minimise the surprisal of sensory states *as evaluated by their own internal model*. But that means the model itself dictates what counts as surprising, and the organism then acts to bring about what the model expects. Isn't that circular?

**Mei**: I don't think it's circular. It's more like a variational principle in physics—the principle of least action doesn't claim particles *compute* anything; it's a description of the trajectory they must take. Similarly, FEP says that any system that maintains its organisation over time *must*, as a mathematical necessity, behave in a way that can be described as minimising free energy. It's not a causal claim about what the organism is consciously doing.

**Arjun**: But that shifts the problem from circularity to vacuity. If FEP is true of *any* persisting system in virtue of the mathematics, then it doesn't distinguish between a bacterium, a rock, and a human reasoner. It can't be falsified because it's always true by stipulation. The genuinely interesting empirical claims—hierarchical predictive coding, precision-weighting, prediction error minimisation in cortex—come from predictive processing, which is a separate research programme with its own history. FEP didn't generate those ideas; it was retrofitted onto them.

**Mei**: I'd push back on two fronts. First, the FEP and predictive processing aren't separate in the way you're suggesting. The FEP tells you *why* a hierarchical generative model is a good solution: because it lets you minimise free energy efficiently by explaining away prediction error at multiple scales. It provides a normative framework that justifies the architecture. Second, unification *is* a genuine theoretical contribution. Showing that perception, action, attention, and planning all fall out of the same underlying optimisation problem isn't nothing—it's explanatory. Not every valuable scientific contribution takes the form of a de novo prediction.

**Arun**: I accept that unification can be valuable, but only if the unifying framework is constrained enough that it *could have* turned out differently. My worry is that FEP uses terms—"generative model," "surprisal," "priors"—in such a flexible way that any behaviour can be re-described in its vocabulary after the fact. If you can always find some model relative to which the behaviour was free-energy-minimising, then the framework doesn't constrain anything. At that point "unification" is just translation.

**Me**: I think that's too uncharitable. Active inference, which follows from FEP, makes specific commitments: for instance, that motor commands are realised through top-down predictions of proprioceptive states, not through separate controller circuits. That's a substantive claim about neural architecture. It also predicts sensory attenuation during self-generated movement—we dampen the precision of sensory evidence during action so that our predictions play through. These aren't post-hoc translations; they're empirical commitments.
