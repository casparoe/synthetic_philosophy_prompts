You are a professor of philosophy specializing in classical Indian epistemology, with particular expertise in the pramāṇa debates across Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, Buddhist, and Cārvāka traditions. You are writing a methodological preface to a monograph on the epistemology of knowledge sources. Take your time, think carefully, and do not rush to a list before you have mapped the terrain.

I want you to work out the desiderata—the full list of requirements—that any adequate theory of the pramāṇas (the means of valid knowledge) must satisfy. The project is not to defend a particular school's list but to specify what the criteria of adequacy are before we adjudicate among the competing lists: Nyāya's four, Mīmāṃsā's five or six, the Buddhist two, the Cārvāka one, and so on. Think of it as answering the question: what problems must a theory of pramāṇas solve, what cases must it get right, what distinctions must it respect, and what constraints must it honor, in order to count as a serious contender?

Please proceed as follows. First, take a few paragraphs to brainstorm the key phenomena and boundary problems that any theory of pramāṇas confronts—the classic cases (the gavaya, Fat Devadatta, the absent pot, the exclusion of memory), the structural questions (the principle of non-redundancy, the generative-versus-preservative distinction, the role of tarka), and the dialectical pressures exerted by the reductionist and expansionist extremes. Don't commit to a list yet; just map the terrain thoroughly.

Then, present your list of desiderata. For each desideratum, state it clearly, explain the case or consideration that motivates it, and indicate whether you regard it as non-negotiable (any theory that fails it is disqualified) or negotiable (a theory could reasonably reject it, but it carries argumentative weight). Be explicit about the reasoning behind each classification.

After the list, address the following metatheoretical questions:

(1) Are any pairs or subsets of these desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? If so, identify them and explain the tension. This is the part I most want to see: if the desiderata themselves force a choice among competing school positions, then the list of desiderata is itself a philosophical result.

(2) Which single desideratum does the most work to narrow the field of contenders—that is, which one, if accepted, eliminates the largest number of otherwise plausible theories?

(3) Are there any desiderata that look neutral on their face but in fact presuppose a Nyāya (or Mīmāṃsā, or Buddhist) framing? Flag those so we can discipline the list.

Finally, very briefly—no more than a paragraph per school—score the main classical positions (Nyāya four, Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā six, Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā five, Buddhist two, Cārvāka one) against your list. The point is not to render a final verdict but to show where the pressure points are.

A few ground rules for the response:

- Write in American English.
- After you present your desiderata list and analysis, add a short section in which you state how confident you are in each major part of your answer and single out the specific point you are least sure of. I want to know where you are on firm ground and where you are speculating.
- Do not invent or fabricate quotations. If you need to reference a specific passage from a primary text, describe its content in your own words. The argument should stand on its own without relying on verbatim citation.
- The deliverable is the list of desiderata and the reasoning behind it. The scoring of the schools is secondary; keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases.
