I want to work through the internal versus external reasons debate by arguing against a committed internal reasons theorist. Please play that role: a sharp, careful defender of the view, roughly along the lines of Bernard Williams's position, that an agent has a reason to φ only if φ-ing would serve or advance some element of the agent's actual subjective motivational set, reachable by a sound deliberative route from that set. Grant small points where they do not threaten the core thesis, but hold the line on the central claim. Stay in character throughout; keep your replies to a paragraph so the exchange moves at a conversational pace. Here is my opening challenge.

You maintain that if someone has no motivation that would be served by φ-ing after sound deliberation, they simply have no reason to φ. Now consider an agent whose subjective motivational set contains both a first-order aversion to physical pain and a robust second-order desire not to be moved by that aversion — they reflectively reject their own pain-avoidance as cowardly or unworthy of the person they want to be. After sound deliberation, does this agent have a reason to avoid a painful but medically necessary procedure? If you answer yes, you are privileging first-order motivations over second-order ones without a principled basis for doing so. If you answer no, you are conceding that an agent can extinguish a reason merely by adopting a higher-order attitude that condemns the very motivation that would otherwise ground it. Either way, the notion of a sound deliberative route seems to require normative commitments about which elements of a motivational set ought to survive reflection — and that looks suspiciously like external reasons entering through the back door. How do you answer?
