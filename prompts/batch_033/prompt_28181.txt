So I just spent like three hours arguing with a friend about Frankfurt cases and now I can't tell if I even know what I think about responsibility anymore. The principle of alternative possibilities says you're only morally responsible for something if you could have done otherwise. Simple enough. But then Frankfurt comes along with these cases where someone *couldnt* have done otherwise but we still want to hold them responsible, and my friend and I kept going in circles.

I'm trying to sort out my actual verdicts on cases, not what I'm supposed to think. Here's where I land:

1. The classic Frankfurt case — Jones is going to kill Smith, Black has secretly implanted a device that would make Jones kill Smith if he showed any sign of wavering, but Jones never wavers and does it entirely on his own. I think Jones is fully responsible. Couldn't have done otherwise, but still responsible.

2. Same setup, but this time Jones *does* waver — he starts to lose his nerve, the device kicks in, and he kills Smith because of the device. I think Jones is not responsible for that killing. Or at least way less responsible. But my friend pointed out that in both cases Jones couldn't have done otherwise, so if PAP is false in case 1 why am I letting it back in for case 2?

3. Someone holds a gun to your head and says "rob this store or I'll kill your family." You rob the store. I don't think your responsible for the robbery. The fact that you couldn't have done otherwise seems to be doing all the work here.

4. A kleptomaniac who genuinely cannot control the impulse steals a wallet. I don't think they're responsible. Again, the "couldn't have done otherwise" seems to matter.

5. But then: someone is raised in a cult and as an adult does something terrible that their upbringing made almost inevitable. I still think they're somewhat responsible, even though the "could have done otherwise" is really strained here. Not as responsible as someone who wasn't raised that way, but not off the hook either.

6. Here's one that really tripped me up: a person is so deeply kind, so constitutively incapable of cruelty, that when they see someone in distress they "couldn't" not help. I think they're praiseworthy for helping. The couldn't-have-done-otherwise doesn't seem to undermine the praise at all. But if that's true, why does it undermine blame in the coercion case?

7. Last one: someone forgets their friend's birthday. They genuinely tried to remember — set a reminder and everything — but their phone died and they just forgot. They couldn't have remembered at the relevant moment. I still think they're on the hook somehow. Not as much as if they'd blown it off, but not zero.

So I've got cases where "couldn't have done otherwise" seems to defeat responsibility (coercion, kleptomania), cases where it doesn't (Frankfurt non-intervention, the kind person), and cases in the mushy middle (cult upbringing, forgetting). My friend kept saying I'm just being inconsistent and that I need to either keep PAP or give it up entirely. But I feel like there's something about *why* the person couldn't have done otherwise that matters, and PAP is too blunt.

Is there a principle that captures this pattern? What does it say about the cases I'm wobbling on? And which of my verdicts, if any, do I have to abandon?
