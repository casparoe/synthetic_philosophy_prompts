I'm giving a presentation next month to the program staff and external reviewers of a philanthropic foundation that requires every grant proposal to carry a reference-class forecast, drawn from base rates of comparable past projects, and that treats the outside view as the main safeguard against overoptimistic proposals. I'm defending the claim that, for genuinely unprecedented interventions, this practice systematically underweights the inside view, and that epistemic modesty cuts the other way: forcing a reference class can manufacture false confidence by hiding contestable similarity judgments inside a number.

My argument in brief: (1) selecting a reference class is itself an inside judgment about which past projects are relevantly similar; (2) the base rate inherited from that class silently carries those similarity assumptions, so it cannot check the inside view without partly presupposing it; (3) for one-off interventions the variance across defensible reference classes is usually larger than the uncertainty the outside view was meant to reduce; (4) so deference to the outside view is often a disguised inside view wearing an exaggerated air of modesty.

The audience is mostly economists and operations-research-trained evaluators who are accountable for the foundation's grant decisions, who take the planning fallacy and optimism bias to be the default explanation of failed programs, and who see the outside view as their protection against being sold a story; a smaller group are epistemologists who work on higher-order evidence, disagreement, and the limits of self-knowledge about bias.
