Analyze the Anglo-French Concorde supersonic airliner project through the lens of the sunk cost fallacy, and use the episode to test the claim that honoring sunk costs is always irrational.

The facts in brief: Development began in 1962 under a treaty between Britain and France. By the early 1970s, it was clear that the Concorde would never recover its development costs or turn an operating profit — fuel costs were high, sonic booms restricted viable routes, and airlines were cancelling their orders. Both governments had commissioned independent economic assessments confirming the project would lose money, and both governments acknowledged this internally. Nevertheless, they continued to fund the project through to commercial service in 1976 and kept it flying until 2003. Throughout, officials on both sides justified continued funding by appealing to the enormous sums already spent and the political cost of cancellation.

The standard verdict is that this was a textbook case of the sunk cost fallacy: the money already spent could not be recovered, so it should have been irrelevant to the decision about whether to continue. I want a more careful analysis.

First, map the decision points as the theory of rational choice would have them: what were the marginal costs and benefits of continuing at each stage, and what would a forward-looking cost-benefit analysis have said? Second, consider what the sunk cost label might be missing. The Concorde was a joint project between two nations under a treaty; cancellation by one party would have had diplomatic and legal consequences. There were also concerns about technological prestige, about the aerospace workforce, and about the precedent that cancellation would set for future joint projects. Are any of these legitimate reasons to continue, or are they just dressed-up versions of the sunk cost fallacy? If they are legitimate, does that mean the sunk cost principle needs qualification, or does it mean that the Concorde case was not, in fact, a case of honoring sunk costs at all? Third, what does the episode reveal about the limits of applying the sunk cost concept to large-scale political decisions — decisions where the "costs" include political capital, international relationships, and institutional momentum — rather than to the individual consumer decisions the concept was originally designed for? Does the Concorde case suggest that the sunk cost principle is sound but narrow, or that it is incomplete as a guide to rational action?
