So I teach a philosophy of mind seminar for undergrads — 14 students, 80-minute sessions, big room with movable chairs and a tile floor we can mark up with tape — and this year I've also gotten weirdly into antkeeping with my kid; we have a little formicarium on the kitchen counter. So now the Ant Fugue chapter from Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach has stopped being just another reading and started feeling like a live question. You know the bit: Aunt Hillary and the anteater go back and forth about whether the ant colony as a whole can have thoughts, feelings, a mind, even though no individual ant does.

I want to run a class where the students are the ants. By the end of it, I want them to have produced something at the group level — a decision, a path choice, a pattern — that none of them individually intended or fully understood, so that the claim "the colony did it" stops sounding like a metaphor. Then a debrief that maps exactly what happened back to the moves in the Ant Fugue: the signal trails, the aggregation, the way a bunch of individually clueless parts can look like one smart thing. And I want them to leave genuinely unsure whether the colony "has a mind," or at least sure why the question is hard.

Can you design the actual activity? I need concrete rules simple enough that 14 people can learn them in five minutes, a list of materials (nothing expensive — paper scraps, tape, colored tokens, string, whatever), a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief script that names the moments in the game that correspond to parts of the ant-colony-as-mind idea. The idea should hit them during the activity, not in a lecture afterwwards — so no framing up front about what they're supposed to notice, and maybe don't even say "Ant Fugue" until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (someone breaks character and starts planning, trails get too crowded, that kind of thing) and how to fix it. And be honest about the one big thing the activity can't give them: they'll probly see smart group behavior, but that doesn't by itself settle whether there's anything it's like to be the colony. I need the debrief to flag that gap rather than paper over it.
