I'm taking an epistemology grad seminar and last week we were discussing the dogmatism paradox — you know, the thing where if you know P, you seem entitled to dismiss any counterevidence as misleading without even looking at it, which can't be right. Two people in the class, Maya and Leo, got into a genuine argument about it and neither would conceed anything. I've been replaying it and I honestly can't tell who has the better case. Here's roughly the exchange:

Maya: The paradox isn't just a cute puzzle — it shows that knowledge as we ordinarily conceive it is incoherent. If I know my car is in the garage, then I know that any evidence suggesting otherwise is misleading. So if my neighbor texts me "your car's not in the garage," I'm rationally entitled to dismiss that text without checking. But obviously I should check. So either I never really knew my car was in the garage, or knowledge doesn't entitle you to ignore counterevidence, in which case the closure principle that generated the paradox has to go.

Leo: I think you're running together two different epistemic moments. Before the text arrives, you know your car is in the garage, and you know that if counterevidence appears it'll be misleading. But when the text actually arrives, you now possess a piece of evidence you didn't have before. Your total evidence has changed. And it's perfectly consistent to say that in light of your new total evidence, you no longer know the car is in the garage — so you no longer know the text is misleading either. The paradox only gets going if you assume knowledge is "sticky" in a way it isn't.

Maya: But that makes knowledge pathologically fragile. On your view, a single text from a neighbor who's been wrong before — a neighbor who's practically a random noise generator — can destroy knowledge I had seconds ago. That means almost all our ordinary knowledge claims are false, because there's almost always some scrap of counterevidence available. And you still haven't explained why I should go check. If, at the moment before the text, I know the car is there and I know any counterevidence would be misleading, why shouldn't I just delete the text unread and preserve my epistemic state?

Leo: Because you can't preserve it — that's the point. The knowledge you had before was knowledge given a certain body of evidence. New evidence changes the body. Think about it this way: I know that whoever wins the lottery, it was astronomically unlikely that specific person would win. But if I win, I can't say "I already knew my winning was astronomically unlikely, so this notification is probably an error." The evidence of winning just is the evidence, and it swamps the prior.

Maya: That analogy actually helps my case. In the lottery, you don't know you'll lose — that's the whole lottery paradox. You're merely justified in believing you'll lose on probabilistic grounds. But in the dogmatism case we're talking about knowledge, not mere high probability. If I know P, I'm in a different epistemic relation to P than the lottery ticket holder is to "I'll lose." So your analogy collapses the very distinction the paradox turns on.
