A few of us were down the pub last week after our mate Niamh found out her boyfriend had been hiding a gambling habit and a fair bit of debt from her for over a year. The awkward bit is that Leo, another friend of ours, was the one who told Niamh she should leave him and called the boyfriend "unforgivable" — even though Leo did almost exactly the same thing to his ex a few years back and, as far as any of us know, never came clean to her. Two of my mates have been having the same arguement about it since, and neither will budge. It went roughly like this.

Freya: Leo's a hypocrite, so his blame is worthless. You can't lecture someone about a harm you've committed yourself and never made right. He simply lacks the standing.
Omar: But everything Leo said was true. Hiding a gambling problem and a load of debt from your partner is a betrayal. Whether a claim is true doesn't depend on who says it — "look who's talking" is just an ad hominem, and a murderer can still be right that murder is wrong.
Freya: Blame isn't just asserting a proposition, though. It's an act, more like a judge passing sentence. A judge who'd committed the same offence wouldn't be fit to sentence someone else for it. Leo wasn't only stating a moral fact; he was positioning himself as someone Niamh should accept correction from, and that's exactly the position his own record disqualifies him from. He's treating the norm as something that binds other people but not him.
Omar: The judge analogy is doing too much work. A judge has an institutional role with formal authority; ordinary blame is just one person calling a wrong a wrong. If clean hands were a precondition, almost nobody could ever criticise anybody, and plenty of genuine wrongs would go unchallenged because the people best placed to spot them are often guilty of something similar themselves. And actually, Leo's first-hand knowledge of how much damage this causes might make his warning more credible, not less.

I honestly can't call it. Please adjudicate it move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it's right, wrong, or partly right, and why. Then, as a fifth point, say what both of them are missing and who is more right overall. Exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
