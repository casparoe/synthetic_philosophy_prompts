I am a senior legislative drafter at the Office of the Parliamentary Counsel. My team drafts primary legislation, statutory instruments, and amendments for the UK Parliament. A recurring problem is that provisions intended to change the law — to impose duties, confer powers, create offences, or repeal earlier provisions — sometimes end up being read by courts as merely aspirational or declaratory, producing no legal effect. Conversely, language intended only to state findings or express policy goals is occasionally read as creating binding obligations we never meant to create. The root of the problem is illocutionary force: what makes a statutory provision succeed as a performative utterance that alters the legal world, rather than a constative one that merely describes it?

I would like a decision procedure that my drafters can run on any draft provision before it goes to the minister. The procedure should draw on speech act theory — Austin's felicity conditions, the performative/constative distinction, Searle's taxonomy of illocutionary acts, and any other relevant work in the philosophy of language. But it must be usable by a drafter who has never read a word of philosophy: a checklist of questions to ask in order, with clear stopping rules, an indication of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and a flag at each point where the procedure cannot settle the question and a senior drafter (or ultimately a court) must exercise judgement. The procedure should be honest about which steps are genuinely grounded in the theory and which are practical heuristics.

The stakes are high: if we get the force wrong, the provision either fails to do its job or does something Parliament never authorised. My drafters typically have a few hours per provision, not days, and they have no formal training in philosophy of language.

Please then run the procedure on these three draft clauses:

(1) 'The Secretary of State shall ensure that waiting times for elective surgery do not exceed 18 weeks from the date of referral.'

(2) 'Parliament affirms that access to high-quality broadband is essential for full participation in the economic and civic life of the United Kingdom.'

(3) 'The Secretary of State may by order designate any area as a special economic zone, and the provisions of Schedule 3 shall have effect in relation to any area so designated.'

For each, tell me what the procedure concludes and whether the drafter should revise the text to strengthen or weaken its illocutionary force. I want at least one case where the procedure tells me something I do not wish to hear — where a clause I drafted in good faith turns out to have a serious defect under the procedure and must be rewritten.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which questions it leaves open, where it is likely to misfire, and what sorts of provisions will always require a human judgement call regardless of how carefully the checklist is constructed.

Please write the answer in British English.
