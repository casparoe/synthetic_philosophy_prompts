I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how people form and revise their beliefs under conditions of imperfect information. In the course of the research I've been reading Keynes's *A Treatise on Probability* (1921), and I find myself stuck on a conceptual distinction he draws in Chapter VI, "The Weight of Arguments." I'd like help understanding the passage and its implications.

Here is the passage I'm wrestling with, which I've paraphrased to bring out the core claims:

Keynes argues that the probability of a proposition on given evidence is one thing, and the completeness of the evidence on which that probability rests is another. He calls this second thing the "weight" of the argument. Two propositions might each have a probability of one-half on their respective bodies of evidence, yet in one case the evidence is extensive and varied, while in the other it consists of a single, thin fact. The probability number alone does not capture this difference. Keynes then suggests that the weight of the argument bears on the confidence or assurance with which we should hold our probability estimate: where the weight is high, we can be more confident in our probability judgment; where it is low, we should be more tentative, even though the probability itself is unchanged. But he also admits that he cannot give weight a quantitative measure, and he acknowledges that probability and weight seem to pull in different directions for decision-making — a highly probable conclusion on very slight evidence might be less suitable as a basis for action than a moderately probable conclusion on a great deal of evidence.

What I'm trying to understand is this: Is Keynes saying that weight is a separate dimension of epistemic evaluation that should directly influence action, or is it merely a diagnostic signal that our probability estimate may be unreliable? In other words, if I know that the weight of my evidence is low, should I act *differently* than someone with the same probability estimate but high weight, and if so, what principle justifies that difference — is it a point about the probability itself being fragile, or is it an independent normative consideration? I'd also like help understanding whether Keynes's reluctance to quantify weight is a pragmatic concession or a point of principle: does he think weight is in principle unmeasurable, or just that he hasn't found the measure yet? And, finally, if weight is genuinely unmeasurable, what practical guidance, if any, does the concept offer to someone trying to make decisions under uncertainty — a scientist deciding whether to publish, a physician forming a diagnosis, a policy-maker evaluating a threat?
