I've been chewing on Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert — you know, his whole thing about how theater is poison for a republic because it turns citizens into passive spectators, makes us sympathize with characters we really shouldn't, and replaces actual community with a bunch of people sitting in the dark having vicarious emotions. And I can't stop thinking about The Truman Show in relation to it. On one hand the movie is basically Rousseau's argument made flesh: Truman's entire life is a spectacle consumed by millions of passive viewers, and his escape is him literally walking off the set into the real world. The bar crowd cheers when he leaves — they get, on some level, that watching him was wrong. But on the other hand, we the actual audience just sat through a whole movie being passive spectators, and we probably felt pretty good about ourselves for rooting for Truman. So is the movie doing the exact thing Rousseau warns about even as it's critiquing it? Or does it somehow escape the trap — maybe by making us aware of our own spectatorship in a way that Rousseau's Geneva theater wouldn't? I'd love a reading that takes both Rousseau and the film seriously and doesn't just settle for "the film is hypocritical," cause I think there's something more interesting and tangled going on.
