I've been trying to work out my own views on algorithmic fairness from the bottom up, mostly because the top-down approach — pick a criterion, defend it, apply it everywhere — has never felt like it survived contact with actual cases. The impossibility results are well known: calibration, equalized odds, and predictive parity can't all hold simultaneously across groups when base rates differ and the predictor isn't perfect. What's less clear is whether there's a principled way to decide which criterion should give way in which context. Rather than reason from abstract commitments, I went case by case and recorded my considered verdicts. I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them, and if not, which ones I should revise.

Here are the cases:

1. **Pretrial risk assessment.** A jurisdiction uses a model to produce risk scores that inform release and bail decisions. I think calibration is non-negotiable here. If the tool outputs a 20% likelihood of failure to appear, that number should mean 20% regardless of the defendant's race. I'm willing to accept unequal false positive rates across groups as a consequence — I'd rather the scores mean what they say than equalize error rates at the cost of making the numbers uninterpretable to the judges and attorneys who have to act on them.

2. **College admissions predictive model.** A university uses a model to estimate each applicant's probability of graduating within six years. Here I find myself caring most about equalized odds, specifically equal false negative rates: the model shouldn't systematically miss qualified applicants from one group more often than another. If the upshot is that a score of 0.7 corresponds to a 75% graduation rate for one group and a 65% graduation rate for another, I can live with that miscalibration. What I can't live with is a model that is systematically more willing to bet against one group's potential.

3. **Automated resume screening.** A large employer uses a model to decide which applicants advance to the interview stage. Here I think demographic parity is the right target — the proportion of applicants advanced should be roughly equal across groups. I know this means the model will be less accurate by standard metrics, and I know it means advancing some less-qualified candidates over more-qualified ones. But I think the relevant consideration is parity of access to being seen by a human decision-maker, given the history of exclusion from these labor markets.

4. **Emergency room triage.** A hospital uses a model to prioritize patients by urgency. Calibration is again non-negotiable for me. A risk score has to mean the same thing for every patient. I don't want a model that has been adjusted to equalize some outcome metric across groups if that adjustment means the numbers no longer track medical reality. The stakes are too high and the relationship between score and outcome is too direct.

5. **Consumer lending.** A bank uses a model to predict default risk and approve or deny loans. Here I care about predictive parity: conditional on being approved, the probability of repayment should be the same regardless of group. If the model says someone is creditworthy, that designation should mean the same thing across groups. I'll accept unequal approval rates and unequal error rates to preserve this.

6. **Child protective services risk screening.** A model flags children for investigation. I want equalized odds here — both false positive rates (investigating families unnecessarily) and false negative rates (missing genuine neglect or abuse) should be equal across groups. The costs of the two kinds of error are both enormous and fall on different parties, and the costs of disparate scrutiny by race or class are severe enough that I'll accept miscalibration to achieve equalized error rates.

The pattern, if there is one, seems to be that I want calibration in medical and criminal justice contexts, equalized odds in education and child welfare, predictive parity in lending, and demographic parity in hiring. But I can't tell whether this reflects an underlying principle or just context-sensitive pattern-matching that doesn't generalize. Is there a principle that makes sense of most of these verdicts — perhaps something about the nature of the decision, the structure of error costs, the relationship between the decision-maker and the person decided about, or whether the score is used directly as a communicated probability versus as a threshold for a binary decision? If you can articulate one, please state the principle clearly, show how it handles each of my cases, name the verdicts it forces me to abandon, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't considered. If no single principle captures even a majority of my verdicts without being gerrymandered, I'd like to know that too, along with which cluster of verdicts seems most defensible and why.
