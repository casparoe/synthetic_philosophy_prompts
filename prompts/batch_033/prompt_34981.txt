I'm taking a course on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I keep noticing a pattern in the readings about self-referential reasoning systems that I can't find a label for. Here's the pattern: Suppose you have a formal reasoning system — a theorem prover, or an AI that operates by deriving conclusions from axioms — and the system is powerful enough to represent facts about its own reasoning process. It can formulate statements like "I have derived such-and-such conclusion" and can reason about the steps it took. Now, you would like the system to be able to trust its own reasoning. That is, you would like it to be able to conclude, from the fact that it derived something, that the thing it derived is actually true. But it seems that whenever you try to set this up, you hit a structural barrier. The system cannot prove that its own reasoning is sound without already presupposing the very soundness it is trying to establish. And this is not just a failure of a particular design — it looks like a principled ceiling: any sufficiently self-aware formal reasoner seems unable to fully certify its own reliability. It is as if reflective self-trust runs into a logical wall that prevents the reasoner from pulling itself up by its own bootstraps. I have seen this pattern discussed in connection with self-modifying AI and with the problem of building an agent that can safely reason about changes to its own code, but I do not know whether the pattern itself has an established name. Is there a term for this specific obstacle — the inability of a self-referential reasoner to establish its own soundness — and if so, what is the standard way the concept is defined? And if there is not a single settled name, what are the closest candidates, and how do they carve up the territory differently? Please answer in American English.
