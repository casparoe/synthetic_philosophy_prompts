I'm putting together a position paper on epistemic dependence on AI, and I'd like you to check whether my commitments actually hang together before I commit them to print. I've been reading around in the extended mind literature, the understanding literature, and the AI ethics literature, and I've ended up with a set of views that all feel right to me individually, but I suspect they can't all be true at once. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I buy the extended cognition thesis in something like the form Clark and Chalmers defend: when an external tool is reliably coupled to my reasoning in the right kind of way, the cognitive process genuinely extends into the tool, and the person-plus-tool system is the proper unit of cognitive analysis — not merely the biological organism consulting an external aid.

(2) I think there's a real epistemic difference between understanding-why and merely having true beliefs or reliable access to correct answers. Understanding requires that the agent grasp the explanatory relationships — that they see why the answer is the answer, not just that it is.

(3) I think epistemic dependence on AI is not different in kind from dependence on calculators, reference books, or human experts. If the latter are generally epistemically fine, so is the former; there's no special problem about AI.

(4) I also think that routinely using AI for tasks like writing, argument analysis, and judgment predictably erodes one's own unaided capacities for those tasks, and that this erosion is a genuine epistemic loss — not just a practical inconvenience, but a loss of something epistemically valuable.

(5) I hold that the erosion worry applies equally to dependence on other humans. The division of cognitive labor means we all rely on others for huge swaths of what we take ourselves to know, and this dependence also erodes our own capacities. Since we don't regard human epistemic dependence as systematically problematic, we shouldn't regard AI dependence as systematically problematic either.

(6) Finally, I think there's no general epistemic obligation to maintain capacities you could instead reliably outsource. If my AI-assisted outputs are more accurate, better-reasoned, or more nuanced than what I'd produce unaided, then from an epistemic point of view that's what matters. The outputs are the currency of epistemic assessment, not the internal processes that produced them.

I need you to go through these six commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and which are mutually supportive. For each genuine tension, tell me how severe it is — whether it's a surface-level mismatch that can be resolved by precisifying terms, or a deep conflict where one commitment really has to be weakened or abandoned. Then tell me what the cheapest overall revision would be to restore consistency, and what it costs. Don't just flag everything as problematic; some of these tensions are real and some are illusory, and part of the job is telling them apart. Also, please separate what's settled in the relevant literatures from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present one side of a live debate as if it were the consensus.
