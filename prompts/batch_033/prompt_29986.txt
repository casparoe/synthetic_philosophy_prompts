I recently joined the data-governance committee at my hospital, and I keep noticing something I do not have a word for. The same piece of information can be entirely unremarkable in one setting and a serious violation in another, even when the person's consent, the amount of information, and the number of people who see it are held constant. A pharmacist reading a prescription is unremarkable; the same pharmacist mentioning it to a neighbour is not. A wearable device that flags an irregular heartbeat feels like care; the same device selling that data to an insurer feels like a betrayal. It seems to me that what matters is not whether the information was secret or whether I consented, but whether it stays within the relationships and expectations that fit the setting. What puzzles me is that these expectations differ from one community to another and shift over time, so I cannot tell whether I am describing a real feature of privacy or merely local convention. Is there an established name for this way of thinking about privacy, and how exactly does it differ from the more familiar idea that privacy is control over information about oneself? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know how the view is best defined, what it rules in and out, why the violations it identifies are genuinely wrong rather than merely surprising, and where its edges are. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would write an email or a letter.
