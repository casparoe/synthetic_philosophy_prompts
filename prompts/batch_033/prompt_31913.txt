For a seminar paper, I am tracking my political philosophy professor's verdicts on what makes an institution count as having legitimate political authority. Her verdicts on the cases we have discussed so far:

1. The text of a model constitution, drafted by philosophers, that no state has adopted or enforces: "No authority. It is a proposal, not an institution that governs anything."

2. A stable constitutional democracy with just laws, popular ratification, and effective enforcement: "Yes — justice and force combined."

3. A military dictatorship that maintains order through terror, making no appeal to justice or right: "It has power, not authority. Force without justice is tyranny."

4. A widely endorsed international declaration of rights with no enforcement mechanism: "Moral weight, but not political authority. A statement of principles, not an institution that governs."

5. A hereditary monarchy that has ruled for centuries, maintains order through custom and armed force, and is widely accepted by the population as legitimate: "It has authority. Custom and acceptance, backed by force, are the real foundation of law."

6. A constitutional court whose rulings are well-reasoned and just but are routinely ignored by the executive, with no mechanism to compel compliance: "It has the form of authority without the substance. It lacks force."

7. A local mediation council with no formal enforcement powers, whose decisions are followed voluntarily by nearly everyone in the community because the council commands deep respect: "It has authority of a kind — it governs through consent and custom. But it is fragile; if compliance ever stopped, it would have nothing to fall back on."

The new case we are discussing next week: a constitutional amendment has been passed through proper democratic procedures with overwhelming popular support. It abolishes a long-standing unjust practice. But a significant minority of the country refuses to comply, and the government currently lacks the resources to enforce the amendment against them. The amendment is followed in most of the country and ignored in a few regions.

Predict her verdict on the new case. Identify the candidate rules that fit her verdicts so far, say which rule you think she is actually running and why, and explain what each candidate rule would say about the new case. If her existing verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say what principle she would be committing herself to with each choice, and name a further case that would settle which rule she holds.
