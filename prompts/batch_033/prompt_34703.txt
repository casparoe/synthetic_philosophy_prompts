My uncle has been reading Renaissance political philosophy on his own for years — he never went to college but he's the kind of person who works through difficult books slowly with a notebook. He's developed a framework for analyzing why political leaders succeed or fail, built around two ideas he calls "strength" (a leader's skill, decisiveness, and willingness to act) and "fortune" (circumstances beyond the leader's control). Over the past year I've kept a list of his verdicts on specific historical figures:

- Cesare Borgia: he rose entirely through strength, but his downfall was pure fortune — a sudden illness at the worst possible moment, which no amount of strength could have prevented.
- Agathocles of Syracuse: he rose through strength, but his methods were so cruel that his success doesn't count as genuine achievement. Strength alone isn't enough if it isn't accompanied by something else.
- Pope Julius II: he succeeded because his impetuous nature happened to match what the times required. He never had to adapt. His success was a lucky fit between personality and circumstances, not a display of strength.
- Piero Soderini: he failed because he was patient and cautious by nature, and when the times changed and demanded boldness, he couldn't change who he was. His failure was a failure of strength — specifically, the inability to adapt.
- Francesco Sforza: he rose from private citizen to Duke of Milan entirely through strength, and he held his position with little trouble. A clear case of strength mastering fortune.

My uncle is now reading about Fabius Maximus, the Roman general who defeated Hannibal by avoiding battle — a strategy of extreme patience and caution. Fabius's approach worked brilliantly for years, saving Rome when bolder generals had failed. But the Roman public eventually turned on him, replaced him with more aggressive commanders, and the result was the catastrophic defeat at Cannae. Fabius was later vindicated and returned to influence, but the question is: was Fabius a case of strength or fortune?

My uncle seems torn. He wants to say Fabius's early success was a Julius II situation — a lucky match between his cautious nature and the military circumstances — but he also thinks Fabius's strategy showed real insight, which sounds like strength. He's asked me to help him think through what his own framework commits him to saying about Fabius, and which of his earlier verdicts the Fabius case most closely resembles.

Please identify the different rules about strength and fortune that are consistent with my uncle's five verdicts, explain what each rule would say about Fabius Maximus, and give your best guess about which rule my uncle is actually running, with reasons. Then propose a further historical case that would most clearly separate the competing rules. Format your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
