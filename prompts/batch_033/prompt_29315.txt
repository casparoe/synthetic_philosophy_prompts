I spent thirty years in animal behavior, mostly birds and fish, and only got into philosophy of mind after I retired. What keeps bugging me is how frozen the argument over which animals are conscious has become, and how little the actual data seems to move anyone. The same observations get waved around by everyone: a fish learns to avoid a hook, a bee detours around an obstacle, an octopus opens a jar, a dog yelps when you step on its paw. One camp says none of this counts without the right neural machinery (a cortex, or something close to it); another says flexible learning and behavior are already enough; a third says any system that integrates information the right way is conscious to some degree, which would include animals I'd never have called conscious. My working hypothesis is that the empirical part was mostly settled a while ago and what's left is a disagreement about what 'consciousness' means and about what should count as evidence for it — but I want that hypothesis tested, not just confirmed. So diagnose the structure of the disagreement rather than survey the positions, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress.

Organize your answer into these sections, in this order:
- The lay of the land: the main positions and the evidence each treats as decisive (about 150 words)
- Candidate diagnoses: the distinct kinds of disagreement this could be — factual, conceptual, about standards of evidence, about burden of proof, or something else — stated precisely (about 300 words)
- Which diagnosis fits best: argue for one, using what each side actually says (about 250 words)
- What would count as progress: given that diagnosis, what experiment, distinction, or reformulation could move the debate, and be honest about whether it actually would (about 200 words)
- Bottom line: a one-paragraph version I could give a non-philosopher colleague (about 100 words)
