I'm taking a course on Gödel, Turing, and the philosophy of mind, and we've been working through the Lucas–Penrose argument against computationalism. I've reconstructed it as a numbered chain, and each premise feels hard to deny on its own, but the conclusion strikes me as too strong to be right — if the argument works, it seems to prove more than its proponents want. I can't find the step where the trouble enters, and I'd like a diagnosis.

Here is the argument:

(1) Suppose, for reductio, that the human mind's capacity for mathematical reasoning is fully captured by some consistent formal system F that is sufficiently strong to express elementary arithmetic.

(2) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, for any such F, there exists a sentence G_F such that: if F is consistent, G_F is true, and neither G_F nor its negation is provable in F.

(3) A human mathematician who studies F can, by working through Gödel's proof, establish the conditional "if F is consistent, then G_F" — and so can come to see that G_F is true, provided she accepts that F is consistent.

(4) Since the human mathematician can see that G_F is true while F cannot prove G_F, the human mind can do something that F cannot.

(5) Therefore, the human mind is not identical to or exhaustively modeled by F.

(6) Since F was an arbitrary consistent formal system, the human mind is not identical to or exhaustively modeled by any consistent formal system.

(7) Any computational process that produces mathematical judgments can be represented as a formal system of the relevant kind, so the human mind is not a computational process. Mechanism is false.

Now, the reason I think this proves too much: suppose we ran the same argument but substituted "F" with a relatively weak system like Peano arithmetic, and "the human mind" with a stronger system like ZFC set theory. The argument would then seem to show that ZFC cannot be a formal system either — because ZFC can prove the Gödel sentence of PA, and PA cannot. But ZFC obviously is a formal system. So the form of reasoning in steps (3)–(5) must be licensed only under some special condition that holds when the "outer" reasoner is a human mind but not when it is another formal system. What is that condition, and is it legitimate to claim that human minds satisfy it?

If I had to bet, I'd say the trouble is in the move from (3) to (4) — perhaps the human mathematician cannot actually know that F is consistent in the relevant sense, especially if F is supposed to be her own mind. But then the argument against mechanism would need a separate, independent proof that we can know our own consistency, and Gödel's second incompleteness theorem seems to block that. I'd like to know whether this is the standard objection, and what the Lucas–Penrose side has said in reply. If the real flaw is elsewhere — say, in the assumption that the mind is consistent, or in the claim that "seeing" a Gödel sentence is the same kind of thing as proving it — I'd like to know that too, and what each diagnosis costs the detractor.
