I am a philosophy professor who works primarily in decision theory, and I have been turning over a pattern that keeps surfacing in discussions of money pumps and diachronic rationality. I suspect it has a name, or at least a recognized place in the literature, but I have not been able to locate it — perhaps because I do not know the right term to search for.

The standard money pump argument runs roughly as follows: if an agent has intransitive preferences (A preferred to B, B to C, yet C to A), then a counterparty can offer a sequence of trades, each of which the agent accepts when considered in isolation, and the agent ends up back where they started but poorer by the sum of the transaction costs. The natural response, if one wishes to resist the conclusion that rationality demands transitive preferences, is to insist that the agent should evaluate the sequence of trades as a whole — a global evaluation — rather than treating each trade as a locally rational choice. But here is the difficulty I keep noticing. If we take seriously the demand that the rationality of any particular choice depends on how it fits into a larger plan or sequence, then we seem to inherit a regress: every choice is embedded in an indefinitely expanding set of possible future choices, and the larger plan is itself composed of choices each of which must in turn be evaluated against a still larger plan, and so on without any obvious stopping point. The global frame within which a choice is supposed to be assessed has no principled boundary, and so the demand for global evaluation threatens to make the rationality of any particular choice ungrounded — not because the agent has intransitive preferences, but because the very act of specifying the "globally relevant" set of choices cannot be completed. This is not simply the standard money pump, nor is it quite the problem of dynamic inconsistency, nor is it merely the distinction between sophisticated and naïve choice. It is a structural problem about what it even means to evaluate a choice globally when the global context is, in principle, unbounded.

I would be grateful to know whether this specific difficulty has an established name — something like "the global evaluation regress," "the planning horizon problem," "the frame-regress problem," or perhaps something else entirely — and, if not, how it sits relative to the nearest existing concepts (money pumps, resolute choice, Bratman-style planning theories, dynamic consistency, the problem of global consequentialism, and so on). I am not simply after a label; I want to understand whether this is a recognized problem with its own literature, or whether it is a gap that the field has not yet named. If there is a definite term for it, please give it in the form \answer{term} at the very end; if not, please end with a one-sentence summary of how you would characterize the phenomenon.
