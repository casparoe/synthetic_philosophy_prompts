I am a member of my school's philosophy discussion group, and for the past several months we have been debating whether we have special moral obligations to our compatriots — obligations we do not owe, or do not owe to the same degree, to people in other countries. The two positions have become entrenched and no one's view has shifted in any noticeable way.

On one side, some members argue that fellow citizens do have a special claim on us: we share political institutions, we participate in a cooperative scheme of law and taxation, our votes directly affect one another's lives, and we inherit a common history and culture that give our relationships a distinctive moral texture. They contend that it would be strange, perhaps even a failure of loyalty, to regard a stranger on another continent as having exactly the same claim on one's time and resources as a neighbour with whom one shares a polity.

On the other side, others argue that nationality is morally arbitrary — no one chooses where they are born — and that impartial moral concern should extend equally to all persons. They point out that the suffering of a child in another country is no less real than the suffering of a child next door, and that a moral theory that permits us to discount distant strangers simply because they are distant is defective. For them, the burden of proof lies on anyone who claims that borders generate genuinely special moral claims.

Both sides appear to accept the same factual claims about global poverty, the nature of political institutions, and human psychology. Both agree that suffering is bad and that all persons deserve equal moral consideration in some fundamental sense. And yet the disagreement persists. I have begun to suspect that we are not really arguing about a single question, or that the disagreement is of a kind that cannot be settled by the introduction of further facts.

I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a substantive clash of incompatible moral principles, a verbal dispute about what phrases like 'special obligation' or 'equal consideration' mean, a conflict of bedrock moral intuitions that neither side can argue for, or something else — for instance, two groups answering different questions under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, a new argument, a way of reframing the question, or is this the sort of impasse where no progress should be expected and we ought simply to acknowledge that we start from different premises? Please write your answer in British English.
