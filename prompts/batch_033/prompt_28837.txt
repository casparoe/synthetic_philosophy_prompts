Scalable oversight asks how humans are to supervise AI systems on tasks too difficult for any single human to evaluate from scratch. One influential answer is debate: two AI systems argue for competing answers while a human judge decides, on the theory that cross-examination lets the judge identify the better answer even when she could not have produced or verified it herself. The research program has now run for several years, yet there is still no convergence on whether debate actually solves the problem it was introduced to solve. Proponents point to idealized results showing that, with capable and truthful debaters, the judge need only check a single contested step. Critics answer that human judges often cannot check even that step, that real arguments do not decompose into individually checkable steps in the way the idealization assumes, and that obfuscated arguments—arguments no single step of which the judge is in a position to verify—break the connection between the idealization and the mechanism as it would actually operate. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are really answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking whether debate yields a reliable oversight signal in the idealized setting of capable debaters, while the other is asking whether it yields a usable oversight signal with human judges and realistic argumentation. Diagnose this impasse. Is it a substantive disagreement about facts, about what the idealized results establish, about what a safety case for scalable oversight must show, or something else? Given your diagnosis, say concretely what would count as progress—or argue that none should be expected and why.
