Two members of my philosophy of mind reading group got into an argument about Dennett's treatment of the color phi phenomenon in *Consciousness Explained*, and I'm hoping you can walk me through who's right about what.

The background: two colored dots flash in quick succession at different locations. Subjects report seeing a single dot that moves between the two positions and changes color mid-trajectory. The puzzle is how you can consciously experience the color change before the second dot has even appeared. Dennett frames two possible stories. The "Stalinesque" story says consciousness delays the whole show until the second dot arrives, constructs the moving-and-changing dot, and only then presents it to the theater of consciousness — so you never actually experienced the first dot as stationary; you only think you did. The "Orwellian" story says you consciously experience the first dot as stationary in real time, but when the second dot appears, your memory of the first experience gets retroactively overwritten — so you had the original experience but lost access to it. Dennett's punchline is that there's no fact of the matter about which story is correct, because both accounts posit the same data entering the system and the same verbal report coming out, and there's no Cartesian Theater where the "real" conscious experience plays out for a definitive audience.

Here's how the argument went:

(1) Mark said Dennett's argument works: if two theories make all the same predictions about behavior, report, and everything else that could count as evidence, then the dispute between them is merely verbal. The Orwellian and Stalinesque stories are empirically equivalent, so there's nothing to choose between them — the question "which one really happened?" is ill-posed.

(2) Elena pushed back: the two stories aren't empirically equivalent, because they make different claims about the timing of conscious experience. In the Stalinesque story, the conscious experience of the first dot as stationary and red never occurs — the system waits and constructs a single moving-dot experience. In the Orwellian story, that experience does occur at t1, but is then forgotten. These are different claims about what happens when, and they'd be empirically distinguishable if we had a way to read off the contents of consciousness moment by moment — the fact that we currently lack such a method doesn't make the question meaningless.

(3) Mark replied that Elena's "if we had a way" is doing illicit work. Dennett's point is precisely that there is no fact about the timing of conscious experience independent of how the system processes information and produces reports. To insist that there must be a fact about "when it was conscious" is to sneak in the Cartesian Theater — the idea that there's a definite moment when content "arrives" in consciousness. Without that assumption, the timing question evaporates.

(4) Elena came back: Dennett's argument proves too much. By the same reasoning, we could say there's no fact of the matter about whether a computer processes data in series or in parallel, as long as the input-output mapping is the same — but that's clearly false; architectures matter. The fact that two processes yield the same report doesn't mean they're the same process. The Orwellian and Stalinesque stories describe different computational architectures, and the question of which architecture the brain implements is a perfectly good empirical question, even if current methods can't settle it.

(5) Mark answered that the computer analogy misses the mark because in the computer case, we can open the box and look at the architecture. The whole point of Dennett's heterophenomenology is that for consciousness, the "box" just is the set of reports and dispositions to report — there's no further inner arena to inspect. So the analogy presupposes exactly what's at issue.

(6) Elena said that's where Dennett begs the question. He defines the data of consciousness as reports and dispositions, and then declares that any distinction not manifest in those data is spurious. But that definition is the controversial move — it rules out by fiat the possibility that consciousness has a temporal structure that isn't fully captured by report-based methods. The Orwellian–Stalinesque distinction might be a case where that structure matters.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who you think has the better overall position, and identify the most important thing neither of them brought up that might advance the discussion. Please write your answer in American English.
