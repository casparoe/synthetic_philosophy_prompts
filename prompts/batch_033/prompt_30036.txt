I have become convinced, largely on the strength of Frankfurt-style counterexamples, that the principle of alternative possibilities is false: a person can be morally responsible for what she did even if, in the actual sequence of events, she could not have done otherwise. I want a map of what would count against this position — not the stock objections from an introductory survey, but the arguments, cases, and adjacent results that should genuinely move someone who holds it, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. Address at least the following. (i) The flicker-of-freedom strategy: can the alternatives that remain in Frankfurt cases be shown to be the ones that actually ground responsibility, and what would that require? (ii) The dilemma defense: the charge that Frankfurt-style cases either presuppose determinism in the actual sequence or fail to eliminate every relevant alternative, so that they either beg the question against the libertarian or refute only a straw version of the principle. (iii) Whether the position's real work is being done by an unargued identification of responsibility with features of the actual sequence, and what would show that identification to be false. (iv) The downstream cost of abandoning the principle outright: what it does to responsibility ascriptions under coercion, duress, addiction, and manipulation, and whether defenders have quietly reinstated the principle elsewhere. Distinguish the objections a committed Frankfurtian would herself concede as counting against the position from those only its critics press, and say honestly whether the view has been insulated by qualifications into unfalsifiability — for example, by retreating to the claim that the principle fails only in its traditional form while some revised principle does the real work — and whether that degree of insulation is a problem for a thesis of this kind. For my part, I would find most persuasive a successful demonstration that the alternative possibilities that remain in Frankfurt cases are exactly the ones needed for responsibility, if that can be made to work; if it cannot, tell me that too. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
