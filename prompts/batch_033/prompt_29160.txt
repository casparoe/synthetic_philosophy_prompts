I've been reading about Ulysses contracts in my philosophy club — the idea that you can bind your future self to prevent yourself from making choices you'll regret. Our advisor mentioned Odysseus having his crew tie him to the mast so he could hear the sirens without steering the ship onto the rocks, and ever since I've been noticing versions of this everywhere. What I can't figure out is where to draw the line between a legitimate precommitment and an unfair restriction on someone's future freedom — even when that "someone" is your own future self.

Here are my verdicts on specific cases. Odysseus tying himself to the mast: clearly fine. Deleting my social media apps the night before final exams: fine. Giving my friend my wallet before going to a casino, so I can't gamble more than the cash in my pocket: fine. A constitution that requires a two-thirds supermajority to amend: fine, even though it ties the hands of future majorities. A person with bipolar disorder, during a stable period, signing a psychiatric advance directive that authorizes treatment during a future manic episode even if they refuse at the time: fine, and honestly admirable. Paying for a full year of gym membership upfront so I'll feel wasteful if I don't go: fine, but something about it feels different from the others, and I can't say what. A teenager asking their parents to lock away their phone every night at 10 PM and not return it no matter how much they beg: fine.

Now the cases that bother me. A worker signing an employment contract that waives their right to join a union: not fine, even if they genuinely prefer the non-union wage premium right now. Someone signing away their right to divorce — like in legal systems where marriage contracts can be made indissoluble: not fine, even if both parties want that binding at the time. And here's the one I keep going back and forth on: suppose I'm a legislator voting on a constitutional amendment that permanently limits government power in a way I deeply support — say, banning warrantless surveillance. I believe in it now, but I can imagine future-me, during a security crisis, being grateful if the restriction weren't there. Is voting for that amendment a legitimate Ulysses contract on behalf of the polity, or is it an illegitimate attempt to govern from the grave?

Is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or at least most of them? What does it say about the surveillance amendment case? And which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to abandon?
