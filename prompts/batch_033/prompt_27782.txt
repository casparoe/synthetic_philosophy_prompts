So my friend and I have been arguing about how to split up resources fairly, and I've been keeping track of her verdicts on different cases. I'm trying to figure out what she'd say about a new one.

Here's her track record:

1. Two coworkers, same position and salary, both up for a one-time bonus. One would get a lot more happiness out of it than the other. She said give it to the one who'd enjoy it more.

2. A wealthy person and a poor person each need the same medication, which would improve their day by an equal amount. Only one dose available. She said give it to the poor person.

3. A health budget choice: fund a surgery that stops one person from going blind, or fund a program that relieves mild seasonal allergies for 800 people. She said prevent the blindness, even though she admitted the total discomfort across 800 allergy sufferers might add up to more than the disutility of one person's blindness.

4. A city can build either a specialized concert hall that would bring intense joy to 10 classical music lovers (+200 wellbeing points each, 2,000 total) or a public walking trail that would bring mild pleasure to 4,000 residents (+0.4 each, 1,600 total). She said build the trail, even though the concert hall has the higher total.

Now here's the new case. Imagine there's a person — call them Z — with a one-in-a-billion neurological condition that makes them derive literally ten thousand times more pleasure from any resource than a typical person. Z is at an average happiness level, not especially well or badly off. A resource is available that could go entirely to Z (generating 1,000,000 wellbeing points) or be divided among 10,000 typical people (1 point each, 10,000 total).

What would my friend say, if she's trying to be consistent with her earlier calls? And if you think her answer on this new case sits uneasily with some of her earlier verdicts, which one is doing the real work — which earlier call would she have to revisit to make everything cohere?
