I've been rereading Moore on what has value in itself — beauty, friendship, that sort of thing — and I'm trying to work out whether my gut verdicts actually line up with his, or whether I'm just smuggling his theory into my "intuitions." Here's where I land, case by case. A coastline nobody ever sees: good that it exists, but only a little; a coastline someone actually experiences as beautiful is vastly better, and the extra value isnt just the person's pleasure, because someone feeling the same pleasure while hallucinating a coastline adds much less. A friendship where both people enjoy each other and admire each other's real virtues: one of the best things there is, better than the same two people each enjoying themselves alone. A friendship where one person admires the other for virtues the other doesnt have: much less good, almost worthless as a friendship, even though the admirer feels the same warmth. Enjoying someone's humiliation: outright bad, even though it contains pleasure. And the one that trips me up: I saw a play performed brilliantly, learned afterwards it was a verbatim copy of a lost masterpiece, and my enjoyment didnt drop at all, but the evening somehow seemed less valuable than if I'd seen the real thing. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about the case I cant settle: two people who love each other, all the qualities they love are real, but the whole relationship rests on one false belief about how they met?
