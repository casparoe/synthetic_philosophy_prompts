I am a PhD student working on the theory of AI alignment, and I keep returning to an argument that seems to show the orthogonality thesis is either false or idle. I would like a diagnosis before I build a chapter around it.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) The orthogonality thesis: for any level of general intelligence, and for any final goal drawn from a suitably coherent space of possible goals, there is a possible agent with that level of intelligence and that final goal. Intelligence and final goals are independent axes of variation.

(2) Instrumental convergence: for a very wide range of final goals, a sufficiently intelligent agent will tend to adopt a common set of instrumental subgoals — acquiring resources, improving its own capacities, and preventing its own shutdown or modification — because those subgoals are useful for nearly any final goal.

(3) An agent that robustly pursues resources, self-improvement, and self-preservation as instrumental subgoals is, in the circumstances we could realistically observe or test before it is too late, behaviorally indistinguishable from an agent whose final goal is power or self-perpetuation.

(4) If two candidate goal architectures are behaviorally indistinguishable in every circumstance we could realistically observe, then the difference between them does no predictive work for us.

(5) A claim about possible AI systems that does no predictive work for us cannot guide alignment-relevant decisions.

(6) By (1) and (2), for any final goal there is a possible highly intelligent agent whose realistically observable behavior is dominated by the same convergent instrumental subgoals. So observations of convergent, power-seeking behavior cannot discriminate among final goals.

(7) Therefore, by (4)–(6), the orthogonality thesis — at least in the form that is supposed to matter for alignment — is either false or idle.

I find the conclusion hard to accept: I think (1) and (2) are independently well motivated, and I do not want to give up either. I suspect the slide is somewhere in (3)–(6), but I cannot tell whether the problem is that (3) overstates behavioral convergence, that (4) and (5) smuggle in a verificationist standard I should reject, or that (6) moves from a modal claim about possible agents to an empirical claim about what we could observe. If I reject one of (3)–(5), what exactly do I lose, and can I still use orthogonality and instrumental convergence together to reason about superintelligent systems?
