I spent forty years in molecular biology before retiring, and I've recently turned my attention to philosophy of mind — specifically the personal identity questions raised by mind uploading scenarios. I've been working through my intuitive verdicts on a set of thought experiments, and I find I can't extract a clean principle from them. I'd be grateful if you, as a professional philosopher specializing in personal identity, would help me identify what principle (or small set of principles) might be driving my judgments, and where I might need to revise.

Let me lay out the cases and my verdicts. In all cases, assume the technology works perfectly at the functional level — the replacements preserve all input-output behavior of the neurons they replace, and where simulations are involved, they are faithful to the finest relevant grain of neural processing.

**Case A (Gradual replacement).** One by one, each of my neurons is replaced with a functionally identical silicon artificial neuron. Each replacement is done while I'm conscious; there's no interruption in my stream of experience. At the end, my skull contains only silicon. I say: I survive. This is still me.

**Case B (Destructive upload).** My brain is cryogenically frozen, sliced into ultrathin sections, scanned at nanometer resolution, and a perfect simulation of my brain is run on a computer. The original biological tissue is destroyed in the slicing. I say: I die. The simulation is a copy — a person very like me, but not me.

**Case C (Non-destructive upload).** A high-resolution scan of my living brain is taken without damaging it. A perfect simulation is run from that scan. I wake up from the scanner, and the simulation also wakes up in its virtual environment. I say: I am the biological one. The simulation is a distinct person who happens to share my memories up to the moment of scanning.

**Case D (The pause-and-transfer).** We begin gradual replacement as in Case A. Halfway through, we pause the process. We scan the partially-replaced brain and run a simulation from that scan. Then we destroy the partially-replaced biological/silicon brain. I find myself genuinely uncertain here. My gut says this is more like dying than like surviving, but I can't articulate why it should differ from Case A, since in Case A the halfway point was just a moment on a continuum.

**Case E (Branching replacement).** We do gradual replacement as in Case A, but each biological neuron, once removed, is kept alive in a nutrient bath. After all neurons are replaced, the preserved biological neurons are reassembled into a working biological brain, which wakes up. I now have a silicon successor and a biological successor. I say: the silicon one is me — it has the continuous stream of consciousness. The reassembled biological brain is a kind of twin, but not me. Though I admit this verdict makes me uncomfortable.

**Case F (The teleporter).** I step into a teleporter. My body is disassembled atom by atom, the information about its structure is transmitted, and I am reconstructed from new atoms at the destination using exactly the same structural information. I say: I die in the teleporter. The person at the destination is a copy. (I know this puts me at odds with many science fiction fans, but that's my intuition.)

**Case G (The fading overlap).** Over the course of a year, each of my neurons gradually delegates its computational role to a silicon partner neuron. For each neuron, there's a weeks-long transition period where both the biological and silicon neuron are active and contributing, after which the biological one goes quiet. At the end, only silicon neurons are firing. I say: I survive. This feels just like Case A to me.

**Case H (The destructive scan with a delay).** My brain is destructively scanned as in Case B, but the simulation is not run until fifty years later. I say: I died at the moment of scanning. The fifty-year gap makes no difference to my verdict — it was already death — but it somehow makes the death feel more vivid.

I can see patterns in these verdicts but not a clean principle. Something about continuity matters (A and G vs. B and F). But continuity of what? Not material continuity — the teleporter in F preserves material continuity (same atoms, same arrangement) and I still call it death. Not merely functional continuity either, or I'd accept B. It seems to be about the right kind of causal continuity of the implementing substrate — but then Case D should be fine, and I'm not sure it is. And Case E raises the problem of branching: if continuity is what matters, why doesn't the reassembled biological brain also count as me?

Please help me work through this. Specifically: (1) Is there a single principle that captures all my verdicts, or do I need more than one? (2) If some of my verdicts must be revised to achieve consistency, which ones are the best candidates for revision and why? (3) What does the resulting account say about cases I haven't considered — for instance, what if the gradual replacement in Case A is done while I'm under general anesthesia, with no conscious experience during the procedure? Or what if the silicon neurons are replaced one by one with biological neurons grown from someone else's stem cells?

Please think through this carefully, starting with a survey of the main candidate theories of personal identity and how each would handle these cases, before offering your own analysis.
