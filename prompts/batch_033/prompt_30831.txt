I work in the compliance office of a mid-sized city government. Our office is now required to review and sign off on every vendor-supplied algorithmic tool used in a public service before a contract can proceed: pretrial risk scores, benefits eligibility triage, school assignment, and a model that decides which properties to inspect for housing code violations. The vendors' fairness documentation is typically a few pages claiming the tool is "unbiased," "equally accurate across groups," or "fair by design," without saying what those terms mean or what was traded off to achieve them. We review several such tools a year, the stakes are high, and the reviewers are lawyers and policy analysts, not statisticians: they can read a base rate and a confusion matrix, but they cannot follow the theorems.

I understand the rough shape of the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness: a risk score cannot simultaneously be calibrated, have equal false positive rates across groups, and have equal false negative rates across groups unless the outcome is equally common in the groups or the prediction is perfect. But I need that result converted into something my office can actually run when a vendor is across the table.

Please write a reusable audit procedure for evaluating the fairness claims of a proposed algorithmic decision tool. The procedure should be written for a reviewer with no graduate-level statistics. For each step, specify the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, whether an insufficient answer stops the review or merely flags the tool, and where the procedure hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure should do at least the following: (1) translate vague fairness language into a small set of defined criteria — at minimum demographic parity, equalized odds, equal opportunity, predictive parity, and calibration — using questions the reviewer can put to a vendor about its validation data; (2) check the vendor's claimed criteria for the combinations the impossibility results rule out, and require the vendor to state which criterion it actually optimized and which it knowingly sacrificed; (3) distinguish cases where the impossibility genuinely bites, because base rates differ materially and the tool is imperfect, from cases where it does not, so that the office does not cry "impossible" on every proposal; and (4) produce a short sign-off sheet recording the chosen criterion, the measured tradeoff, and the value judgment that selected it.

Then run the procedure on three concrete proposals. (a) A pretrial risk score whose vendor claims the score is calibrated and also reports equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates across racial groups; the base rate of the predicted outcome differs materially between the groups, and the tool is known not to be perfect. (b) A benefits eligibility triage tool whose vendor claims both equal positive predictive value across groups and equalized odds; again, the underlying outcome is substantially more common in one group. (c) A housing code inspection model whose vendor claims calibration and equalized odds, but the two neighborhoods it scores have nearly identical base rates and the office wants a single threshold. For each, state what the procedure concludes, what documentation or changes it requires, and where it hands the decision to a person. At least one case should end in a conclusion the office will not like — for example, rejecting a favored vendor or being told that no technical fix can deliver everything the public wants.

Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do: which fairness conflicts it cannot detect, which claims it must take on trust, and where the impossibility results leave open a choice that no procedure can close.
