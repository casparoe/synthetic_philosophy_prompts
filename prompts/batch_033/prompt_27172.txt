I chair the priority-setting subcommittee of a regional health authority. At Thursday's meeting we must decide whether to recommend funding a preventive migraine therapy that produces modest quality-of-life gains for a large number of patients, or instead expanding a home-ventilation program that produces larger quality-of-life gains for a small number of patients with severe respiratory disability. Our analysts' cost-effectiveness model ranks the two options by quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), which requires treating one person's reported quality-of-life score as comparable with another's: a year lived with daily migraine and a year lived with ventilator dependence are each assigned a numeric utility value, and the model then adds these values across different people. Two subcommittee members contend that this is the classic problem of interpersonal utility comparisons: there is no fact of the matter about how much one person's pain is worth relative to another person's immobility, so the model manufactures a precision the evidence cannot support. Others reply that any choice between the two programs implicitly makes such a comparison, and that declining to quantify it merely lets the comparison operate unexamined. Draft the short written opinion I will circulate at Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — fund the migraine therapy, expand the ventilation program, or adopt a defined compromise — engage the skeptical position seriously rather than dismissing it, and state what role, if any, the QALY comparison should play in the final decision. Write the opinion as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; the first item should state the recommendation.
