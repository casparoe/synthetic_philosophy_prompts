I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so I might be missing something obvious here. But I've been going back and forth with a friend about what makes consent valid, and I tried to lay out the standard conditions cleanly — like you would spec out a system — and the reasoning led me somewhere that seems obviously wrong. Can you help me find the bug?

Here's the argument:

(1) Consent is valid only if the person is competent, informed, and uncoerced.

(2) To be "informed" about X, you need to know all the facts about X that would matter to a reasonable person's decision.

(3) In almost any nontrivial situation (medical procedure, job offer, sex, loan agreement), there are material facts you don't know — the other party knows more than you do, sometimes by a lot.

(4) So in almost any nontrivial situation, you aren't fully informed.

(5) Therefore, by (1), almost no real-world consent is actually valid.

(6) But that's absurd. Consent is a real thing we manage to give and recieve all the time — if the argument says it basically never happens, the argument has to be wrong somewhere.

My friend says the problem is premise (2): "informed" in the consent sense doesn't mean omniscient, it just means nobody lied to you or hid anything they had a duty to disclose. But if that's the move, then consent is compatible with pretty big informational gaps, and I'm not sure I like where that goes either — it seems like it lets people off the hook too easily for not volunteering information.

So: I need a professional philosopher who knows the consent literature to walk me through this. Which premise would you reject, and what's the cost? Or is there a way to keep (1) and (2) and still avoid (5)? Think through each step carefully and give me the diagnosis. And if you can, explain it like you're talking to someone who debugs code for a living — because that's basically what I'm trying to do here.
