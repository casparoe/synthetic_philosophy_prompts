In my causal inference seminar, we recently worked through Simpson's paradox, and I have been unable to shake a worry that began there. I have reconstructed it as an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, and I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

(1) For any treatment–outcome association in observational data, there is some third variable C — actual or constructible, measured or unmeasured — such that, conditional on C, the association disappears or reverses direction.

(2) If every association can be overturned by some conditioning variable, then no observational association is robust under all possible conditionings.

(3) An association is evidence for a causal claim only if it is robust under all possible conditionings.

(4) Therefore, no observational association is evidence for a causal claim.

(5) But some observational associations are evidence for causal claims; the smoking–lung cancer case is the standard example. So at least one of (1)–(3) must be false.

I am most reluctant to give up (5), and I am also inclined to keep (1), since it looks to me like a natural generalization of the lesson of Simpson's paradox. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to about the relationship between statistical association and causal inference?
