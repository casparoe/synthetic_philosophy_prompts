I've been going round in circles on Bradley's regress and I need someone to just tell me which solutions are actually defensible and which are non-starters. Here are five broad approaches people have taken to the problem of how a relation unifies its relata (whether we're talking about propositions, facts, or just ordinary predication). Could you rank them from most promising to least promising, and give a sentence or two for each about why it lands where it does?

1. The regress is vicious, so relations don't really exist — the world is a seamless whole and all relational thought is ultimately inadequate (Bradley's own view, more or less).
2. The regress is benign — it's just the ontological cost of doing business with relations, and it doesn't prevent them from doing their unifying work.
3. Relations are fundamentally different in kind from their relata: they're 'unsaturated' or incomplete entities that reach out for completion, so no extra relation is needed to glue them to their terms (the Fregean move, also picked up by Russell at one stage).
4. Don't build facts out of thinner constituents in the first place. Take states of affairs or facts as ontologically basic — they come pre-unified, and the regress never gets going.
5. Replace universal relations with particularised relations (tropes). Since a relational trope is itself a particular that essentially involves its relata, there's no gap that needs bridging.

Lead with your ranking (something like "1. Most promising: [approach]. 2. ...") and then explain. No preamble, no restating the question. British English, please.
