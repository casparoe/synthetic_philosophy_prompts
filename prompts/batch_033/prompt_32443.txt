My friend who did a philosophy minor tried to explain the Liar paradox to me and summarized the landscape like this: "Tarski's solution was basically to ban self-reference from formal languages—you just can't have a sentence that talks about itself, which is a bit of a hack but it works. Kripke came along and said the Liar sentence is neither true nor false, it falls into a truth-value gap, which is more elegant because you don't have to throw out self-reference altogether. Then there are the dialetheists who bite the bullet and say the Liar is both true and false—they just accept that some contradictions are true and move on. And the revision theory says the Liar keeps flipping between true and false and never settles, so it's 'pathological' in a different way. The killer for all of these is the revenge paradox: for any solution, you can cook up a new sentence that says 'this sentence is not true according to the proposed solution,' and you're back to square one. So really nobody's solved it."

I'm not sure how fair this is. I've heard that Tarski's hierarchy is more subtle than just banning self-reference, and I vaguely remember that Kripke builds on Kleene's three-valued logic in a specific way rather than just declaring a gap. But maybe my friend's simplifications are basically right for a non-specialist? I'd like to know which parts a philosopher of logic would accept, which are misleading or wrong, and whether the revenge problem is really as devastating as he made it sound.
