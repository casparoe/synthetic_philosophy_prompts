For as long as I have worked in set theory, the debate over the continuum hypothesis has seemed to me the paradigm of a dispute that cannot end. Since Cohen's forcing proof showed that CH is independent of ZFC, the field has divided into camps that appear to talk past one another rather than to one another. One camp holds that CH has a determinate truth value and that the task is to find new axioms — large cardinal axioms, forcing axioms, or principles like the Ω-conjecture — that a rational set theorist ought to accept and that will decide CH one way or the other. A second camp draws the opposite lesson from independence: there is no single intended universe of sets, the continuum is not a unique object, and CH is settled only relative to a chosen set-theoretic context. A third strand suspects that the question is ill-posed, because the notions of set and membership are too indeterminate to support a fact of the matter about the size of the continuum.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a mathematical disagreement — everyone accepts the same independence results — but a disagreement about what could count as evidence for a new axiom, and behind that, about what 'set' refers to. But I am not confident: there may be a hidden formal question, about the consequences or consistency strength of the candidate axioms, doing the real work, or the camps may simply be answering different questions under one heading.

Diagnose this impasse. Is it substantive or verbal? Do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which considerations count as data for an axiom, or about starting intuitions neither side can argue for? Is 'the continuum hypothesis' one question or several? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a sharper distinction, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected?
