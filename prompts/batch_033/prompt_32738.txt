I am a professor of philosophy, though my own work is in moral and political philosophy, and the question below falls outside my specialty. In my university's planning and risk committees I keep noticing a pair of related patterns for which I lack the exact vocabulary. A single figure — say, "80 percent confidence" — gets used for two very different things. In one case it rests on a long run of comparable cases with stable frequencies: many institutions like ours, many renewal cycles, a known failure rate. In another case it rests on a single analogy to a vaguely similar institution, or on the judgment of one expert who has never encountered anything quite like the proposal before us. The number is identical, but the second claim is somehow thinner than the first, and I want a precise way to say what that thinness consists in. Relatedly, I see the same committees convert radically different situations into a single "risk" framework: a well-understood decision with actuarial tables on one side, and a genuinely novel decision — a new financial instrument, an unprecedented public-health measure, a political rupture — on the other, with a probability attached to the latter as if it were the same kind of quantity as the former. My sense is that there is an older literature on probability that draws something like a distinction between measurable and unmeasurable uncertainty, and something like a distinction concerning the amount of evidence behind a probability, but I do not know the exact concepts, whether they have settled names, or how they are related.

I am not satisfied with a bare label. I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are: whether I have described one concept or two, how the nearest established concepts differ from what I have described and from each other, and which fits best. If nothing established fits, say so and propose a term with a definition and boundary cases. And throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among those who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
