After a long career in physical chemistry, I have recently begun reading epistemology, and I find the debate over luminosity and the KK principle to be the first philosophical issue that genuinely reminds me of problems of measurement and calibration. As I understand it, a condition is luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains; the KK principle says that if one knows that p, one is in a position to know that one knows that p. The anti-luminosity argument runs a sorites-style sequence—say, a subject slowly warming from feeling cold to not feeling cold—and concludes that because adjacent stages are indiscriminable, a belief that one has just crossed the cutoff could easily have been false, so the subject fails to know at the last cold moment; hence no non-trivial condition is luminous. I would like a handout for a reading group of retired scientists that sorts members by their actual responses to cases rather than by the labels they find attractive.

Please write a 'which view about self-knowledge do you actually hold?' quiz with eight to ten questions. Each question should present a concrete case—the slowly warming room; a subject asked to report a barely perceptible color change; a pilot relying on an instrument in marginal visibility; a proof checker verifying a long derivation step by step; a subject asked whether she knows that she knows the time—and offer two or three answer options. Then provide a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the main positions in logical space: the safety-based anti-luminosity view that rejects KK; the brute self-intimation view on which phenomenal states are luminous; the hybrid view on which phenomenal states are luminous but knowledge is not; the epistemicist view that there is a sharp but unknowable cutoff; and any further leaf the questions genuinely distinguish. Build the quiz from the questions and answer combinations first, and locate the named positions in that space afterward; flag any positions the quiz cannot separate, and briefly diagnose any combinations of answers that appear to be unoccupied.
