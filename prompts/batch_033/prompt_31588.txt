Two people in my office got into an argument about how you should update your beliefs when evidence is less than certain, and neither would give ground.

Maya: Take a radiologist who reads an X-ray and ends up 80% sure there's a tumour. Standard Bayesianism handles this fine: she conditions on the proposition describing what she actually saw—the shadow on the film, the pattern, whatever. Her new credence in 'tumour' is her old credence conditional on that observation.

Dan: But that's exactly what she didn't learn for certain. She didn't become certain of some precise proposition about the film; she just found herself more confident that there's a tumour. If you force her to condition on 'I saw such-and-such a pattern,' you need likelihoods for that pattern given tumour and given no tumour, and those likelihoods are precisely the judgement the X-ray was supposed to supply. The honest move is to say her credence in the tumour moved straight from 0.2 to 0.8, while her credences conditional on 'tumour' and on 'no tumour' stayed fixed.

Maya: But 'move straight to 0.8' isn't a rule, it's just a number pulled out of the air. Where does 0.8 come from if not from something she learned? And why should the conditional probabilities stay fixed? Sometimes an observation should change what you'd expect given a tumour, not just how likely a tumour is.

Dan: The 0.8 is the input—the new state of uncertainty her experience puts her in—not something the rule has to derive. Strict conditionalisation also leaves its input unexplained: it tells you to hold everything else fixed when you learn a proposition, and that's a substantive assumption, not a theorem of probability. At least my story doesn't pretend the experience was a proposition she became certain of.

Maya: Fine, but if your story just says 'set your credence over the tumour/no-tumour alternatives to whatever the experience dictates,' its not a theory of updating. It's a description of the problem. A theory should say which posterior is rational given a prior and an experience, and yours says nothing about that.

Dan: Neither does yours, actually—strict conditionalisation only applies once you've already settled which proposition was learned with certainty. The question is which idealisation is less distorting for uncertain evidence, not which rule is provable.
