I've been trying to get clear on when someone "has a reason" to do something, and my gut verdicts on cases keep pulling me in different directions. I'd like to know whether a single principle can capture them, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases.

A neighbor of mine is cruel to his dog. He isn't moved by the dog's suffering, doesn't care about being kind, and gets nothing out of treating it well. I still think he has a reason not to hurt the animal.

A friend of mine has no interest in exercise and no goal that exercise would serve. He doesn't care about his health, doesn't enjoy it, and isn't doing it for anyone else. I find myself thinking he has no reason to exercise, even though it would be good for him.

Someone pours himself a gin and tonic, but the bottle actually contains paint thinner. I think he has no reason to drink from it, even though he very much wants a gin and tonic; his desire rests on a mistake.

A student hates studying and has no desire to do it, but she does want to pass the exam at the end of the term. I think she has a reason to study.

A person is severely depressed and no longer wants to see friends, work, or get out of bed, and she doesn't want to want those things either. I think she has a reason to take her medication, even though she doesn't want to take it and nothing she currently wants points toward taking it.

A parent feels no affection for his child, has no desire to raise or provide for her, and feels no guilt about that. I still think he has a reason to care for her.

Someone has lived for years in a situation where she is treated badly and has adapted to it: she no longer wants things to be better and can't imagine wanting them. I think she has a reason to leave, or at least to seek better conditions, even though she has no desire to.

Two of these cases bother me because they look alike and I judge them differently: the friend with no desire to exercise, and the depressed person with no desire to take her medication. In both cases, the person lacks any current desire, and in both cases the action would be good for them. Yet I say the first has no reason and the second does. I can't articulate what the difference is, and I'm not sure the line I'm drawing is defensible. Is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or close to all of them? What would that principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: an adult child who feels no gratitude toward his elderly parents and has no desire to help with their care — does he have a reason to help? And if no coherent principle gets all of my verdicts, which ones should I overturn?
