I recently read Douglas Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" (1985), and I'm having trouble seeing how anyone could resist its conclusion. For context: the essay is a satire in which a fictional columnist, writing in a world where "white" functions as the generic term for all people (the way "man" does in ours), indignantly defends words like "chairwhite" and "forewhite" against the proposal to use "person" instead. The columnist's arguments are structurally identical to real-world defenses of generic "man" — and in the swapped context, they sound absurd. The implicit claim seems to be that resistance to gender-neutral language rests on nothing more than familiarity, and that if we could see the asymmetry from outside, we'd recognize it as indefensible.

I find this persuasive, but I'm worried I find it persuasive because the satire is so clever, not because the argument actually holds up. So here's what I want: take the position that the essay's core argument is sound, and then systematically ask what would count against it. What are the strongest objections — not just the lazy ones Hofstadter satirizes, but serious challenges to the analogy itself, or to what the analogy is supposed to license? Are there relevant differences between race and gender that make the substitution misleading? Does the satire prove something about language, or only about the psychology of people who read it? What linguistic facts, philosophical distinctions, or empirical findings would, if they held up, undermine the essay's central claim? I'd like these defeaters ranked from "this would be fatal" to "this would require only a minor qualification." And I want an honest assessment of whether the position is insulated against counterargument by being "just satire" — and if so, whether that's a problem.

Please approach this as a philosopher of language who has thought seriously about the relationship between linguistic convention and social critique. Start by laying out what you take the essay's argumentative claim to be (as distinct from its rhetorical effect), and then work through the possible defeaters carefully.
