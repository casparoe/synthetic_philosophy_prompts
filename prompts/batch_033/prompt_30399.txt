I'm taking a graduate seminar on the prehistory of speech act theory, and we've just started reading Thomas Reid's *Essays on the Active Powers of the Human Mind* from 1788. I'm getting really stuck on Essay V, Chapter VI, where he discusses the nature and obligation of contracts. Reid's basic move seems to be this: a promise is not just an internal resolution or a report about my present intention. It is what he calls a social act, or social operation, of mind. Some mental acts, he says, essentially involve another intelligent being as object; promising, commanding, asking, and contracting are his examples. A solitary being could form intentions and volitions, but Reid says it could not promise, because in promising I have to address another person and thereby, if the promise is accepted, give that person a right to my performance.

The thing I don't get is what exactly the second person contributes. Does Reid think the promisee's acceptance is part of the act itself, or merely a condition for the act to take effect? And if no right is transferred unless the promisee accepts, does that mean the promiser's obligation arises only from the promisee's act of acceptance, rather than from the promiser's act of promising? That feels like it turns promising into a two-party contract before the contract even gets off the ground, and I'm not sure if I'm overreading him. I'd like help unpacking that, especially because my class wants to connect Reid's social acts to Austin's performatives and the role of uptake in illocutionary acts.
