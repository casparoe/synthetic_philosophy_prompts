I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for, and I want to know whether it has been carved out in the literature — and if not, how you would carve it out.

Consider two cases. A junior colleague, the only woman in her group, has a two-part objection to a proposed policy: one part is about the formal procedure, and the other is about how the policy will affect junior members. She predicts that if she raises the second part, her senior colleagues will hear her as "difficult" or emotional, and that this will discredit the first part as well. So she raises only the procedural point, and it is accepted. In another case, a patient with a chronic condition reports only some of her symptoms to a specialist because she expects the remaining symptoms to be read as anxiety and to undermine the credibility of the rest.

In both cases, the person is not simply being tactful or deciding what is relevant. The omitted content is something they would otherwise have wanted to convey, and they omit it because they anticipate that the audience is unable or unwilling to receive it properly. Notice also that the audience never actually disbelieves them: the problematic testimony is never offered. This seems adjacent to the familiar idea that prejudice can cause people's testimony to be discounted, but that idea concerns what an audience does to a speaker, whereas here the speaker does the truncating in advance.

Does this phenomenon have an established name? If it does, what exactly is its definition, and how is it distinguished from the audience-side phenomenon I just mentioned and from ordinary audience-tailoring? If it does not, what would you call it, and where exactly are the boundaries — for example, when does anticipating an audience's limitations shade into this, and when is it just competent communication? I am not satisfied with a bare label; I want to know the carving.
