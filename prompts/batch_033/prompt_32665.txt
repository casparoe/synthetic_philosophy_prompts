My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether I really knew something, and I walked away unsettled. The situation is this: I was attending a conference a few weeks ago, and I saw someone I took to be my colleague, David, walk into the keynote hall. I formed the belief, "David is in the hall." And I was right — David was in the hall. What I didn't know, and only learned afterward, is that the person I saw walk in was actually David's identical twin brother, whom I'd never met and didn't know existed. David himself had entered the hall fifteen minutes earlier through a side entrance and was seated near the front. So my belief was true, and I had what seemed like excellent grounds for it — I saw a person visually indistinguishable from David walk through the door. But the truth of my belief was entirely disconnected from the evidence I relied on; I was right by accident, so to speak. My friend insists that because my belief was both justified and true, I knew David was in the hall — those are the conditions, and they were met. That strikes me as deeply wrong. It feels like I merely got lucky, and luck is not knowledge. But I found myself struggling to articulate exactly why the justified-true-belief picture breaks down here, and whether I should give up on that picture or modify it. We kept circling back to the same impasse. Can you help me think through who is right in this case, and more importantly, what this kind of case reveals about what knowledge actually requires?
