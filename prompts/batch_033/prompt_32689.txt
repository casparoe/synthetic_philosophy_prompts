I've been watching my friend Alex make decisions for years, and I'm trying to reverse-engineer whatever stopping rule he's actually running. He's a satisficer — he read Simon in college and it stuck, he'll tell you that himself — but "satisficing" covers a lot of different rules and I swear he's not consistent at the level of the rule itself. Or maybe I'm just missing the deeper pattern.

Here are the cases I've observed:

1. Buying a laptop (last year): He walked into Best Buy, looked at three models, bought the third. His requirements were "at least 16GB RAM, SSD, under $1,200," and the third one met them. Didn't check online, didn't go to another store. When I asked why not at least glance at Amazon, he said "it met my requirements, why would I keep looking?"

2. Choosing a grad school (three years ago): Got into six programs. Visited the first two. The second was solid — good advisor fit, decent funding, city he liked. He cancelled the remaining four visits and accepted. Told me later he'd been anxious about the whole thing and "just wanted it settled." He didn't seem to have fixed criteria going in; he said the second one "felt right."

3. Apartment hunting (six months ago): Looked at eight apartments over three weekends. The first five were clear no's — too small, too expensive, bad location. The sixth was fine but he kept looking. The seventh was marginally better than the sixth. He took the eighth, which was about the same as the seventh, and said "I'm done, I can't look at another apartment." He later admitted number six was probably good enough.

4. Buying a used car (two years ago): Test-drove four cars in one day. The second one was within budget, no obvious problems — honestly a solid find. He kept looking. The fourth one was objectively worse than the second, but he bought it. He said he was "sick of car shopping" and the fourth one was "good enough, I guess." When I pointed out the second was better, he got defensive and said he'd already mentally moved on from it by the time he saw the fourth.

5. Choosing a restaurant (all the time): He scrolls Yelp until he finds something with at least 4.2 stars in a cuisine he's in the mood for, then stops. Never compares more than two or three. Says the difference between a 4.2 and a 4.7 isn't worth the scrolling time.

Now the new case: he's job hunting. He's been unemployed for two months, applied to maybe fifteen places. Two interviews so far. First one went nowhere. Second one resulted in an offer — $72k, fine benefits, 25-minute commute, work is a little boring but not terrible. He has four more interviews scheduled in the next two weeks, including one at a company he was genuinely excited about when he applied. He's asked me whether he should take the offer or keep interviewing.

I want to predict what he'll actually do, not what he should do. And more importantly, I want to know what rule — or what combination of rules — best fits the five cases. Sometimes he has a clear threshold and stops at the first option that meets it (laptop, restaurants). Sometimes he overshoots past a good option and then takes something worse just because he's exhausted (car, apartment). The grad school case looks like anxiety-driven early stopping, not exhaustion and not a fixed threshold. The cases seem to pull in different directions.

What's the best candidate rule, or set of rules, that fits his whole track record? What does it predict for the job offer? And if you had to design a sixth case — one more data point — that would definitively rule out the second-best candidate, what would it look like?
