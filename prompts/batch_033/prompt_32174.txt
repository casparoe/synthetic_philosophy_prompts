I've been running a philosophy reading group for about three years now—mostly retired colleagues from the sciences and a few younger people who indulge us. We've been working through the modal epistemology literature, and we've hit the point where the debate about whether conceivability is a guide to possibility has become tangled enough that everyone in the room has a different sense of what the real disagreements are. One person thinks the whole thing reduces to whether you accept Kripke's examples. Another thinks the key move is distinguishing kinds of conceivability. A third thinks the real question is what kind of possibility we're even talking about. We've been going in circles.

I'd like to produce a decision tree for the group—a single-page handout that starts from one entry question and branches through the commitments that actually separate the major positions, terminating in named leaves. The tree should be built from the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of famous names: first identify the diagnostic questions, then map the combinations of answers they allow, and then locate the actual positions within that space. Every branching question should be answerable by someone who hasn't learned the labels yet—it should test what they believe about cases and principles, not which philosopher they've read. Please flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and note any unoccupied combinations of answers, with a brief diagnosis of why nobody is there (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

At minimum, the following positions should appear as leaves: a strong rationalist view (ideal conceivability entails metaphysical possibility), a moderate or defeasible rationalist view, a Kripke-style view on which a posteriori necessities break the inference from conceivability to metaphysical possibility, a Humean view that treats conceivability as a guide to a broader notion of possibility without the logical/metaphysical distinction, and a skeptical view that denies conceivability has any epistemic bearing on possibility. Please also include at least one leaf that turns on the distinction between logical and metaphysical possibility. If important sub-positions exist within any of these—for example, different accounts of what makes conceivability "ideal"—please include them as sub-branches rather than collapsing them.
