I am a philosopher of mind supervising a doctoral student who has three years of funding to work on the philosophical significance of pain asymbolia. The student's background is in philosophy of mind and philosophy of cognitive science; they can read the neuroscientific literature carefully, but they have no clinical training and cannot run empirical studies. We are trying to decide which open questions in this area deserve the bulk of their time. I would like your considered view: which questions should they prioritize, and in what order?

As background, recall the phenomenon. Patients with pain asymbolia — typically after damage to the insular cortex or a disconnection between insular and limbic structures — continue to report that they feel pain and can characterize its location, intensity, and sensory quality. But they deny that it is unpleasant, that it bothers them, or that they want it to stop; they may be indifferent to it rather than distressed by it. Since Nikola Grahek's book Feeling Pain and Being in Pain brought these cases to philosophical attention, the phenomenon has been used to argue that the sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational components of pain are doubly dissociable, and that pain's badness is not intrinsic to the painful sensation itself.

Here are some of the questions one might pursue. Please do not feel bound by this list; it is meant to indicate the sort of thing I have in mind.

1. Does pain asymbolia show that the unpleasantness of pain is a dissociable component rather than an essential feature of the pain state? And what notion of "essential" or "component" is even appropriate when the evidence is empirical and the target is a conscious state?

2. If pain and its unpleasantness can come apart, what does this do to pain-based arguments for hedonism about well-being? Does the hedonist need to say that only the unpleasantness — not the sensory pain itself — is intrinsically bad, and is that a stable retreat?

3. What does pain asymbolia imply for evaluative or representational theories of pain, on which pain represents bodily damage or disvalue? Does the asymbolic patient represent damage without the evaluative force, and would that count for or against such theories?

4. Is pain asymbolia best understood as a change in the very phenomenology of pain, or as a deficit in the affective and motivational response to a pain whose phenomenology is otherwise intact? What sort of evidence — first-person report, lesion data, experimental intervention — could settle this, given how heavily we must rely on what patients say?

5. Does the case change how we should think about the badness of pain in beings whose relevant cortical or limbic structures are immature or absent, such as neonates or some nonhuman animals? Does it license downgrading the badness of their pain, or does it rather show that we should not infer from absent complaints to absent suffering?

6. More generally, does pain asymbolia support the idea that valence — the pleasantness or unpleasantness of an experience — is a distinct dimension of consciousness that can in principle vary independently of sensory content? And what would that imply for the project of explaining phenomenal consciousness?

For each question you recommend, I want the reasoning made explicit: (a) why it is upstream of other work in the area, in the sense that answering it would settle or reframe downstream debates; (b) how tractable it is for someone with this student's training, using conceptual analysis and careful engagement with the empirical literature rather than new experiments; and (c) what evidence, argument, or result over the next couple of years would most change your ranking. I am asking for a defended agenda, not a survey of the literature, and the defense of the ordering matters more to me than the list itself.
