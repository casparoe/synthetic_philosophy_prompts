Over the last few months I've been putting together the conceptual side of a project on mechanistic anomaly detection — the idea that a system should be flagged not when its outputs go wrong but when the internal machinery producing those outputs stops being the machinery it usualy is. I have a list of commitments I keep appealing to, and I'd like someone to check whether they actually hang together before I write them up as a framework. Here they are, roughly in the order I'd defend them: (1) mechanisms are individuated by their components and the organized activities those components perform, not by the input-output mapping they happen to realize; (2) mechanisms are multiply realizable, so sameness of mechanism at the type level doesn't require sameness of parts — what matters is the pattern of activities and interactions; (3) anomaly detection should target changes in mechanism, where a mechanism change is a change in causal-explanatory structure, not mere covariate shift or drift in the input distribution; (4) two token systems realize the same mechanism when their causal graphs are isomorphic and matched interventions on corresponding variables produce the same effect signatures; (5) small parameter tweaks — where a node's local function changes slightly but the graph and the rest of the system's behavior are preserved — don't amount to a change of mechanism; (6) if an image classifier stops using object shape and starts keying off background texture, that's a mechanism change even if its accuracy and the topology of its causal graph stay basically the same; and (7) catching switches like (6) before they show up in the loss is exactly what mechanistic anomaly detection is for.

I'd like an audit rather than a defense: which of these are in genuine tension, which pairs only look like they conflict, how severe each tension is, and what the least costly revision would be — and what it would cost me.
