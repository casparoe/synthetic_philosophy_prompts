Ok here's something I've been thinking about for my teaching. I teach a course on practical rationality and decision theory, and every year I do the money pump unit -- you know the drill, if your preferences are intransitive (A > B > C > A) then a clever trader can extract an infinite amount of money from you through a series of trades, each of which you'll accept because you prefer the offered option to what you currently hold. The students get the abstract point fine. But I've become convinced that actually applying this to real life is harder then it looks, because most of us don't walk around with explicit preference orderings in our heads, and the contexts where we get money-pumped aren't helpfully labeled as such.

So here's what I want: a procedure that a non-specialist can actually use to audit their own preferences for money-pump vulnerability. This is something I'd give to my students as a take-home exercise -- spend twenty minutes, run through the steps, see if you're vulnerable. It should be a checklist or a set of diagnostic questions that surfaces the kind of intransitivities that actually show up in real decision-making, like when you prefer A to B on one criterion, B to C on another, and then C to A because the criteria shift again without you noticing.

The procedure needs to handle some hard cases. It should flag when the procedure itself will give a false positive -- preferences that look intransitive but aren't because the options aren't really comparable or the decision context changed in a relevant way. It should also flag the vulnerabilities it won't catch (false negatives). And it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and which are genuinely mechanical.

Then run it on two or three worked examples: one where the procedure catches a genuine vulnerability, one where it flags something that turns out fine on closer inspection, and one where the procedure can't decide and you have to punt to the person.
