I am presenting a paper next month to my department's philosophy of mind workshop, and I would like a question-and-answer prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. The thesis I am defending is a version of the higher-order theory of phenomenal consciousness: a mental state is conscious, on my account, in virtue of being the target of a suitable actual higher-order representation, where that higher-order state need not itself be conscious, and where misrepresentation by the higher-order state is treated as a genuine route to conscious experience rather than as a refutation of the theory. I am not retreating to a merely dispositional or availability-based version of the view, and I am not offering an account only of introspection or self-knowledge; the claim is meant to explain what it is for a state to be phenomenally conscious.

The audience is the department's mind and cognition group: roughly a dozen people, mostly first-order representationalists of a Tye- or Dretske-influenced sort who hold that phenomenal character is exhausted by first-order representational content, plus two global workspace theorists and one committed illusionist. There is no card-carrying higher-order theorist in the room, and the chair tends to let questions run long. I expect to be pressed on over-intellectualization worries about infants and nonhuman animals, on whether the higher-order state must itself be conscious and the resulting regress, on whether the view conflates consciousness with introspection, on whether misrepresentation cases are a feature or a bug, and from the global workspace and illusionist corners on why the view is not simply adding an inert accompaniment to states that are already conscious or already explicable.

Please draft the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered roughly as they are likely to arise but with a note on how damaging each one is; each objection phrased the way these particular people would phrase it; a two-minute reply for each; a note on where each reply is soft; what I should concede up front and what I should hold; and, at the end, the question I am hoping nobody asks, with an explanation of why it would be the hardest.
