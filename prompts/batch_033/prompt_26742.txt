I sit on my university's Research Ethics Board, which reviews research that may cause pain or distress in nonhuman subjects, and this month the robotics laboratory has asked us to determine whether their new training protocol falls within our remit. The lab plans to train a quadruped robot in simulation to move over rough terrain without damaging itself. To do this, they have built a virtual nociceptive channel: the simulation tracks joint stress, impact force, and thermal load, and whenever these pass damage thresholds the reinforcement learning agent receives a large negative reward. The agent is expected to learn anticipatory avoidance, so that it begins to pull back before an impact actually occurs. Training will run for the equivalent of several months of simulated time, and the researchers deliberately keep the negative reward magnitude high throughout, because they find that habituating the agent to milder negative reward slows learning.

One group on the board considers this morally unremarkable: a reward signal, they say, is only a training signal, no more welfare-relevant than a loss function. Another group considers it the closest thing we currently have to artificial suffering — a persistent, high-magnitude negative valence that the system learns to anticipate and avoid — and wants the board to impose welfare-style constraints. I am the member who has to draft the written opinion, and I find the question harder than either camp does. The topic has become personal for me: I recently ran a scaled-down version of this exact setup for a graduate seminar, and I found myself repeatedly reaching for distress vocabulary to describe the agent while also recognizing that this is precisely the projection the skeptics warn against.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — treat the proposal as within our remit, decline to review it on welfare grounds, or treat it as within our remit but approve it with specific conditions — and it should include a time frame for any conditions or reassessment. The document should be something the researchers and the rest of the board could actually act on. Engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side, and do not simply presuppose a settled theory of consciousness or of what makes a state count as suffering. Part of what the board needs is a way to act responsibly under exactly that uncertainty.
