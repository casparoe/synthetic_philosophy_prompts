I've been thinking about Dennett's sphexishness idea — the Sphex wasp that keeps repeating its burrow-inspection routine even when you move the cricket, never catching on that it could just skip the loop. The concept is meant to make us worry about whether our own apparent flexibility is just a fancier version of the same thing: a more complex but equally rigid set of dispositions, just with more branching conditions.

I'm writing a paper that tries to use sphexishness as a wedge to figure out what people actually *want* from free will. Not whether we have it, but what the "it" is that we're after. So here's what I want you to do.

Below are a bunch of candidate questions that sphexishness raises. Rank them by how much they'd clarify what we actually care about in wanting free will — not by how easy they are to answer, not by how philosophically interesting they are in the abstract, but by how much the answer would change our picture of what the desiderata for free will even are. Then give me a sentence or two for each explaining its placement.

Here are the questions:

1. Can we draw a principled line between sphexish rigidity and genuine flexibility, or is it just complexity all the way down?
2. If sphexishness is a matter of degree, does that make the free will debate itself a Sorites problem?
3. Are there real-world human behaviors that are demonstrably sphexish in the original sense — not just habit or bias, but the kind of looping obliviousness the wasp shows?
4. Does the sphexishness worry apply differently to libertarian, compatibilist, and hard determinist accounts, or does it cut across them evenly?
5. What would it take for an AI to be non-sphexish — and would satisfying those conditions also satisfy what we want from free will, or would we discover that flexibility and free will are different things?
6. Is the sphexishness critique just the problem of "acting for reasons" in disguise, or is it pulling at something distinct?
7. If we are sphexish in more sophisticated ways, does that change anything about moral responsibility, or is responsibility orthogonal to the rigidity/flexibility axis?

I'm especially interested in rankings that put surprising things at the top. If you think one of these is way overrated or underrated relative to the others, flag that.
