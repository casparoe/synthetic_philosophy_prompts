I'm taking a metaethics seminar this term and we've been picking at reasons for attitudes, which I'm finding pretty slippery. My professor keeps coming back to these incentive cases, and so far her rulings have been really consistent. If a demon offers you a big pile of money to believe the sky is green, she says there's no reason to believe it — at most there's a reason to want to believe it, or to take steps to make yourself believe it. If a demon threatens to torture you unless you admire him, she says there's no reason to admire him, at most a reason to want to admire him. Same thing for a demon who threatens you unless you fear a harmless puppy: no reason to fear the puppy, only a reason to want to fear it. And as controls, she says there is a reason to fear a dog when your evidence says its dangerous, and a reason to admire a friend who has genuinely done something admirable. So she isn't just hostile to reasons for attitudes in general.

Next week we're doing the saucer of mud case: a demon will destroy something you deeply care about unless you desire a saucer of mud. I'm trying to figure out what she'll say. Is there a reason to desire the saucer of mud, or will she treat it like the others and say there's at most a reason to want to desire it? What I actually want to know is what rule she's running — in particular whether state-given considerations like threats and rewards can ever count as reasons for the attitude itself, and if so for which attitudes — and what other rules would fit her track record just as well. If her verdicts so far underdetermine the answer, tell me which further case I should ask her about to force the issue, and why that one.
