Someone with bipolar disorder, while stable, signs a psychiatric advance directive — sometimes called a Ulysses contract — saying that if they're ever manic and refusing treatment, they can be hospitalized and medicated against their will. Clinicians and courts mostly honor it. But the same legal system won't let a person sign a contract binding their future self in a lot of other ways: you can't sell yourself into servitude, can't make an irrevocable promise to give away your future earnings, can't even contract away your right to change your mind about a marriage. So the practice seems to be that a past self can override a future self's refusal of treatment, but not a future self's other choices. What theory of rationality and personal identity does that practice presuppose? Does it secretly depend on Parfit's hitchhiker idea — that it can be rational to make yourself unable to back out, or even to make your later self irrational — or does it instead presuppose that the manic self's refusal isn't really an expression of the person's will, or that the manic self isn't really them in the relevant sense? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practice is underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change if you took seriously Parfit's line that personal identity is just psychological continuity in degrees rather than a deep further fact.
