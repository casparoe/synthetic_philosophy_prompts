My political theory reading group has been stuck on the boundary problem for months — half the group leans all-affected-interests (anyone whose interests are affected by a decision should get a say in making it), and the other half leans all-subjected (only those systematically subject to the coercive power of a state count as part of the demos). We've been talking past each other and I want to force some clarity. Please draft the adversarial-collaboration document they should produce. I want:

- A numbered list of claims both sides can genuinly sign — about what the boundary problem is, why it matters, what's at stake in getting it wrong, and any shared methodological commitments. Don't make these vacuous; they should do real work.
- Then a table of disagreements, with each row giving the sub-question, the all-affected camp's answer phrased so they'd own it, and the all-subjected camp's answer phrased so they'd own it.
- Then a cruxes section: the sub-questions that, if resolved, would actually move at least one side. For each crux, include what kind of evidence or argument both sides agree would bear on it, and what each side predicts.
- I suspect one crux is whether the all-affected principle collapses into requiring global democracy (and whether that's a bug or a feature), and another is what to do about people who are clearly affected by a polity's decisions but aren't formally subject to its laws — long-term residents without citizenship, or people downwind of a country's pollution. Don't limit yourself to those if you see others.

The document should be something both camps could actually put their names to — no strawmen, no slanted wording. And if part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why.
