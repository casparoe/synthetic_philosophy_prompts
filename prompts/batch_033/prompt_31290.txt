Question: "Explain John Taurek's central argument in 'Should the Numbers Count?' for the claim that the numbers do not count when deciding whom to save. Then evaluate whether his positive proposal—flipping a coin—follows from that negative argument. If it does not, what additional commitments are needed to bridge the gap?"

Student's answer:

"Taurek argues that the numbers do not count because we cannot aggregate the losses of different individuals in the way we can aggregate the value of objects. When we choose between saving five apples and one apple, we rightly save the five because the value of apples is impersonal and additive. But persons are not like apples. The loss each person suffers—the loss of their entire life—is a loss from their own perspective, and we cannot sum these individual losses into a larger total loss. To do so would be to treat persons as mere containers of value rather than as beings whose subjective experience of harm matters. Therefore, the fact that five people will die rather than one does not, by itself, give us a reason to save the five.

Taurek's positive proposal is that we should flip a fair coin. This gives each person an equal 50% chance of being saved, which expresses equal concern and respect for each. The coin flip follows directly from his negative argument: if the numbers do not count, then the only remaining consideration is the equal moral status of each person. Since each person's life is equally valuable, we must give each an equal chance. The coin flip is therefore the uniquely rational way to respect the equal worth of all persons when we cannot save everyone."
