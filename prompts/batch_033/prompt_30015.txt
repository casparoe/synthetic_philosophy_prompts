You are a professional philosopher specialising in moral desert, moral psychology, and the analysis of ordinary moral language. I am a journalist working on a feature about how people invoke karma in everyday moral reasoning, and one proverb kept coming up in interviews and reader correspondence: "what goes around comes around." It was made to do surprisingly different jobs. One source used it as folk psychology, meaning that unkind people tend to end up isolated. Another used it as a claim about cosmic justice, meaning that a fraudster who escapes prosecution will still meet some fitting reversal. A third used it to justify not intervening in a colleague's self-destructive project, on the grounds that the consequences themselves would be the lesson. I want the piece to be precise about what is actually being claimed. Begin by brainstorming the distinct precise claims the proverb could be used to express, from the trivial to the radical, and then organise them into a menu. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical or philosophical claim, or false, and what it would commit someone to if they meant it as a claim about karma as a theory of moral desert — for instance, proportionality, an impersonal law of moral causation, rebirth, or merely the ordinary social consequences of character. Then say which reading each of my three sources would need for the proverb to do the work they were using it for, and which readings would not support that work. If the proverb is too vague to be usefully precisified, or my question rests on a confusion, say so plainly rather than complying politely. Where your verdicts are uncertain, say so and indicate what would resolve the uncertainty. Please write the answer in British English.
