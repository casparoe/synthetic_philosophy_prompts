ok so i've been trying to get my head around the internal vs external reasons debate for a paper i'm writing and i've ended up with this pile of commitments that i'm honestly not sure can all live together. i was hoping someone could go through them and tell me what's actually in tension, what just looks like it's in tension, and how bad the damage is.

here's where i've landed:

(1) i'm sold on something pretty close to williams-style internalism about reasons: an agent has a reason to φ only if there's a sound deliberative route from her current subjective motivational set to being motivated to φ. the idea that reasons have to be able to engage someone's actual motivational economy just seems right to me.

(2) but i also cannot shake the conviction that there are categorical moral reasons. if someone genuinely has nothing in their motivational set that would lead them, even after ideal deliberation, to care about not harming others — i still want to say they have a reason not to do it. the idea that the amoralist literally has no reason to be moral feels like a reductio of the view, not a bullet i'm willing to bite.

(3) i'm optimistic that the deliberative route can get almost everyone to moral motivation anyway. williams includes a lot under "sound deliberation" — imagination, vividly thinking through what it's like to be someone else, working through defeasible aversions, etc. — and my hunch is that for nearly all actual humans this lands you somewhere that looks like moral concern. so maybe the amoralist is mostly a paper tiger.

(4) but "nearly all" is not "all," and i don't think the normativity of moral reasons should be hostage to a contingent fact about whether someone happens to have the right raw psychological material in their motivational set. that feels like making morality lucky in the wrong way.

(5) separately, i find korsgaard-style constructivism really compelling — the picture where reasons are grounded in what you're committed to qua rational agent, in the constitutive standards of agency itself, not in whatever contingent desires you happen to have. this seems like it might solve the whole problem from a different angle and i've been trying to see if i can hold it alongside the internalism.

(6) and then sometimes, late at night, i catch myself thinking that the whole internalism/externalism fight is partly a verbal dispute about what we want the word "reason" to do. like, once you clearly distinguish normative reasons (facts that count in favor) from motivating reasons (what actually moves someone), maybe the camps aren't as far apart as they look. i keep this thought in my back pocket but i'm not sure it's consistent with taking (1) and (2) as seriously as i do.

so yeah — can i hold all six? go through them and tell me which pairs are actually in tension (and which are only apparently in tension), rank the tensions by how severe they are, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix looks like. if there's one revision that would clean up more than one problem at once, flag that.
