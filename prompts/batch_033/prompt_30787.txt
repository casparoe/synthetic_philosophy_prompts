I review claims at a state disability benefits office — I'm the person who decides whether a claimant's condition counts as a "disease or medical disorder" for coverage, and I do maybe a dozen of these a week. Our manual says we cover diseases and disorders but not "normal variations" or "lifestyle conditions," and then leaves me to sort out which is which. I keep running into the same two frustrating cases: someone with a clear abnormality on their scans who says it's never bothered them and only wants a form signed, and someone with no findings at all who is in real documented distress and can't work. Our staff physician says he can only tell me whether something is statistically unusual or not working the way it's built to; he says wether that's a "disease" is not a medical question. I once heard a podcast where someone said calling something a disease is like calling a plant a weed — it's a judgment about what we don't want, not a fact about the plant. Other people seem to think a disease is just a part of the body failing to do its job, and whether we like it is a separate question. I don't have the background to settle that debate, but I need a procedure I can actually run, and my supervisor will sign off only if the rule is consistent and not just my vibes.

Please write me a decision procedure for classifying a condition as a disease/disorder for my purposes, grounded in whatever is actually known about that debate, but written for someone who has never read the philosophy. It should be a checklist or set of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three cases: a claimant with situs inversus (organs mirrored, no symptoms) who wants us to declare it a disorder after being denied an insurance rider; a claimant with chronic fatigue syndrome who can't work but whose tests are all normal; and a woman claiming coverage for infertility treatment who says she has never wanted children and is only filing because her employer's plan pays out for it. Include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure can't settle.
