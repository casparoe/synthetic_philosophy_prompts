A type designer on the team and I have been going back and forth about how to model letterforms, and I'd like a careful outsider's audit of the analogy he keeps using. His claim is that a typeface stands to the alphabet the way an accent stands to a language: the accent changes the surface of the speech, but the words underneath stay the same, and no one thinks a Texan and a Glaswegian are speaking different languages. From this he concludes that every lowercase a across every font is, underneath, the same letter; that the visual differences are presentation-only; and that our data model should store a single canonical a per script, with each typeface treated as a rendering parameter rather than as its own object with its own identity. I suspect the opposite — that a letter only ever actually appears as some particular glyph, that there is no font-neutral a lying around to store, and that what we call "the letter a" is an abstraction we impose after the fact.
