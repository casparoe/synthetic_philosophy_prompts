I spent my career as a developmental biologist, and in retirement I've turned to philosophy. The problem that has most unsettled me is origin essentialism — the claim, defended most prominently by Saul Kripke, that an individual's origin is essential to its identity. Applied to persons: you could not have developed from a different sperm fertilizing a different egg. Had your parents conceived on a different night, the resulting child would not have been you. This is not science fiction about parallel worlds; it is a metaphysical claim about what makes a particular person that person.

I find myself wanting to explore this not through argument but through narrative. Write a short story — perhaps 1,500 to 2,500 words — in which a character is forced to reckon with the idea that their very existence was decided in the moment of fertilization, and that any other outcome would have meant someone else entirely. The story should engage with the philosophical substance of the question: for instance, what does it mean to say a person couldn't have come from different gametes? Is the claim about the identity of the organism, the person, or the self? And what does it imply about the relationship between biological contingency and the sense that one's own existence is, somehow, a matter of necessity?

The story should not be a philosophy paper in disguise. Let the idea work on the character — and the reader — through situation, image, and emotional consequence. A reader familiar with Kripke or with the broader literature on essentialism about origins should feel that the story takes the argument seriously, but the story should be able to stand on its own as fiction.
