Last night I had a long argument with a friend about the presumption of independence in heuristic mathematical reasoning, and I have been unable to settle it on my own. My friend's view was that when two events have no known connection, treating them as independent is not merely a convenient approximation: it is forced by the principle of maximum entropy, since any dependence we cannot articulate is information we do not possess, and a rational probability assignment must not import information we lack. That sounds right to me, but it leads somewhere strange. If the independence step is forced by a general principle of rationality, then it is not a guess, and a heuristic argument that uses it should be as strong as a proof once the remaining quantitative estimates are checked. Yet mathematicians continue to describe such arguments as merely heuristic. Which of us is confused, and about what exactly? I would like a committed response: take a definite position on the correct relationship between the presumption of independence, maximum-entropy reasoning, and the gap between heuristic and rigorous argument. Present it as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
