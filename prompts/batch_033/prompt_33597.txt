So I'm a novelist and I've gone down a rabbit hole reading philosophy of language, because I'm starting to think conditionals are basically the engine of plotting: every outline I write is full of "if she goes to the house, she'll find the letter" and "if she had gone to the house, she would have found it." And there's this fight in the literature that's been stuck for decades, and I cant tell what kind of fight it is. One camp treats indicative conditionals and counterfactual conditionals as two species of one thing: the word "if" has a single meaning, something about possible worlds, and the only difference between "If the butler didn't do it, the gardener did" and "If the butler hadn't done it, the gardener would have" is which worlds end up in play. The other camp says they're fundamentally different: indicatives are probability claims — to accept "if A then C" is roughly to have a high conditional probability of C given A — while counterfactuals are about something else, like closeness between worlds or causal structure. Both camps handle the obvious cases fine, and they even handle the cases where the two come apart fine, and still nobody moves. My working theory is that this isn't a disagreement about any fact about language; it's a disagreement about what a semantic theory is supposed to be doing in the first place — modeling truth conditions, or modeling what it is to accept and reject a sentence in a conversation. Is that right? Or is there a real empirical question underneath it about how people actually evaluate these sentences, something psychology could settle? Or is it a verbal dispute about the word "meaning"? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions. And then tell me honestly what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a reframing — and whether any of it should change how I think about the counterfactual reasoning I do when I'm outlining, or if its just a seminar-room impasse.
