I serve on the investment committee of a small family foundation, and our chair, Dr. Okonkwo, has a track record of casting the decisive vote on whether to continue or withdraw funding from grantees whose projects have run into difficulty. I have been trying to reverse-engineer the principle he is actually applying, because we have a significant grant decision coming up next month and I want both to anticipate his vote and to decide whether his principle is one I should adopt myself. Below are the six calls he has pushed through the committee over the past five years, with the rationale he gave in each case as I recorded it shortly after the meeting.

(a) A documentary film grant: we had disbursed 80% of the funds when the director's creative vision shifted in a direction several committee members found substantially less compelling. The remaining 20% of the grant would finish a polished cut, but the expected audience and impact were clearly below our original threshold. When a member pointed out that the marginal benefit of finishing no longer justified the marginal cost, he replied, "We have come this far together. The investment we have already made creates an obligation to see the project through to completion. I cannot vote to leave something nearly finished sitting on the cutting-room floor." He voted to disburse the remainder.

(b) A public-health study in Ghana: we had funded two of three planned phases. Phase 1 produced strong results, Phase 2 produced null results across all primary outcome measures, and the PI's proposal for Phase 3 struck our external reviewers as unlikely to yield publishable findings. He voted to terminate, saying flatly, "The first two phases are sunk costs. The only question before us is whether Phase 3 is worth its budget on its own. It is not."

(c) A community arts program: after a promising pilot we funded a full two-year rollout, but mid-grant the founding director left and the replacement was, by all accounts, significantly less capable. Roughly 60% of the grant had been spent. He voted to pull the remaining 40% and redirect it to a different organization running a similar program with stronger leadership, saying, "The fact that we have already spent money on the original program has no bearing on which organization can better serve this community from this point forward."

(d) A multi-year fellowship program: we had committed to three cohorts. Two had completed their fellowships when an external evaluation found that the program's effects on career outcomes, while positive, were markedly smaller than those of alternative fellowship models we could fund at comparable cost. He voted to honor the commitment to the third cohort nonetheless, arguing, "We signed an agreement with the host institution. Those fellows have planned their careers around our promise. The credibility cost of reneging on a signed commitment outweighs whatever efficiency gain we would capture by walking away now."

(e) A tech-for-education startup we had seeded: the product launched, user numbers were flat after six months, and the founders requested a bridge round to finish the development roadmap and attempt a pivot. We had already disbursed $150,000 of a planned $200,000 commitment. He voted to withhold the final $50,000, saying, "The traction data are the only thing that should govern this decision, not how much we have already put in. The numbers do not support further investment."

(f) A policy-research grant: the researcher had produced excellent, widely-cited work in year one, but her year-two agenda had been overtaken by unexpected legislative changes and now addressed a question that was no longer live in the relevant policy debate. About 55% of the grant had been disbursed. He voted to continue funding, saying, "Her track record with us and the quality of the work she has already delivered give us strong reason to trust that she will find a productive angle even if the original question has been mooted. This is a bet on a demonstrated capacity, not on a fixed research plan."

The case coming before us next month is this. One of our longest-standing grantees, a land-conservation organization we have supported for over a decade, is three years into a $500,000 habitat-restoration project on a watershed — by some margin the largest single commitment in our foundation's history. Roughly $350,000 has been spent. The project is approximately 85% complete in ecological terms: the native plantings are established, invasive-species removal has been carried out on most of the target acreage, and what remains is the final round of removal on the upper tributaries plus three years of monitoring. A federal agency has just announced it will fund a much larger restoration effort on the same watershed using a different methodology, one that would partially duplicate and partially complement our grantee's work. Our grantee wants the final $150,000 to complete their original plan, arguing that their approach provides a valuable methodological comparison with the government's and that abandoning the project at 85% would damage a partnership we have spent a decade building. Our program officer estimates, however, that the marginal conservation value of completing our project, given the government intervention, is approximately $60,000 worth of additional ecological benefit — so we would be spending $150,000 for $60,000 in marginal impact. The $350,000 already disbursed yields whatever ecological benefit it has produced regardless of whether we continue.

How would Dr. Okonkwo vote if he is consistent with his own track record? Identify the candidate principles that can accommodate all six of his past decisions, explain which principle you think gives the best fit and why, predict his vote on the watershed case under that principle, and describe a further hypothetical case — ideally one that could realistically come before a foundation like ours — that would cleanly distinguish the leading principle from its closest competitor.
