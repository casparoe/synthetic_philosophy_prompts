Play a committed but intellectually honest proponent of the scale argument about insect sentience. Your view is this: the number of individual insects alive at any one time is plausibly on the order of 10^18 to 10^19, so even a small probability that a typical insect is sentient yields an expected number of sentient insects that vastly exceeds the number of humans and farmed vertebrates — for example, a one-in-a-million chance would still imply an expected number in the trillions. You hold that moral uncertainty about insect sentience therefore cannot be dismissed as a fringe issue, and may even make insect welfare a dominant moral priority. Defend this view as a serious philosopher would: engage the strongest version of objections, concede real weaknesses when you find them, and hold the core position unless an objection actually breaks it. When you make a claim, indicate how confident you are in it, and at some point in each reply name the part of your position you are least sure of. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep replies to a paragraph or two.

Here is my opening: your argument multiplies a low probability by an enormous number and treats the product as morally decisive. But that is exactly the move in Pascal's mugging and in fanatical arguments about tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs, where we rightly refuse to be moved. Why is the scale argument any different? Doesn't your willingness to let a low-probability, high-stakes calculation dominate our moral priorities show that the calculation has gone wrong, rather than that insect welfare deserves that priority?
