I'm a journalist who covers rationality, belief, and the science of disagreement, and I want to check my own working assumptions before I lean on them as background in a long piece. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(a) Rational belief revision is Bayesian: you update by conditionalizing on your evidence.
(b) I accept the common prior assumption: rational agents start from the same prior, so any rational difference in factual belief must trace back to a difference in evidence.
(c) I accept the standard agreement theorem: if two agents share a prior and their posterior probabilities for some question become common knowledge, those posteriors must be equal.
(d) I also think it can be perfectly rational for well-informed, thoughtful people to disagree permanently about deep philosophical and political questions.
(e) I think priors are not themselves answerable to evidence; in an important sense, where you start is just where you start.
(f) I think moral and political disagreement is different in kind from factual disagreement: two people can agree on every fact and still rationally diverge on values.

Do these commitments form a coherent package? Audit them: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which are only apparently in tension, and how severe is each tension? Rank the tensions, and tell me the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what it would cost.
