Two colleagues of mine got into an argument at a gallery opening after we learned that a painting we had all spent time admiring earlier that evening had been revealed as a forgery. The debate went through several rounds, and I have been turning it over ever since.

(1) Marcus said the revelation changed nothing aesthetically: the painting looks exactly the same as it did an hour ago, it produces the same visual experience, and if nobody in the room could tell the difference before the forensic report came in, then whatever we valued in the work was present in the forgery too. He said prizing the "real thing" over a perceptually indistinguishable copy is status anxiety dressed up as connoisseurship — we are really just upset that we paid for the wrong certificate of origin.

(2) Elena replied that this conflates the object with its surface. The forgery presents itself as something it is not — a work by, say, Vermeer — and our appreciation of a Vermeer includes appreciating how a particular human being solved particular pictorial problems at a particular historical moment, with particular materials and constraints. The forgery falsifies that entire relationship between maker and work. She said Marcus's position would force him to say that if we could 3D-print a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an archaeopteryx fossil, it would be just as scientifically valuable as the original, which she found obviously absurd.

(3) Marcus answered that the fossil analogy begs the question: a fossil is valued precisely as evidence about the deep past, so of course a duplicate carries no evidential weight — it was never embedded in the causal history it purports to record. But a painting, he said, is valued for how it looks and what it does to a viewer. If the forgery does exactly the same things to a viewer, then calling it aesthetically worse is like saying a perfect replica of a chair is worse for sitting. It confuses the function of the object with the story we tell ourselves about it.

(4) Elena said he had just given away the game: he conceded that for some kinds of object — fossils, love letters, the shirt your favourite band signed — the object's history is internal to what the thing is for. The question is whether artworks belong in that category. She thinks they do, because art-making is an intentional act embedded in a tradition, and the forgery severs the connection between the act and the object while retaining the appearance of it. She added that Marcus's view cannot explain why we feel betrayed upon learning the truth: if nothing of aesthetic value was lost, the feeling of betrayal is irrational, but it does not feel irrational.

(5) Marcus replied that the feeling of betrayal is real but it is a social emotion about having been deceived by another person, not an aesthetic judgment about the work. He said we feel the same way when we learn that a charming anecdote a friend told was fabricated — the story has not become less entertaining, but we are annoyed at the friend for misrepresenting himself. That does not mean the story was a worse story. The forgery, he said, is a worse social act, not a worse painting.

They went back and forth a while longer but those were the main moves. Do not just declare a winner — go through the dialectic and say what each move establishes and what it leaves open.
