I'm putting together a handout for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and I want something that forces students to take positions on underlying commitments rather than just pick a label they recognize. Specifically, I'd like a decision tree that starts from a claim I expect nearly all of them to accept — that at least some mental events, such as deciding to raise one's hand, cause physical events, such as the hand's going up — and then sorts them, through yes/no answers at each node, into a named position in the mental causation debate. Each branch node should be one of the claims on which the main positions genuinely come apart, phrased as a question a student can answer before knowing which position it leads to. Please build the tree from the logical space first rather than from a list of names. The nodes should include, at minimum: whether mental events are identical with physical events; whether the physical domain is causally closed in the sense that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause; whether mental-physical distinctness combined with causal closure would make mental causes excluded or redundant; whether being a cause requires producing an effect or can be satisfied by difference-making or counterfactual dependence; and whether a nonreductive physicalist can accept systematic overdetermination or downward causation. The leaves should include reductive type identity, nonreductive physicalism with mental properties as causally relevant but not efficacious in their own right, epiphenomenalism, interactionist dualism, overdeterminationism, and eliminativism; add further leaves if you think the space requires them.

After the tree, add a short diagnosis of the empty and underdetermined regions: for each combination of answers that no standard position occupies, say whether it looks empty because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because the literature has simply not developed it. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate given the nodes you used, rather than papering over the gap. The audience is advanced undergraduates who have read a little about the causal exclusion argument but do not yet have settled views, so keep the questions in plain terms and explain any unavoidable technical term in the node itself.

Finally, where the taxonomy forces a choice that the literature treats as open or contested, say so explicitly. At the end of your answer, tell me how confident you are in the overall mapping and single out the one branching judgment you are least sure about.
