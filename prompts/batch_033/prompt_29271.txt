I'm one of four co-founders of a small software company, and for the past six weeks we have all been performing confidence in a pivot that each of us, privately, has stopped believing in. Each of us arrived at the same conclusion on our own — the new direction won't work, and the longer we wait to say so, the worse the eventual reckoning gets. None of us has said this in a full-team meeting. Instead, over the past month, we have had a series of one-on-one conversations: I told Priya I had serious doubts; Priya told Marcus; Marcus told Dana; and Dana told me. So every one of us has confided in exactly one other person, and every one of us has been confided in by exactly one other person. And the strange thing is that nothing changed. We all still walk into our Monday meeting and talk as if the pivot is fine. The private confessions might as well not have happened.

Last week I described this to a friend who reads the same kinds of blogs I do, and he said the problem is that our doubts have never been voiced in a setting where everyone is present at once and everyone can see everyone else hear them. The one-on-one confessions, he said, only made the doubts known pairwise; what is missing is something about publicity — about the fact being out in the open in a way that everyone knows everyone else has registered it, and everyone knows that, and so on. When I pointed out that each of us already knows there is serious doubt on the team (we each have it), and that after our round of confessions each of us also knows at least one other person has it, he said that isn't the same thing, and that a single candid sentence in a full-team meeting would do real causal work even though it would tell us nothing we don't already know.

I keep going back and forth. On the one hand, I have watched private knowledge fail to move us. On the other hand, I cannot say what the public sentence would add, and I am suspicious that my friend is reciting a puzzle from LessWrong without checking whether it applies to four actual people with equity on the line. What I want is not a generic pep talk about transparency. I want to know whether there is a real structural difference between our pairwise confessions and a full-team statement, what exactly that difference is, and whether it is the kind of thing that would actually change our behavior or just a formal curiosity. If the story my friend is telling only works in toy models — if real teams do not need this everyone-knows-that-everyone-knows business, or if what is actually keeping us stuck is something else, like fear or misaligned incentives — say so plainly instead of humoring the question. And where you are uncertain about how far the theory carries over to a real team, mark it as uncertain rather than smoothing it over.
