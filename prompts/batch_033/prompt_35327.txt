I'm a PhD student in philosophy, taking a seminar that sits at the intersection of formal epistemology and decision theory. We've been working through a number of classic results, and last week we covered Good's theorem on the value of information. The formal statement is clean: for an expected-utility maximizer with a coherent prior, the expected value of acquiring any costless piece of information is non-negative. The proof is elegant in its simplicity — before you see the signal, you can always adopt the policy of ignoring it and doing whatever you would have done anyway, so the optimal policy conditional on the signal must do at least as well in expectation. I can follow the proof without difficulty.

What's bothering me is that the theorem seems to prove too much. It appears to rule out, a priori, the very possibility of information being harmful to a decision-maker — and yet that possibility seems not just intuitively plausible but practically important. I want to understand exactly where the tension is coming from.

I can think of several kinds of case that seem to challenge the theorem. One is the game-theoretic case: you're playing against an opponent who observes your information state and conditions their strategy on it; having more information changes what they do, and that can make you worse off. Another is the bounded-rationality case: you're a finite agent who doesn't process information perfectly, and additional information can overwhelm your reasoning or trigger predictable biases that you can't fully correct for, even if you know about them. A third is the case where the information is about something that stands in a causal relationship to your own decision to acquire it — so that the act of acquiring the information changes the world in a way that isn't captured by a simple state-signal partition. A fourth is the case where the information itself changes your preferences or your ability to act, as when learning something demoralizes you to the point that you can no longer bring yourself to choose the option you know is best.

I'd like a careful, nuanced explanation of what Good's theorem actually rules out and what it doesn't. Which of these cases are genuine counterexamples to the theorem as stated, and which are cases where the theorem still holds but we've misdescribed the decision problem? What are the precise assumptions of the theorem that each case violates, and are those assumptions substantive or merely definitional? And once we've drawn the boundaries properly, what is the theorem's real importance — what does it tell us about rationality and information that we didn't already know?
