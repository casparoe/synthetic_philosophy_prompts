Question (worth 10 points):

Parfit's teletransporter thought experiment is meant to motivate a particular view about what matters in survival. Explain the teletransporter case, the conclusion Parfit draws from it, and how that conclusion connects to his broader reductionism about personal identity. Then identify one objection to Parfit's view and say whether you think it succeeds.

Their answer:

Parfit asks us to imagine a machine that scans your entire body and brain, records the exact state of every cell, destroys you, and then transmits the information to Mars where a perfect replica is built from new matter. The replica wakes up with all your memories, personality traits, intentions, and everything else that makes you psychologically you. Parfit's question is: would you step into this machine? And if you would, does that mean you believe you'd survive the process?

Parfit thinks the answer is yes — you would survive, and the person on Mars would be you. He takes this to show that what we really care about in survival isn't numerical identity or bodily continuity, but rather psychological continuity and connectedness, which he calls Relation R. As long as Relation R holds between you and some future person, that's enough for survival. This is the conclusion he draws from the teletransporter.

The connection to his reductionism is that Parfit thinks facts about personal identity aren't deep further facts — they just consist in more particular facts about psychology and bodies. There's no Cartesian ego or soul that persists. If that's right, then the teletransporter case doesn't involve any mysterious loss of a self; it just involves a causal process that preserves the psychological facts that matter. So reductionism about identity makes the teletransporter conclusion more plausible, and the teletransporter intuition in turn supports reductionism.

One objection is the branching case. Suppose the machine malfunctions and doesn't destroy the original — now you've got two people, one on Earth and one on Mars, both psychologically continuous with the original. Parfit admits that in this case, neither is numerically identical to the original, because identity is one-to-one. But he still says the original survives in both branches, because Relation R holds between the original and each branch. The problem is that if Relation R is what matters for survival, then in the original teletransporter case, we shouldn't say the person survives as the replica — we should just say the replica bears Relation R to the original and that's what matters. But Parfit does want to say the person survives in the simple case, which seems to sneak identity back in. So there's a tension: either Relation R is enough for survival without identity, in which case the simple teletransporter doesn't actually show that you survive (it just shows that something relation-R-related to you exists), or Relation R isn't enough, and the branching case shows that surviving qua identity is what we care about. I think this objection has some force, though I'm not sure it completely defeats Parfit's view — it might just show he needs to be more careful about what "surviving as" means.
