I'm a sophomore on my university's academic conduct board, and I'm also taking a seminar on practical reason this semester where we've been reading the internal vs. external reasons debate — roughly, Williams's line that a consideration only counts as a reason for someone if it can reach them through the motivations they already have, versus the idea that some reasons apply to you whether or not you happen to care about them. It's turned out weirdly relevant to a case we have to decide this week.

The case: a first-year, M., got caught submitting LLM-written essays in a required writing course, which violates the honor code. At the hearing M. didn't deny it and didn't really apologize. They said, roughly, that they knew the rule existed, but they had no reason to follow it: they don't care about writing, they were only in the course because its required, and the rule just didn't hook onto anything they already wanted. So now half the board wants to treat that statement as aggravating — it looks like defiance and a lack of remorse — and the other half wants to treat it as not aggravating, maybe even mitigating, because M. was being honest about their actual motivational state and it seems wrong to punish someone extra for candor.

I keep going back and forth, and I think the internal/external reasons stuff matters here: if reasons really are internal, then M.'s claim that they had no reason to follow the rule might be a true description of their situation, and we'd have to decide what follows from that; if external reasons exist, the claim looks more like a refusal to count a reason that was there all along. Could you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? It should recommend a concrete decision — specifically, whether the board should treat M.'s statement as aggravating, mitigating, or neutral, and what penalty follows from that (we're choosing between a standard one-semester suspension and something harsher) — and it should give a real argument for the recommendation, including an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side. I don't need citations or a reading list, just the memo.
