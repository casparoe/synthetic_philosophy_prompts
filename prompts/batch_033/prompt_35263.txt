Two members of my graduate seminar on rational choice stayed after class this week, and I found myself listening to an argument I have not been able to referee to my own satisfaction. I would like you to adjudicate it. They were discussing Douglas Hofstadter's idea of superrationality as applied to a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two ideal reasoners in exactly symmetric circumstances: same information, same decision procedure, no communication, and no causal influence between their choices. The payoffs are the usual ones, T > R > P > S: the temptation to defect against a cooperator, the reward for mutual cooperation, the punishment for mutual defection, and the sucker payoff. Call the student who defended cooperation Simone and the student who defended defection David. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

(1) Simone: The standard dominance argument says that whatever she does, I do better by defecting. But that argument holds her choice fixed while varying mine, and she is not a fixed feature of my environment. She is an ideal reasoner in exactly my position. If our situations are genuinely symmetric and the same reasoning yields the same output, then the only live outcomes are that we both cooperate or that we both defect. Since R is greater than P, I should cooperate.

(2) David: That is an elegant redescription, but it does not address the decision I actually face. I choose among my acts and take her act as a state I do not control. Cooperation is dominated: if she cooperates, my defecting yields T rather than R; if she defects, my defecting yields P rather than S. In every state, defection is better. The fact that the pair is better off under mutual cooperation is true but beside the point, because I cannot choose the pair's joint act — I can only choose my own.

(3) Simone: Your dominance table treats her act as conditionally independent of mine, but the symmetry gives me a conditional dependence. Conditional on my cooperating, the most reasonable expectation is that she, running the same reasoning, cooperates; conditional on my defecting, she defects. Under those conditional probabilities, cooperation has expected value R and defection has expected value P, and R exceeds P. Dominance looks decisive only because you are conditioning on the state instead of on your act.

(4) David: Now you have left game theory for evidential decision theory, and its problems come with you. In a one-shot game there is no causal channel from my act to hers. The correlation you are using is purely doxastic — a relation between my possible acts and my beliefs about her. But an act's expected utility should track what the act can bring about, not what it would merely be evidence for. This is why evidential reasoning gets Newcomb-like cases wrong: if a reliable predictor has already made its prediction, my paying a bribe does not cause the good outcome even if it is evidence for it. Superrationality makes the same mistake with the other player cast as the predictor: it lets a diagnosis do the work of an action.

(5) Simone: The predictor analogy fails. A predictor is a separate mechanism whose output is causally upstream of the payoffs. My counterpart in the symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma is not upstream of anything; she is running the very deliberation I am running. When I ask what an ideal reasoner in this situation should do, I am also answering what she will do. And notice where your dominance argument leaves us: if defection is the rational answer, she too defects, and we each get P; if cooperation is the rational answer, we each get R. A principle of rationality that guarantees the worse symmetric outcome for every symmetric pair is not a neutral arbiter between acts — it is a self-fulfilling prophecy, and rational agents should not want it.

(6) David: That last move is doing the real work, and it is a generalization test, not an individual decision principle. Symmetry alone does not single out cooperation, since mutual defection is just as symmetric as mutual cooperation. To break the tie you must add something like "act on the policy whose universal adoption leaves you best off" — a collective or rule-level criterion. In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, my act does not cause hers, so the fact that the cooperative policy is better for the pair gives me no reason to cooperate now. Worse, if I actually believed your superrational policy would be followed by both of us, I would then have a decisive motive to defect: she cooperates, and my defecting yields T, which is better than R. So superrationality, if believed, seems to undermine itself. The only way to block that is to declare the unilateral deviation unthinkable, and that is a stipulation, not an argument.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say which student is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, I would like you to indicate what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different overall verdict, or where you take your own reasoning to be most likely to fail.
