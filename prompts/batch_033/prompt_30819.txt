I am teaching George Eliot's Silas Marner and would like a reading of the novel through the lens of her mature views on religion. The view I have in mind is the one she reached after abandoning her evangelical faith and after her early translation work on the German higher critics and on Feuerbach's humanist account of religion: that the supernatural and doctrinal elements of Christianity can be set aside without losing its moral core, and that religious feeling does its real work only when it is redirected from God to sympathy, fidelity, memory, and duty within human relationships. The novel appears at first to be a fictional enactment of precisely this thesis. Silas passes from a betrayed faith in a sect that convicts him by prayer and lots, through a solitary idolatry of his gold, to a redemptive love for the child he comes to raise; the narrative keeps the language of providence and blessing while locating every genuine redemption in human action. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions and does not stop at the neat fit. Where does the novel complicate Eliot's stated position? Does Eppie's arrival at the weaver's cottage function as a secular grace, and, if so, does that retain the shape of supernatural providence after its doctrinal content has been removed? Is the Lantern Yard congregation a fair representation of the faith she left, or does the novel stack the deck in favour of the humanist alternative? Does Dolly Winthrop's undogmatic, practical piety represent Eliot's ideal of a religion after doctrine, or does the novel quietly show something about ordinary religious life that the essays miss? And what does the form of the novel add to the philosophical argument — what can fiction show about how a post-Christian conscience is actually acquired and lived that discursive prose cannot? Conclude by saying where an honest critic should concede that the novel resists the reading. Please write your answer in British English.
