Please comment on the following notes for an essay. The essay would defend the view that the Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics is better treated as two distinct problems than as a single one. The first is a normative problem: in a deterministic branching universe, a pre-measurement agent has a successor on every branch, and the branches carry unequal Born weights, given by the squared moduli of their amplitudes; the question is which weights a rational agent should use when evaluating actions whose outcomes differ across branches. The second is a metaphysical problem: because every outcome with nonzero amplitude occurs, it is unclear what probability, chance, or self-locating uncertainty could even mean for an agent before branching. The essay would argue that decision-theoretic approaches to the Born rule are best understood as solutions to the normative problem, while self-locating-uncertainty approaches are best understood as solutions to the metaphysical problem, and that the common objection that each approach assumes the Born rule often rests on expecting one solution to discharge the other's burden. It would conclude that the interesting open question is not whether either approach succeeds alone, but whether the two can be combined so that an agent's rational branch weights coincide with whatever self-locating uncertainty the metaphysics can sustain.

I would welcome comments on the following: (1) Is the proposed distinction between a normative and a metaphysical problem faithful to the actual dialectic, or does it misdescribe how the arguments are related? (2) What is the strongest objection to the essay's central interpretive claim that decision-theoretic and self-locating approaches are solving different problems? (3) Is the proposed conclusion substantive enough to merit an essay, or would it merely redescribe a familiar impasse?
