I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether higher-level explanations in science are genuinely autonomous or just convenient shorthand for the underlying physics. The conversation kept circling back to a specific example: the renormalization group in condensed matter physics and the phenomenon of universality — the fact that systems with radically different microphysical constituents (a ferromagnet and a liquid at its critical point, say) exhibit identical critical exponents and belong to the same universality class. My friend, who is a fairly hard-nosed reductionist, insisted that the renormalization group is just a mathematical technique for coarse-graining: it shows you how irrelevant microscopic details wash out when you zoom out, but nothing about the higher-level regularities is ontologically autonomous — it's all still governed by the underlying Hamiltonian, and the fact that many different Hamiltonians flow to the same fixed point is just a fact about the dynamics of parameter space, not a discovery of a new kind of causation. I pushed back, arguing that this way of describing things gets the epistemology backward: the universality classes are the robust, stable features, and the microscopic details are the fragile ones that vary while the macro-level pattern stays fixed. If you want to explain why the critical exponents are what they are, you don't appeal to the microphysical details — you appeal to symmetries, dimensionality, and the structure of the fixed point itself, which are irreducibly macro-level considerations. The argument got detailed enough that we were drawing renormalization-group flow diagrams on a napkin, and we never reached a resolution.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters — call them Mira (the reductionist) and Jules (the emergentist) — who are having roughly this argument. They should both be competent and well-informed: neither should be a strawman. Jules should not simply appeal to mystery or "more is different" as a slogan; they should make the case that the renormalization group provides a genuine example of ontological emergence, where the higher-level pattern is not merely a summary of the lower-level dynamics but exerts a kind of top-down constraint. Mira should not simply dismiss emergence as hand-waving; they should offer a serious account of how universality is fully compatible with a reductionist picture — perhaps by distinguishing epistemological emergence from ontological emergence, or by arguing that the explanatory autonomy of the fixed point is just a reflection of our ignorance about which microphysical details happen to be relevant rather than a fact about the world's causal structure. The dialogue should have real back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other's actual points rather than talking past each other. It should get into the specifics of the renormalization group — the flow toward fixed points, the distinction between relevant and irrelevant operators, the idea that the macroscopic theory is the one you get by integrating out short-wavelength degrees of freedom — but it should remain accessible to someone who has encountered these ideas at the level of, say, an undergraduate philosophy of physics course. The goal is not to resolve the argument but to show why it's a genuinely hard one, where both sides have resources that the other has to take seriously.
