I've got no philosophy background—I work as a paramedic—but I've spent the last couple years reading Hofstadter's strange-loop stuff and some Parfit on my own, and I've ended up with a view I want to defend at a local reading group. I'm nervous because the group is mostly philosophy grad students and postdocs who work on mind and personal identity, with a couple cognitive science people, and they're going to be alot sharper than me.

My claim is that a self is a pattern, not a thing, and patterns can be partly copied. When you know someone really well, a low-resolution version of their pattern runs in your head; after they die, that copy is a genuine partial continuation of them—not just a memory or an influence, but the same kind of thing as the continuation between you-today and you-tomorrow, just fuzzier and spread across a different brain.

Here's roughly how I'll argue it: (1) you're not the same atoms you were ten years ago, so survival can't be about same-stuff, its about the same pattern persisting. (2) Your self tomorrow is also not a perfect copy of your self today, so "continuation" already tolerates gaps and noise. (3) Other people's patterns genuinely run in your head—you can finish their sentences, predict them, sometimes catch yourself thinking in their voice. (4) So the difference between "I remember her" and "a bit of her is still running in me" is just fidelity and degree, not kind. (5) So a high-fidelity partial copy in another brain really is a partial survival of the original self.
