You're a working epistemologist who actually does speech act theory, not someone who files assertion under propositional attitudes. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the data points before you organize them into requirements — I want to see the structure, not just the final list.

Before I read any more on the norm of assertion, I want the full spec: what does a theory of the norm of assertion have to deliver to count as adequate? Adequate for what: for explaining ordinary conversational practice — which assertions are proper and which arn't, why we challenge and retract them in the ways we do, and how assertion passes information between people. Not a complete theory of language; just good enough that the standard cases fall out without epicycles.

Give me the desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each. Include at least:

- the phenomena it must explain and the cases it must get right, and which of those are genuine data rather than artifacts of some camp's pet theory;
- the distinctions it can't collapse: content asserted vs. act of asserting, propriety vs. blamelessness, flat-out vs. hedged assertion, the norm of assertion vs. norms of belief or action, violating the norm vs. being merely conversationally odd, and the norm of assertion vs. merely prudential reasons to make the speech act (being paid to say p doesn't make the assertion proper as an assertion);
- the results it has to respect — closure under conjunction, the way testimony transmits knowledge, whatever you think are actual constraints.

Make sure you hit these pressure points and say for each whether it's a hard requirement or a negotiable preference: assertions of the p but I don't believe p and p but I don't know p forms; lottery propositions like my ticket lost in a large fair lottery; selfless assertion, where someone with excellent evidence asserts p while sincerely disbelieving it; Gettiered assertions (justified true belief where the justification is disconnected from the truth) and lucky true guesses; the challenge how do you know and the retraction I guess I shouldn't have said that; the contrast between asserting one lottery proposition and asserting the giant conjunction of all of them; apparent stakes-sensitivity in what you may flat-out assert; and what hedging does (probably p, I think p) to the propriety conditions.

Then the part I most want: tell me which subsets of these desiderata can't be jointly satisfied by a single invariant norm. I suspect the lottery data and the selfless-assertion data are in tension with each other, but show me exactly where. And say which single requirement does the most to knock out the main contenders.

Finally give me a short scorecard — a few sentences each, a table is fine — for the truth norm, the belief norm, the justification or reasonable-belief norm, the knowledge norm, and one contextualist or relativist variant against your list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their cases, not the scorecard; I'll do the scoring myself, I just need the list to be doing real work.

One more thing: if some requirement on your list is really asking a norm to do something norms generally don't do — e.g. make every improper assertion blameworthy — flag it and tell me its a bad desideratum rather than a real one.
