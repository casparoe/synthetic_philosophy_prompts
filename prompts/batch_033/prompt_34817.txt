I recently came across the scholarly discussion of Frege's political diary from 1924, and it has unsettled something I had long taken for granted. I used to think the question of whether a philosopher's life bears on their work had a straightforward answer — something like "evaluate the arguments, not the person." But reading about Frege has made me realize my own intuitions vary from case to case in ways I cannot currently justify. I am trying to work out what principle I actually hold, by tabulating my verdicts on specific cases and asking you to extract the rule behind them, if one exists.

Here are my verdicts:

**1. Frege.** The diary, written late in his life, is a sustained expression of anti-Semitism, anti-democratic politics, and admiration for Hitler and Ludendorff. It is repellent. But Frege's philosophical achievement — the Begriffsschrift, the logicist program in the Grundlagen, the sense–reference distinction — seems to me untouched. I cannot locate any point at which the politics enters the logic. My verdict: the diary does not bear on his philosophical work.

**2. Heidegger.** His Nazi party membership began in 1933, and Being and Time was published in 1927. I am prepared to say the politics does not bear on Being and Time taken on its own. But in the later work — the rectoral address, the Introduction to Metaphysics, the essays on technology — I feel the politics and the philosophy are not merely simultaneous; they share a deep structure. The anti-modernism, the talk of destiny and rootedness and the forgetting of Being, all seem to me to do philosophical work that is also political work. My verdict: the politics bears on the later philosophy but not on Being and Time.

**3. Carl Schmitt.** His concept of the political — sovereignty as the power to declare the exception, the friend–enemy distinction as the essence of politics — was developed in explicit service of the Nazi legal order after 1933, and even his pre-1933 work on dictatorship was laying the groundwork. I do not see how one could separate the philosophy from the politics here even in principle. My verdict: the politics bears on the work entirely; the distinction collapses.

**4. Rousseau.** He abandoned all five of his children to foundling hospitals and later wrote Émile, one of the most influential works on child-rearing in the history of ideas. That is staggering hypocrisy. But I cannot identify a philosophical claim in Émile that is falsified or logically compromised by the biographical fact. My verdict: the biography discredits the man but does not bear on the content of the philosophy — though I notice it changes how I read the text, infusing it with an irony I would otherwise miss.

**5. Paul de Man.** As a young journalist in occupied Belgium he wrote anti-Semitic literary columns; decades later he became a central figure in deconstruction. I do not think those early articles directly infect the theoretical arguments in Allegories of Reading. Yet I find myself unable to say the biography is irrelevant. The question it raises is not whether deconstruction is false but whether its characteristic moves — the resistance to fixed meaning, the insistence that determinate moral judgment is always premature — might hold a particular appeal for someone with de Man's history, and whether that fact should alter the scrutiny we bring to the theory. My verdict: the biography does not refute the theory, but it does bear on how the theory should be evaluated, because it gives us reason to ask whether the theory's appeal is partly the wrong kind of appeal.

**6. (The case I keep returning to.)** A colleague of mine, a philosopher of mind, holds an uncompromising eliminativism about folk psychology. In conversation he has said things I find chilling — that grief is just a pattern of neural firing we tell ourselves a story about, that remorse is an adaptive illusion, that we would be better off without the vocabulary of belief and desire. His published papers are technical, rigorous, and show none of this. But I cannot shake the suspicion that what attracts him to the view, and what makes him untroubled by what it seems to imply, is a kind of interpersonal coldness that the papers are designed not to display. My verdict: I do not know whether this bears on his philosophy or not, and I need a principle to tell me.

What I am asking for is a principle — or a small set of viable candidates — that captures as many of these six verdicts as possible. State it precisely. For each clause, say which case or cases motivate it. Then apply the principle to my colleague and tell me what it says. Finally, tell me honestly: which of my six verdicts, if any, must be overturned if the principle is to be coherent? If the principle can accommodate all six only by growing baroque and gerrymandered, say so, and offer the cleaner alternative that overturns the fewest verdicts.
