In most democratic countries, the state spends considerable sums to make voting possible — polling stations, mail-in ballots, voter registration drives — and public figures routinely describe casting a ballot as a civic duty, not merely a right. School curricula teach children that good citizens vote, and pundits treat low turnout as evidence of democratic malaise. Yet the paradox of voting, articulated most sharply in the rational-choice literature, suggests that for a purely instrumentally rational individual, the expected utility of voting is effectively zero: the probability that a single ballot will decide an election is so infinitesimal that even trivial costs — waiting in line, reading a voter guide, finding parking — should swamp the expected benefit. The practice of mass electoral participation, and the elaborate normative architecture built around it, thus appears to rest on something other than the straightforward instrumental calculus that governs most other individual decisions in a market society.

I am trying to understand what theory of practical reason — what account of what it means to act rightly or well — this entire institutional and rhetorical apparatus presupposes. Does the practice of encouraging and valorizing voting implicitly reject the instrumental conception of rationality that generates the paradox? If so, what does it replace it with? Is it committed to a Kantian view on which voting expresses autonomy or equal standing regardless of consequences, to a rule-consequentialist view on which one should follow the rule "vote" even though no single act makes a difference, to an expressive view on which voting is a performative act whose point is not causal but symbolic, or to something else? And how much of this is strictly presupposed — such that abandoning the theory would force a revision of the practice — versus merely compatible with it, so that several rival theories could underwrite the same institutions?

I am also struck by what looks like an inconsistency. The same political culture that treats voting as a special obligation treats most other forms of political participation — attending a protest, writing to a legislator, donating to a campaign — in broadly instrumental terms: you do them if they are likely to make a difference, and no one calls you a bad citizen for skipping a rally. If the practice around voting really does presuppose a non-instrumental theory of political action, why does that theory not extend to these other acts? And if it does not, what feature of voting — its formal equality, its secrecy, its connection to authorization — singles it out? I would like a reconstruction of the implicit theory, a diagnosis of whether the asymmetry is defensible, and an account of what would have to change in our electoral practices or civic rhetoric if we took the instrumental logic of the paradox seriously and stopped treating voting as a sui generis moral category.
