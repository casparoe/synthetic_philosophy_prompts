**Exam question:** "Explain Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment and the argument it supports. What does the argument purport to establish about the nature of meaning and mental content? Then discuss one significant objection to the position Putnam defends, and evaluate whether that objection succeeds."

**Student's answer:** "Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment asks us to imagine a planet identical to Earth in almost every respect, except that the clear, potable liquid that fills its lakes and falls as rain is not H₂O but a different chemical compound, XYZ. On both Earth and Twin Earth, speakers use the word 'water' to refer to this liquid, and in 1750 — before the development of modern chemistry — the mental states of Earthlings and Twin Earthlings with respect to 'water' were indistinguishable: they had the same beliefs, the same perceptual experiences, and the same linguistic dispositions. Putnam argues that despite this internal identity, the word 'water' as used on Earth refers to H₂O, while 'water' as used on Twin Earth refers to XYZ. The extension of the term differs because of a difference in the external environment, not because of any difference in the speakers' heads. This is the core of Putnam's argument for semantic externalism: meanings are not determined solely by internal psychological states. Putnam further argues that this externalism extends to mental content itself — what we think about when we think about water depends on the actual substance in our environment, so the content of our thoughts is also externally determined. Putnam's argument draws on the same basic picture as Kripke's causal theory of reference: a term's reference is fixed by an initial baptism and then passed along a causal-historical chain, and what matters for meaning is the actual nature of the stuff at the end of that chain, not what speakers happen to believe about it. One major objection to Putnam's view comes from internalists who argue that Twin Earth is not a counterexample to the claim that mental content determines reference. The internalist can say that the content of 'water'-thoughts on Earth and Twin Earth is actually different because it includes a relational component — e.g., 'the liquid that actually fills the lakes around here' — and this relational content is fully internal. Putnam's response is that this misses the point: the relational content still depends on which liquid is actually there, and that fact is not in the head. I think the internalist objection has some force because it shows that Putnam may have underestimated how much content can be built into internal states, but ultimately the externalist conclusion stands: you cannot determine what someone is thinking about just by looking inside their head."
