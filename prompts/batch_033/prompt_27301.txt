I run a monthly ideas salon — bit of a pretentious name, but it's basically thirty people in a pub back room, some philosophy grads, mostly curious laypeople, and me, a science journalist who got bored of writing about CRISPR and decided to inflict moral philosophy on my friends. Next month's session is on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism — the idea that impartial moral theories demand 'one thought too many' of us, alienating us from our own deepest commitments. I've read the stuff, I think I understad it, but I've never had to teach it.

I need a plan for the evening. Not the explanation itself — I'll do the talking — but the architecture of it. What has to go first so the rest makes sense? I'm thinking I need to get them to feel the pull of utilitarianism before I let Williams tear it down, otherwise they'll just shrug and say 'well obviously you save your wife.' But maybe that's wrong. What's the one example or case that should carry the whole thing — the drowning wife, or something less freighted? What misconceptions should I expect? I'm guessing people will conflate 'integrity' with just being stubborn, or think Williams is saying utilitarianism gets the wrong answer rather than that it gives the right answer for the wrong reasons. Where in the sequence do I head those off? And what's the one question I can ask at the end to know whether they've actually got it, rather than just nodding along beacuse the beer is good?
