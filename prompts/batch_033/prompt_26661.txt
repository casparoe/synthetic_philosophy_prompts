I spent my career in biochemistry, and in retirement I have been reading the philosophical literature on predictive processing — Clark, Hohwy, Seth, and the broader free energy principle framework. One analogy I encounter repeatedly, in both academic and popular writing, is that perception is "controlled hallucination."

As I understand the claim: the brain is a hierarchical generative model that issues top-down predictions about the causes of sensory input. What we experience as perception simply is the brain's current best prediction, held in check — "controlled" — by bottom-up prediction-error signals. On this picture, ordinary perception and clinical hallucination are not different kinds of process; they lie on a single continuum, differing only in how tightly sensory signals constrain the predictions. The analogy is not ornamental. It is used to warrant substantive conclusions: that there is no sharp line between veridical and non-veridical experience, that the external world plays a much smaller role in determining perceptual content than common sense supposes, and that naive realism about perception is untenable.

I find the analogy striking, but I am unsure how seriously to take it. I would like you to:

1. Provide an explicit mapping. In ordinary perception, what corresponds to the generative model, the top-down prediction, the prediction error, and the "control" — and what, if anything, plays the role of the hallucination's defining absence of an external object?

2. Identify the disanalogies. What features of a clinical hallucination does the analogy quietly import that normal perception does not share? Which of these disanalogies matter for the philosophical conclusions the analogy is meant to support, and which are harmless?

3. Assess whether the conclusions survive. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the claim that perception and hallucination differ only in degree hold up? Does the analogy genuinely support a demotion of the external world's role in perception, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion from the start?

4. If the controlled-hallucination analogy is misleading, propose a better one — or explain why no analogy of this sort can do the work being asked of it.
