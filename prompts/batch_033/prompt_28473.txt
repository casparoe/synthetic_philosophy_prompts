I recently found myself in a drawn-out argument where a participant kept insisting that every inferential move be stated as an additional premise before he would grant the conclusion, and each time I supplied the premise, the demand simply reappeared one step further back. A friend told me this was precisely the situation Lewis Carroll dramatised in his 1895 dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," and directed me to the following passage. In it, Achilles has already persuaded the Tortoise to accept (A) "Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other," (B) "The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same," and (C) "If A and B are true, Z must be true." He now tries to force the Tortoise to accept (Z) "The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other":

"You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."

"And why must I?"

"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

"Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it

(D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true.

Have you entered that in your note-book?"

I think I can see that the Tortoise is doing something illegitimate, but I cannot state precisely what it is, and I am unsure how much the dialogue actually shows about the justification of deduction. Please help me by responding in the following four sections:

"The diagnosis" (about 200 words): explain precisely what the Tortoise is doing that generates the regress — in particular, whether he is confusing a premise with a rule of inference, refusing to perform an inference, or something else — and why Achilles's move of adding (D) cannot halt the regress.

3. "What the passage does and does not show" (about 200 words): state how much this dialogue settles about the justification of deduction. Does it show that deductive inference rests on something that cannot itself be formulated as a premise? Does it show that deduction cannot be justified? Distinguish the claim about what rules of inference are from any claim about whether we are entitled to use them.
