After forty years in condensed matter physics, I retired last year and have been reading philosophy of science — something I had always meant to do. One thing that now strikes me as philosophically puzzling is a practice I never questioned while I was working: the way thermodynamics and statistical mechanics coexist as distinct subjects. In every physics department I have been in, they are taught in separate courses, by different faculty, often using textbooks that barely acknowledge each other. Thermodynamics is presented as a self-contained theory with its own laws (the Zeroth through Third), its own concepts (temperature, entropy, free energy), and its own explanatory strategies — Carnot cycles, Maxwell relations, thermodynamic potentials. Statistical mechanics is taught later, sometimes years later, and it is supposed to show why the thermodynamic laws hold, by deriving them from the statistical behavior of microstates. But the derivation never replaces the thermodynamic concepts; it only connects them to statistical ones. In research, the separation persists: a paper that solves a problem using thermodynamic reasoning alone is considered complete and publishable; no one demands a statistical-mechanical derivation as a condition of acceptance. And some problems — phase transitions in the thermodynamic limit, for instance — seem to require thermodynamic concepts that do not have a straightforward statistical-mechanical translation without taking the infinite limit, which is a mathematical idealization, not a physical fact about any finite system.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Specifically, does it presuppose that thermodynamic properties are emergent in a sense stronger than mere epistemic convenience — that is, that the thermodynamic description captures something real that is not fully present in the statistical-mechanical description of any finite system? Or is the practice compatible with a thoroughgoing reductionism, according to which thermodynamics is in principle reducible to statistical mechanics, and the practice of treating them as separate is purely a matter of computational tractability and pedagogical tradition? If the latter, what does "in principle" reducibility amount to here, given that the practice — the actual scientific activity of the community — treats the two levels as genuinely autonomous explanatory enterprises? And finally: if we were to take the reductionist view seriously and reorganize the practice accordingly — teaching statistical mechanics first, treating thermodynamics as a derived approximation, and requiring that thermodynamic claims be justified by statistical-mechanical arguments — what would be lost, and would that loss be merely practical or would it be conceptual?
