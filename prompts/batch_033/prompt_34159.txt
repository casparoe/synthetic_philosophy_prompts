For more than four decades, a curious impasse has persisted among economists, philosophers, and computer scientists working on the foundations of rational belief. In 1976, Robert Aumann proved that two agents who share a common prior probability distribution and whose posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge cannot have different posteriors — they cannot "agree to disagree." The proof is elegant, the result is uncontroversial as mathematics, and yet the debate about what the theorem means for actual human disagreement has barely moved since the 1980s.

One camp — call them the importers — argues that the theorem has real normative bite: if two people persist in disagreeing after full discussion, at least one of them must be irrational, or they must have started from different priors for which neither has a justification they can give the other. On this view, the theorem gives us reason to treat intractable disagreement as a sign that something has gone wrong epistemically. The other camp — call them the deflationists — counters that the theorem's conditions are so demanding (common priors, common knowledge of posteriors, perfect Bayesian updating) that real-world disagreement tells us nothing about anyone's rationality. The theorem, they say, is a piece of formal mathematics that simply does not engage with the messy realities of human belief.

Both sides agree on the proof. Both agree on the conditions. Both agree that real people are not ideally rational Bayesian agents. And yet the argument has stalled. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about the word "rational"? Is it a disagreement about how far an idealization can be stretched before it loses all contact with the phenomenon it was meant to illuminate? Is one side asking a normative question — what should we infer from the persistence of disagreement? — and the other a descriptive one — what actually causes it? Or is there a hidden empirical claim buried in the importer's position, about how closely real deliberators approximate the theorem's conditions, that could in principle be tested but never has been? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new formal result, a conceptual distinction, an empirical study, or simply abandoning the question as ill-posed?
