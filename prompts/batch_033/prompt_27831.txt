Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Mira and Kael, who disagree about whether infinite ethics poses a genuine, unresolved problem for consequentialist moral reasoning — or whether the difficulty is largely an artifact of how we set up the models and dissolves under careful analysis.

Mira's position: She takes the problem seriously. If the universe is spatially infinite, or if the future is temporally infinite and our actions can affect its trajectory, then the total amount of value or disvalue at stake is infinite. Standard expected-value reasoning then seems to break down: any finite contribution is zero relative to an infinite total, so all actions come out formally indifferent — a result she calls infinitarian paralysis. She thinks this is not just a technical curiosity; it matters for longtermist reasoning about existential risk, because if we want to argue that reducing extinction risk is overwhelmingly important, we need a decision theory that can coherently compare actions when infinite value is in play. She is skeptical that ad hoc fixes — like only comparing actions within a finite region — can be justified without begging the question.

Kael's position: He thinks the problem is overblown and largely dissolves when we attend to the structure of actual decision problems. We never directly compare infinite totalities; we compare the causal consequences of actions, which are finite differences in finite regions. He argues that the cases where infinities seem to cause trouble are precisely those where we have improperly specified the decision problem — for instance, by using an unbounded utility function over an infinite domain without a well-defined way to aggregate. He draws an analogy to the St. Petersburg paradox: just as that puzzle does not undermine expected utility theory in finite cases, infinite cases do not undermine consequentialism in the finite decisions we actually face. When infinities do enter, as in existential-risk reasoning, he thinks they are handled by comparing actions under uncertainty about whether the future is infinite, and under reasonable probabilistic assumptions the infinities cancel.

The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: each character must respond to the other's specific arguments rather than talking past them. Neither side should be a strawman; both should make their best case and concede points when the other lands a fair blow. The dialogue should explore at least the following pressure points: whether "compare causal consequences, not total states" actually solves the problem or merely relocates it; whether uncertainty about infinity genuinely cancels the trouble or simply replaces one kind of divergence with another; and whether the St. Petersburg analogy holds up or obscures a disanalogy.

Limit the dialogue to no more than 1,200 words.
