I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I serve on my country's Climate Futures Commission, an independent statutory body that advises parliament on long-term emissions policy. We are now drafting our formal recommendation on the emissions pathway for the next three decades. The commission's economists have framed the choice in terms of avoiding future harm: the aggressive decarbonization pathway, they argue, prevents damage to the health, livelihoods, and environments of our citizens' descendants. But one commissioner — a political theorist — has raised a version of the nonidentity problem. She argues that the emissions pathway we choose will, over a generation, alter where people live, whom they meet, when they have children, and which children they have, so that the specific individuals who exist in 2100 under the aggressive pathway will be entirely different from those who would exist under the moderate pathway. If so, she contends, we cannot coherently say the moderate pathway harms those future people, since those particular individuals would not have existed at all under the alternative, and therefore cannot be worse off than they would otherwise have been. The commission is split. Some find this a decisive objection to the harm-based justification for aggressive action; others find it a philosophical curiosity with no legitimate bearing on a policy recommendation. I need to draft the formal recommendation memo. It should recommend a specific emissions target and a concrete timeframe, and it must engage the nonidentity objection seriously — not dismiss it, not sidestep it, but show why it does or does not defeat the case for aggressive action. Provide the memo.
