I have been trying on my own to work out when a person's "yes" to sex really counts. I have collected my reactions to a set of cases, and they feel mostly consistent to me, but I cannot state the rule behind them. I would like to know whether there is a single principle that captures my verdicts, or nearly all of them, and which of my verdicts a clean principle would force me to give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A man tells a woman he is a surgeon at a large hospital. He actually works in a warehouse. She would not have slept with him had she known. I think her yes did not count.

2. A married man tells a woman he is single and looking for a serious relationship. She sleeps with him on that basis. I think her yes did not count.

3. A man tells a woman he loves her in order to get her into bed. He does not love her. I think he has done something wrong, but I do not think her yes failed to count: she agreed to the sex she actually had, with the person she actually had it with.

4. A man tells a woman he is 32 when he is 34. She sleeps with him. I think her yes counted, though the lie was unnecessary and dishonest.

5. A boss tells an employee he finds her attractive and that she is "exactly the sort of person who gets promoted here." She is afraid for her promotion and sleeps with him. I think her yes did not count.

6. A boyfriend tells his girlfriend that he will end the relationship if they do not start having sex. She agrees because she does not want to lose him. I think her yes counted, even though I think less of him for saying it.

7. A man tells a woman that if she does not sleep with him, he will post an embarrassing video of her online. She agrees. I think her yes did not count.

8. A woman is so drunk that she can barely stand, but she is conscious and says yes. I think her yes did not count.

9. A man is drunk enough to be louder and more confident than usual, but he knows where he is, who he is with, and what he is doing, and he says yes. I think his yes counted.

10. A young woman with a mild intellectual disability lives semi-independently and says, clearly and repeatedly, that she wants to have sex with her boyfriend. I think her yes counted, though I am less certain here than I would like to be.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this. A man tells a woman he has had a vasectomy. He has not. They agree to have sex without a condom, which she would not have agreed to otherwise. Part of me says this is like the surgeon lie or the marriage lie: the agreement rested on a falsehood about something that mattered to her, so her yes did not count. Another part says she consented to the sex itself and the lie is only about a possible consequence, so her yes counted—but then I cannot say why the surgeon lie is different, since that is also a lie about something outside the act itself.

I can see at least two places where my verdicts may be hard to reconcile. Cases 1 and 2 say that some lies undo a person's yes, while cases 3 and 4 say that other lies do not, and I have no way of drawing that line. Cases 5 and 6 are both pressure from someone who has something the person wants, yet I treat them differently, and again I cannot say why.

What I would like is this: a principle, stated precisely but in ordinary language, that gets as many of these ten as possible; an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; and the principle's answer to the vasectomy case, plus any further case you think shows what I am actually committed to.
