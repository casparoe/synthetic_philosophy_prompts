I am a graduate student writing a paper on the ethics of aggregation, and I keep running into a slogan I want to get clear on before I commit myself. In seminar discussions of rescue cases — cases in which an agent can save either one stranger or five strangers, with no special obligations to any of them — a participant will dismiss the claim that the agent should save the five by saying that "the numbers don't count." The slogan is always said as if it names a single, decisive objection: the bare fact that five is more than one is supposed to settle nothing. But I have begun to suspect that the phrase is doing several different jobs at once, and that different speakers mean quite different things by it.

What are the distinct precise claims that "the numbers don't count" could be making in this debate? Lay them out as a menu of readings. For each reading, specify the exact thesis, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or outright false, and indicate what it would imply about a rescue case. Then identify which reading, or readings, actually support using the slogan to block the inference from "there are more people on this side" to "you are required to save this side," and note which readings people who deploy the slogan in that context are most likely to have in mind. I want the readings separated and assessed rather than a single interpretation defended as the real one.
