I've been thinking about this ever since I stopped liking a band I used to love. I genuinely can't tell if my taste changed (the music sounds the same but I'm just not into it anymore) or if the music itself sounds different to me now—like I'm literally hearing it differently. And I got to wondering, is there even a fact of the matter about which it is? That led me back to Dennett's Chase and Sanborn case, which I read in a philosophy of mind class last semester, and now I've built myself an argument that seems to show qualia are incoherent, and I can't find the hole in it.

Here's the argument:

(1) If qualia—private, intrinsic, directly-known properties of experience—are real, then for any shift in how someone responds to a stimulus, there must be a determinate fact about whether the qualia changed or whether only the person's reactive attitudes (preferences, standards, judgments) changed.

(2) Consider Chase and Sanborn: both used to enjoy Maxwell House coffee and now find it unpleasant. Chase says his experience tastes the same but his standards have gotten more sophisticated; Sanborn says his standards are exactly the same but the coffee tastes different to him now. Both are sincere, both claim direct introspective certainty.

(3) No possible investigation—introspective, behavioral, or neuroscientific—could determine which of the two is correct. (If you point to brain scans, ask: how would you calibrate which neural pattern corresponds to "qualia changed vs. standards changed" without begging the question?)

(4) If a purported fact cannot be settled even in principle by any method, then it's not a genuine fact.

(5) So there's no fact about whether Chase's qualia stayed constant or changed.

(6) But if there's no fact about qualia constancy vs. change in this case, then qualia lack the very determinacy that the concept requires.

(7) And what goes for Chase and Sanborn goes for qualia in general: there are no determinate facts about qualia.

(8) That's an absurd conclusion—it means there's no fact about whether I'm in pain or just reacting aversively, whether the sunset looks red to me or I just think it does.

I feel like I've been cornered by my own reasoning. Where does this argument go wrong? Which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'm especially curious whether the problem is in step (3)—can neuroscience actually settle it?—or step (4)—is that just verificationism and should I reject it?—or maybe the whole setup is confused in some way I'm not seeing.
