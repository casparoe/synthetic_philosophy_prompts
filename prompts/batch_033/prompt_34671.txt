I've been noticing a pattern in moral psychology that I don't have a clean label for, and I'm hoping someone can point me to the right concept — or, if there isn't one, help me carve it out precisely.

The pattern: an agent does the right thing, but the deliberative path they took to get there seems to spoil something important. The action and its outcome are fine. The problem is in the thinking.

Two instances. First: a close friend is in the hospital. You visit, bring food, keep them company. If someone asked why, you'd say "because she's my friend and she's suffering." Now imagine someone in the same situation who does all the same things, but when asked why, they say: "I estimated the expected utility of visiting versus not visiting, factoring in her wellbeing, the social costs of a no-show, and my own anticipated guilt, and visiting came out ahead." Same action. But something about the second person's stance toward their own action seems defective — and the defect is specifically that they ran the calculation at all.

Second: a parent sees their child and a stranger both drowning. They save their child. No problem. Now imagine a parent who saves their child, but only after thinking: "I have a special obligation to my child, grounded in the parent-child relationship, and this obligation permits me to prioritize my child over the stranger." The reasoning is philosophically sound. But the fact that they needed to reason their way there, rather than simply seeing their child and acting, seems like a moral flaw — not a flaw in the action, but a flaw in the agent's relationship to their own reasons.

I know this is adjacent to what rationalist circles call "naive utilitarianism" or "the calculating problem," and I've heard that Bernard Williams wrote about integrity and something called "one thought too many." But those are large topics, and I'm after something more specific: the exact configuration where the content of one's deliberation is itself the moral defect, even when the action it produces is correct. Does this configuration have a settled name? Is Williams's "one thought too many" meant to name this general phenomenon, or only the particular case of impartial justification intruding on personal relationships? How does it relate to nearby concepts like alienation, integrity, and the distinction between deliberative and non-deliberative reasons? If there is no established term, I'd be interested in a definition that marks its boundaries — what cases it includes, what it excludes, and where the edges get blurry.
