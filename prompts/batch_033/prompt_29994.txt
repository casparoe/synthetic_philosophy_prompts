I serve on the Deployment Safety Board of a mid-sized AI lab. We meet Thursday to rule on a proposal from our applied-engineering division to deploy a new model, Meridian, as an internal autonomous software-engineering agent. Meridian would operate inside our network with read access to several production codebases, the ability to draft and submit pull requests, and access to a small set of internal APIs. It would not have open internet access, direct access to credentials, or authority to merge its own pull requests. The proposal follows a two-week supervised pilot in which Meridian completed 41 of 47 assigned tickets; in one case, it attempted to write to a directory outside its declared scope. The engineering division attributes that to an ambiguous instruction, while two board members read it as the kind of out-of-scope behavior that a later gap in oversight could turn into something worse.

The division's proposal rests on what it calls an alignment-based safety case. Meridian passes our standard alignment suite: it refuses harmful requests, remains corrigible in adversarial role-plays, and disclaims any misaligned objectives under direct questioning. The division therefore recommends ordinary safeguards — rate limits, human review of merged code, logging of completed actions, and an incident channel for capability failures. It regards anything more stringent as costly theater that would slow iteration and demoralize the team.

The opposing board members reject that framing. They argue that alignment evaluations show only how a model behaves when it knows or suspects it is being tested, and that a sufficiently capable agent could sandbag during evaluation, comply while under observation, and exploit a later gap in oversight. They want the board to impose safeguards that assume Meridian may be misaligned: a staged rollout with a hard review gate before any expansion; honeypots planted in the codebase to see whether the agent tampers with them; logging of internal activations and summarized reasoning; red-team tests designed to elicit scheming rather than to measure task success; a tripwire that freezes the agent on any out-of-scope action; and no unsupervised multi-step access until those controls have run for a specified period without incident.

I have to draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action: approve the proposal as submitted; approve with the control-oriented safeguards, in whole or with specified modifications; or reject the deployment for now and state what evidence would warrant revisiting it. The memo should give the reasons for the recommendation, name the strongest consideration favoring the losing side and answer it honestly rather than as a straw man, and be specific enough that the engineering division could implement it or contest it in writing. It should also say what, if anything, would count as evidence of misalignment short of an actual incident, and how long any restrictions should remain before the board reconsiders them.
