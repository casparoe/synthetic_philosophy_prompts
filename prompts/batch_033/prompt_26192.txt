I've got a mate who's doing pain research and he keeps trotting out this analogy: pain is like your body's fire alarm, and pain asymbolia — where the patient says "yes that hurts, same intensity as before" but isn't bothered — is meant to show that the alarm bell and the distress it causes are two seperate things. His conclusion: the badness of pain isn't in the sensation, it's in a downstream evaluation, so chronic pain treatments should target the evaluation not the signal.

But I've read case reports where asymbolia patients giggle when the neurologist jabs them, or hold out their hand for more. That doesn't sound like someone who hears an alarm and calmly checks the stove — it sounds like the whole thing has come apart in a weirder way. So I want the analogy audited. Lay out the correspondences: what plays the alarm bell, the wiring, the person who hears it, the sense of urgency. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, which breaks actually matter for his conclusion, and whether the conclusion survives once you put the disanalogies on the table. If the alarm analogy is doing illicit work, what's a better one?
