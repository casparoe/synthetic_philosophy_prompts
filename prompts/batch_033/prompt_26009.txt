I've been working through the mind-body problem on my own, mostly from popular books and online resources, and I recently read a chapter that summarizes the philosophical zombie argument against physicalism. The summary went roughly like this:

"Chalmers argues that zombies — beings physically identical to human beings in every respect, down to the last neuron, but who have no conscious experience whatsoever — are conceivable. And in philosophy, conceivability is a guide to possibility: if you can coherently conceive of something, that's reason to think it's genuinely possible. So if zombies are conceivable, they're possible. But if zombies are possible, physicalism is false, because physicalism claims that all facts supervene on physical facts — fix the physical facts and you've fixed everything. A zombie world has all the same physical facts as our world but different facts about consciousness, so physicalism can't be true. The standard reply, which most philosophers accept, is that zombies only seem conceivable because we're not imagining the physical facts in enough detail. Once you fully imagine a brain in all its physical complexity, you'll see that consciousness isn't something extra you can just subtract — the experience comes with the physical structure."

I've since read a bit more — some SEP entries, a few blog posts by philosophers — and I've picked up enough to suspect that this summary flattens some important distinctions, especially around the conceivability-to-possibility step. I gather that not all kinds of conceivability are supposed to entail possibility, and that Chalmers has a much more careful framework than "whatever you can imagine is possible." I also have a vague sense that the objection at the end — "you're just not imagining the physics in enough detail" — isn't the knockdown reply the book presents it as, and that the debate is still very much alive. But I don't have the training to sort out exactly which parts of the summary are fair, which are misleading, and whether the book's bottom line — that the argument fails — actually follows from what's accurate in the summary. Could you walk me through it?
