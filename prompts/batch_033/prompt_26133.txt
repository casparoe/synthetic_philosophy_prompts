I'm a journalist who covers psychology and the replication crisis, and over the years I've developed a set of working views about how to handle evidence that I might be getting things wrong. I'm planning a long essay on the topic and want to make sure my position is internally consistent before I commit to it in print. Here is where I stand:

(1) Higher-order evidence — evidence that bears on the reliability of my own cognitive processes rather than directly on the target claim — always gives me a reason to adjust my confidence. If I learn I am sleep-deprived, or that I am reasoning about a domain where I have a known bias, I should be less confident in the conclusions I reach.

(2) Peer disagreement is a species of higher-order evidence. When someone I regard as roughly my equal in intelligence, background knowledge, and carefulness reaches a different conclusion from the same body of evidence, that tells me something about the likelihood that my own reasoning has gone wrong.

(3) Epistemic akrasia is irrational. I cannot coherently hold a belief while simultaneously believing that my total evidence does not support it. There is something structurally broken about the state of mind that says "P, but my evidence overall says not-P."

(4) In at least some cases of peer disagreement, the correct response is to conciliate — to move my credence substantially toward my peer's — while continuing to acknowledge that my first-order evidence, considered in isolation, genuinely supports my original view. I do not have to pretend the evidence looked different than it did.

(5) There should be a unified account of how we respond to higher-order evidence. The norms that govern my response to learning I am biased should be the same in structure as the norms that govern my response to learning you disagree with me. Carving out separate principles for each type of higher-order evidence would be ad hoc.

I would like you to go through these five commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. Then tell me what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency across the set. I do not have a favored fix — I want to know where the problem is before I decide what to give up.
