I've been thinking about the old rule — still found in plenty of style guides, legal writing, and everyday speech — that "he" is the correct gender-neutral pronoun when the antecedent is indefinite ("everyone should do his best"), and that "man" and "mankind" are perfectly fine as generic terms for the human species. I recently read Hofstadter's "Person Paper," where he satirises this by flipping the default to "white" and treating that as the supposedly inclusive term, which makes the absurdity of the move painfully obvious. But my question isn't about the satire. It's about the practice the satire is targeting.

What philosophy of language does that practice — using masculine forms as generics and insisting they're neutral — actually presuppose? What view of meaning, reference, connotation, or the relationship between language and thought does it only make sense under? Does it, for instance, presuppose that a word's meaning is just it's stipulated reference, so that if we all agree "he" is neutral, it is neutral? Does it presuppose that connotations and mental imagery are separable from denotation and don't affect cognition? Does it presuppose that the fact that one form is treated as the default and the other as the special case is a harmless grammatical quirk with no psychological consequences? And which rival views of language does it quietly rule out?

And here's a further puzzle. Some of the same institutions that used to enforce generic "he" now enforce "he or she" or ask you to reword the sentence to avoid the problem, but they still resist singular "they" as ungrammatical. What does this second practice presuppose that the first didn't, and what does it still presuppose that the Person Paper's implied view would reject?

I'm not after the standard feminist critique — I know the basic arguments about exclusion and invisibility. I want the underlying philosophy of language reconstructed: what tacit theory of how words mean and what they do to thinking is endorsed by the practice, and what would have to change in that theory if the practice were abandoned. If you think the question is ill-posed — that the practice doesn't presuppose any single coherent philosophy of language, or that the presuppositions depend on which defender you ask, or that the whole framing of "presuppositions" is the wrong tool here — say so plainly. Don't just give me a tidy answer to a messy question. And please write in British English.
