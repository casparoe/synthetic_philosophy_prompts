I am preparing a handout for an advanced undergraduate epistemology seminar on hinge epistemology, focused on Wittgenstein's On Certainty and the debate it has generated. Please write me a classification instrument: a decision tree, or a taxonomy in tree form if you find that cleaner, that sorts readers into one of six positions on the status of hinge certainties based only on their answers to concrete cases and probes.

The six leaves I want on the tree are these:

1. The non-propositional reading: hinge certainties are not truth-evaluable propositions at all; they are rules, norms, or ways of acting that make inquiry possible, so both knowledge-talk and doubt-talk fail to get a grip on them.
2. The entitlement reading: hinge certainties are truth-evaluable propositions that we may rationally accept or trust, but not on the basis of evidence; the positive epistemic status is a non-evidential entitlement, and it falls short of knowledge.
3. Ordinary-knowledge contextualism: hinge propositions can be known in everyday contexts but not under skeptical standards; Moore and the skeptic are each partly right, and the conflict between them is an artifact of shifting standards.
4. The dogmatic or proper-basic view: hinge propositions can be known immediately, or by the reliable operation of our faculties, without inference; Moore is simply right and the skeptic is misdescribing our situation.
5. Epistemic relativism: the rationality of hinge commitments is internal to a system or framework of evaluation, and rival hinge sets cannot be adjudicated from a neutral standpoint.
6. Skeptical invariantism: hinge propositions are truth-evaluable, and we lack knowledge or adequate justification for them; Moore's proof does not work.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names. First work out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the six positions within that space. Each branch node should be a question an undergraduate could answer without knowing which position it leads to. For any combination of answers that no position occupies, say briefly whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended.

At least these probes should appear among the questions: the Moorean hands case; the claim that the earth has existed for a long time; an arithmetic hinge such as 2+2=4; a cross-framework case in which a community's cosmology rests on a different hinge than the reader's; and a clinical case in which a person with advanced dementia denies a bodily hinge. Use each as a concrete scenario rather than asking students whether they identify with a label.

Aim for six to nine diagnostic questions. Each leaf should give the position's name, one sentence saying what it is, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. End with a short note flagging which leaves, if any, the tree cannot cleanly separate, and propose one further case that would do better.
