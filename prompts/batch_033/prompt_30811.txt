I'm the director of individual giving at a mid-size human rights nonprofit. Every campaign we run involves some mix of email subject lines, donation page design, suggested gift amounts, and canvasser scripts, and I keep having the same argument with my team: someone proposes a technique, someone else says it's manipulative, and the first person says "it's true and the donor can always say no, so its just persuasion." We already have a rule against outright lies and fake match offers. What I need is a decision procedure we can run in a planning meeting — under an hour, no philosopher in the room, ideally one page — to decide whether a technique is manipulation or legitimate persuasion. I sign off on every campaign, and the board's development committee reviews the big ones, so I need something I can defend to them in plain English. Donor trust is the main asset, and we also have to stay inside state fundraising rules and platform policies.

Please write the procedure as a checklist or ordered set of tests. For each step: the question we ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of manipulation and persuasion actually has to say, but keep the theory in the background; tell us which distinctions you're treating as load-bearing and which you're dropping for usability. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire.

Then run the procedure on these three:
1. A donation page where "give monthly" is pre-checked and "give once" is grayed out, with a real but time-limited match and a photo chosen because it's upsetting.
2. An email with the subject line "We need you" and a message built around guilt — people will suffer if we miss the goal — that contains no new information about the work.
3. A canvasser script that opens by asking the person to sign a small petition, then pivots to a donation ask, and tells canvassers to mirror the person's body language and use flattery.

I want to know which of these the procedure flags, which it lets through, and at least one case where it gives an answer my team won't like. And tell me what the procedure can't settle — the cases where two reasonable versions of it come apart.
