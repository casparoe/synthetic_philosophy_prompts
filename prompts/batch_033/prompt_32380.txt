I serve on the board of a mid-sized condominium association. We have a long-standing rule that no unit may be rented for fewer than thirty days without prior written approval from the board. The rule was adopted because short-term guests have produced noise complaints, damage to common areas, and gaps in our master insurance coverage. The penalty for a violation is a fine of two hundred dollars per day, continuing until the unauthorized rental stops.

Two weeks ago, an owner filed a formal, documented complaint against another owner. The complaint shows, and our property manager has confirmed, that the reported owner has been renting her unit through a short-term rental platform for at least five months, with dozens of separate guest stays. On the facts, this is an open-and-shut violation of the rule.

The complication is the complaining owner. Two years ago, she ran an unapproved short-term rental out of her own unit for roughly a year. It ended only because our insurance broker noticed it during a renewal review. The previous board never fined her or issued a formal warning; the matter was dropped, and she has never acknowledged that she did anything wrong. When a board member raised this history, she replied that she is not the one under complaint and that the board should simply do its job.

The board is now split. One side says the reported violation is documented and serious, and we have a duty to enforce the rule no matter who filed the complaint; declining to act because the messenger is unappealing would be a failure of governance, and the reported owner should not get a pass because someone else once got one. The other side says we should not let this owner use the board as her instrument: she broke the very rule she now demands we enforce against someone else, she was never held to account for it, and acting on her complaint would signal that the rules exist to be aimed at a neighbor once one's own turn is safely past.

We meet in ten days. I need to circulate a short written opinion that recommends a concrete course of action and a time frame, and that takes seriously the strongest version of the position we end up rejecting. Whatever you recommend, the memo should say plainly whether the complaining owner's own history is relevant to our decision, and why. The decision before us is whether to proceed against the reported owner on the basis of this complaint, to decline to act on the complaint while investigating the reported unit on the board's own initiative, or some third course. The board members are not lawyers or academics, so please reason from the situation itself rather than from philosophical or legal texts, and write in plain, formal American English.
