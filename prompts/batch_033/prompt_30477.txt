At our formal epistemology reading group last week, two members got into an argument about the Reflection principle that I have not been able to referee. The principle, as we were discussing it, says that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition should equal her current expectation of her future credence in that proposition: for any proposition A and any future time t, P_now(A | P_t(A) = r) = r whenever the conditional is defined, or equivalently P_now(A) = Σ_r r · P_now(P_t(A) = r). So if you are certain now that tomorrow you will be 0.9 confident it will rain, you should already be 0.9 confident it will rain.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Marta defended the principle as a requirement of diachronic rationality: an agent who violates it can be exploited by a bookie who offers her a sequence of bets over time, each fair by her own lights when she accepts it, and which together guarantee a net loss; being systematically exploitable is a mark of incoherence, and incoherence is a mark of irrationality. (2) Ravi replied that the Dutch strategy argument does not settle the matter, because the later bets are accepted by a future self who may be predictably irrational. If you now know that tonight you will be badly sleep-deprived, or that someone will feed you misleading evidence, you should not now align your credences with the confused credences you expect to have later; deferring to a future self you have reason to distrust is not rational. (3) Marta answered that this misreads the principle. Reflection, she said, only constrains you with respect to future credences that you now regard as the product of a rational process — future selves that have updated properly on their evidence. When you know your future self will be impaired or misled, that future self is not the kind of self the principle tells you to defer to. (4) Ravi said this response saves the principle only by draining it of content. Either Reflection is a purely formal coherence constraint, in which case the sleep-deprivation and misleading-evidence cases are genuine counterexamples; or it is a substantive claim about which future selves are trustworthy, in which case it is no longer a principle of rationality but an ordinary judgment about evidence and reliability. He then offered a cleaner case: suppose you now know a coin landed heads, and you know you will later take a drug that makes you forget the toss. Your future credence in heads will be one-half, and you now assign credence one to heads conditional on your future credence being one-half. That violates Reflection even though forgetting is not irrational and no one has been misled. (5) Marta granted that the forgetful agent violates the formula, but said this shows only that the formula needs a scope restriction — roughly, that the future self is expected to have no less information and no worse reasoning than one now has — not that the underlying norm is wrong. In the cases that matter, when you expect your future self to have more evidence, more time, or better reasoning, deferring to that future self is clearly what rationality requires, and Reflection is a partial formalization of that requirement. She accused Ravi of confusing an imperfect formalization with the norm it approximates. (6) Ravi replied that the norm she is left with does no work from the agent's point of view. To apply it, you first have to decide whether your future self will be a better judge than you are now; but that is exactly the hard question, and Reflection does not answer it. If the principle only says to defer to your future self when you already judge that your future self will be more rational, then it is not a constraint on rationality at all — it is a recommendation to do what you already think you should do.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of the six moves, say what is right in it and what is overstated; then say who is more right overall, and whether there is a version of Reflection — or a nearby principle — that survives the objections. I am particularly interested in whether the forgetful-agent case is fatal to Reflection as a rational requirement, or whether it only shows that the principle must be restricted, and whether any restriction can be stated without making the principle vacuous.
