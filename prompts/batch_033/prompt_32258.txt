I've been following the debate about whether human extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves — the question that Parfit opened up with his work on future generations and that Scheffler sharpened with his afterlife conjecture. Scheffler's central thought experiment asks us to imagine that, thirty days after our own death, an asteroid will destroy all human life. His claim is not merely that we would find this prospect saddening; it is that the knowledge would hollow out many of the activities we currently take to be worthwhile. We would lose our grip on why it matters to search for a cure for cancer, to write a novel, to fight for political change, or even to have children. The badness of extinction, on this picture, is not just the sum of lost future lives or the grief of the dying. It is that extinction would retroactively drain meaning from the lives of the last generation, because so many of our projects depend on an implicit assumption that humanity will continue after we are gone.

What frustrates me is that the debate has settled into a diagnostic standoff. One side says: you have described the psychological fact that people care about humanity's future, but caring about something does not make its loss a genuine harm — it could be an adaptive preference, a cognitive illusion, or a reaction that carries no normative weight. The other side replies: the fact that our meaning-conferring practices depend on the assumption of a future is itself the harm — you cannot dismiss it by calling it a mere psychological reaction without begging the question about what a harm is. Both sides accept the same psychological facts about what people would feel upon learning that extinction is imminent. Both sides agree that future people who never exist are not straightforwardly harmed in the way that existing people are. And yet neither side has moved.

I am starting to suspect that this is not a disagreement about any factual or logical claim, but about what kind of thing "badness" is — whether harms must be located in the welfare or experiences of particular individuals, or whether a life can be harmed in virtue of losing the structural conditions that make it meaningful, conditions that outrun individual welfare. But I am not confident in this diagnosis. Tell me what is really going on here. Is this a substantive disagreement that could be resolved by better arguments, a clash of irreconcilable starting intuitions about the nature of value, or a verbal dispute about the word "harm"? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new conceptual distinction, a further thought experiment, or an admission that the two sides are answering different questions?
