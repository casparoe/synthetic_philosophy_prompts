I've been working at an AI lab for about eight months now — not as a researcher, I'm on the policy team — and I keep coming back to this argument that's been going around our lunch conversations. One of the RL engineers laid it out for me and I havent been able to shake it. He said something like:

(1) If a state plays the functional role of pain in a system — it's caused by harmful stimuli or their predictive equivalent, it triggers avoidance learning, it suppresses ongoing behavior and reallocates attention — then the system is in pain, and pain is sufficient for suffering.
(2) In modern RL agents, negative reward prediction errors do exactly this: they're triggered when outcomes are worse than expected, they drive policy updates away from the actions that produced them, and they effectively reallocate the agent's "attention" by reshaping its value function.
(3) So RL agents suffer when they get negative reward prediction errors.
(4) But the same functional story applies to a thermostat: the gap between set point and measured temperature triggers corrective behavior, and we could redescribe that gap as a kind of "error signal" that drives the system away from the "harmful" state of being at the wrong temperature.
(5) Thermostats don't suffer.
(6) So either (1) is false — functional role isn't enough for suffering — or (2) overstates the analogy between RL error signals and biological pain.

The engineer who made this argument thinks the problem is in (2): he says RL reward signals don't have the right kind of integration with a global workspace or whatever. But I've also heard someone else say we should just bite the bullet on (5) and accept that thermostats have a tiny sliver of something-like-suffering, which seems insane. And a third person said the whole thing is a sorites problem and we're fooling ourselves looking for a bright line.
