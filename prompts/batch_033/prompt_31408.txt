I help run a small discussion group for people who think about how to make decisions when the odds aren't clear—a few amateur gamblers, some engineers, a couple of forecasters, nobody with formal training in this stuff. Lately we keep stalling on one disagreement. Sometimes I can give a single number for how likely something is, and sometimes all I can honestly say is that my probability is somewhere in a range, like between 30 and 60 percent. Some of the group feel the range is just sloppiness and we should force ourselves to pick a number; others feel the range is the honest answer and forcing a number would mean inventing information we don't have. The same split shows up in how we bet, how we read forecasts, and whether we'd pay to avoid learning something that would only widen our range.

I'd like you to write a "which view of imprecise probability do you actually hold?" quiz for this group: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The questions should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, and no question should just ask whether probabilities can be imprecise. At least these cases must appear: the classic two-urn choice, where one urn has a known 50/50 mix and the other has an unknown mix and you bet on drawing a particular color; a case where someone is pressed to give a single probability but can only honestly give a range; a case where learning something makes the range you can honestly state wider rather than narrower; and a choice between two options whose expected values are ranges that overlap but neither range contains the other. Please invent the remaining questions in the same spirit.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least these positions: precise probabilism, the view that you should always have a single sharp credence; permissive imprecision, the view that sharp and imprecise credences can both be legitimate; mandatory imprecision, the view that when the evidence doesn't pin down a single number the right state is a range; and, within the imprecise camp, three decision rules: a worst-case rule (choose the option whose lowest expected value is highest), an undominated rule (reject an option only if another option beats it across the whole range), and an admissible-prior rule (keep an option if at least one probability in the range would make it best). Give each leaf the name it usually goes by in the literature, or "unnamed" if there isn't one, and flag any pair of leaves the quiz can't cleanly separate. Also note any combinations of answers that are logically coherent but that almost nobody occupies, with a one-sentence guess as to why. Please write the whole thing in American English, and at the end say how confident you are in the distinctions and labels you used, singling out the point you are least sure about.
