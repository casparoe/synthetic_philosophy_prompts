The exam question was:

"Explain what the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons is, why it matters for policy evaluation, and critically assess at least one major attempt to address it."

The student's answer is reproduced below:

---

The problem of interpersonal utility comparisons arises because utility, as understood in modern economics, is a purely subjective magnitude. Each individual has their own utility function, and these functions represent nothing more than the person's preference ordering over bundles of goods. Since utility is ordinal rather than cardinal—meaning we can only say whether someone prefers A to B, not by how much—there is no common scale on which to compare the intensity of one person's satisfaction with that of another. This creates a serious difficulty for any ethical framework that requires summing or aggregating utility across persons, such as classical utilitarianism, because we cannot meaningfully say that a policy change that benefits person A by some amount of utility while harming person B by some other amount produces a net gain or loss.

The problem was most famously articulated by Lionel Robbins in his 1932 Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science. Robbins argued that interpersonal utility comparisons are, strictly speaking, meaningless—there is no empirical test that could verify or falsify a claim like "A gets more utility from this change than B loses," and therefore such comparisons have no place in scientific economics. Robbins's conclusion was that economics must restrict itself to Pareto efficiency as its sole normative criterion, since Pareto improvements require no interpersonal comparisons.

This problem matters enormously for policy evaluation. Consider a progressive tax policy that redistributes income from the rich to the poor. Standard economic reasoning suggests that the marginal utility of income is diminishing, so a dollar taken from a wealthy person costs them less utility than the utility gained by a poor person receiving that dollar. But this reasoning implicitly assumes that we can compare the utility functions of the rich person and the poor person—that their "utils" are commensurable. If Robbins is right, this assumption is unfounded, and the case for redistribution on utilitarian grounds collapses.

One major attempt to address the problem comes from John Harsanyi. Harsanyi argued that we can make interpersonal utility comparisons by adopting the perspective of an impartial observer who imagines being each person in turn, with that person's preferences, tastes, and circumstances. The observer then makes judgments about which outcomes are preferable overall. Harsanyi showed that if individual preferences satisfy the axioms of expected utility theory and the observer's preferences also satisfy those axioms, then the observer's social welfare function will be a weighted sum of individual utilities—effectively resurrecting utilitarianism. The key move is that the observer's empathetic preferences provide the missing common scale: the observer can compare, from their own unified perspective, what it is like to be each person in each situation.

However, Harsanyi's approach has been criticized on the grounds that it merely relocates the problem. The impartial observer's empathetic judgments are themselves a kind of interpersonal comparison, and critics like Amartya Sen have argued that we have no more reason to trust these judgments than we do to trust direct interpersonal comparisons of utility. Sen has proposed instead that we should shift the focus from utility to capabilities—the real freedoms and functionings that people can achieve—which are, he claims, more objectively comparable across persons.
