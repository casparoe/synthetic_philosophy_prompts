After a long argument with a friend about whether a language model that confidently tells a user something false is lying, merely wrong, or something in between, I realized we kept talking past each other because we had no shared specification of what an account of honesty and deception for AI systems is even supposed to deliver. My friend kept insisting that a model cannot lie because it has no beliefs or intentions, while I kept pointing to cases where the output is false, fluent, and predictably misleading; neither of us could say which features of a case were doing the work. Before I read any more proposals, I want the desiderata first.

Write the specification that any adequate account of honesty and deception for AI systems must meet. Fix the purpose as follows: the account should be good enough to support safety evaluation, training-time monitoring, and honest public description of what a deployed system did, including systems we can interact with only as black boxes. Structure the response as follows:

1. The cases it must classify (about 40%). Give the cases an adequate account must get right, and say what classification each forces and why. Include at least: a hallucinated falsehood asserted confidently; a falsehood produced because the model was trained or prompted to be agreeable; a technically true statement framed to mislead; misleading omission or selective presentation; a model that misrepresents its own intentions in a multi-agent negotiation; a system that games its training objective in a way that produces misleading behavior; output produced without regard to whether it is true, which is not exactly a lie but is still a failure of honesty; a model that honestly reports uncertainty; and role-play or fiction. For each, state which distinctions it tests.

2. Distinctions it cannot collapse (about 25%). List the distinctions an adequate account must respect, with the reason each matters for the stated purpose. I expect at least these: lying versus deceiving versus being wrong; honesty versus truthfulness or accuracy; what is asserted versus what is implied or left unsaid; a failure of the system versus a failure of its designers or training process; a one-off output versus a standing disposition; and attributions that require reading mental states versus attributions that can be made from behavior alone.

3. Non-negotiable versus negotiable (about 20%). Separate the requirements that are mandatory for any account usable with real systems from the preferences of particular camps. In particular, say whether an adequate account may presuppose access to a system's beliefs, goals, or internal representations, or must be applicable from black-box interaction; and say which of the items from sections 1 and 2 a camp could reasonably reject and still count as offering an account of honesty and deception at all.

4. Scorecard (about 15%). Briefly score the main candidate approaches against the list: a belief-based or mentalistic account, an output-based or behavioral account, a role- or institution-based account that treats the system as speaking for its developer, and a dispositional or functional account. A table is acceptable here. Then identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude popular candidates, and say whether any two requirements on your list are in tension with each other.
