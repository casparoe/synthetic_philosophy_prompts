I'm trying to figure out whether the doctrine of double effect actually hangs together or if I'm just pattern-matching to paradigm cases. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases—I'd like to know what principle (if any) captures them, which ones I might need to revise, and what the principle says about some cases I haven't made up my mind on.

Switch: fine. Footbridge: not fine. Loop: fine, even though the one on the loop is causally necessary for saving the five, which makes it feel closer to footbridge than I'd like.

Hysterectomy on a pregnant woman with uterine cancer (fetus dies as side effect): fine. Craniotomy during obstructed labor (fetus's skull crushed to extract it): not fine. I get that the first is "removing a diseased organ" and the second is "directly killing," but I worry I'm just being squeamish about the description.

Tactical bombing (munitions factory, civilians foreseen but not targeted): fine, assuming proportionality. Terror bombing (civilians targeted to break morale): not fine. Now here's where it gets weird for me: imagine the exact same bombing run—same bombs, same factory, same civilians die—but the general's purpose in ordering it is to kill the civilians, with the factory damage as a welcome bonus. I want to say this is not fine, but the physical act and outcome are identical to the tactical case. Only the general's mental state changes. Is that really where I want to locate the moral difference?

Morphine for pain in a terminally ill patient, foreseeing it will hasten death: fine. Same dose, same patient, but the doctor's purpose is to hasten death (pain relief as side effect): not fine. Again, purely mental.

Now the case that's really giving me trouble. Someone pushes a boulder off a cliff to clear a hiking path, foreseeing it will kill a hiker below. They genuinely don't intend the death—they'd be thrilled if the hiker somehow survived. The good effect (clearing the path) isn't caused *by* the death, and the death isn't a means. By the textbook criteria, this should be permitted. But my gut says absolutely not. Something about the bad effect being so direct, or so disproportionate to the good, or maybe the fact that you're using the exact same causal chain that kills someone. But I can't pin down which criterion it violates.

Is there a principle that gets all of these (or most of them) without just being a list of my gut reactions? Which verdicts should I give up, and what does the resulting principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a self-driving car that swerves to avoid five pedestrians, foreseeing it will hit one person on the sidewalk, where the swerve path was chosen specifically because it would hit *that* person rather than swerving the other way into a crowd of two?
