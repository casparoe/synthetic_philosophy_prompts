I've been listening to a lot of modern Stoicism podcasts lately — Ryan Holiday, the Daily Stoic, that whole scene — and the one thing that gets repeated like a mantra is "the dichotomy of control." It's presented as the foundational insight: draw a line between what's up to you and what isn't, and only invest your energy in the first category. But the more I pay attention, the more I realize people are using this phrase to mean wildly different things, and nobody seems to notice they're talking past each other.

Here's what I've noticed. Some people treat "in your control" as basically your voluntary actions — you can control whether you study, you can't control whether you pass. Fair enough. But then others (especially the ones who've actually read Epictetus) say no, even your actions aren't fully in your control because someone could physically stop you; the only things truly in your control are your judgments, desires, and aversions. That's a much more restrictive claim. And then there's a third group who use it in a looser, self-help-y way where "in your control" just means "something you can influence at least a little" — like, your health is partly in your control because you can eat well and exercise, even though you can't guarantee you won't get cancer. That's barely a dichotomy at all it's a spectrum.

And beyond what "in your control" means, there's a separate question about what the prescription actually is. Is it a claim about what you should care about (axiology, I guess)? About what you should spend time deliberating about (decision theory)? About what emotions are appropriate (psychology)? Or is it just a mental trick — tell yourself something is out of your control so you stop feeling bad about it, regardless of whether it really is?

What I want is a kind of menu. Lay out the distinct precise theses that "the dichotomy of control" could be expressing, from the most trivial (like "don't worry about the weather on Mars") to the most radical (like "your own bodily actions aren't up to you"). For each one, say whether it's actually defensible. And then tell me which reading is doing the real work when modern Stoics deploy this to argue that you shouldn't be upset about losing your job, your partner leaving you, or getting a bad diagnosis — because those are exactly the cases where the slogan is supposed to earn its keep.
