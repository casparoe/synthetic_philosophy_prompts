A couple of months ago I was at a dinner party where two people I know got into a long argument about whether soldiers on the unjust side of a war are morally permitted to kill enemy soldiers. I've been turning it over in my head ever since, and I'd like someone to go through their exchange and tell me who was right about what.

Here's roughly how it went:

(1) David said that in war, soldiers on both sides have the same moral standing when it comes to killing each other. A soldier who shoots an enemy combatant isn't committing murder, regardless of which side started the war or which side has the better claim. The whole point of the laws of war is that we treat combatants symmetrically.

(2) Maria pushed back: that can't be right as a matter of basic morality. If your country invades another country without justification, and you're part of that invasion, then the soldiers on the other side are defending their homes against an unjust aggressor. How could it be morally permissible for you to kill them? That would mean the aggressor and the defender have the same moral permission to kill each other, which seems absurd on its face.

(3) David replied that individual soldiers almost never know whether their side's war is just. They're not in a position to evaluate the geopolitical merits, they've been told by their government that the cause is righteous, and they're operating under orders. Given that epistemic situation, you can't hold them morally responsible for fighting.

(4) Maria said that epistemic limitation doesn't get you off the hook in other contexts. If I join a group of people beating someone up because I've been told he's a threat, and it turns out he's an innocent person defending himself, the fact that I believed I was in the right doesn't make my violence permissible. Why would war be different?

(5) David said war is different because it's a collective enterprise where individual choice is heavily constrained. A soldier who refuses to fight faces court-martial, imprisonment, or worse. And even setting that aside, soldiers fight primarily to protect the people next to them — their fellow soldiers. That's not a morally blameworthy motive, and it operates the same way on both sides.

(6) Maria answered that the "protecting my comrades" argument proves too much. A member of a criminal organization could say the same thing — he's just protecting his fellow gang members. The fact that you have bonds of loyalty to the people you're fighting alongside doesn't tell us anything about whether the fighting itself is justified. And as for coercion, most soldiers in modern professional armies are volunteers. They signed up.

(7) David came back with: even if you're right about volunteers, your position would mean that enormous numbers of ordinary people throughout history — conscripts in wars they didn't choose, young men who had no real alternative — were engaged in mass murder. That seems like a deeply implausible moral picture. It's too demanding.

(8) Maria said that a moral conclusion being uncomfortable doesn't make it false. Maybe the truth is that war involves vast amounts of unjustified killing that we've collectively decided not to call murder because doing so would be too destabilizing. That doesn't mean the killing is actually justified — it means we've made a pragmatic choice to look the other way.

They went back and forth a bit more after that, but those were the main moves. I'd like you to adjudicate this exchange point by point: for each of the eight moves above, say whether the argument is strong, weak, or somewhere in between, and explain why. Then tell me who you think has the better overall position and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
