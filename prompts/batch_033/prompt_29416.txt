I'm a retired physical chemist — spent most of my career fitting kinetic models to data that was always noisier than the journals wanted to admit. Recently I've been reading Quine on the indeterminacy of translation, the gavagai buisness, and my old reflex is to hear it as something I already know from statistics: two models can fit the same data equally well, and without further constraints you can't pick between them; the translation manual is just another model and the linguist's data is just a finite set of observations. But I keep having the uneasy feeling that Quine meant something stronger than "the data underdetermines the model," something about there being no fact of the matter at all, not just no way to know which is right.

What I'd like is a systematic translation of Quine's indeterminacy thesis into the vocabulary I actually think in — training data, model classes, identifiability, underdetermination, regularization, inductive bias, hypothesis space, maybe even the difference between fitting and predicting. Walk me through how the pieces map: occasion sentences and stimulus meaning, analytical hypotheses, rival translation manuals, the linguist's behavioral evidence. Be honest about the mapping: don't smuggle in the assumption that there's a true data-generating process and call that a translation. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Quine's claim survive the translation cleanly, which parts get distorted, and which parts have no counterpart at all in the statistician's vocabulary? I especially want to know whether the "no fact of the matter" part is just the familiar point that the likelihood function can be flat, or whether it's saying something a statistician's framework genuinely cannot express. And I'd like you to commit: is Quine's thesis best understood as a special case of underdetermination, or is it a different claim that my vocabulary would flatten into something weaker? Give me a definite verdict and defend it, not a balanced survey.
