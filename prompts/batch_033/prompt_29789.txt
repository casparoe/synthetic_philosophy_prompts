At a Jewish philosophy reading group, two members got into a long argument about Maimonides' view of animal sacrifice, and afterward they asked me to referee it, but I could not do it to anyone's satisfaction. Sarah argued that in the Guide of the Perplexed Maimonides treats the entire sacrificial system as a divine concession: the Israelites were so habituated to worshiping through animal offerings that God could not reasonably have commanded them to abandon sacrifice all at once, so the Torah redirected the practice to God alone, in one place, under strict rules, as a way of gradually weaning the people toward prayer and intellectual worship. "Sacrifice was never the ideal," she said; "it was a remedial measure fitted to a particular historical condition."

Daniel replied that this cannot be the whole story. "Maimonides also insists that every commandment has a reason and that the Torah is a perfect, eternal law," he said. "He explains the details of the sacrificial code at length, as if they are wise in themselves, and he never treats those laws as temporary. If sacrifices were merely a concession to an ancient habit, why would he regard them as binding for all time rather than as a temporary accommodation?" Sarah answered that a concession can still be wise: God acts like a physician who prescribes a regimen suited to the patient's present condition, so the perfection of the law consists in its being the right remedy for human nature as it then was, not in its matching some abstract ideal. "The Torah can be both eternal and remedial," she said, "because the remedy remains appropriate so long as human nature remains what it is." Daniel came back that this only sharpens the problem: "It makes the Torah's eternity the eternity of a compromise, which sits badly with Maimonides' own insistence that God's wisdom is not time-bound or dependent on human weakness. Either Maimonides must hold that sacrifices have some intrinsic value after all, or he is committed to saying that God's perfect law eternally commands something God would have preferred humanity never needed." Sarah replied that the alternative is worse, since it would require explaining why a perfect God would desire animal offerings for their own sake, which Maimonides, on her reading, explicitly denies.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right about which specific point, which inferences are sound or unsound, and what, if anything, both parties are missing about his position. Please write the response in American English.
