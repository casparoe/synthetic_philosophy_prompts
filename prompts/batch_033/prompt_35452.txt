I'm about to evaluate a batch of proposed debate protocols for scalable oversight, and before I read them I want the specification of what an adequate protocol has to deliver. Treat "debate" broadly: two or more agents argue for competing answers to a hard question in front of a judge who is much less capable than the debaters, and the judge must end up believing the answer that is true, or making the decision the truth would license, without independently checking the answer. Assume the questions are far beyond the judge — proofs of theorems the judge cannot follow end to end, predictions about codebases too large for the judge to audit, factual claims about records the judge cannot inspect — and assume that at least one debater may be adversarially optimizing to make the judge believe a false answer, including by exploiting the judge's biases rather than by arguing honestly.

Write the desiderata: the full list of requirements any such protocol must satisfy to count as a solution to scalable oversight via debate, rather than an interesting game that happens to use arguments. Do not stop at the obvious candidates — truth-inducingness, robustness to lying, bounded judge effort, independence from a ground-truth oracle — though each of those must be defended with its failure mode; the value is in the requirements that are easy to miss. For each requirement, give the concrete failure mode or case that motivates it — a scenario in which a protocol lacking that property visibly fails. Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and defend the classification rather than merely asserting it.

The part I most want is the consistency analysis. Identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied given the setup — for example, anything forced by the judge's boundedness, by the absence of any ground-truth oracle, or by the adversary being at least as capable as the honest debater — and say exactly which requirement has to give and why. Also identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding existing proposals: name it, say what it rules out, and say what it still leaves standing.

Take a committed position. Do not give me a survey of possible desiderata with the tradeoffs left open; make definite calls about which requirements are genuine, which are non-negotiable, where the list is jointly unsatisfiable, and what an honest designer should sacrifice first.

Structure the answer in exactly these sections:

1. "Setup and scope" — the working definition of debate, the class of questions, and the assumptions about judge and debaters; flag any assumptions you need to add for the desiderata to be well-defined. (~150 words)

2. "The fixed points" — the non-negotiable requirements, each with its motivating failure case and a sentence on why no protocol may drop it. (~350 words)

3. "The contested requirements" — the desiderata that some camps treat as mandatory but you judge to be preferences, each with whose preference it is and what that camp buys by insisting on it. (~250 words)

4. "Where the list breaks" — the jointly unsatisfiable subsets, the argument that they conflict, and which requirement gives way first. (~300 words)

5. "The single most diagnostic requirement" — the one criterion that most efficiently separates live proposals from dead ones, with what it excludes and what survives it. (~150 words)

6. "Verdict" — if you had to build a protocol, which requirements you would hold fixed, which you would relax, and in what order you would relax them under pressure. (~150 words)
