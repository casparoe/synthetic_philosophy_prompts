I'm a journalist working on a piece about how people make decisions when they're not sure what's right — not just uncertain about the facts, but uncertain about their values themselves. In my research I keep running into this whole academic literature on "moral uncertainty," and honestly I'm not sure I buy that it's a distinct thing.

Here's what I don't get. If I'm uncertain between two moral theories — say utilitarianism and Kantian ethics — that's just uncertainty about a fact: the fact of which moral theory is correct. And we already have a perfectly good framework for making decisions under factual uncertainty: expected utility theory. You assign probabilities, you weigh the outcomes, you pick the action with the highest expected value. So why can't I just do that here? Assign a 60% credence to utilitarianism, a 40% credence to deontology, and then choose the action that maximizes expected moral value across those theories.

The only reason I can see that this wouldn't work is if the two theories don't share a common scale of value — like, utilitarianism says "this action is worth 10 utils" and deontology says "this action is forbidden, full stop," and you can't take an average of "10" and "forbidden." But if that's the real problem, then the literature shouldn't be called "moral uncertainty" — it should be called "how to compare actions across incommensurable value systems." The uncertainty part is a red herring; the hard part is the incommensurability, and that's a problem even if you're 100% certain about which theory is true but the theory itself contains incommensurable values.

Am I missing something, or is the moral uncertainty literature kind of just an elaborate way of rediscovering the problem of value incommensurability?
