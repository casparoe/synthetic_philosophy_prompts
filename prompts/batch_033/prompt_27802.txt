A friend and I have been reading L.A. Paul on transformative experience, and he came up with the following argument, which he now thinks refutes the whole view. I cant find the exit, and I'd like a second opinion.

(1) For a choice to be rational, you have to know the value of the outcome you're choosing. (2) An experience is epistemically transformative when you can't know what it's like before you've had it. (3) If you can't know what an outcome is like, you can't know its value to you. (4) So you can never rationally choose to undergo an epistemically transformative experience. (5) But by Paul's own criterion, nearly every unfamiliar experience is transformative: your first taste of a new fruit, your first time hearing a genre of music, your first move to a new city. You don't know what any of those are like in advance, not in the way she requires. (6) So if Paul is right, trying a new fruit is never a rational choice. (7) That's absurd — people can clearly rationally decide to try a mango. (8) So the framework proves too much and has to go.

My friend wants to reject (3). I'd rather keep (3) and blame something else, because rejecting (3) seems to open the door to saying you can know what a life with a child is worth without knowing what it's like, and that's exactly the kind of self-knowledge Paul says you can't have. But I can't make the case cleanly. Which step should go, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up?
