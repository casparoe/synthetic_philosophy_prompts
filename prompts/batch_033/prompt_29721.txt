A colleague of mine has been arguing for a proposal to replace our city's elected council with a standing legislative body whose members are chosen by lot from the adult residents, with full power to set its own agenda and pass ordinances. His central argument is an analogy with the trial jury. We already trust randomly selected juries, he says, with verdicts that can send people to prison; a jury is meant to be a fair cross-section of the community; ordinary citizens, once given a clear task and real responsibility, deliberate seriously; and the jury is widely regarded as a safeguard against elite control and special-interest capture. He concludes that a lottocratic legislature would be similarly competent, impartial, and accountable, and that the usual objections about amateurism and accountability are overblown.

I would like a careful audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Give me an explicit correspondence table: what in the jury system maps onto what in a legislature chosen by lot—the jury pool, selection and exclusions, the judge, the charge to the jury, the verdict, post-verdict review, and anything else you think matters. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's intended use from the ones that do not, and say which features of juries the analogy quietly imports that a randomly selected legislature would lack. Finally, give me a committed verdict rather than a survey of positions: once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion—that a legislature chosen by lot would be competent and accountable enough to replace an elected council—survive, or was the analogy carrying the argument all along? If it fails, say what a better analogy would be, if any, and what conclusion that better analogy actually supports.
