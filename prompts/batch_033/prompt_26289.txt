In discussions of the hedonistic imperative—the proposal that humanity should use biotechnology to abolish suffering in all sentient life—proponents often draw an analogy between biological pain and a fire alarm. The alarm's shriek is aversive, but its function is informational: it signals that something is wrong and that action is needed. We do not need the building to burn in order to be warned of fire; likewise, the argument runs, we do not need the raw awfulness of suffering to be warned of tissue damage or threat. The analogy is deployed against the objection that suffering is indispensable for survival, and it is meant to license the conclusion that we could engineer a signaling system that preserves motivation and avoidance behavior while replacing the felt quality of suffering with a non-aversive gradient of well-being.

Audit this analogy. Identify what corresponds to what, where the mapping breaks down, which features of the fire-alarm source domain the analogy quietly imports without justification, and whether the conclusion—that suffering can be replaced with a non-awful signal while preserving all necessary functions—survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy is available, say what it is and whether it supports or undermines the same conclusion.
