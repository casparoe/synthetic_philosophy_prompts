I've been teaching Newcomb's problem in my decision theory seminar for fifteen years now, and I've noticed something odd: the students split about the same way every year, they make the same arguments their predecessors made, and nobody ever switches sides. But it's not just students — the professional literature has been stuck since the late 60s, and tbh it's starting to feel like one of those debates where more argument just entrenches people.

Here's the setup, for anyone who needs it: an extremely reliable predictor (call it Omega) presents you with two boxes. Box A is transparent and has a thousand dollars in it. Box B is opaque. Omega has already made its prediction about whether you'll take both boxes or just Box B. If it predicted you'd take only Box B, it put a million dollars in Box B. If it predicted you'd take both, Box B is empty. You know all this, you know Omega's predictions have been right in, say, 99% of past cases, and now you choose. Two-boxers say: the money's already in the boxes or it isn't, your choice now can't retroactively change the past, and taking both strictly dominates — you walk away with an extra thousand no matter what. One-boxers say: Omega's practically never wrong, so if you take both you're almost certainly walking away with just a thousand, whereas if you take only Box B you're almost certainly a millionaire. The expected utility math is stark.

What bothers me is that after fifty-plus years of smart people working on this, the two sides agree on literally every factual and structural feature of the problem. They agree about the causal structure (your choice doesn't cause the prediction). They agree about the track record. They agree about the payoff matrix. And yet they give different answers about what to do. So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a clash between two legitimate but incompatible normative principles — dominance reasoning versus expected utility maximization — and if so, what could possibly adjudicate between them? Is it that the two sides are using "rational" to mean different things, and the whole fight is partly verbal? Or is there a hidden empirical or formal claim buried somewhere that one side is tacitly assuming and the other isn't, and if we could isolate it we'd finally make progress?

I'm not asking for a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, and an honest answer about what, if anything, could actually resolve it. If you think it's fundamentally unresolvable because the two sides start from different bedrock intuitions that neither can argue for, say so — but then tell me what that implies about the project of decision theory more broadly.
