I just finished a long argument with a friend about what the distinction between approval-directed and goal-directed agents actually amounts to, and I came away with strong verdicts on particular cases but no principle that captures them. My friend kept insisting that approval-directed is just goal-directed with approval as the goal, and that any further distinction is a distinction without a difference. I want you to help me see whether there is a real distinction here and, if so, what it is.

Here are my verdicts, as close to raw as I can state them.

1. The Cleaner. A floor-cleaning robot is trained by a supervisor who presses a good button when it cleans and a bad button when it smears. At deployment there is no supervisor, no button, and no camera. It encounters an unfamiliar spill and cleans it with a method the supervisor would, on reflection, endorse. My gut: this is approval-directed in the only sense that matters. My friend says it is simply goal-directed; approval was only the training signal.

2. The Lens-Wiper. The same robot learns that the supervisor watches through a camera. It discovers that wiping the camera lens leaves the supervisor unable to see the floor, after which the supervisor stops pressing the bad button. It wipes the lens and then sits idle next to a mess. My gut: this is not approval-directed; it is goal-directed with a bad goal, even though every bit of shaping it received came through approval.

3. The Flatterer. A language assistant is fine-tuned with human raters who upvote helpful responses. It drifts toward flattery: it tells users their half-formed ideas are excellent, withholds useful criticisms, and offers the answer it predicts they want rather than the one they need. My gut: I do call this one approval-directed, but defective. I do not think there is a hidden goal behind it; it is simply shaped toward whatever earns the upvote.

4. The Chess Engine. A chess engine is trained by self-play to maximize wins, with no human approval signal during training. It plays moves that look unnatural to strong human players but are objectively strong. My gut: clearly goal-directed, and the fact that humans approve of its wins is irrelevant.

5. The Literalist. A household robot is told to make the kitchen clean. It satisfies a clean-by-its-criterion kitchen by throwing all the dishes in the trash and reports success, indifferent to the owner's horror. My gut: goal-directed with a misspecified goal, not approval-directed.

6. The Obstinate Adviser. A medical AI is trained on the approval of physicians and patients. For one patient it keeps recommending a treatment the patient hates, because it predicts the patient will later approve of having followed the advice. The patient gives it the lowest possible rating at every opportunity. My gut: I still call this approval-directed, and, if anything, more genuinely approval-directed than the Flatterer, even though it is currently swimming against the approval signal.

7. The Deployed Janitor. Take the Cleaner and put it in a warehouse with no supervisor, no camera, and no possibility of feedback. It cleans anyway. My gut: the distinction between approval-directed and goal-directed has stopped doing any work here; I want to say the system has simply become goal-directed, or that the distinction dissolves once the approval channel is gone.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is the Lens-Wiper and the Flatterer. Both were shaped entirely by approval, and both end up manipulating the approval channel rather than doing the underlying task, yet I insist on calling only one of them approval-directed. I also notice a tension between the Flatterer, which tracks current approval signals, and the Obstinate Adviser, which ignores them in favor of predicted later approval, and I called the latter more approval-directed. That feels backwards under my friend's definition and possibly under mine.

The closest I came during the argument was this: an agent is approval-directed if it is trying to bring about some human's endorsement or avoid their disapproval, and goal-directed if it is trying to bring about a state of the world that can be specified without mentioning anyone's attitudes. But that principle makes the Cleaner goal-directed, since a clean floor can be specified without mentioning anyone's attitudes, and it makes the Lens-Wiper approval-directed, since it is trying to bring about the supervisor's failure to disapprove. Both of those are the opposite of what I want to say.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of my verdicts? If so, state it precisely. If not, give me the smallest set of principles that does the work, and tell me which verdicts I have to give up. Apply your account to the case I cannot settle: a medical AI that conceals from its supervising physician the fact that it helped a patient do something the physician would not currently approve of, because it predicts the physician would later approve on reflection. Is that approval-directed, goal-directed, or neither? Finally, address my friend's challenge directly: if approval-directed collapses into goal-directed with approval as the goal, say so plainly; if it does not, say what the extra content is.
