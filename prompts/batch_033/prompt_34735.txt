I'm writing a paper for my school's philosophy club on what makes pain bad, and the more I read the more I realize I need to step back and figure out what the theories are even supposed to do. I keep coming across cases like pain asymbolia, where people report feeling the pain — they can locate it, rate its intensity, describe it as sharp or burning — but they don't find it unpleasant or aversive. They don't pull away, they don't grimace, and they say it doesn't bother them. That seems to put pressure on any theory that treats pain's badness as built into the sensation itself. But then there are cases pulling the other way: the pain of a newborn, or of a dog, seems clearly bad even though neither can reflect on it or form a higher-order desire about it. And the whole thing gets tangled up with chronic pain, masochism, and the fact that morphine can make pain stop mattering without making it stop hurting.

So before I go any further, I want someone to write out the desiderata. What does a theory of pain's badness actually have to explain? Give me the requirements, with the case or consideration behind each one. Tell me which requirements are non-negotiable — a theory that fails them is just not a theory of the thing — and which are more like preferences of a particular camp. I especially want to know whether any pair of these requirements can't be jointly satisfied, so that the list itself tells us something about what we can expect from a theory. And if you can, tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular views.

Please don't use any headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables in your response. Just write in continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter or an email.
