I'm putting together a "philosophical profile" on anthropic reasoning for a reading group, and I want a consistency check before I have to defend it. Here's where I've landed:

(a) I think the self-sampling assumption is basically right: when I'm unsure which observer I am, I should reason as if I'm randomly selected from the relevant set of observers, weighting each equally.
(b) I'm a halfer about Sleeping Beauty, and I take that to be SSA applied correctly.
(c) I think the doomsday argument is unsound: learning that I'm roughly the 100-billionth human shouldn't substantially raise my confidence that humanity dies out soon.
(d) My reason for (c) is the reference-class problem. There's no fact of the matter about whether I'm being sampled from all humans, from all humans with my kind of evidence, from all observers who can ask this question, or from some other class, so the doomsday shift is an artifact of a choice nothing forces on us.
(e) But I don't think the reference-class problem is fatal to SSA across the board — in cases like Sleeping Beauty the setup fixes the relevant class, and SSA does real work there.
(f) I think Gott's version, where your birth rank alone is supposed to give 95% bounds on total human population, is even worse, but for a different reason: it tries to get probabilities without any prior.
(g) I have no sympathy for the self-indication assumption — the idea that your existence is evidence for there being many observers feels like getting evidence for free.

Does this hang together? I want to know which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what the least costly fix is. Rank the problems by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad.
