I'm taking an epistemology course, and last week's discussion section got stuck on a case that two of my classmates kept arguing about after the section ended. I'm hoping you can adjudicate their exchange.

The case was this: suppose you're given a logic puzzle, you work through it carefully, and you reach conclusion P with high confidence — the reasoning feels solid, you've double-checked it. Then you're told that earlier you were given a drug that, in controlled studies, causes 80% of subjects to make at least one unnoticed error on logic puzzles of exactly this kind. (You had no idea you'd been given it.) The question is whether you can rationally maintain your belief in P.

Here's roughly how their argument went:

(1) Marcus said you cannot rationally maintain your belief in P. The drug evidence is higher-order evidence that your reasoning is likely flawed, and ignoring it is epistemic recklessness — you'd be treating your own reasoning as mysteriously immune to a known impairment.

(2) Lena replied that this can't be right, because if you abandon P, you're doing so on the basis of reasoning about the drug statistics — but that reasoning might itself be impaired by the drug. You can't coherently treat your reasoning as reliable enough to assess the higher-order evidence but not reliable enough to sustain the first-order conclusion.

(3) Marcus said that's a false symmetry. The statistical evidence about the drug is public and checkable by others; your private reasoning about the puzzle is exactly the kind of thing the drug corrupts. Deferring to the public evidence doesn't require trusting the corrupted faculty in the same way.

(4) Lena answered that the distinction between public and private reasoning doesn't solve the problem — you still have to do the private reasoning that the public evidence applies to your case, that the study was sound, that 80% is high enough to warrant deference, and so on. At every step the drug might be at work.

(5) Marcus then shifted ground: he said that even if there's no clean way to avoid epistemic akrasia entirely, the rational thing is to minimize it. Sticking with P leaves you akratic about the higher-order evidence (you believe P while believing your evidence probably doesn't support P), whereas deferring to the statistics leaves you akratic about P itself (you believe not-P while your reasoning still screams P). He claimed the second kind of akrasia is less bad because it's akrasia about a first-order matter rather than about what your evidence supports — it's akratic in the object-level belief but not in the epistemic-policy belief.

(6) Lena said that's just stipulating a ranking without justification, and that if anything, akrasia about what your evidence supports is worse because it severs the connection between reasoning and belief entirely.

They went back and forth like this until the library closed. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what — not just who wins overall, but which points are sound, which are mistaken, and what if anything both of them are missing?
