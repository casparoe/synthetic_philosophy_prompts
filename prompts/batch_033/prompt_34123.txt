Two friends of mine got into it at a party over whether our other friend Maya is being irrational. Maya is about to sign a two-year lease on a studio for her pottery business, and an acquaintance who works in the city planning office offered to tell her, confidentially and for free, whether a big commercial redevelopment is likely to go through next year. Maya said she'd rather not know. Her reasoning was that if it's bad news she'll lose her nerve and not sign even though the numbers still work, and if it's good news she'll get overconfident and overpay for renovations. Dan said this is exactly the kind of thinking Good's theorem rules out: free information can't have negative expected value for a rational decision maker, because you can always ignore it and do what you'd have done anyway. Priya said the theorem doesn't apply to someone who knows she won't use the information well. It turned into a long back-and-forth, and they asked me to referee and I honestly couldnt. Here's roughly how it went.

Dan: Maya's just wrong. Good's theorem says costless information never has negative expected value for a rational agent, since at worst you ignore it and pick the action you'd have picked without it. Refusing the information is like throwing away a free option.

Priya: But that assumes the agent updates by conditioning and then maximizes. Maya just told us she won't. She predicts that bad news will make her panic and choose the option she currently thinks is worse. So for her the information isn't free; it comes with a predictable cost, namely the worse decision she'll make after hearing it.

Dan: That's not a counterexample to Good's theorem, that's just admitting Maya is irrational after she learns. The theorem is about ideal agents. You can't use the fact that someone will fail to live up to it later to show that refusing the information is rational now.

Priya: Why not? Rationality now is about expected consequences given what you know about yourself, including your future irrationality. Ulysses had himself tied to the mast so he couldn't act on the sirens later, and nobody calls that irrational. Maya is doing the same thing: binding her future self to the plan her present self thinks is best.

Dan: But Ulysses wasn't offered free information, he faced a known temptation. If the information really is costless, Good's theorem says Maya can take it and ignore it, and she'll be at least as well off as if she never took it. If she can't ignore it, then she's not describing costless information; she's describing information with a cost. So the theorem is untouched, and the real question is just whether her predicted panic is itself something a rational person would have.

Priya: Right, that's the real question, and that's my point. Good's theorem is a theorem, not a piece of practical advice. Once you allow that beliefs themselves can matter to a person, or that someone can predictably misuse what she learns, the theorem doesn't license calling her irrational for looking away. It just tells you what would be true for an agent without those features.

Dan: But then what work is the theorem doing? If every case of avoiding information gets redescribed as "information with a cost," the theorem is unfalsifiable and useless.

Priya: It's not useless; it's diagnostic. It forces Maya to say what the cost is. She did say: she knows she'll make a worse decision if she knows. Whether that self-prediction is itself rational is a separate issue, and Good's theorem doesn't settle it.

Dan: Okay, so you agree she's irrational, just maybe at the later stage rather than now.

Priya: No, I agree she would be irrational later, on her own lights. That doesn't make her irrational now for taking steps to prevent it. Those are different evaluations.
