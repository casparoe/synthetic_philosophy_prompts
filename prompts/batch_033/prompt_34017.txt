I'm in a political philosophy class and we're doing Rawls, and a friend sent me a blog post that sums up his views on religion and politics like this:

"Rawls's idea of public reason says that when citizens debate the basic structure of society — constitutional essentials and questions of basic justice — they should restrict themselves to reasons all reasonable citizens could accept. In practice, that means secular, neutral reasons: religious citizens are expected to leave their faith at the door and not appeal to God, scripture, or church teaching when arguing about laws, since those aren't shareable reasons. Rawls thought this was a fair price because religion can still be practiced freely in private life and in the background culture. Critics object that this stacks the deck against believers, who are forced to translate their deepest commitments into a supposedly neutral vocabulary that is really just secularism in disguise, so the view effectively excludes religious voices from democratic politics."

Something about this smells off — it sounds like the caricature people argue against more than the position Rawls actually defended — but I can't pin down exactly where it goes wrong, and maybe I'm just being defensive because I like Rawls. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful Rawlsian would accept, which are overstatements or conflations, and whether the closing criticism about exclusion follows from what's accurate in the summary? I'm especially confused about whether public reason really equals "secular reasons only," and what difference the proviso makes — the idea that religious reasons can be brought in as long as proper political reasons are eventually supplied as well. I also remember seeing people claim his later, more permissive view lets citizens introduce religious reasons pretty freely as long as public reasons eventually show up; does that rescue him from the blog's criticism or not? I definately don't want citations or a reading list, I want to actually understand the distinction.
