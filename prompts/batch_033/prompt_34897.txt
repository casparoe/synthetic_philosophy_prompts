I spent my working life in biochemistry — mostly drug metabolism — and retired a few years ago, and lately I've been reading philosophy of medicine. The thing I keep coming back to is the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I'm trying to figure out whether I actually have a view or just a pile of case-by-case reactions. So I'd like someone to run my verdicts through the wringer and tell me if there's a principle behind them.

Here are my gut calls, roughly as I find myself making them:

- Insulin for a diabetic whose pancreas makes none: obviously fine.
- A measles vaccine: fine, even though the immune system isn't broken, you're just making it better than it would have been.
- Eyeglasses for a nearsighted kid: fine.
- LASIK for a thirty-year-old who'd just rather not wear glasses: fine, no real hesitation.
- A cochlear implant for a child born deaf: fine.
- Antidepressants for someone with clinical depression: fine, though I have a faint worry that they're changing the person rather than fixing the disease.
- Testosterone for a man whose levels are below the reference range and who has symptoms: fine.
- Testosterone for a healthy sixty-year-old whose levels are in the normal range but who wants more energy: not fine.
- Coffee to get through an afternoon of writing: fine.
- Modafinil for a healthy graduate student studying for quals: not fine, though I can't say why its worse than the coffee.
- A beta-blocker for a violinist with performance anxiety: I'd allow it, even though it's a healthy person using a drug to perform better.
- Blood doping for a cyclist, reinjecting his own red cells: not fine.
- Living and training at altitude to raise your red cell count for the same race: fine, even though the physiology ends up near enough the same.
- Reconstructive surgery for someone whose face was destroyed in a car crash: fine.
- A nose job for someone with a perfectly normal nose who just dislikes it: I don't think it's wrong exactly, but I'm not comfortable calling it medicine.
- Growth hormone for a short child with no deficiency, predicted to end up 5'2": I'm genuinely torn, and I lean not fine.
- Leg-lengthening surgery for a 5'9" adult who wants to be 6': not fine.
- Selecting embryos to avoid Huntington's disease: fine.
- Selecting embryos for eye color: not fine, even though it harms no one.
- Metformin or rapamycin taken by a healthy seventy-year-old to slow aging: I lean fine, or at least I don't see the principled objection, and this bothers me because it looks like enhancing a perfectly normal process.

I thought the principle might be something like: it's fine to restore or protect species-typical functioning, but not to push beyond it. But that doesn't handle the vaccine, the coffee, or the altitude training, and it's not clear why treating aging isn't just protecting normal function. And it gives me no help on why altitude training is fine but blood doping isn't when the body ends up in the same state.

So: is there a single principle, or a small set of them, that gets most of these? Which of my verdicts would a defensible principle force me to give up, and why those rather than the others? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a hypothetical safe pill that raises a healthy adult's IQ by ten points for the rest of their life?
