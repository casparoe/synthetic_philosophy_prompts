I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting centers on a character who, after a decade of addiction, signs a legally enforceable agreement authorizing his family to place him in treatment against his will if he begins using again. In trying to think clearly about whether that agreement can be justified, I have settled on a set of commitments, and I want to know whether they hang together before I let them structure the novel's moral argument.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) Autonomy is fundamentally a matter of acting on one's own reflectively endorsed values, not on whichever desire happens to be strongest at the moment.
(2) A person remains the same person over time in the sense that makes promises to oneself, and not only to other people, binding.
(3) A person at one time has no general authority to override the present choices of a later version of themselves merely because the earlier decision came first.
(4) Ulysses contracts are sometimes justified, but only when the later self's attempt to break them is itself a failure of autonomy, as in akratic relapse, rather than a genuine change of mind.
(5) Constitutional precommitment is legitimate only when it protects the conditions of self-government, not when it entrenches substantive ends that later generations cannot revise.

I suspect some of these are in tension, but I cannot tell which pairs are genuinely inconsistent, which only appear to be, and which turn on a distinction I have not drawn. Please go through the set and identify the real tensions, order them by severity, say what would have to give in each case, and tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set cohere.
