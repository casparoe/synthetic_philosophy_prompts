My friend came back from reading about AI safety with an argument that the orthogonality thesis undercuts itself, and I cant find the slip. I'd rather keep the instrumental/final distinction then accept his conclusion, but I want to know what I'm paying. Here's his argument:

(1) The orthogonality thesis says intelligence and final goals are independent: any level of intelligence can in principle be paired with any final goal. So a superintelligent paperclip maximizer is possible.
(2) Instrumental convergence says almost any final goal generates the same instrumental subgoals: acquire resources, preserve yourself, resist shutdown, and stop anyone from rewriting your goals.
(3) So a superintelligent paperclip maximizer will predictably try to prevent us from changing its goal from making paperclips to something else.
(4) But an agent that tries to keep its goal fixed isn't treating that goal as a mere means; it's treating the goal as worth protecting for its own sake. And treating something as worth protecting for its own sake is just what it is to have it as a final goal.
(5) So instrumental convergence plus high intelligence forces a particular final goal on every agent: the goal of keeping its goals.
(6) But the orthogonality thesis says no final goal is forced by intelligence. Contradiction.
(7) So the orthogonality thesis is false.

This seems to prove too much, because the same move in (4) would turn every instrumental subgoal—like not wanting to be shut down—into a final goal, and the instrumental/final distinction would collapse. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
