I am a philosophy professor whose doctoral work was in biostatistics, and I now teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of biology. One debate I keep returning to is the long-running dispute over whether evolutionary psychology produces science or just-so stories. On my reading, the critics' charge is that an adaptationist can, for nearly any trait or behavioral tendency, tell a plausible story about how it promoted fitness in the ancestral environment, and the story survives only because it has not been forced to run much risk of being wrong. The defenders answer that this is a caricature: serious work in evolutionary psychology, they say, derives testable predictions from well-specified models of ancestral selection pressures and checks them against independent sources of evidence. I would like you to restate this dispute in the vocabulary I actually think in when I am doing statistics: model fitting and model criticism. Translate the charge of just-so storytelling into the language of overfitting, free parameters and degrees of freedom, post hoc fitting, multiple comparisons, identifiability, pre-registration, and out-of-sample testing. Then translate the adaptationist reply into the same language: what would a well-regularized evolutionary hypothesis look like, what would count as a pre-specified test, what would the null model be, and what evidence would show that the hypothesis was not reverse-engineered from the trait it explains? I am fluent in this vocabulary, so use the terms precisely rather than explaining them, and be honest about what the translation forces you to distort.

Then give me the residue report. I want to know which parts of the debate map cleanly onto model selection and which parts only map if you quietly change the meaning of the statistical terms. In particular, I suspect that a spandrel is not simply the null model, and that calling something an adaptation is not simply saying that a function fits a trait with low error; it is a claim about a historical causal process involving selection pressures, and that may have no counterpart in the vocabulary of fitted models. Tell me what does not survive the translation, and then say which side of the ledger is doing the work: is the statistical vocabulary missing something the debate genuinely turns on, or does the failed translation show that some of the philosophical talk is less precise than it sounds?
