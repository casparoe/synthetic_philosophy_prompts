I've been sitting with Chesterton's fence for years now — the principle that you shouldn't reform or tear down an institution until you understand why it was put there — and I've come around to the view that it's not just a conservative heuristic but the right epistemic default. I mean this as a claim about reasoning under uncertainty: evolved social arrangements encode more information than we can articulate, so the burden of proof on the reformer should be genuinely heavy. Call it Chesterton by way of Hayek, if you want a label.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this view. Not the standard progressive objections — I've taught those for two decades and I know where they land. I want the actual evidence, historical cases, and arguments that should move someone who holds this position. What would the world have to look like for the fence principle to be false or systematically misleading? Are there unambiguous historical cases where following the fence principle gave the wrong answer, and what do those cases tell us about it's proper scope? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Distinguish the ones a fence-defender would themselves concede as counting against them from the ones only critics think land. And give me an honest assessment of whether the principle has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's a problem for an epistemic principle of this kind, or whether heuristics are allowed to be insulated in exactly that way.
