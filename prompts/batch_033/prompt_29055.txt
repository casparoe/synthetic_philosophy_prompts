I've been collecting my own reactions to cases about when a person is in a position to make a flat-out assertion, and I'd like to know whether a single principle captures them or whether some of my reactions have to go. By "flat-out" I mean asserting that something is so, rather than hedging with "I think," "probably," or "I'd guess." In each case, I'm asking whether the speech act itself was in order, not merely whether the speaker was blameless or whether things turned out well.

Case 1 (the lottery). The drawing has been held but not announced, and there is one winning ticket among three hundred million. A friend is anxious, and I say flat out, "Your ticket didn't win." As it happens, the ticket is a loser. I think my assertion was improper: I was not in a position to say that, even though it was overwhelmingly likely to be true and was in fact true. Saying "It's overwhelmingly likely your ticket didn't win" would have been fine.

Case 2 (the stopped clock). I glance at a wall clock that reads 3:00 and tell someone, "It's 3 o'clock." Unbeknownst to me, the clock stopped exactly twenty-four hours earlier, and it happens to be 3 o'clock now. I think the assertion was proper: it was true, and I had no reason to distrust the clock.

Case 3 (the outdated departure board). A gate agent and the departure board both say my flight departs at 6:00. I tell a fellow passenger, "It departs at 6:00." In fact the gate was changed and the flight departs at 7:00, and every source I consulted was outdated. I think my assertion was proper—I did what any careful person would do—even though what I said was false.

Case 4 (the self-undermining assertion). I say, "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." Both halves could be true, but I think there is something deeply wrong with asserting this. Whatever the weather and whatever my state of mind, I am not in a position to put that forward flat out.

Case 5 (insincere but true). I tell a colleague, "The meeting is at noon," while fully believing it is at 2:00. It happens to be at noon. I think the assertion was improper: it came out right only by luck.

Case 6 (high stakes). I am about to miss a deadline to deposit a check, and from a hazy memory I tell my partner, "The bank is open Saturday." It is open Saturday. I think I should not have said that flat out; I should have checked or hedged.

Case 7 (lucky guess). Having no idea where the keys are, I say, to stop someone's searching, "The keys are in the drawer." They are in the drawer. I think the assertion was improper: I had no basis for it.

Case 8 (baseless certainty). I am seized by a powerful hunch—I would sincerely describe myself as completely certain—that the keys are in the drawer, though I have no evidence either way. I say, "The keys are in the drawer." They are in the drawer. I think the assertion was improper, my certainty notwithstanding: bare certainty without a basis is not enough.

The obvious rules each seem to fit some of these and collide with others. A knowledge rule—assert only what you know—agrees with me on 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8, but it would condemn 2 and 3, which I am reluctant to condemn. A truth rule—assert only what is true—agrees with me on 2, but conflicts with 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8. A justified-belief rule—assert only what you justifiably believe—agrees with me on 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, and 8, but it would license 1 and arguably 6. A certainty rule—assert only what you are certain of—agrees with me on 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7, but it would license 8, and its verdict on 4 is at best delicate.

Is there one principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all eight verdicts, or nearly all of them? If not, which of my reactions should I overturn, and why? And what does the best principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a doctor tells a patient, "The test came back negative," on the basis of a lab printout that is, unbeknownst to the doctor, erroneous—the test was actually positive. Is the doctor's assertion in order, and should the stakes of the case make a difference?
