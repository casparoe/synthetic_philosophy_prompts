So I run this small philosophy-of-mind reading group that's turned into a sort of informal podcast, and next week we're talking to someone who's been defending an Orwellian account of the timing of experience — specifically about the color phi phenomenon and related temporal illusions. She's argued that when subjects report seeing a moving dot change color midway between two flashed dots, the right story is that the brain retrospectively edits the conscious record: you had one experience and then, after later information comes in, that earlier experience gets overwritten. The Stalinesque alternative — that consciousness is just delayed until all the evidence is in and then constructs a seamless experience — is, on her view, either empirically false or conceptually confused.

I want to push her, not just have her restate the view. Here's what I've got so far for questions. I want the interview to feel like she's being made to earn the position, not just describe it. I'd like at least eight questions, each with a follow-up that anticipates her likely reply. And I want the whole thing to end with a question that asks her to take a definite, committed stand on something she might prefer to stay cagey about — so please don't give me questions that let her just say "well it depends what we mean by consciousness." I want her pinned down.

Here's where I'm starting from:

1. The standard knock on the Orwellian view is that it's a difference that makes no difference — if the net result is the same report and the same behavioral trace, haven't you just traded labels with the Stalinesque account? What's the most concrete thing she can point to that would actually come out different on her view, and what's her follow-up when I say "but that difference isn't about consciousness itself, it's about memory"?

2. I want to press on the metaphor. "Editing the record" makes it sound like there's a fixed experiential timeline that gets revised. But if the brain is just doing its thing, and there's no little archivist in there, what does "editing" actually refer to? My guess is she'll say it's about the temporal content of the representation vs. the time of the representing — so I need a follow-up that asks whether that distinction itself smuggles in a Cartesian theater.

3. The color phi case involves a very short interval — something like 200 ms. If the Orwellian revision happens that fast, in what sense is it a *revision* rather than just the normal settling time of a perceptual process? Is there a principled threshold between "normal processing" and "retrospective editing," and if she gives me one, I want to ask whether it's empirically motivated or just stipulated.

4. Dennett himself, who coined the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction, seemed to think the whole debate was a trap — that both positions assume a "finish line" in the brain where consciousness happens. Does she think Dennett was right about that, and if so, how does her Orwellian view avoid the trap? If she thinks Dennett was wrong, I want to know exactly where she parts ways with the multiple drafts model.

5. I want to bring in the cutaneous rabbit illusion (taps on the wrist, then elbow, but you feel them hopping up the arm). To me that feels like it favors the Stalinesque read — the brain waits for the later taps before constructing the conscious locations. Does she treat the rabbit differently from color phi, and if she gives me a unified treatment, what's the most awkward case for that unification?

6. There's a move in this literature where people say the whole debate is about "what the subject would say if probed at various intermediate moments," and that since we can't actually do that without disrupting the experience, the question is empirically empty. I want to know whether she thinks there's any possible experiment — even a sci-fi one — that could settle Orwellian vs. Stalinesque, and when she names one, I want to ask what she'd say if the experiment came out the other way.

7. The Libet timing results get dragged into this a lot — the readiness potential preceding the conscious decision, and so on. Does she think the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction sheds any light on Libet, or is that a different kind of timing question entirely? If she connects them, I want to know whether her view predicts anything about the timing of conscious will that a Stalinesque view wouldn't.

8. Finally, I want a question that forces commitment. Something like: if she had to bet her view on a single empirical finding about the brain that we don't yet have but might get in the next twenty years, what would that finding be? And then — this is the real follow-up — what would make her abandon the Orwellian view? I want her to state, in advance, what would count as falsification, because my sense is that people in this debate rarely do that.

Are these actually good questions? I feel like questions 2 and 3 might overlap too much, and maybe I'm missing a whole angle — like the relationship between this debate and the "overflow" argument (where people claim conscious experience is richer than what gets reported). Also, is there a question I should be asking about the connection to the hard problem or to higher-order thought theories that would put her on the back foot in a way these don't? I want her to leave the interview feeling like she had to defend the view, not just perform it. And I want the whole set to have a real arc, not just be eight disconnected challenges.
