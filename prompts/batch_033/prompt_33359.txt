I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I have been asked to serve as a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics course. The instructor would like me to grade the following exam question, and before I assign a mark and write my comments, I would appreciate a careful second opinion. Please provide a point-by-point assessment: identify what the student gets right, what the student gets wrong or conflates, and whether the reasoning in each part of the answer holds up. I would also like a suggested grade on a standard letter-grade scale, with a brief justification for the grade.

The exam question:

In the debate over the metaphysics of laws of nature, Humeans and anti-Humeans offer competing accounts of what it is for something to be a law. Lay out the core commitments of each camp, identify at least one major argument each side offers against the other, and assess whether the Humean can adequately distinguish laws from merely accidental regularities.

The student's answer:

The Humean view holds that laws of nature are nothing over and above the regularities that actually occur in the world. On this picture, a law such as "all electrons have charge –e" is simply a true universal generalization: it holds because every electron there is has that charge, and there is no further fact that makes it hold. The anti-Humean, by contrast, holds that laws govern or produce the regularities. Laws are necessary connections between properties, so it is not merely a coincidence that all electrons have the same charge; something about the nature of charge itself necessitates that electrons bear it. David Lewis's view is the classic anti-Humean account here, since he posits relations of nomic necessitation between universals.

The main anti-Humean argument against the Humean is that mere regularities cannot explain why the regularities hold. If all we have is a cosmic coincidence, then we have no reason to expect that the sun will rise tomorrow, that future electrons will behave like past ones, or that any inductive inference is warranted. The Humean replies that we can distinguish laws from accidental regularities by appealing to the fact that laws support counterfactuals: if something were an electron, it would have charge –e, whereas an accidental regularity such as "all the coins in my pocket are copper" does not support the corresponding counterfactual. But this reply seems circular, because counterfactual support is itself explained by laws, so the Humean is using the very notion of a law to explain what a law is.

The anti-Humean, for their part, has trouble explaining how we can ever come to know what the laws are. If laws are necessary connections between universals, then they go beyond anything we can observe. The Humean, by contrast, says that laws are just patterns in the observable world, so we can discover them through ordinary empirical investigation. This is a clear epistemic advantage for the Humean.

In my view, the Humean can adequately distinguish laws from accidental regularities. The Best System Account, developed by David Lewis, says that the laws are the axioms of the deductive system that achieves the best balance of simplicity and strength in describing the total history of the world. An accidental regularity such as "all the coins in my pocket are copper" would not appear in the best system because it is too localized and would add complexity without a corresponding gain in strength. So the Humean does have the resources to draw the distinction, and the anti-Humean's explanatory worries are misplaced.
