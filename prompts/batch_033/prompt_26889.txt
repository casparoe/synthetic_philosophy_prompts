I retired from molecular biology a few years ago and have been using the time to read philosophy of science seriously — something I always meant to do. One debate I keep returning to, and keep finding myself stuck on, is the one around Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks, together with the associated charge of greedy reductionism.

As I understand the landscape: Dennett argues that all the design we see in the biological world is the product of cranes — processes that build complexity step by step from simpler precursors, with natural selection as the paradigm case — and that appeals to skyhooks, or unexplained sources of order that somehow descend from above, are never legitimate. He also distinguishes his own view from what he calls greedy reductionism, which tries to collapse higher-level explanations directly into the lowest level without acknowledging that higher-level patterns are real and do genuine explanatory work. His critics, particularly those who defend some version of strong emergence or the causal autonomy of the special sciences, reply that Dennett's crane–skyhook dichotomy is itself question-begging: by labeling any appeal to irreducible higher-level causation a skyhook, he rules out their position by terminological fiat rather than by argument. Dennett in turn says that his critics are demanding a skyhook while calling it something else.

I have now read a fair amount on both sides, and what strikes me, coming from the sciences, is how little the debate seems to have moved in decades. The same moves and countermoves reappear. Both sides accuse the other of begging the question. And unlike the disputes I am used to, no one seems able to specify what new data or experimental result would settle the matter, or even shift the burden of proof.

So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a substantive disagreement about how the world is — about whether higher-level causation is genuinely autonomous or merely a useful façon de parler — or is it a disagreement about what counts as an acceptable explanation, such that the two sides are applying different standards and talking past each other? Could it be a verbal dispute about the word reduction that has hardened into a metaphysical one? Or is it something else? I want a diagnosis that engages with what each side actually says, not a survey of the positions. And given your diagnosis, I would like to know what, if anything, could move the debate forward — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, or perhaps the recognition that the question itself is ill-posed. Please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what remains contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
