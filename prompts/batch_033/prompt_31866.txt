My biology teacher recently introduced the species problem — the difficulty of giving a single definition of "species" that works across all organisms. She offered an analogy to help us think about it: a species, she said, is like a family. Membership in a family is determined by lineage and historical connection, not by checking off a list of traits (you don't stop being family just because you don't share your cousins' eye color), and there's no sharp fact about where the family ends. Is your fourth cousin still family? Your sixth? The boundary is vague because the concept itself is built around descent, not around a threshold of similarity. She used this analogy to support the conclusion that species boundaries are inherently fuzzy and that questions like "Are Neanderthals a separate species from Homo sapiens?" or "Are these two ring-species populations one species or two?" don't always have determinate answers — it depends on where we choose to draw the line.

I found this analogy persuasive, but I'm trying to figure out how much work it's actually doing. Does the conclusion survive the audit, or does the analogy quietly import assumptions — about conventionality, about the absence of sharp boundaries in nature — that the species case doesn't actually share?
