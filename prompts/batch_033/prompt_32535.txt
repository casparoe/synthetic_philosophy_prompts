I recently read a magazine column about conservatism that ran Burke and Chesterton together, and I want to know how much of it I should trust. The column's summary, as best I can reconstruct it, went roughly like this:

Burkean conservatism is, at bottom, the same idea Chesterton later illustrated with his fence: when you encounter a social institution, custom, or rule whose purpose you don't understand, you should leave it alone. The very fact that an institution has survived for generations is strong evidence that it is doing some important job, even if nobody alive can say what that job is. The accumulated wisdom of the past is stored in our inherited practices, so anyone who proposes to abolish or replace one of them bears a heavy burden of proof. Chesterton's point is that the reformer is usually wrong to tear the fence down, because the fence's mere persistence shows that earlier people, who in some respects knew better than we do, had good reason to put it there. So Burke and Chesterton are both best read as defending a general presumption in favor of the status quo, and a view on which most deliberate social change is a mistake.

Is this a fair account of either thinker? I suspect it runs them together too quickly and inflates a modest point into a sweeping one, but I don't know enough to say exactly where the summary stops being defensible. Please go through it claim by claim: which parts would a careful reader of Burke accept, which parts fit Chesterton's actual argument, where the column overstates or conflates distinct claims, and whether the concluding picture—a general presumption for the status quo—follows from anything accurate in the summary. Where you are least certain, tell me what would change your mind: which textual or argumentative considerations would push you toward a different verdict, and where your reading is most likely to go wrong.
