I'm a retired research biologist who has been reading philosophy for the last few years, and I've recently been working through the literature on personhood in African philosophy. One phrase I encounter constantly is "a person is a person through other persons" — the standard English rendering of the proverb associated with ubuntu. I've seen it put to work in strikingly different ways: sometimes to make the modest anthropological point that selves are socially formed, sometimes to ground a full-blown normative theory in which moral obligations are derived from relational rather than individual properties, and sometimes to argue that personhood itself is something one can achieve or fail to achieve depending on the quality of one's relationships with others. I'm struggling to disentangle what exactly is being claimed. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "a person is a person through other persons" might be asserting, ordered from the least to the most philosophically demanding? For each reading, I'd like to know: what exactly it asserts about the relation between personhood and others, what would count as a counterexample or evidence against it, whether it's a platitude or a substantive and defensible claim, and — crucially — which of these readings is actually doing the work when the slogan is deployed to challenge liberal individualist conceptions of the self or to ground duties of communal care.
