I keep returning to an argument I reconstructed from a debate about whether species are real, and I cannot find the step I am willing to give up, even though the conclusion strikes me as untenable. It runs as follows.

(1) Species are real units of the living world just in case, for any two organisms, there is a fact of the matter whether they are conspecific — whether they belong to the same species.

(2) For sexually reproducing organisms, the only serious candidate for supplying that fact is the interbreeding criterion: two organisms are conspecific just in case they can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.

(3) The interbreeding relation is not transitive. In a ring species, each population interbreeds with its neighbours along the chain, while the two end populations, though they meet geographically, do not interbreed with one another.

(4) By (2), conspecificity inherits this non-transitivity: an organism in one end population is conspecific with an organism in the next population, which is conspecific with an organism in the next, and so on around the ring, yet the organisms in the two end populations are not conspecific with each other.

(5) But conspecificity is a sameness relation — being the same species — and sameness is transitive.

(6) From (4) and (5), the organisms in the two end populations are both conspecific and not conspecific. Contradiction.

(7) The interbreeding criterion is therefore not the correct account of conspecificity, and no other criterion is available that could do better.

(8) So there is no fact of the matter about conspecificity.

(9) By (1), species are not real.

That conclusion seems to prove too much. If it were right, systematics, ecology, and conservation biology would be quantifying over fictions, and by parity of reasoning any category defined through overlapping local relations — dialects, perhaps, or languages — would be unreal as well. I would most like to keep the reality of species, and I am prepared to surrender whichever premise is doing the illicit work, but I cannot identify it. Which premise or inference would you reject, why did it look compulsory, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, lay out what each of the main positions — species as classes, species as individuals, species pluralism, and species anti-realism — has to pay to escape the contradiction.
