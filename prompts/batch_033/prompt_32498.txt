I'm taking a philosophy of medicine course this semester, and after a lecture on the concept of disease, two classmates got into an argument that I've been turning over ever since. I'm hoping you can help me work through who's right about what.

The lecture covered the debate between naturalism and normativism about disease. The naturalist view, roughly, is that health and disease are objective, value-free concepts: a disease is a deviation from species-typical biological functioning, and calling something a disease is a factual claim about how the organism is working. The normativist view is that the concept of disease is irreducibly value-laden: to call something a disease is to judge that it's a bad state to be in, one that warrants medical attention. The professor gave examples on both sides, and then the discussion broke out after class. Here's what I remember of the exchange:

(1) David started by saying he found naturalism obviously correct. He said: "Take a bacterial infection. The bacteria are disrupting normal tissue function. That's just a biological fact about the body. Whether we *like* it or not is a separate question. The concept of disease has to be grounded in biology, otherwise we're just calling things diseases because we don't like them."

(2) Amara pushed back: "But what about infertility? It's a deviation from species-typical reproductive function, so on the naturalist account it's a disease. Yet whether someone actually *has* a disease seems to depend on whether they want children. A postmenopausal woman isn't considered diseased, and neither is someone who's had a voluntary sterilization. The biological fact alone doesn't settle it."

(3) David replied: "Those are cases where the dysfunction has a known, non-pathological cause — aging or a surgical procedure. The naturalist can say that the concept of disease only applies to *pathological* deviations from typical function, not all deviations. Infertility from blocked fallopian tubes is a disease; infertility from a tubal ligation is not. The underlying biology is different."

(4) Amara said: "But now you're smuggling in the very value judgments you said you were avoiding. You're distinguishing 'pathological' from 'non-pathological' deviations, but what counts as a normal process is partly a social judgment. Aging is normal, yet we still call osteoporosis a disease. A surgeon cutting into someone is normally a harm, but when it's consensual surgery we call it therapeutic. So you're already making evaluative distinctions just to draw the boundary you're relying on."

(5) David came back: "I think you're conflating the *concept* of disease with the *application* of the concept. We can agree that there are borderline cases where our values affect how we draw the line, but that doesn't mean the concept itself is fundamentally evaluative. The paradigm cases — infections, cancer, genetic disorders — are clearly biological dysfunctions. The borderline cases don't change what the concept is about."

(6) Amara answered: "But the history of medicine is full of cases where the 'paradigm' shifted because of changing values, not changing biology. Homosexuality was classified as a disease and then declassified — not because the biology changed, but because our values did. If the concept were truly value-free, that shouldn't be possible. The fact that we can debate whether something like ADHD or obesity is a disease shows that values are doing work right at the core of the concept, not just at the margins."
