I'm a novelist — I've published five books, I teach fiction workshops, and I've spent two decades watching readers sob over people who never drew breath. I've done it myself. And yet every time the conversation turns to how fiction gets its grip on us, someone — a reviewer, an interviewer, a student — reaches for the phrase "willing suspension of disbelief" as though that settles the matter. It has never settled anything for me. When a reader weeps at a death I wrote, they are not, in any ordinary sense, believing that someone has died; they know perfectly well the character is ink on paper. And "suspension" suggests a voluntary act, something we decide to do and can stop doing, but the grief fiction produces is often involuntary, and it can outlast the book by days. So I want to know what this slogan could actually be claiming. I suspect it's doing several different kinds of work for different people, and I need those teased apart. Lay out the distinct precise theses that "willing suspension of disbelief" might be expressing — from the most deflationary, almost trivial reading to the most metaphysically ambitious — and for each one, tell me whether it's plausible, whether it's merely a redescription of the phenomenon rather than an explanation of it, and whether it genuinely accounts for the involuntary and enduring emotional engagement with fiction that the slogan is routinely invoked to explain. I am not looking for the one right answer; I want a menu I can think with.
