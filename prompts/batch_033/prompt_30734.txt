I'm a novelist, and I'm working on a book set around the Mauerschützen trials — the prosecutions of East German border guards after reunifcation for shooting people who tried to cross the Berlin Wall. My protagonist is a guard who keeps saying he was just following the law as it then was, and the novel turns on how much weight that defense should carry. I know the broad shape of the Hart–Fuller debate, and I know the German courts leaned on something like the Radbruch formula, that extreme injustice is not law. But I don't want the standard summary; I want the case run through both positions properly. Start with Hart: on his positivism, was the guard following law when he pulled the trigger, and if the court wants to convict him anyway, what does Hart say it should do — pass a retrospective statute, bite the bullet on retroactivity, what? Then do Fuller: given how the border regime actually worked, the shoot-to-kill standing orders, the total ban on exit, the fact that the victim never had any say or notice or chance to contest anything — does Fuller think this was law at all, and what specifically fails in his internal morality of law? Then the harder part: where does each theory genuinely illuminate the real trial and where does it have to strain — does Hart's line that the guard was legally covered but morally guilty actually fit what the guard experienced as legality, and does Fuller's account let him dodge the fact that the guard took himself to be obeying a legal system? Finally, what does the episode do to the debate — does it make positivism look like the honest position that history vindicated, or make Fuller look prescient, or does it show both of them were never quite aimed at cases like this? I want the mapping tight and the silences named, not a canned verdict.
