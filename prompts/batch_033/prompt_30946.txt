Draft the joint statement that a Hartian liberal about the criminal law and a Devlin-style legal moralist could both sign at the conclusion of a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the Hart–Devlin debate over the enforcement of morality. Work from the actual positions, not the caricatures: the Hartian holds that the prevention of harm to others is the only firm ground for legal coercion and that the enforcement of morality as such is not a proper aim of the law; the Devlinian holds that a society's shared morality is partly constitutive of that society's existence, so that a society is entitled to use the criminal law to protect its moral structure against conduct that would undermine it even where no individual is directly harmed.

The document should contain the following components.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims, non-vacuous and stated so that both sides would sign them. They should cover at least: why some shared moral standards matter for the existence of a legal order; the limits on what the criminal law may properly punish; what the proposed decriminalization of private consensual homosexual conduct actually involved; and what the bare fact of majority disapproval does and does not establish.

2. A disagreements table. For each genuine residual disagreement, give the question at issue, the Hartian answer phrased so a Hartian would accept it, and the Devlinian answer phrased so a Devlinian would accept it. At minimum include their disagreement about whether the criminal law may enforce a society's shared morality as such, and their disagreement about what should count as a threat to a society's existence.

3. A cruxes section identifying the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side. Include at least these: (a) whether there is historical or comparative evidence that relaxing the legal enforcement of a shared moral rule causes social disintegration rather than mere moral change; (b) whether the criminal law can reliably identify a society's genuinely shared morality without collapsing into the enforcement of majority prejudice; (c) whether, from the fact that some shared morality is a condition of a society's existence, it follows that the criminal law is entitled to enforce whatever morality is in fact shared; and (d) whether the ordinary person's intolerance, indignation, or disgust at conduct is a reliable indicator that that conduct threatens social cohesion.

4. For each crux, specify the kind of case, argument, or evidence both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

5. A closing note identifying any part of the dispute that is, on inspection, verbal rather than substantive — for instance, disagreements traceable to different senses of 'society' or of 'enforcing morality' — and state what remains once those ambiguities are clarified.
