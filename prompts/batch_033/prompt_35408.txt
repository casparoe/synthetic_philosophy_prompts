I'm a novelist working on a book whose central plot turns on a moral question I cannot afford to get wrong: whether, and in what way, the gains to a majority can justify severe losses imposed on a minority. Over the past year I've read deeply on both sides of the separateness-of-persons debate—the objection, pressed most famously by Rawls, that utilitarianism illicitly extends the principle of rational choice for a single life to society as a whole, ignoring the fact that no one experiences the aggregated utility across separate lives; and the various utilitarian replies, from those who argue that equal consideration of each person's utility already respects separateness, to those who distinguish the criterion of rightness from the decision procedure, to those who bite the bullet and argue that aggregation across lives is no more mysterious than aggregation across moments within a life.

I now have two characters in my head, each a stand-in for one of these positions, and I need to know exactly where they genuinely disagree and where they have been talking past each other. I want you to draft the joint statement that a committed defender of the separateness-of-persons objection and a committed utilitarian could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should have the following structure:

First, a numbered section of claims both sides can accept. These should be substantive—not the thinnest common ground, but the strongest claims each is willing to grant the other. They should cover what utilitarianism actually says, what the objection actually charges, what kinds of cases are at stake, and what each side agrees would count as a genuine problem for its own view.

Second, a table or clearly demarcated section stating the residual disagreements. Each disagreement must be formulated so that its own side would accept the wording—no straw men, no caricatures. I want to know whether the disagreement is about the fundamental structure of morality, about the implications of each view in hard cases, about the relationship between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure, about the metaphysics of persons, or about something else.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, provide the case, argument, or empirical finding that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case.

Fourth, if any part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal—a disagreement about words rather than substance—identify it and explain why.

I am not looking for a dramatized dialogue or a verdict on who is right. I want a constructive document that both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact and the terms of any future resolution laid out clearly. American English, please.
