I am a novelist at work on a book whose central subject is the way a single decision — whether to build a seawall or retreat inland — plays out across five generations of a coastal family. The novel's sections move backward in time, so the reader first meets the last generation and only later discovers what the first generation chose and why. I want to open the book with a villanelle that serves as an epigraph and captures the philosophical problem at the novel's heart: pure time preference, the question of whether we are rationally justified in caring less about future wellbeing simply because it lies further in the future.

The two refrains of the villanelle should voice the two sides of the debate — roughly, the Sidgwickian principle that temporal location is no more relevant to value than spatial location, and the claim that a rational agent may defensibly discount the distant. The villanelle's form, with its refrains taking turns and finally meeting in the closing couplet, should do the structural work of showing how these positions keep circling each other without resolution, just as the novel's generations keep inheriting the same dilemma in different weather.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the philosophical literature on intertemporal discounting would recognize the positions, and good enough as a poem that a reader who has never heard of Ramsey or Parfit would feel the question's weight.

Please provide the poem, followed by a section titled "Commentary" (roughly 200–300 words) explaining the formal and philosophical choices you made and how the villanelle's structure tracks the argument.
