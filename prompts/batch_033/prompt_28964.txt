So I've been going to this philosophy of mind reading group — we're working through some papers on consciousness, mostly Dennett and Frankish and people responding to them — and the phrase "consciousness is an illusion" keeps coming up in ways that seem completely different each time. Like, one person will say it and mean "the sense that there's a Cartesian theater with a little homunculus watching the show is false" (which, fine, nobody actually believes in the homunculus, right?). But then someone else will say it and seem to mean something much stronger, like there literally are no qualitative experiences, the whole thing is a user-interface metaphor the brain presents to itself. And then a third person chimes in with something about how meditation reveals that the self is an illusion and they seem to think that's the same claim. Last week the discussion got genuinely heated because one guy insisted the slogan means we should stop talking about "what it's like" altogether, and someone else shot back that that's just changing the subject.

I'm trying to get clear on what's actually being asserted versus what's being denied when this sentence gets deployed, because I suspect people are using the same three words to pick out five different philosophical positions and then arguing past each other. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that "consciousness is an illusion" could be expressing? For each one, I want to know: what exactly is being called illusory (phenomenal properties? the subject? introspection? the hard problem?), what would be left if the claim were true, whether it's a trivial point or a radical one, and which philosophers or traditions are actually defending that specific reading. Also: are any of these readings genuinely incompatible with each other, or could someone consistently hold several of them at once? I basically want a menu I can bring to the next meeting so that when someone drops the slogan I can say "wait, which of these do you mean" instead of everyone nodding along to different claims.
