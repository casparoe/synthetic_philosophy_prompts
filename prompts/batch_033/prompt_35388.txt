In my AP Biology class we spent a week on the definition of life, and I ended up constructing an argument that seems to prove something I don't believe. I have been going back and forth with it and cannot figure out where it goes wrong. I would like your diagnosis of the argument, and when you respond, please write in continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; just plain paragraphs, the way you would write a letter.

The argument runs like this. (1) Either there is a fact of the matter about whether something is alive, or labeling something alive is just a decision about how to use the word. (2) If there is a fact of the matter, there must be a set of necessary and sufficient conditions that pick out all and only the living things — a definition that does not simply track our intuitions about particular cases. (3) Any such definition will either include some things we strongly want to exclude, or exclude some things we strongly want to include. (4) Consider metabolism. If we require autonomous metabolism, we rule out viruses, which have no metabolism of their own. But we also seem to rule out bacterial endospores that show no detectable metabolism for years, and arguably obligate intracellular parasites like Rickettsia that depend on host ATP — yet we confidently call both of those alive. (5) If we drop the metabolism requirement to admit viruses, we then have to explain why fire is not alive: fire consumes fuel, grows, and spreads. If we answer that fire lacks genetic material and does not evolve, we are back to the same problem — we have already seen that borderline cases pressure us to relax criteria, so why should lacking genetic material disqualify fire when lacking metabolism did not disqualify viruses? (6) It looks as though we can keep adding criteria — carbon-based, cellular, membrane-bound — but each new addition just pushes the problem to a new borderline case: synthetic cells, self-replicating nanobots, prions. The project starts to look less like discovering a real boundary in nature and more like patching a definition to match our pre-theoretical judgments about what should count. (7) If no definition can be made to work in a principled way, then there is no fact of the matter about whether something is alive. The concept is just a cluster of properties with no sharp boundary, and calling something alive is a practical decision, not a discovery. (8) But if that is true, then the entire field of biology — and astrobiology, and the search for life on other planets — is studying something whose boundaries are a matter of convention rather than nature. That seems absurd.

I suspect the argument slips somewhere around step (6) or (7), but I am not sure how to stop it without either giving up on the idea that life has a real boundary in nature or accepting that the boundary is vague in a way that does not threaten biology's status as a science. I want to know which premise you would reject, and what it costs you to reject it. If I have to choose, I would rather keep the idea that biology studies something real and that the question of whether something is alive is not merely verbal. But I want to know honestly what I am paying.
