I'm a journalist working on a longread about whether religious arguments have a legitimate place in democratic politics, and I keep tripping over the civil rights movement. You know the Rawlsian public reason story: when we're hashing out constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, citizens owe each other reasons that everyone can reasonably accept — so no appealing to your particular comprehensive doctrine, religious or otherwise. But then you look at the civil rights movement and it's drenched in explicitly religious argument. MLK's Letter from Birmingham Jail is basically a sermon, and the whole movement ran on the language of imago dei and divine justice and the beloved community. And it's not like this was some periphral thing — the religious arguments seem to have been doing real normative work, not just motivational cheerleading.

So here's what I want: a careful analysis of the civil rights movement as a test case for public reason. I don't need the history — I know the history. What I need is the mapping. Does the movement fit the public reason framework, and if so, at what cost? Walk me through the options a Rawlsian has. The translation proviso says you can bring in comprehensive doctrines as long as in due course you supply public reasons too — did MLK do that? Was he supplying public reasons alongside the theological ones, or was the theological stuff actually doing the justificatory work? Then there's the move that says the movement was operating in a non-ideal context where the basic structure was unjust, so the usual rules don't fully apply. And there's the move that says the movement wasn't addressing constitutional essentials at all, though that seems like a stretch when they were literally challenging the constitutional order.

And then I want you to flip it around: what does this episode show about public reason that the theory might not have seen coming? Does it reveal that the translation proviso is too neat — that some normative insights really do depend on comprehensive doctrines and can't be cleanly translated without loss? Or does it show the opposite: that the movement's arguments were translatable all along, and the religious language was just the idiom, not the engine? Be honest about where the theory strains and where it holds. I'm not looking for a verdict so much as a map of the difficulties.
