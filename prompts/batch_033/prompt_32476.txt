I've been going down a modal logic rabbit hole lately and a friend showed me a proof that I can't shake. I write code for a living, so I keep thinking there's got to be an off-by-one error in here somewhere, but I can't spot it. I'll number the steps.

(1) Take the principle that every true statement is knowable. Not that it's actually known, just that it could in principle be known by someone. Like, if it's true that there's a teapot on my desk, someone could know that.

(2) Now take any true statement that nobody ever actually knows. Say, "the number of coins in this jar right now is even." Suppose it's true, but nobody ever checks and nobody could ever check.

(3) Build a bigger statement out of it: "that statement is true, and nobody ever knows that statement." Since both halves hold, this bigger statement is true.

(4) Apply the principle from (1) to the bigger statement. It has to be knowable. So there's some possible situation where somebody knows the whole conjunction.

(5) Knowing a conjunction means knowing each conjunct. So this person knows the original statement, and they also know "nobody ever knows the original statement."

(6) Knowledge implies truth. So if they know "nobody ever knows the original statement," then it's true that nobody ever knows it.

(7) But that contradicts the fact that they know the original statement. If one person knows it, then it's false that nobody ever knows it.

(8) So the bigger statement from (3) can't be knowable after all. But by (1), every true statement is knowable, so the bigger statement must not be true.

(9) That means: for any statement p, it can't be the case that p is true and nobody ever knows p. In other words, every true statement is actually known.

Step (9) is obviously false — the jar again. So a principle that sounded totally harmless apparently proves that every truth is known. My friend says this is a real named result (Fitch? Church? I forget) and that people take it seriously. I don't want to just throw out (1), because "truths are knowable" seems like the whole point of thinking truth is the kind of thing minds can in principle get at. I also don't want to throw out (5) or (6), since those feel like the definition of knowing a conjunction and the definition of knowledge. So where's the bug? Is it a scope issue with "nobody ever knows," like variable capture in a closure? Or is (1) secretly way stronger than it sounds? I don't know wether to trust my own reading here. Tell me which step to give up and what it costs, and don't just say "it's a known paradox" — I want the actual diagnosis.
