I've been going back and forth on whether "all-things-considered better than" is transitive, and my verdicts on concrete cases are inconsistent enough that I can't tell what principle I'm actually committed to. I'm hoping someone can look at the pattern and extract the rule I'm implicitly using — or tell me which verdicts I need to revise.

Here are the cases I keep returning to:

1. **The headache chain.** A migraine is worse than a moderate headache, which is worse than a mild headache, which is worse than a barely-detectable twinge, which is worse than no pain at all. If you chain fifty "just barely worse than" comparisons, you can get from a migraine to perfect comfort. I do not think this means perfect comfort is worse than a migraine. My verdict: the chain does not preserve "worse than." Transitivity fails here, or the sorites character of "barely worse than" means the relation was never transitive to begin with.

2. **The population chain.** Start with ten billion people living genuinely excellent lives. Compare that to eleven billion people whose lives are 99.9% as good — slightly less flourishing per person, but more total wellbeing. I think the second world is at least as good. Now iterate: each step adds a billion people and shaves off a tiny fraction of per-person wellbeing. After enough steps, you reach a vast population of people with lives barely worth living. I do not think that world is better than the starting world of ten billion excellent lives. So transitivity seems to fail here too.

3. **The penny pump.** I prefer $10.01 to $10.00, $10.02 to $10.01, and so on. If you offer me a chain of trades where I pay a penny to swap each amount for the next one down, I end up paying you to cycle back to where I started. But here I don't think "better than" is intransitive — I think my pairwise choices were individually rational given the trivial stakes, and the pump exploits the fact that I shouldn't have bothered expressing a preference at that granularity. The underlying betterness relation is transitive; I just failed to track it. This case feels categorically different from the first two.

4. **Commute and salary.** I'd trade a 30-minute commute for a 25-minute one at the same salary. I'd trade a 25-minute commute and $80k for a 20-minute commute and $79.5k. If I chain enough of these — shaving five minutes of commute for a $500 salary cut — I eventually "prefer" a zero-minute commute and $30k to a 60-minute commute and $120k, which I emphatically do not. But my intuition here aligns with the penny pump: I have vague preferences at the margin, and the chain exploits vagueness rather than revealing a genuine intransitivity. The relation "determinately better than" is transitive; "marginally preferred in a forced choice" is not, and the chain conflates them.

5. **AI safety and capability.** We're comparing AI systems along two dimensions. System A is slightly safer but slightly less capable than B; B is slightly safer but slightly less capable than C; and so on. After a long chain, I'm comparing a provably safe but commercially useless system to a transformative but existentially dangerous one. I want to say the safe system is all-things-considered better, even though I endorsed each pairwise trade along the chain. This feels like it might be a genuine intransitivity rather than vagueness, because safety and capability might be incommensurable in a way that headache intensity and dollar amounts are not. But I'm not confident.

6. **Intergenerational choice.** Take the population chain from case 2, but now imagine each step is chosen by a different generation that slightly prefers the next world. Each generation's preference seems reasonable given their circumstances. If "better than" is transitive, the first generation ought to endorse the final world. They don't, and I don't think they're making a mistake. But if I deny transitivity here, I need to explain why the chain of reasonable pairwise preferences doesn't add up to a reasonable overall preference — and I find myself wanting transitivity to hold in this intergenerational setting more than I do in the headache setting, without a clear justification for the asymmetry.

So I have three cases where I want to deny transitivity (headaches, population, intergenerational choice), two where I want to affirm it and blame vagueness instead (pennies, commute-salary), and one where I'm genuinely unsure (AI safety-capability). Is there a single principle that draws the line in the right place — that tells me when an apparent intransitivity is real and when it's an artifact of vague predicates or forced choices at the margin? If no one principle covers all six, what's the smallest set that does, and which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon?

I'm especially interested in what the principle predicts for a case I haven't been able to settle: comparing different long-term futures for humanity, where each step in a chain trades off a small amount of average wellbeing for a slightly lower probability of existential catastrophe. Does the chain preserve "better than" or not?
