Question (20 points). State the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument for mathematical platonism in premise–conclusion form. Then explain how a nominalist might resist the argument, and assess whether that response succeeds. Be explicit about which premise the nominalist rejects.

Student answer. The Quine–Putnam indispensability argument runs as follows.

(1) We ought to have ontological commitment to all and only the entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
(2) Mathematical objects are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
(3) Therefore, we ought to have ontological commitment to mathematical objects.

Premise (1) derives from Quine's criterion of ontological commitment. On that criterion, a theory is committed to the entities that must be the values of its bound variables if the theory is to be true. Since science is our best guide to what exists, and since mathematics is indispensable to science, platonism follows.

A nominalist might resist premise (2) by saying that mathematics is merely a useful instrument. But this response fails, because mathematics is not dispensable in practice: Newtonian physics needs differential equations, and quantum mechanics needs Hilbert spaces. Hartry Field tried to reject premise (1) instead. He showed that Newtonian gravitation theory can be reformulated without quantifying over numbers, by using comparative predicates and representation theorems. If this kind of reformulation could be extended to all of science, mathematical objects would not be indispensable and the argument would collapse. But Field only succeeded for Newtonian physics, and there are known obstacles for quantum mechanics. So the indispensability argument's conclusion is secure.
