I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on democratic epistemology, and I'm trying to get my views into defensible shape before my prospectus meeting. I've been reading through the Condorcet jury theorem literature — List and Goodin, Estlund, Landemore, and the surrounding debates — and I've found myself pulled in several directions that I'm not sure are compatible. I want to know whether the set of commitments I've settled on actually hangs together, or whether I'm papering over tensions that my committee will spot immediately.

Here's roughly where I've landed. (a) The Condorcet jury theorem is the most promising epistemic justification for democratic majority rule going. If each voter has a better-than-chance probability of being right about some question, and votes are independent, the probability that the majority gets it right converges to certainty as the electorate grows. That's a genuine normative reason to prefer democracy to non-democratic arrangements, not just a procedural one. (b) That said, the independence condition is almost certainly violated in any actual democracy — voters share media ecosystems, party cues, and cultural narratives, and their judgments are correlated in ways that can dramatically reduce or even reverse the theorem's epistemic benefits. So the CJT cannot be straightforwardly applied to real political life. (c) Even so, I think majoritarian democracy is epistemically superior to the available alternatives — epistocracy, sortition, expert delegation — for most of the questions political communities face. This isn't just a pragmatic concession; I think there's something about the aggregation of diverse perspectives under majority rule that tracks the truth better than the alternatives do. (d) However, I also think there are identifiable domains — monetary policy, climate science, pharmaceutical regulation — where the competence differential between experts and lay voters is so large that policy should be insulated from majoritarian pressure and placed in the hands of expert bodies. I don't think this is just a matter of efficiency; I think it's an epistemic requirement. (e) I hold that all citizens have equal moral standing and that political institutions must express equal respect for persons, which is why I reject epistocracy even where weighting votes by competence would yield epistemically more accurate outcomes. (f) Finally, I'm sympathetic to the idea that the value of democracy is partly constitutive rather than purely instrumental — that democratic procedures don't just track independently specifiable truths but partly constitute what it is for a political community to govern itself well.

I can feel that some of these are pulling against each other, but I'm not confident I can identify exactly which pairs are the real problems and which look like problems only at first glance. Please go through and tell me which commitments are in tension, rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Also flag any apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection — I want to know what I don't need to worry about. Keep the response to no more than 1,000 words.
