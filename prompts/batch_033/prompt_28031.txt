I've been turning over an argument from the philosophy of physics that seems to prove we live in a block universe — one in which past, present, and future events are all equally real, and the passage of time is an illusion. Each step feels hard to deny, but the conclusion strikes me as incredible. I want to know where the argument goes wrong, or if it doesn't, which premise I should give up and what I lose by doing so.

Here is the argument as I understand it:

(1) In special relativity, there is no absolute, frame-independent simultaneity relation between spatially separated events. Whether two such events occur "at the same time" depends on one's reference frame, and no inertial frame is physically privileged over any other.

(2) Two events are simultaneous in some admissible inertial frame if and only if they are spacelike separated — that is, if the spatial distance between them exceeds the distance light could travel in the time that separates them, so that no causal signal can connect them.

(3) If two events are simultaneous in a given reference frame, then an observer using that frame is correct to regard them as equally real and co-present. Simultaneity in a frame is that frame's criterion of what exists now.

(4) Whether an event is real cannot be a merely frame-relative matter. If an event counts as real in any admissible frame, then it is real simpliciter. Reality does not depend on how fast one happens to be moving.

(5) Now take any event we all agree is real — say, your reading this sentence right now. And take any event in your absolute future — say, your sneezing tomorrow. These two are timelike separated (the sneeze lies in this event's future light cone), so no frame makes them simultaneous. But introduce a third event E: a lightning strike on a planet in a galaxy two million light-years away, occurring right now in our frame. E is spacelike separated from your reading this sentence (spatial separation two million light-years, temporal separation zero), so by (2) they are simultaneous in our frame, and by (3) E is real. Now consider E and your sneezing tomorrow: their spatial separation is still roughly two million light-years, and their temporal separation is only one day — far less than the light-travel time — so they too are spacelike separated. By (2), there exists another frame, moving rapidly relative to us, in which E and your sneezing tomorrow are simultaneous. By (3), in that frame, your sneeze is real. And by (4), reality is not frame-relative, so your sneezing tomorrow is real simpliciter, right now.

(6) The same chaining strategy works for any pair of events whatsoever. Therefore every event in the past, present, and future of every reference frame is equally real. The entire four-dimensional manifold exists tenselessly. The block universe is inescapable.

I find (6) hard to accept, but I cannot see a clean way out. I am especially reluctant to reject (1), since that would amount to denying special relativity, which is extraordinarily well confirmed empirically. I am also uneasy about denying (4), since that seems to make reality itself depend on how fast you are moving, which strikes me as a kind of idealism about existence — as though speeding up could bring things into being. The move from (3) and (4) to the chaining in (5) feels like the place where something might have slipped: perhaps an equivocation on "real" between its intra-frame and trans-frame uses, or a hidden assumption that co-reality is transitive across reference frames when simultaneity plainly is not. But I cannot make the diagnosis precise.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If you think it is valid, which premise is least costly to abandon, and what does abandoning it commit you to? If you think there is a subtle flaw, identify the step and explain why it looked innocent.
