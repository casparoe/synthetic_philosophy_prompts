I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on interactive epistemology and the foundations of game theory. I've been asked to guest-teach a 75-minute session in an advanced undergraduate seminar on rational choice (about 18 students, most with some exposure to normal-form games but not to extensive-form games with perfect information). The topic is the backward induction paradox and the centipede game. I want to run an activity where students actually play a version of the centipede game before they've heard the term or seen the paradox formally laid out, so that they feel the tension between the backward-induction prescription and what they actually do, and between what they do as a player and what they believe about the other player's reasoning.

The goals are: (1) they experience the pull of the cooperative outcome viscerally, (2) they see that backward induction has genuine force when you reason through it step by step, and (3) they grasp that the paradox is not merely about a failure of rationality but about a deep tension between reasoning from the end of the tree and reasoning from the beginning, and about what it means to model another player as rational.

Please design the activity. Specify: the centipede game variant I should use (number of moves, payoff structure, and why that variant rather than the standard six-move version), whether to run it as a sequence of one-shot anonymous pairings or as a tournament or as something else, how to assign partners, what stakes to use that don't require real money, and a timeline for the 75 minutes. Include a structured debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the logical structure of the backward induction argument, the relationship to Aumann's result that common knowledge of rationality implies the backward induction outcome, and the main families of resolution (e.g., players doubt each other's rationality, players have social preferences, players treat the game as a signaling problem, bounded rationality or limited depth of reasoning). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—common confusions, cynical play, the activity falling flat—and how to handle each. Be honest about which features of the philosophical debate the activity cannot reproduce and what I will need to supply separately through lecture or discussion.
