I'm writing a paper for an upper-level aesthetics course on the problem of separating the work from the artist, and I've grown frustrated with how much of the philosophical literature stays at the level of tidy hypotheticals — the imaginary Nazi whose landscapes are sublime, the thought-experiment paedophile whose sonnets are exquisite. I want to test the standard positions against a real institutional crisis where the question stopped being a seminar-room exercise and became a live dispute with real stakes, real institutions, and an actual public that had to decide what to do.

The episode I have in mind is the 2020 César Awards ceremony. Roman Polanski's film *An Officer and a Spy* received twelve nominations, and when he won the award for Best Director, several actresses — prominently Adèle Haenel — walked out of the ceremony in protest. Polanski had fled the United States in 1978 after pleading guilty to unlawful sexual intercourse with a thirteen-year-old, and by 2020, in the wake of the #MeToo movement, the French film industry was bitterly divided over whether honoring him was defensible. The protestors held that conferring the award sent a public message that artistic achievement outweighs moral accountability, effectively telling survivors that their claims do not matter. Defenders of the award argued that the work should be judged on its own terms, independently of the filmmaker's biography, and that a prize for directing is a judgment about craft, not a referendum on character. The Académie des César itself was already under fire for its opaque governance and its lack of diversity, which meant the Polanski controversy was never just about one director — it became a proxy for deeper questions about who gets to make art, who gets honored, and what institutions are for.

I want you to analyze the César Awards controversy through the lens of the philosophical debate about whether an artist's moral character can affect the aesthetic value of their work. Map the four standard positions onto the episode: autonomism (the aesthetic and the moral are independent domains, so the jury's job was simply to assess the film's artistic merits and nothing else), ethicism (a moral flaw in the artist is always an aesthetic flaw in the work, so awarding Polanski was an aesthetic mistake as well as a moral one), moderate moralism (the artist's moral character is aesthetically relevant only when it is manifest in or relevant to the work's content or reception), and the "immoralist" position (a morally transgressive artist might produce aesthetically better work precisely because of, not despite, their transgressions). For each position, tell me what it would say about whether the jury should have awarded Polanski the prize, and whether it would side with the protestors or the defenders. Then I want the harder part: tell me what the episode reveals about the limits of these positions. Does the fact that this was a public ceremony — an act of institutional endorsement, not a private act of aesthetic judgment — change the analysis in ways the philosophical debate isn't equipped to handle? Does the institutional context (an awards body with a history of exclusion, a set of power dynamics in the French film industry, an audience that extends far beyond the jury) introduce considerations that the standard positions, with their focus on the individual critic confronting the individual work, simply don't capture? Are any of the four positions forced into conclusions that look plausible in the abstract but become hard to defend when you imagine actually standing in that auditorium? And finally: does the episode push us toward any particular position in the debate, or does it suggest that the whole framing — the question of whether the work can be "separated" from the artist — is the wrong question to be asking?
