I've been following the philosophical literature on promising for a couple of years now, and I've hit a wall that I suspect is a feature of the debate rather than a failure of my reading. The question is deceptively simple: why do promises bind? But the leading answers — practice-based accounts, expectation-based accounts, and will-based accounts — have been refining themselves against counterexamples for decades without any apparent convergence. Each side can handle the others' paradigm cases by adding refinements, and each side has a story about why the others' central intuitions are misguided. The debate hasn't moved, and I want to know whether that's because it can't.

Here's my working hypothesis. I think the disagreement isn't really about which moral principle best explains promissory obligation. It's about what kind of thing a moral explanation is supposed to do. The practice theorist looks at promising and sees a social institution that needs justifying: how does the convention of promising generate obligations, and what makes the convention itself justified? The expectation theorist looks at promising and sees an individual transaction: what is it about my voluntarily leading you to rely on me that makes my failure to perform a wrong against you? The will theorist looks at promising and sees a normative power: how does an agent, by an act of will, transfer a right or impose a duty on herself that wasn't there before? If I'm right, then the three camps are answering different questions under the same heading, and the appearance of a single substantive dispute is sustained by the fact that all three questions can be asked using the same English sentence: "Why do promises bind?"

But I'm not confident in this diagnosis. What worries me is that each camp does seem to think it's disagreeing with the others — they don't just say "that's a different question," they say "that account gets the wrong answer in the following case." So here's what I want: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it, as I suspect, three explanatory projects talking past each other, with the surface clash generated by counterexamples that each project can redescribe in its own terms? Or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath — and if so, what is it about? Is it a disagreement about which cases are central and which are peripheral, about bedrock moral intuitions that neither side can argue for, about the metaphysics of obligation, or about something else altogether? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a new kind of case, a formal result, a change of question — or should we expect the impasse to persist because the question, as asked, is ill-posed?
