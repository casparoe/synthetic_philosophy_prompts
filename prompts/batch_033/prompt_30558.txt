I'm trying to work through the Grand Inquisitor chapter of *The Brothers Karamazov* for a reading group and there's a bit I keep going back and forth on. Here's the passage (from the Garnett translation). The Inquisitor is speaking to Christ, who hasn't said a word:

"Thou didst not come down from the Cross when they shouted to Thee, mocking and reviling Thee, 'Come down from the cross and we will believe that Thou art He.' Thou didst not come down, for again Thou wouldst not enslave man by a miracle, and didst crave faith given freely, not based on miracle. Thou didst crave for free love and not the base raptures of the slave before the might that has overawed him for ever. But Thou didst think too highly of men therein, for they are slaves, of course, though rebellious by nature. Look round and judge; fifteen centuries have passed, look upon them. Whom hast Thou raised up to Thyself? I swear, man is weaker and baser by nature than Thou hast believed him! Can he, can he do what Thou didst? By showing him so much respect, Thou didst, as it were, cease to feel for him, for Thou didst ask far too much from him—Thou who hast loved him more than Thyself! Respecting him less, Thou wouldst have asked less of him. That would have been more like love, for his burden would have been lighter. He is weak and vile."

I keep getting tangled up in the move the Inquisitor makes around respect and love. He says that by respecting people too much, Christ actually stopped feeling for them — that asking so much from someone *isn't* loving, and that you'd ask less if you respected them less. So the Inquisitor seems to accuse Christ of a kind of callousness disguised as high regard, and he positions his own authoritarian project as the genuinely compassionate one.

What I can't decide is whether the Inquisitor is supposed to be *consistent* here. Is he genuinely redefining love as something patronizing and managerial (and if so, what does that say about the idea that giving people bread and authority *is* loving them)? Or is Ivan writing a character who is self-deceived — someone who talks about love but is really after control, and the "respecting him less" line is meant to tip us off to his contempt? I feel like Dostoevsky wants us to feel the force of the Inquisitor's argument while also seeing it as a kind of category mistake about what love and freedom even are, but I'm having trouble pinning down exactly where the argument goes wrong, if it does. Any help parsing the logic here would be appretiated — especially on whether the paradox of "loving by disrespecting" is a real challenge to the Christian idea of freedom or just rhetorical sleight-of-hand.
