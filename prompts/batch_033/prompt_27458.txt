I'm a backend engineer at a prediction market startup — we run markets on everything from election outcomes to whether a specific tech CEO will step down this year. I maintain the automated market maker, and recently I fell down a rabbit hole reading about the foundations of probability. Dutch book arguments keep coming up: the idea that if your subjective probabilities don't obey the Kolmogorov axioms, a clever bookie can offer you a package of bets that you'll accept individually but that guarantees you lose money overall. That's supposed to show rationality requires probabilistic coherence.

I was explaining this to a coworker over lunch and ended up sketching an argument that, if the Dutch book reasoning is sound, seems to prove way more than anyone wants. Now I can't find the flaw and it's bugging me. Here's the argument:

(1) Dutch book theorem: if an agent's credences violate the probability axioms, there exists a finite set of bets, each individually fair by the agent's own lights, whose combined payoff is negative in every possible state of the world. So rational credences must satisfy the probability axioms.

(2) But the exact same style of construction works for logical structure the agent hasn't noticed. Say I assign credence 0.9 to P, 0.9 to Q, and 0.4 to R, where P&Q logically entails R. I'm vulnerable to a Dutch book that sells me bets on P and Q while buying a bet on R from me, exploiting the fact that my credence in R is lower than it "should" be given my credences in P and Q.

(3) To be immune to all such constructions, an agent's credences would need to respect every logical entailment — meaning credence 1 in every logical truth, credence 0 in every contradiction, and P(A) ≤ P(B) whenever A entails B.

(4) That's logical omniscience. No finite computational system can achieve it — not humans, not our market-making algorithm, not any program running on silicon.

(5) So the Dutch book argument, if it works as advertised, proves that rational agency is computationally impossible. A theory of rationality that rules out every actually existing agent is a bad theory.

(6) So something must be wrong with the Dutch book argument.

I have a hunch the problem is in the step from (1) to (2) — maybe the Dutch book theorem only works when the agent explicitly represents the logical relationships, and doesn't apply to entailments the agent hasn't put together. But then I worry: if I can dodge a Dutch book by just not thinking through the consequences of my own credences, doesn't that make the whole argument toothless? Like, couldn't I avoid any Dutch book by simply refusing to combine bets in my head?

I'd love a diagnosis of where this argument actually breaks, and what the fix costs for the original Dutch book argument's force.
