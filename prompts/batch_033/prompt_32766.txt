I've been keeping an informal log of a colleague's moral judgments over the past year. She's thoughtful and her verdicts are consistent with one another as far as I can tell, but I can't pin down the principle she's running. I'm a software engineer by training, so I've been thinking about this as a kind of reverse-engineering problem: given a set of input-output pairs, what's the function that maps them? I have no formal philosophy background, and I suspect I'm missing distinctions that a trained philosopher would spot immediately.

All of the cases involve deception. Here they are, with her verdicts:

1. You're hiding a Jewish family in your attic. Nazi officers knock and ask whether you're harboring anyone. You say no. She says: permissible.

2. You're selling a used car. The buyer asks whether it's ever been in an accident. It was in a minor collision five years ago, fully repaired, no ongoing issues. You say no. She says: impermissible.

3. A friend asks whether you like her new haircut. You think it's unflattering. You tell her it looks great. She says: permissible.

4. Your spouse asks whether you remembered to pick up the prescription. You forgot, but you say yes, planning to grab it on the way home before they notice. She says: impermissible.

5. Your young child asks what happened to the family dog, who was just put down. You say the dog went to live on a farm with lots of room to run. She says: permissible.

6. You're applying for a job. The application asks whether you hold a bachelor's degree. You don't, but you check yes. She says: impermissible.

7. A close friend introduces you to their new romantic partner and later asks what you think. You have serious concerns about the partner's character, but you say they seem great. She says: impermissible.

8. A telemarketer calls during dinner. You say you're in the middle of something and can't talk, even though you're just eating. She says: permissible.

Now a new case has come up that she hasn't weighed in on. A doctor has a patient with a condition that causes chronic pain but is not life-threatening. The doctor prescribes a sugar pill but tells the patient it's a real medication. The placebo works, the patient's pain subsides, and the patient never learns the truth.

What I want to know: what candidate rules would fit her pattern of verdicts so far? Which of those rules can be ruled out by her existing verdicts? Of the rules that remain in contention, what does each predict about the placebo case, and do they diverge on it? If you had to bet, which rule is she actually running, and what single additional case would settle the matter between the remaining candidates?
