Two friends of mine got into it over drinks last weekend and I still can't sort out who had the better argument. The topic was whether there can be reasons that aren't reasons *for* anyone — like, normative facts that just float there with no mind to register them.

Dan kicked it off by saying he'd been reading about moral realism and couldn't shake the feeling that the whole thing falls apart if you ask "a reason *to whom*?" He said reasons are like debts: you can't have a debt without a debtor and a creditor, and you can't have a reason without a reasoner. A reason is always someone's reason.

Maya pushed back. She said that's like saying mathematical truths need a mathematician to be true. The fact that suffering is bad doesn't depend on anyone noticing it's bad — it just is. If all conscious life went extinct tomorrow, it would still be true that the extinction was a bad thing, even with no one left to think so.

Dan said she was smuggling in the conclusion. "Bad" is an evaluative term — it's a reason to avoid or regret something. If there's no subject left to do the avoiding or regretting, in what sense is it "bad"? It's not a property of the physical events themselves; it's a property of how those events relate to valuers.

Maya said okay, but then he's committed to saying that before any minds evolved, there were no normative truths whatsoever. Which means the very first organisms had no reason to do anything — not to flee predators, not to seek food, nothing. That seems like a reductio.

Dan replied that of course the first organisms had reasons — reasons relative to their own goals or interests, however rudimentary. The point isn't that reasons require *sophisticated* reasoners, just that they require *some* reasoner. A bacterium's "reason" to move toward glucose is grounded in what's good for that bacterium. No bacterium, no reason.

Maya said he'd just shifted his ground. First he said reasons require a reasoner in the sense of a mind that can appreciate them. Now he's saying any goal-directed system counts. But a thermostat is goal-directed — does a thermostat have reasons? And if not, where's the line?

Dan admitted he wasn't sure where to draw the line but said that doesn't make the line unreal. Lots of concepts have fuzzy boundries — that doesn't mean there's no distinction between having reasons and not.

Maya said the deeper problem is that his view makes reasons depend on the contingent existence of reasoners, which means if all reasoners happened to value torturing each other, that would make torture good. He's backed into a relativism he didn't want.

Dan said no — he can still say torturing is bad because actual reasoners, reasoning well, would reject it. The standard comes from what reasoners *would* endorse under ideal conditions, not what they happen to endorse.

Maya said "ideal conditions" is doing all the work, and those conditions are themselves normative — they're standards for good reasoning. And now we're back to norms that don't depend on any actual reasoner. He's just pushed the free-floating reasons into his account of ideal reasoning.

They went back and forth a while longer but that was the core of it. I'd like you to adjudicate this move by move. Who has the better of each exchange, and what's the overall state of the argument by the end? Are there moves available to either of them that they missed?
