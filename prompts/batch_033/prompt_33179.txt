Two members of my philosophy of mind reading group got into a long argument about cognitive penetration. I took notes on the exchange and want someone to adjudicate it move by move.

Maya opened with the Müller-Lyer illusion: you can measure the lines, know they're the same length, and they still look different. That's the textbook case for impenetrability.

David replied that low-level illusions don't settle the question. He pointed to studies where participants who believed a gray object was actually yellow reported seeing it as yellower, and to the everyday experience of perceiving a neutral expression as hostile when you believe someone is angry at you.

Maya said those cases are precisely the kind that collapse under scrutiny. The color-report case could be response bias rather than a genuine change in perceptual experience, and the expression case involves an ambiguous stimulus, so it's consistent with the same perceptual input being interpreted differently — which isn't penetration.

David called this question-begging: if every top-down effect gets reclassified as post-perceptual judgment or interpretation, the impenetrability thesis is unfalsifiable. The distinction between perception and judgment is itself part of what's at stake.

Maya denied this. She said there are rigorous methods for distinguishing perceptual from post-perceptual effects — signal detection, adaptation paradigms, tilt aftereffects — and when those methods are applied, the evidence for cognitive penetration is weak. She referenced the Firestone and Scholl critiques.

David said those critiques set an impossibly high bar, effectively requiring that cognition alter the outputs of early visual cortex before counting anything as penetration. That's not what most philosophers mean by the term.

Maya said the bar isn't impossibly high, just well-defined. She then asked David what he thought the best case for penetration was among those that survive methodological scrutiny.

David offered two: the racial-bias weapon-priming studies, where participants detect a weapon faster when primed with a Black face — a reaction-time measure, not a self-report — and the case of expert radiologists who literally see a tumor where novices see noise, their perceptual experience reshaped by training and knowledge.

Maya replied that the weapon-priming case is about attention directing perception, not cognition penetrating it, and the radiologist case is about perceptual learning, which restructures the perceptual system over time — a process everyone agrees happens and which is distinct from a current belief altering a current experience. She said David was conflating attention, perceptual learning, and cognitive penetration.

David said this was the problem: Maya kept adding distinctions — attention isn't cognition, learning isn't cognition — to protect the thesis. But if beliefs and expectations shape attention and drive perceptual learning, and those in turn shape perception, then the impenetrability thesis is true only in a narrow sense that doesn't carry the epistemological weight Maya wants it to.

They went back and forth for another twenty minutes without resolving it. Please adjudicate: go through each move in the exchange and assess who is right about what, where the real deadlock lies, and whether there's a path out of it that neither of them saw.
