I was chatting with a mate from my metaphysics seminar the other day about Black's two spheres — you know the one, an otherwise empty universe with just two iron spheres, identical in every qualitative respect, same size, same composition, same temperature, the lot. And we got properly stuck on something our lecturer sort of gestured at and then moved on from. Black's case is meant to sink the identity of indiscernibles, the principle that if two things share all their properties they've got to be the same thing. But here's the rub: what even counts as a property? If you let historical properties in — facts about where a thing came from, how it was made, its causal pedigree — then loads of apparently indiscernible objects turn out to be discernible after all. Two factory-fresh iPhones straight off the same production line: same in every intrinsic way, but one was assembled slightly earlier, or in a different plant, or by a different robot. Does that historical difference save the principle? Or is that just cheating by expanding the property set until the principle becomes trivial?

Here's what I'd like you to build. Construct a thought experiment that isolates exactly this issue. I want a case with two ordinary artefacts — say two wooden chairs or two ceramic mugs — that are qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect right now: same materials, same dimensions, same colour, same scuffs and scratches. But they've got different origins: made in different workshops, by different people, from different source timber or clay (even if the materials are type-identical). The only thing that distinguishes them is their history. Now the task: does this case refute the identity of indiscernibles, or does it show the principle can be rescued by admitting historical properties into the set of individuating properties?

Constraints: keep the scenario itself to about a paragraph, use ordinary macroscopic objects (no quantum particles, no transworld identity, no exotic metaphysics), and you must grapple with the objection that historical properties are really just relational properties wearing a temporal hat — that saying "this mug was thrown by Potter A" is really saying "this mug stands in the was-made-by relation to Potter A," and if relational properties already cause trouble for the identity of indiscernibles (as Black's spheres show, since each sphere stands in the relation "is two feet away from the other sphere" to something the other doesn't), then historical properties don't add anything new to the dialectic.

And here's the kicker: if you stripped the historical difference out of the case entirely, would it collapse into something structurally identical to Black's spheres, or would there be something else going on?
