I'm taking a philosophy of law seminar this semester, and we spent a week on Nomic — the game, invented by Peter Suber, in which players take turns proposing and voting on rule changes, and the rules that govern how rules are changed are themselves subject to change. The professor presented it as a toy model of legal self-reference, meant to illuminate puzzles about constitutional amendment, entrenchment, and the foundations of legal validity. I've been turning over an argument that seems to show the model is incoherent in a way that might also threaten any legal system that permits amendment of its own amendment procedures. I can't find the flaw, and I'd appreciate a careful diagnosis.

Here is the argument. Consider the initial rules of a game of Nomic. Among them is a mutable rule — call it Rule A — that specifies the procedure for amending rules: say, adoption requires a simple majority of the players. Now suppose a player proposes amending Rule A to require a two-thirds supermajority for all future amendments. The proposal is itself an amendment, so it is voted on under the current Rule A, by simple majority. It receives a simple majority (but less than two-thirds) and is adopted, replacing the old Rule A.

Now we face a difficulty. The new Rule A — the two-thirds rule — is now in force. But the new Rule A was adopted by a procedure (simple majority) that does not satisfy its own standard (two-thirds). If the new Rule A is valid, then there exists a valid rule whose own pedigree fails the test the rule itself imposes on all amendments. That is not yet a formal contradiction, but it is uncomfortable: the rule set now contains a rule that, had it been in force at the time of its own adoption, would have rendered that adoption invalid. One might reply that the new Rule A governs only future amendments, not its own. But that reply — the claim that amendment rules are inherently prospective — is itself a substantive principle that must either be codified in the rules or justified as a background norm. If it is codified, then it is just another amendment rule, and the same problem can be raised about amending it. If it is a background norm, then the rules of Nomic are not self-contained; they depend on an external principle of prospectivity, which contradicts the game's premise that the rules are the sole source of their own validity.

Now generalize the point. Any amendment to an amendment procedure that makes the procedure more stringent — simple majority to two-thirds, two-thirds to unanimity — will have been adopted under a procedure that fails its own new standard. Any amendment that makes it less stringent — two-thirds to simple majority — will have been adopted under a procedure more demanding than was necessary, which is not paradoxical but reveals that the players were following a stricter rule than they needed to, raising the question of why they were following it at all. The only way to avoid both the paradox and the inefficiency, it seems, is to make the amendment procedure unamendable. But if the amendment procedure is unamendable, then Nomic is not a game of pure self-amendment; it is a game with a fixed procedural constitution, and the only rules that can be changed are the ordinary rules that do not govern their own modification.

The argument seems to prove that any rule system that permits amendment of its own amendment procedures is either paradoxical (in the direction of greater stringency) or normatively idle (in the direction of lesser stringency). And since Nomic is supposed to be a coherent system that permits amendment of its own amendment procedures, Nomic is impossible. But this conclusion is too strong: people play Nomic, they do amend amendment rules, and the game does not collapse into paradox. So the argument must go wrong somewhere. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to about the nature of rules, validity, or prospectivity? I am particularly interested in whether the problem is genuinely a paradox or merely a confusion about the temporality of rules — and if the latter, whether that confusion has any bite against real legal systems that amend their own amendment procedures, as many constitutions do. Please write your answer in American English.
