I've been going down a rabbit hole on Henry Sidgwick and the early Society for Psychical Research, and I keep coming back to the same thought: Sidgwick basically had the right stance on post-mortem survival, and most later scoffing misses what he was actually doing. The position I'm drawn to is this: whether we survive death isn't something physics or a priori argument can settle; it's an empirical question about a messy body of reports — apparitions, deathbed visions, mediumship, spontaneous cases. The right method is Sidgwick's: treat each report as data that could be explained by fraud, error, coincidence, or genuine post-mortem agency, and ask which explanation handles the cumulative evidence best, without stacking the deck in advance. On that method, survival deserves to be treated as a serious live hypothesis even if it's nowhere near proven, and people who dismiss the whole topic without doing this work are being unscientific in the name of science. I'm not saying survival is true; I'm saying this is the right way to have the argument, and I also find it compelling that Sidgwick thought the question mattered for ethics — whether there's any moral order to the universe that would make the demands of morality and self-interest finally line up.

What I want is a map of what would count against this position — not just "mediums cheat," because Sidgwick and the SPR already knew that and built it into the method. I mean: which findings, arguments, or developments should actually move me off this stance, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? What would a commited defender of the Sidgwick approach concede as counting against it, as opposed to what critics say? Be honest about whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — whether "the cumulative case" can just absorb every failed case as fraud or error and never be disconfirmed — and whether, if so, that's a problem for a position of this kind or just the normal condition of historical and forensic reasoning. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
