I lead a small model-risk and controls team at a mid-sized insurance firm. We review the quantitative targets, key performance indicators and reward functions embedded in our underwriting, claims and fraud-detection systems before they are deployed, and again at each quarterly re-approval. Each review has at most two analyst-days behind it, and the reviewers are statisticians, actuaries and compliance officers rather than philosophers or economists. The final sign-off sits with a product owner and, for high-risk systems, with the firm's conduct committee. The stakes are high: a target that gets gamed can mean wrongful claim denials, aggressive collections behaviour, or a regulatory finding, and by the time the damage shows up in aggregate results the harm has already been done to individual customers.

I keep seeing the same failure in different clothing. A metric is chosen because it correlates with something the business actually values; the business is then set to optimise that metric; and within a quarter or two the metric has drifted away from the underlying value it was meant to stand in for. I understand this as Goodhart's law, and the machine-learning community describes the same phenomenon as specification gaming, in which a system satisfies the letter of a reward function or specification while defeating its intent. What I need is not another restatement of the slogan. I need a procedure my team can actually run, one that operationalises the mechanisms behind the law — for example, when a metric can be improved without improving the target, when pressure to improve the metric changes the process that generates it, and when the metric and the goal diverge under distribution shift.

Please write that procedure as a reusable checklist or decision tree in words. It should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a 'no', and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run the procedure on two proposals: (1) a fraud-detection model whose reward function is the number of confirmed fraud cases it flags each month, and (2) a claims-handling system whose performance target is average settlement amount per claim. Include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer the business will not like, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do. Lead with the procedure itself, then the worked cases, keep the whole response to no more than 1,000 words, and write in American English.
