I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, working on a dissertation chapter about the relationship between moral responsibility and rational deliberation. Over the past several months, I've developed what I take to be a fairly compelling argument against a certain compatibilist strategy — roughly, the one that says an agent can be morally responsible for an action even under determinism, provided the agent's deliberative processes satisfy certain conditions of responsiveness to reasons. My argument, in brief, is that the compatibilist's conditions on deliberation collapse into either a libertarian picture (if interpreted robustly) or a trivial one (if interpreted minimally), and that neither horn leaves the compatibilist with what they wanted. I was, until recently, quite confident in this line of reasoning.

I presented a draft of the chapter at our department's work-in-progress seminar two weeks ago. The discussion was productive, but one exchange has stayed with me. A fellow graduate student — someone I consider a genuine intellectual peer: we entered the program the same year, have similar training, have taken almost all the same seminars, and I've consistently found her objections sharp and well-motivated — raised a detailed objection during the Q&A. She argued that I had mischaracterized the compatibilist's notion of "responsiveness to reasons," and that once the notion is properly understood, my dilemma doesn't go through. We discussed it for perhaps five minutes after the talk, and then again over coffee the following week for close to an hour. I found her counterarguments thoughtful, but I was not persuaded that my argument fails. She, for her part, was not persuaded by my responses either. We left the conversation at an impasse.

What's troubling me is not the disagreement itself — disagreements in philosophy are routine. What's troubling me is what I ought to do next. Before our conversation, I would have assigned something like 0.7 credence to the claim that my argument is sound. Now I'm unsure whether I should lower that confidence simply in virtue of the fact that someone I regard as an epistemic peer has examined the same considerations and reached a different conclusion. On the one hand, it seems arrogant to carry on as if our disagreement had not happened. On the other hand, I had my reasons for my view — reasons that, even after hearing her out, still seem to me to be good ones. I'm not sure what rationality demands of me here. Should I revise my confidence downward, and if so, by how much? Or is it permissible to maintain my original level of confidence given that I've carefully considered her objection and remain unpersuaded?
