I've been part of an informal philosophy discussion group that meets at a coffee shop every other week. Lately we've been working through puzzles about identity over time — the Ship of Theseus, personal identity, that kind of thing. One of the regulars, a retired engineer named Jim, has been unusually consistent in his verdicts, and I've been keeping track of where he lands. He doesn't give elaborate justifications; he just says what he thinks and then listens to the rest of us argue. Here's what I've recorded him saying so far:

- On the original Ship of Theseus (planks replaced one by one over years until none of the original wood remains): "Still the same ship."
- On the reassembled planks (the removed planks are put back together in the original arrangement): "That's also the same ship." When someone pointed out that this gives us two ships both claiming to be the original, he shrugged and said, "So?"
- On a person who gets a prosthetic arm: "Same person."
- On a person who steps into a teleporter that destroys the original body and assembles an exact replica elsewhere, down to the memories: "Not the same person. The original is dead."
- On a river — the water is entirely different from a century ago: "Same river."
- On a social club that has replaced every member, changed its name, and moved to a different city over thirty years: "Same club."
- On a person with late-stage Alzheimer's who has lost essentially all memories and personality: "Same person. Just changed."

Next week we're discussing a new case. Suppose a person undergoes a procedure in which, over the course of a year, individual neurons are replaced one at a time with functionally identical synthetic neurons. The person is awake and aware throughout the process, reports no change in experience, and retains all memories, personality traits, and skills. At the end of the year, every original neuron has been replaced by a synthetic one. I want to walk in with a prediction of what Jim will say — and, more importantly, an account of what rule or rules he seems to be running.

Can you lay out the candidate principles that would make Jim's verdicts consistent, say what each principle predicts about the neural replacement case, and give your best guess about which principle he's actually using and why? Also, what additional case would do the best job of telling the candidate principles apart — something I could bring up in the discussion that would force a clear choice? I'm less interested in the right answer to the puzzle than in understanding the structure of Jim's commitments.
