I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on metaethics, and I've been trying to work out whether evolutionary debunking arguments can be contained — that is, whether they can target moral beliefs without spilling over into other domains we're unwilling to give up. I've constructed what I think is the strongest version of the "proves too much" objection, but I'm stuck: I can't tell whether it's sound, and if it isn't, I can't identify the exact step where it goes wrong.

Here's the argument, numbered for clarity:

(1) An evolutionary explanation E of why we hold belief B is a defeater for B if E accounts for our holding B without any appeal to the truth of B — that is, if E shows we would have held B regardless of whether B is true. (This is the core debunking principle, drawn from Street and Joyce.)

(2) There are plausible evolutionary explanations for a wide range of our moral beliefs — for instance, that we believe causing harm to ingroup members is wrong because inhibitions against such harm were adaptive — and these explanations nowhere appeal to the truth of those moral beliefs; they appeal only to the fitness effects of the behaviors those beliefs motivate.

(3) Therefore, by (1) and (2), we have a defeater for those moral beliefs.

(4) But there are equally plausible evolutionary explanations for our basic mathematical beliefs — for instance, that we believe two and two make four because organisms that tracked numerical relationships in their environment made better foraging and predator-avoidance decisions — and these explanations, at least on their face, also nowhere appeal to the truth of the mathematical propositions themselves. They appeal to the adaptive advantages of behaving as if those propositions were true, which is consistent with the propositions being true but does not require it.

(5) By parity of reasoning from (1) and (4), we have a defeater for our basic mathematical beliefs.

(6) We do not, in fact, take ourselves to have a defeater for basic mathematical beliefs. Moreover, if we did, the defeater would extend to the scientific beliefs that underwrite the evolutionary explanations in (2) and (4), and the whole argument would self-destruct.

(7) Therefore, something must be wrong with the debunking principle in (1), or with its application — and if the principle cannot be fixed, the debunking project fails.

I find myself unable to reject any single premise with confidence. The obvious move is to challenge (4) by arguing that evolutionary explanations for mathematical cognition do appeal to mathematical truth — because the world really does contain discrete objects and two and two really do make four, and that is why tracking these facts was adaptive. But the debunker's opponent can reply that this just pushes the question back: the evolutionary explanation only needs to cite the fact that organisms who behaved in ways that conformed to arithmetic survived, not that they grasped arithmetic truths, so the truth of the propositions still does no explanatory work. I am not sure the wedge holds.

If you were defending the evolutionary debunker, which premise would you reject, and what is the theoretical cost? Alternatively, if you think the argument is sound and the debunker simply must accept that their argument self-destructs, explain why the standard responses fail.
