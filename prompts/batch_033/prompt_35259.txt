A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the paradox of tragedy — why we deliberately seek out artworks that make us feel sad, frightened, or grief-stricken. He defended a version of the conversion theory: the negative emotions are transmuted into pleasure by the formal properties of the work. I found this unsatisfying but couldn't quite articulate why, and the conversation kept circling back to the same examples. Afterwards I realized the work I should have brought up is Ian McEwan's *Atonement*, which seems to put pressure on the conversion story in a way that *Oedipus* or *King Lear* do not.

Here is why *Atonement* is a hard case. The novel's final section reveals that the narrative we have been reading — including the reunion of the lovers Cecilia and Robbie — was Briony's fictional invention. The real Cecilia and Robbie both died in 1940, never reconciled. The reader is told that the happy ending was a lie Briony told because she could not bear the truth and because she hoped fiction might do the work that her life could not. The artistry of the novel is inseparable from the deception it perpetrates on the reader, and the pleasure of the well-made plot is revealed to have been, in a sense, fraudulent. If the conversion theory says we enjoy tragedy because craft transforms pain into aesthetic pleasure, what happens when the craft itself is implicated in the pain — when the formal elegance is the mechanism of a moral failure the reader has been made complicit in?

I can see several ways the conversation might go from here. An Aristotelian catharsis reading might say the novel does purge pity and fear, just not through the happy ending. A meta-response theory might say we take pleasure in our own appropriate sadness because it confirms our moral sensitivity. And there are views that reject the pleasure framework entirely, arguing that we value tragic art for the understanding it provides. But I am not sure any of these fully capture what the novel does, and I would like your assessment.

Read *Atonement* through the lens of the paradox of tragedy. Which philosophical account of the paradox does the novel most naturally support, and which does it resist? Does the novel's self-consciousness about its own fictionality — its thematization of the very question of what art can do with suffering — give it a kind of philosophical authority on this question, or does it merely muddy the waters? And is there something about the experience of reading *Atonement* that the philosophical literature on the paradox has systematically missed — something the novel shows that the theories cannot quite say?
