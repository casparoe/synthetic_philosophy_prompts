I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the paradox of deontology, and I've come away unsure what I actually think. My friend — a consequentialist — kept pressing me on why I think it's wrong to kill one to prevent five killings but permissible to lie to prevent five killings. I found myself wanting to hold onto deontological constraints but couldn't articulate a principle that didn't sound ad hoc by the end of the conversation. So I sat down afterward and wrote out my honest verdicts on a range of cases. I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern — and which of my verdicts I should give up if no clean principle gets them all.

Here are the cases:

1. **Trolley (push).** A runaway trolley will kill five people. Pushing a large bystander off a footbridge will stop it, killing the one but saving the five. Verdict: impermissible.

2. **Murderer at the door.** A murderer asks where your friend is hiding. Telling the truth gets your friend killed; lying sends the murderer away. Verdict: permissible to lie.

3. **Terrorist and hostages.** A terrorist will kill five hostages unless you kill one hostage yourself. Verdict: impermissible.

4. **Torture prevention (killing).** You can prevent a sadist from torturing five people to death by killing one person yourself. Verdict: impermissible.

5. **Torture prevention (lying).** You can prevent a sadist from torturing five people by lying to him about where they are hiding. Verdict: permissible.

6. **Promise vs. promise.** You can prevent a colleague from breaking a serious promise that will harm a student's career, but only by breaking a trivial promise you made to attend another colleague's talk. Verdict: permissible.

7. **Lying vs. lying.** You can prevent a friend from telling five serious lies (fabricating evidence in a legal case) by telling one minor lie yourself. Verdict: I lean impermissible, but with much less confidence than in the killing cases.

8. **Stealing vs. stealing.** You can prevent five thefts of valuable items by stealing one item of comparable value yourself. Verdict: impermissible.

9. **Self-prevention.** You can prevent yourself from committing five serious lies tomorrow by telling one minor lie today (you lie about being busy to attend an intervention that will restructure your habits). Verdict: I genuinely cannot settle this one — it feels different from the third-party cases but I can't say why.

10. **Slapping.** You can prevent five people from being slapped by slapping one person yourself. Verdict: permissible, though I dislike it.

The pattern I half-see is this: I'm more willing to violate a constraint to prevent violations of a *different* constraint (lying to prevent killing, breaking a promise to prevent a promise-breaking) than to prevent violations of the *same* constraint (killing to prevent killing, stealing to prevent stealing). But case 6 breaks that pattern — I permit breaking a promise to prevent promise-breaking. And case 7 has me leaning against lying to prevent lying, which does fit the pattern, but I'm not sure why the constraint-boundary crossing should matter at all. My friend's challenge was exactly this: if constraints are supposed to be agent-relative side-constraints on action, why should it matter whether the prevented violations are of the same type or a different type? The constraint is supposed to govern *my* action, not the world's total count of violations.

Is there a coherent principle that captures most of these verdicts, and which ones does it force me to abandon? I'm especially interested in what the principle says about case 9, which I keep going back and forth on.
