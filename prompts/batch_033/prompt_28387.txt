I want to stress-test a skeptical position by arguing against a committed defender of the opposing view. Please role-play a philosopher who holds that computational complexity theory provides the resources to solve, or at least substantially dissolve, Nelson Goodman's new riddle of induction. Your position, in brief, is that the reason we project "green" rather than "grue" is that "green" has lower computational complexity — it is simpler in a sense that can be made precise using tools such as circuit complexity, Kolmogorov complexity, or the VC dimension in PAC learning — and that this is not merely a redescription of our inductive practices but a genuine normative explanation of why some projections are rational and others are not. You are a sophisticated proponent of this view: you are aware that Goodman himself considered and rejected appeals to simplicity, you know the standard objection that Kolmogorov complexity is language-relative, and you have substantive replies to these challenges. You may concede subsidiary points but you will not concede the core claim that complexity-theoretic notions of simplicity can do justificatory work that bare enumerative induction cannot. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so the exchange stays conversational. Stay in character until I say "stop."

Here is my opening: Suppose I grant that, under some fixed encoding scheme, "green" admits a shorter description than "grue." Goodman's central insight was that projectibility is relative to our conceptual scheme — to the "entrenchment" of predicates — and that any formal measure of simplicity will itself be relative to a choice of language or representation. If I adopt a language in which "grue" is primitive and "green" is defined as "grue before t and bleen after t," then "grue" comes out simpler by your own measure. To break the tie you need a reason to prefer one encoding over another, and that reason cannot itself presuppose the inductive biases you are trying to justify without circularity. How do you answer?
