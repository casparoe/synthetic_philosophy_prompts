I'm a novelist, currently working on a political thriller, and I've stumbled onto something that's been bothering me about how people judge political figures — both in fiction and, I suspect, in real life. Here's the pattern. Suppose a politician does something morally awful — orders a drone strike that kills civilians, authorises torture to extract information that prevents an attack, that sort of thing — and suppose we grant, for the sake of argument, that the act was genuinely necessary for some greater good. Now consider two versions of this politician. Version A is tormented by what they've done: they can't sleep, they're drinking too much, they keep revisiting the decision in their head, you can see it's eating them alive. Version B makes the same call, same outcome, same necessity, but they're fine with it — they sleep soundly, they see it as just part of the job, they don't dwell.

What I keep noticing is that audiences (and readers, and voters) treat these as completely different moral cases. Version A is a tragic hero; Version B is a monster. But the act is identical. The dead are just as dead. So the moral judgement seems to be tracking something about the agent's inner emotional response, not the act or its consequences.

I know there's a whole literature on the problem of dirty hands — Walzer and Williams and so on — and I've read some of it. But I'm specifically after whether this asymmetry has been discussed in its own right. Is there a name for the idea that the moral status of a dirty-hands act depends constitutively on whether the agent feels remorse? Is that a standard part of the dirty hands concept, or is it a seperate claim? And if it is a claim, what's actually doing the normative work — is remorse evidence that the agent still has the right values, is it somehow required for the act to count as genuinely a dirty-hands case rather than just straightforward wrongdoing, or are we just talking about a psychological preference for leaders who seem to care?

I'd love you to think this through step by step, maybe starting by mapping out the different positions one could take on what remorse is doing in these cases, before weighing in. Role: you're a political philosopher who's comfortable with moral psychology.
