I can't tell whether the evolutionary psychology debate is actually about evidence or about something else entirely, and I'd like a diagnosis. The critics' move is always the same: for any trait you name — jealousy, depression, language, a taste for sugary food — an adaptationist can tell a plausible story about why it would have helped fitness in the Pleistocene, and that is exactly the complaint. The stories are unfalsifiable just-so stories; had the opposite trait been the one we observe, the same machinery would have produced an opposite story just as smoothly. The adaptationists' reply is also always the same: critics are demanding an impossible standard for a historical science, evidence of good design is real evidence, and the critics' own alternatives (byproduct, cultural construction) are usually even less testable. I've been reading this for a couple years and it feels completely frozen — both sides accept the basic data and accept that you can't run experiments on the ancestral environment, and neither moves.

My working theory is that the real disagreement is about what counts as a scientific explanation: whether a plausible reconstruction of a trait's history is an explanation or just a hypothesis-generator. Is that right, or is it really about standards of evidence, about what the word adaptation even means, or about politics leaking into the science? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, with reasons, and an honest answer about what would actually count as progress — a new method, a distinction, a formal result, or a different question.
