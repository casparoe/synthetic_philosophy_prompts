A longread in a tech magazine summarizes the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence like this:

"Nick Bostrom and others in the AI alignment community argue that there's no necessary connection between how intelligent a system is and what it wants. A superintelligent AI could be built to pursue any goal at all — making paperclips, solving math problems, maximizing human happiness — and it would be just as good at pursuing a stupid goal as a noble one. This is the orthogonality thesis. From this, they draw a further conclusion called instrumental convergence: because any goal requires resources and self-preservation to achieve, all sufficiently advanced AIs will converge on the same behavior regardless of their final goals. They'll all try to seize power, acquire resources, and prevent themselves from being shut down. So the orthogonality thesis plus instrumental convergence together imply that any superintelligent AI we build will inevitably become a power-seeking threat, no matter what goal we program into it. Critics have pointed out that this argument proves too much: humans are intelligent agents, and as humans become more intelligent and knowledgeable, they tend to converge on more universal moral values rather than on power-seeking, which seems to directly contradict the instrumental convergence claim."

I've read enough philosophy to suspect this summary is doing something wrong, but I can't put my finger on exactly where the misrepresentations are. Go through the summary and tell me: which parts would a defender of the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence accept as accurate, which parts are subtle distortions or conflations, and whether the critic's objection at the end actually lands against the real views or only against the summary's version of them.
