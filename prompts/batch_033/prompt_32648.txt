I'm taking a course on anthropic reasoning, and after reading a half-dozen papers on the doomsday argument I've come away with the impression that the debate has been frozen for something like twenty years. The basic structure is familiar: if you reason as though you are randomly sampled from all humans who will ever live, your existence is more probable under a short future for humanity than under a long one, which shifts your posterior toward doom. The self-indication assumption — roughly, that your existence is evidence that there are many observers — cancels this shift. The people who favor one anthropic principle find the doomsday argument compelling; the people who favor the other find it obviously wrong. And then there are the reference-class objections, the worries about infinite populations, the arguments from the fact that we haven't observed many other civilizations, and so on. But none of these moves seems to have changed anyone's mind.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't a disagreement about probability at all. It's a disagreement about what we're doing when we engage in anthropic reasoning — about what "finding yourself in a world" licenses you to infer. The two sides have different pictures of the relation between an observer and the class of observers they're supposed to reason about, and the formalisms are downstream of those pictures. If that's right, the debate won't be settled by a new theorem; it needs a different kind of work.

But I'm not sure my diagnosis is right, and I'd like to hear a more precise account from someone who knows the literature. What kind of disagreement is the doomsday argument debate, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "probability" or "evidence"? Is it a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is it, as I've speculated, a disagreement about the aims of anthropic reasoning that the formalisms obscure? Or is there a genuine formal or empirical question buried in it that I'm missing? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a theorem, a new empirical fact, a change of question — or should we expect the impasse to be permanent?
