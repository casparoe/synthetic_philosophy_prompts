I am at work on a novel whose central character is an economic historian who, late in life, becomes convinced that the moral significance of economic activity has been systematically misunderstood, and who attempts to live according to a corrected understanding of it. In developing this character's intellectual commitments, I keep returning to the argumentative structure of the essay in which John Maynard Keynes imagines the economic condition of his grandchildren, and in particular to his distinction between needs that are absolute, in the sense that we feel them whatever the situation of our fellow human beings may be, and needs that are relative, in the sense that we feel them only because their satisfaction lifts us above, or makes us feel superior to, our fellows. The character would maintain, drawing on this distinction, that a great deal of what passes for economic rationality is really a kind of arrested development: the relentless pursuit of income beyond the point at which absolute needs are met is not a neutral means to a free and leisured life, but a habit that actively unfits people for the very leisure it is supposedly meant to secure.

I would like you to make a sustained argument for that position, treating Keynes's essay as a point of departure rather than as an authority. In particular, argue that Keynes's account of relative needs carries a substantive ethical implication: that beyond a fairly modest threshold, additional wealth should be regarded not as a genuine good but as a dispositional vice, something close to the love of money as an end in itself, and that the good life consists less in the satisfaction of ever-expanding preferences than in the cultivation of activities enjoyed for their own sake, such as friendship, beauty, contemplation, and the unhurried enjoyment of present goods. Please address the strongest objection you can find to this line of argument, namely that Keynes's treatment of relative needs can be read as a realistic description of a permanent feature of human psychology rather than as a moral criticism of status-seeking, in which case the endless pursuit of more would be a stable fact about us rather than a transitional pathology to be overcome. Your argument should reach a definite conclusion and justify it.
