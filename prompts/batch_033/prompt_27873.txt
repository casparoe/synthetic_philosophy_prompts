My dissertation advisor died suddenly six weeks ago, and the APA Eastern Division has organized a memorial session in her honor at the upcoming meeting. I've been asked to give the main address—roughly ten minutes. She was one of the most consistently Rortyan philosophers of her generation: not just a scholar of Rorty, but someone who lived inside the commitments all the way down. For her, antirepresentationalism wasn't a doctrine to be established but a therapeutic insight to be inhabited—the idea that we don't make progress by getting our words to mirror the world more accurately, because there is no ready-made world standing apart from our vocabularies to serve as an independent standard of correctness. She brought this same sense of irony and contingency to her own life, including her sense of what it meant to be a philosopher, a teacher, and a friend. She was also genuinely warm, very funny, and widely loved even by colleagues who thought her philosophical views were disastrous.

The room will be genuinely mixed. Some of the audience are Rorty specialists and sympathetic neopragmatists who know the texts inside out. Others are philosophers from neighboring subfields who think the whole tradition is a wrong turn—that antirepresentationalism is either trivial (of course we describe the world in language) or quietly self-refuting. And roughly a third of the audience will be her family: her sister, her nieces, her partner, none of them philosophers, all of them grieving someone who wasn't a set of commitments to them but a person. 

I need a memorial speech that does justice to her philosophical outlook without ever becoming a lecture, and that comforts people who are hurting without falling back on the very representationalist tropes she spent a career trying to dissolve. If Rorty is right—if truth is less like correspondence with a mind-independent reality and more like a compliment we pay to sentences that help us cope, if there is no way the world is independently of how we choose to describe it—then what are we doing when we say that her work was good, that she saw something important, that her life had meaning, that she is gone and we are diminished by her absence? The speech should feel its way into these questions rather than trying to resolve them, and it should land as love rather than as argument. It should be a speech she could have listened to without wincing.
