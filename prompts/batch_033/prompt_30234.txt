I've spent the past few evenings trying to build a deflationary response to Wigner's puzzle about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences, and the response has worked too well, which makes me suspect it contains a bug. The context: a physicist friend takes Wigner's puzzle seriously and thinks there is a genuine phenomenon to explain in the fact that mathematics developed for purely internal reasons — complex numbers, non-Euclidean geometry, Lie groups — later turns out to be load-bearing in physics. My prior is heavily against miracle stories, so I wanted a selection-effect account. But when I write the account out, it seems to prove that there is no difference between prediction and curve-fitting, which is a conclusion I would only accept at gunpoint.

Here is the argument I ended up with. (1) The evidence any theory ever confronts is finite: some finite list of measurements and observations. (2) For any finite body of data, some mathematical structure fits it to any desired accuracy; with enough free parameters you can pass a curve through any finite set of points. (3) We only ever meet the structures that happen to fit, because the failures are either never tried or never published; this is a straightforward selection effect. (4) Therefore the fact that a piece of mathematics developed for its own sake — complex numbers in quantum mechanics, or the SU(3) classification scheme that predicted a previously unseen particle before it was observed — fits physics is not surprising: among a large enough space of mathematical structures, some will fit, and those are the ones we notice. (5) But the same reasoning applies to every scientific theory whatever: any finite dataset can be accommodated by some theory, and we only hear about the theories that survived. (6) So there is no evidential difference between a theory that made a precise, novel prediction before the observation and a theory that was tuned after the fact to the same data. (7) But that is absurd: the entire practice of testing predictions, and the difference between physics and numerology, rests on there being such a difference. (8) Therefore the deflationary story in (1)–(4) must be wrong somewhere, even though each step looked forced.

I want to keep the deflationary impulse behind (1)–(4); I would rather not go back to treating the applicability of mathematics as a miracle, and my instinct is that the puzzle is largely an artefact of counting the hits and ignoring the misses. But I also refuse to accept (6)–(7). Then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question, and say what rejecting the step costs — which alternative I am forced into.
