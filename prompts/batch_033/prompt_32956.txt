Been chewing on an argument a friend dropped in a Discord server and I can't find the exit. It's about old evidence, and it seems to prove way too much — like, if it works, then basically none of the actual history of science counts as confirmation, which can't be right. Here's the argument as he laid it out:

(1) On Bayesianism, evidence E confirms a hypothesis H just in case P(H|E) > P(H).
(2) If E is old evidence, you already knew E before H was ever on the table, so your P(E) = 1.
(3) Bayes' theorem says P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E).
(4) Plug in P(E)=1 and you get P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H).
(5) In the interesting cases, H was built precisely to entail E (or at least P(E|H)=1 once H is around), so P(H|E) = P(H).
(6) Therefore old evidence can never confirm a theory.
(7) But that's absurd: Mercury's perihelion precession was known for decades and then got treated as confirming general relativity; same story with Brownian motion, the photoelectric effect, you name it.
(8) So either Bayesianism is false, or "confirmation" isn't the same thing as probability-raising.

I'd much rather keep Bayesianism and keep the idea that old evidence can confirm. Wich step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost the standard Bayesian picture? I keep feeling like the slide is somewhere around (2) or (5), but every time I try to deny one of them the whole rest of the epistemology starts sagging, so I want to know what I'm buying.
