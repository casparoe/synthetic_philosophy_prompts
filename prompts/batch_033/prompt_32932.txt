Okay so I've been going around in circles on the Taurek thing about whether the numbers count, and I've built an argument that seems to prove too much, and I cant find the exit. It goes like this:

(1) Suppose you can save one stranger or five strangers, but not both. If you should save the five, the only reason could be that the five's claims to be saved add up to more weight than the one's claim.

(2) But claims don't add across separate people. The one person's claim isn't made weaker by there being five others, and there's no sixth person who has the sum of the five's claims. So the one's claim and the five's claims are on a par.

(3) So saving one versus saving five is a genuine tie, and flipping a coin is fine.

(4) Same reasoning for one versus a million: still a tie.

(5) But if a coin flip between saving one and saving a million is really permissible, something has gone wrong.

(6) And the anti-aggregation thought in (2) seems to generalize. If harms can't be added across persons, then one person's being tortured can never be outweighed by any number of mild headaches spread across different people, since there's no one who has the headache-total. But then there's no number of headaches such that we should prefer preventing the headaches to stopping the torture. That would also mean we can't justify imposing even tiny costs on one person for the sake of large benefits to many others, which would rule out almost every actual policy decision.

So either I give up (2) and owe a story about how adding across persons can be okay, or I keep (2) and I'm stuck with (4) and (6). Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (2) if I can, but I want to know what I'm paying.
