Construct a thought experiment in which an agent whose degrees of belief satisfy the probability axioms at every time is nevertheless vulnerable to a diachronic Dutch strategy — a sequence of bets, each acceptable when offered, that collectively guarantees the agent a net loss. The vulnerability must be a violation of the principle of Reflection: the agent's current credence in a proposition, conditional on their future self having credence r in that proposition, must fail to equal r.

Constraints. The scenario must be ordinary-life rather than science-fictional: no memory-erasing drugs, no hypnosis, no perfect predictors. There must be only finitely many relevant states. Every bet in the strategy must be one the agent regards as fair or favorable at the moment it is offered, judged by the agent's credences at that moment. The bookie may be no better than a shrewd human who has observed the agent's public betting behavior and knows their announced plans. And the change in the agent's credence must be an ordinary cognitive change a thoughtful person could undergo — such as losing information or reassigning trust — rather than a stipulated irrationality.

After presenting the scenario, do all of the following. State the agent's prior credences, the possible evidence or information states, and the anticipated future credences; show explicitly that the synchronic probability axioms hold at each time and that Reflection fails. Write out the bookie's bets at each stage, compute the net payoff in every state, and confirm the sure loss. Identify the structural feature of the case that makes the Dutch strategy possible. Then explain what a defender of the agent's rationality would say in response: in particular, whether the strategy shows that the agent was irrational at the earlier time, and what happens to the strategy if the agent is allowed to precommit to declining the bookie's later offers or if the anticipated change in credence is removed.
