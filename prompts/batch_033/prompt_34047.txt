Over the last several decades, referendums have become an increasingly common tool for settling major constitutional and policy questions — membership in the European Union, the legalization of same-sex marriage, independence movements, revisions to national charters. When the votes are counted, the winning side is routinely described, by politicians and the press alike, as the expression of "the will of the people." Yet the same political systems that resort to referendums on some questions decline to hold them on others — tax policy, criminal sentencing, environmental regulation — and the criteria for which issues merit a popular vote seem anything but principled. Moreover, the very same actors who invoke "the will of the people" after a 52–48 result will, the next day, treat the 48 percent as belonging to the people no less than the majority does.

I want to know what philosophical commitments this practice — the practice of holding referendums and, more specifically, of describing their outcomes as "the will of the people" — presupposes. Does it presuppose that there exists a unified popular will prior to the vote, which a binary question can elicit? Does it presuppose that a majority vote constitutes the will of the whole, or merely that it serves as the best available proxy for it? What view of collective agency and group intentionality is quietly being assumed here, and does the practice rule out plausible rivals — for instance, the view that "the people" is not a group agent at all, and cannot have a will in anything but a metaphorical sense? Does the selective use of referendums (this issue gets a vote, that one does not) presuppose an additional theory about which questions are fit for popular willing and which are not — and if so, is that theory consistent with the rationale for having referendums in the first place? And what about the framing of a referendum question itself: does the practice presuppose that the question's wording merely surfaces a will that already exists, rather than partly constructing it? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of collective will could license it. Finally, tell me what would have to change — in the practice, in the surrounding institutions, or in the public rhetoric — if we replaced the presupposed view with a more deflationary one, on which referendums are simply one decision procedure among others rather than an expression of a collective subject's will.
