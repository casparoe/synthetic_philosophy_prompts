alright so i had this massive argument with a mate last night — we were at the pub for hours — about whether dreams are something you actually experience while you're asleep, or whether they're more like stories your brain cobbles together when you wake up. i took the second side, he took the first.

and here's the thing i kept noticing, not just in his arguements but in mine too when i wasn't careful. every time he wanted to prove dreams are real experiences, he'd point to some feature of the dream report. "but i remembered it in such detail." "but i felt scared when i woke up." "but the dream had a whole narrative structure." and every time, i wanted to say — those are all features of the waking memory, or the waking feeling, or the story you're telling me right now. you're using properties of the report to prove properties of the thing the report is about, but the report is the only access we have.

and then i realised i've seen this exact move in loads of other places. someone says "my childhood was happy" and when you push them, they point to how vividly they remeber happy moments — but the vividness of the memory is a property of the memory now, not of the childhood then. or someone says "the trip was terrifying" and when you ask why, they say "because when i think back on it my heart races" — but that's a current physiological response, not evidence about the past event. or even in bigger debates: people argue that a near-death experience must be a glimpse of the afterlife because the reports are so consistent across cultures — but the consistency is a property of the reports, not of whatever (if anything) the reports are about.

i'm not making the general sceptical point that we can't know the past. i'm asking about this specific argumentative move: treating a property of a representation (a memory, a report, a story) as if it's direct evidence of a property of the thing represented, when the representation is the only evidence you have for the thing in the first place. it's not quite begging the question, because the person isn't assuming the conclusion — they're genuinely trying to provide evidence. but the evidence they're providing is always one step removed from where they think it is.

does this move have a name? if it's a known fallacy or a recognised problem in epistemology, i want to know what it's called and how the literature carves it up. and if it doesn't have a name, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from related things like circularity, the homunculus fallacy, or the transparency assumption about introspection?
