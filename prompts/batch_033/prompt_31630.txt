I teach an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics seminar: 14 students, one 80-minute session. Design a runnable classroom activity on verbal disputes in metaontology. The idea I want the students to feel, before any technical vocabulary is introduced, is that two people can disagree about how many objects there are in a scene — and even about which answer is correct — while agreeing on every ordinary empirical fact about the scene; the remaining disagreement concerns which way of using "object" and "there is" to adopt, not what the world is like. The activity should force this discovery from the inside rather than announce it: no lecture up front, and the terms "verbal dispute," "metalinguistic negotiation," and related vocabulary should not appear until the debrief.

Give me the full design: the rules or roles; the materials (physical objects and any handouts, with handout text described or supplied); a timeline for the 80 minutes; and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the relevant distinctions: first-order factual disagreement versus disagreement about which framework to use; a merely verbal dispute that dissolves once each side fully understands the other's rules versus a metalinguistic negotiation that survives that understanding; and the special composition question (under what conditions some things compose a further thing). It should also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: in particular, the live question of whether one counting framework is metaphysically better than another — more joint-carving, more structural — or whether the choice between frameworks is only pragmatic. The activity can stage that question but not settle it, and the facilitator will have to supply it. Keep the entire design to no more than 1,200 words.
