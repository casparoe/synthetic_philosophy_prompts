ok so i've been thinking about Frankfurt cases and i stumbled into an argument that seems to show they prove way too much, and i can't figure out where the mistake is. here goes:

(1) Frankfurt cases are supposed to show that you can be morally responsible for an action even if you literally couldn't have done otherwise. the key move is that the counterfactual intervener (Black and his fancy device) never actually does anything — Jones decides on his own, the device stays idle, and the "actual sequence" is a perfectly normal free action. so PAP is false.

(2) If (1) is right, then what matters for responsibility is the actual sequence — the real causal history of the decision and action — not facts about what would have happened in other possible scenarios.

(3) Now take a manipulation case. Suppose a neuroscientist secretly implants a desire in Jones to vote for candidate X. the implant works by slightly tweaking Jones's existing preference structure so that he ends up endorsing X for reasons that feel entirely his own. Jones deliberates, weighs considerations, and freely decides to vote for X. the actual sequence of his deliberation is indistinguishable from a normal case. the implant is part of the actual causal history, sure, but so is everything else — his childhood, his genes, whatever he ate for breakfast.

(4) By (2), Jones is responsible. the implant is just another causal factor in the actual sequence.

(5) But that seems wrong. a secretly manipulated agent isn't responsible, or at least not fully. the manipulation undermines responsibility even though the actual sequence of deliberation looks normal from the inside.

(6) The natural fix is to say: wait, the actual sequence includes the implant, and the implant is a responsibility-undermining factor. but then why isn't Black's device also a responsibility-undermining factor? it's also part of the actual sequence — it's physically present in Jones's brain, monitoring his neural activity. the only difference is that the implant actively shapes the decision while the device merely stands ready to intervene. but both are actual features of the situation that remove Jones's control in some sense.

(7) So either Frankfurt cases fail (because the device IS part of the actual sequence and DOES undermine responsibility), or manipulation cases don't undermine responsibility (which seems absurd), or we need a principled distinction between "active" and "merely standing ready" in the actual sequence — and i'm not sure such a distinction exists.

where does this go wrong? i have a feeling the problem is somewhere around step (2) — maybe the actual sequence criterion is being stated too broadly — but i want to know exactly which premise to reject and what i'd be giving up. also if you think the manipulation case and the Frankfurt case are disanalogous in some important way i'm not seeing, i want to hear about that.
