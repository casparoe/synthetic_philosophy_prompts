I am a software engineer with a background in typed functional programming, and over the past year I have been working through an introduction to the Curry–Howard correspondence and to proof assistants such as Coq and Lean. This has made mathematical constructivism personally relevant to me in a way I did not anticipate: what began as a practical interest in writing verified code has hardened into a philosophical position I now want to defend. I have drafted a short essay for a reading group, and I would like comments on it before I present it. I am not trained as a philosopher or a logician, so I am especially worried that I have over-assimilated a technical idea and drawn a philosophical conclusion from it too quickly.

The essay argues, in outline, as follows:

1. The Curry–Howard correspondence shows that a proof of an implication is a function and, more generally, that a proof of a statement of the form "for every x there is a y such that R(x, y)" is a procedure that computes, from any given x, a witness y. In classical mathematics, by contrast, one can prove an existence statement from the mere inconsistency of its denial, and such a proof need not yield any witness at all. Classical existence is therefore only a double-negated notion of existence: it tells us that it would be contradictory for no such object to exist, not that an object is actually available.

2. Under the Curry–Howard correspondence, the law of excluded middle is realized computationally by control operators such as call/cc, whose type is Peirce's law. Classical logic is therefore not an alternative theory of truth but constructive logic extended with a single control operator. Since the double-negation translation embeds classical logic into constructive logic, classical logic is, for all mathematical purposes, a conservative extension of constructive logic; its distinctive proofs are just constructive proofs that manipulate their own continuations.

3. The historical objection to constructivism was that it cripples mathematics by rejecting principles on which working mathematicians rely. Modern proof assistants undermine this objection: they allow large parts of mathematics to be developed constructively, and when a classical axiom is imported, the system records exactly where the nonconstructive step occurs. This turns the rejection of excluded middle from a prohibition into a kind of instrumentation.

4. I conclude that the burden of proof has shifted. A classical mathematician who uses nonconstructive existence proofs owes an account of what "there exists" means if it does not mean that a witness is available. Absent such an account, constructive mathematics should be treated as the default interpretation of mathematical language.

I would be grateful for comments of the following kinds. First, on technical accuracy: which of the claims in (1) and (2) are correct, which are overstated, and where have I conflated distinct results? Second, on the philosophy: does the argument from proof assistants to constructivism actually go through, or does it establish only something weaker about how mathematics is formalized? Third, on the conclusion: would a classical mathematician have a fair reply to my characterization of classical existence, and would a committed constructivist object to any part of my framing? Finally, I would welcome your view on whether the essay's thesis is interesting enough in its present form, or whether I should weaken it, and if so, to what.
