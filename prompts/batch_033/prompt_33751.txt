I've been reading some epistemology on the side, and I keep running into two positions that seem to be doing the same work but are treated as distinct: epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment. Both want to explain why, when a lot is at stake, we hesitate to say someone "knows" something even when their evidence hasn't changed. Contextualism says the word "knows" is context-sensitive — the standards for correctly applying it rise with the stakes. Pragmatic encroachment says the stakes literally help determine whether the knowledge relation holds, not just whether we're willing to assert it.

I can see these are different at the level of what they claim is shifting — the semantics of a word versus the obtaining of a relation. But I'm having trouble seeing where they actually come apart. Do they make different predictions about any concrete scenario, or are they empirically equivalent? If they are genuinely distinct views, what kind of argument or evidence would decide between them? And is there a practical or philosophical difference that turns on which one is right, or is this a case where the distinction matters only to specialists?
