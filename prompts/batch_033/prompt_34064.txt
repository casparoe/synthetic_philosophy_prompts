I just came from a long argument with a friend about whether moral status comes in degrees. She defends what I'll call the threshold view: the capacity for valenced experience — felt pleasure or pain — is necessary and sufficient for full moral status. A being either has it or doesn't; if it does, its interests count fully, and if it doesn't, they count for nothing. I defend a gradualist view: moral weight scales continuously with the richness of a being's sentience, so a dog's interests matter more than a mouse's, a mouse's more than a beetle's, and so on down. We kept talking past each other because in all the stock examples — the puppy and the rock, the chimp and the carrot — the two views agree on what to do. I need a case where they don't.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications:

1. Both views must agree that every being in the scenario has moral status — i.e., the gradualist must not classify any being as a zero-weight patient that the threshold theorist counts as a full moral patient, and vice versa. All beings must be ones that both views clearly place above the threshold for valenced experience.

2. Despite agreeing on the status of each individual being, the two views must deliver conflicting verdicts about which of two available actions is morally preferable.

3. The scenario must be realistic: a farming decision, an ecological management problem, a research protocol, a consumer choice, or the like. No science fiction, no speculative technologies, no hypothetical beings.

4. One paragraph.

After the scenario, provide the following:

- Walk through the reasoning each view applies to the case and state the verdict each arrives at.
- Identify the specific structural feature of the two views that generates the disagreement. Since both views agree on which beings matter, the divergence must come from how they handle trade-offs across beings of different degrees of sentience — pinpoint what that feature is, and explain why it is not simply a disagreement about harm magnitude.
- Explain what happens if you remove or alter that feature: does the case collapse back into agreement, and if so, how?
- Briefly note how a defender of the view that comes out looking worse in your scenario would attack the case — which premise or move they would reject, and whether that move has independent costs for their view.
