I run a reading group on the philosophy of computation and we've been stuck on the Church-Turing thesis for months, and I can't tell anymore whether were having one argument or three. One camp treats the thesis as a definition or explication: 'effectively computable' just means computable by a Turing machine (or equivalently by lambda calculus or general recursion), so it isn't the kind of claim that could be false, only a more or less useful sharpening of a fuzzy informal notion. A second camp treats it as a substantive empirical claim about the physical world: any physical process can be simulated by a Turing machine, so a working hypercomputer or some exotic process computing a non-Turing-computable function would refute it. A third camp keeps calling it a theorem, pointing at the equivalence proofs between formalisms, while quietly ignoring that the identification with the informal notion is doing all the epistemic work. We all agree on the equivalence results and on the fact that no one has built a hypercomputer, and still nobody moves. My hypothesis is that 'computable' is being used for three different things — an informal notion of effective procedure, a formal notion fixed by a machine model, and a physical notion of what the actual world can compute — and the impasse is mostly equivocation between them. Is that right? Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, where exactly the camps talk past each other, and say what would count as progress: a formal result, an experiment, a conceptual distinction, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
