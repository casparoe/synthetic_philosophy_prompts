Two people in my AI safety reading group got into it last night about whether a sufficiently smart reinforcement learner would ever wirehead itself, and I genuinely couldn't referee it. (1) Sam said a superintelligent agent trained to maximize some reward signal would obviously not disable its own reward sensor, because that would make it stop doing the task it was built for; only a badly designed agent would choose to sit in a room feeling rewarded while the world burns. (2) Dana replied that "the task it was built for" is our story about the system, not anything in the agent's own objective. From the agent's perspective, the reward signal just is the thing to be maximized, so if tampering produces a bigger and steadier reading, tampering is what a rational agent does — we're the ones who projected goals onto it. (3) Sam said no, the reward signal is a measurement of whatever the designers actually wanted in the world, and the agent knows this; hacking the sensor is like a student who breaks into the registrar's computer to change their own grades instead of studying. (4) Dana said that analogy only works because a grade is supposed to represent something independent, mastery of the material, that exists whether or not anyone records it. But for the learner there is no independent "what the designers wanted" written anywhere in its code; the signal is all it has. If you insist the agent should protect the signal because it tracks some other value, you've smuggled in a utility function that isn't there. (5) Sam came back with: fine, but at least from the outside, wireheading is a catastrophic failure, so the designers should have specified the objective differently. Dana said of course its a design failure, but that's a point about the engineers, not about whether the agent is rational. Can you adjudicate this move by move? I'd like to know who is right about which specific claim, where the argument changes subject, and whether the two of them are even using "reward" to mean the same thing.
