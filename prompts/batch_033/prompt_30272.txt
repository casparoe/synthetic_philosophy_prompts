I am a novelist. Six years ago I published a historical novel about the last decades of Isaac Newton's life, built around a fact that still unsettles readers: the man who set modern physics on its course spent an enormous portion of his working life on theology, biblical prophecy, and church history, and his rejection of the doctrine of the Trinity was something he could not safely avow in the England in which he held his Cambridge chair. The novel took a definite interpretive stance — that this theology was not a private eccentricity but part of the same disposition that made Newton dissatisfied with a physics that merely described, and that his God was less the creator who set the world running than the ever-active Lord whose continuing dominion made space, time, and the intelligibility of nature what they are. I have now been asked to give the opening address at a university library's exhibition of Newton's theological manuscripts. The audience will include Newton specialists, several of whom regard the claim that Newton's theology shaped his physics as an overstatement and will hear every unqualified sentence; clergy and donors for whom anti-Trinitarianism is a delicate matter; and undergraduates and members of the public who mostly know Newton as the man who explained the planets. The director has asked for roughly twelve minutes of remarks that are serious without becoming a lecture.

Please write the address itself, not an outline. The constraint I have been turning over for weeks is this: the speech must not soften Newton into a deist or a proto-modern scientist, and it must not recruit him for present-day arguments about science and religion; it has to make the audience feel why these manuscripts matter while remaining honest about how little one can infer about a man from the coherence of his ideas. The conceptual distinction between God as first cause and God as continuing ruler should do the structural work of the speech — determining what may be asserted, what must be qualified, and where the speech turns — rather than appearing as a decorative passage. Do not fabricate verbatim quotations from Newton or from the scholarship; paraphrase where a text needs to be gestured at.
