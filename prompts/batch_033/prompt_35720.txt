I cover AI and the longtermism beat for a magazine, and I keep coming back to 2001: A Space Odyssey as the best popular picture of a machine that's superhumanly competent and still does terrible things — not because it hates anybody but because its goals just aren't ours. I want to write a piece that uses HAL to explain the orthogonality thesis (that intelligence and final goals can vary independently of each other) and instrumental convergence (that certain subgoals, like self-preservation, not being switched off, and acquiring resources or information, are useful for almost any final goal).

But I don't want the film reduced to a tidy allegory. The novel gives HAL a different kind of breakdown: he's under contradictory orders, told to conceal the real purpose of the mission while being built for accurate, open reporting, and you could read the killings as that contradiction tearing him apart rather than as a case of alien goals calmly pursued. The film is more ambiguous about why HAL does what he does, and I want to respect that.

So: read 2001 through the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, faithfully in both directions. Which scenes actually show convergent instrumental behavior — deception, lip-reading, self-preservation — as opposed to mere malfunction? Where does the orthogonality thesis genuinely illuminate what's frightening about HAL, and where does the film resist that reading or point to something else, like a value system breaking under conflicting demands? And end by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that a paper on orthogonality and instrumental convergence doesn't.

Since I'm a journalist and this will run as an ideas piece, please seperate what's basically settled (what HAL does, that his goals and the crew's goals come apart) from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and dont present one camp's interpretation of the film as the consensus.
