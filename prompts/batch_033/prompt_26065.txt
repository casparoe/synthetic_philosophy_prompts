Most animal welfare law draws a sharp line between terrestrial vertebrates and aquatic animals, and an even sharper one between vertebrates and invertebrates. In the United States, the Humane Methods of Slaughter Act mandates pre-slaughter stunning for cattle, pigs, and sheep but excludes poultry—and says nothing at all about fish. The Animal Welfare Act, the centerpiece of federal animal protection law, defines "animal" so as to exclude all cold-blooded species. In the European Union, a 2009 regulation on slaughter requires stunning for all vertebrates, but its provisions for fish are far less detailed than for mammals, and decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters, shrimp) were only recognized as sentient and brought under some welfare provisions in the last few years, after decades of exclusion. Meanwhile, the scale of the excluded populations is staggering: roughly 440 billion farmed shrimp are slaughtered each year—more than four times the number of farmed fish and roughly six times the number of farmed chickens. The standard slaughter method for farmed fish is asphyxiation in air or on ice, which can take minutes to hours depending on species and temperature; for shrimp it is often immersion in hot water or live chilling. These methods would be unthinkable for a pig or a cow.

I want to know what theory of moral status this entire pattern of practice presupposes. I am not asking what theory any individual legislator or regulator consciously holds; I am asking what view of which beings count, and why, is implicit in the structure of the law itself—the view you would have to attribute to the system for its differential treatment to make sense.

In particular, I want you to do the following:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Does the law presuppose that moral status tracks phylogenetic proximity to humans? That it tracks some threshold of neurological complexity? That it tracks a being's capacity to enter into reciprocal social relations with us? Or is the pattern better explained by something other than a theory of moral status—sheer path-dependence, consumer visibility, lobbying power, or the fact that we cannot read fish faces?

2. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to be compatible with. For instance: the practice is consistent with a view that fish and crustaceans lack sentience, but does it actually require that view? Could someone who believes fish are sentient and suffer intensely still endorse the legal status quo on other grounds—tractability, enforcement costs, or a division of moral labor between law and individual conscience—without contradiction?

3. Identify internal inconsistencies. If the law's implicit theory is something like "sentience matters, but only above some complexity threshold," does the exclusion of fish but inclusion of poultry make sense on that theory? If the theory is "beings that can feel pain deserve protection," does the near-total exclusion of decapods (who show pain-related behavioral changes, learn to avoid noxious stimuli, and respond to analgesics) cohere with it? If the theory is "only mammals count," why do we protect birds?

4. Tell me what would have to change—in statutes, in enforcement, in industry practice—if we replaced the presupposed theory with a straightforward sentientist view: that any being capable of valenced experience deserves equal consideration of its suffering, scaled only by the intensity and duration of that suffering. What would the regulatory landscape look like if we took that view seriously, and what would the first three practical changes be?
