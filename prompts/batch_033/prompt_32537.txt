Question: Explain the Ellsberg paradox and what it is standardly taken to show. In doing so, set out the structure of the example, state the choices people typically make, and assess whether these choices are rationally defensible or whether they reveal a mistake.

Student answer:

The Ellsberg paradox is an example due to Daniel Ellsberg. An urn contains 90 balls: 30 are red, and the remaining 60 are black or yellow in an unknown proportion. In the first choice, a person chooses between a bet that pays £100 if a red ball is drawn and a bet that pays £100 if a black ball is drawn. Most people prefer the red bet, since the probability of red is known to be 1/3, whereas the probability of black could be anywhere from 0 to 2/3. In the second choice, they choose between a bet on red-or-yellow and a bet on black-or-yellow. Most people prefer black-or-yellow, because that has a known probability of 2/3, while red-or-yellow is ambiguous, with a probability somewhere between 1/3 and 1.

These two choices cannot be represented by any single subjective probability assignment to black and yellow. Let p be the probability of black and q the probability of yellow, so p + q = 2/3. Preferring red over black requires p < 1/3, and preferring black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow requires q < 1/3. But p and q cannot both be less than 1/3 and still sum to 2/3. So the choices violate Savage's sure-thing principle, and show that subjective expected utility theory is descriptively inadequate.

The usual moral is that people are averse to ambiguity, not merely to risk. But I think the paradox is less damaging than it looks. We can keep expected utility theory by allowing the agent to have subjective probabilities over the possible compositions of the urn. The red bet has the same chance of paying out under every composition, whereas the black bet's chance varies with the composition. A risk-averse agent could therefore prefer red to black: red gives a sure 1/3 chance, while black gives an uncertain mixture of chances with the same average. Likewise, black-or-yellow gives a sure 2/3 chance, while red-or-yellow gives an uncertain mixture of chances, so risk aversion again explains the second preference. On this view, ambiguity aversion just is risk aversion about the second-order distribution over possible urn compositions.

So the Ellsberg choices do not reveal any irrationality. They merely show that the standard version of the theory was too crude, and that subjective expected utility should be extended to include second-order probabilities. Once extended in that way, it remains the correct normative theory of choice under uncertainty.
