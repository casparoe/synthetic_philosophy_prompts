At a fortnightly departmental reading group, two of my colleagues got into a protracted argument about what norm governs assertion. The dispute outlasted the biscuits and I have been asked to adjudicate it, but I am not confident I can do so fairly. Please go through their exchange move by move and assess who is right about what.

Here is roughly how the argument went:

(1) Eleanor began by defending the knowledge account: one should assert that p only if one knows that p. She offered two pieces of evidence. First, we routinely challenge assertions by asking 'How do you know?' — a question that would be beside the point if knowledge were not the relevant standard. Second, Moore-paradoxical sentences such as 'It is raining, but I do not know that it is' sound not merely odd but self-defeating in a way that suggests asserting p commits one to knowing p.

(2) James replied that the 'How do you know?' challenge is equally well explained by a truth norm: we ask how someone knows because knowing is a reliable route to truth, and truth is what we really care about. As for Moore-paradoxical sentences, 'It is raining, but I do not believe it is' sounds just as bad, yet no one thinks belief is the norm of assertion. The oddness of Moorean sentences is a general feature of representing oneself as committed to p while simultaneously disavowing that commitment — it tells us nothing specific about assertion.

(3) Eleanor countered that the belief version of the Moore paradox is parasitic on the knowledge version. The reason 'I do not believe it' clashes with asserting p is that asserting p represents oneself as knowing p, and knowing entails believing. If assertion only represented belief, then 'I assert that p but I do not believe that p' should be the fundamental Moorean absurdity — but it is not; the knowledge version is the paradigm.

(4) James shifted ground: consider a lottery with one winning ticket among a million. You hold a single ticket. Before the draw, you say to a friend, 'Your ticket will not win.' This seems like a perfectly proper assertion, and it is true, but you do not know it — for knowledge requires safety or some other anti-luck condition, and your belief could easily have been false. So the knowledge norm is too demanding; it incorrectly rules out ordinary, blameless assertions.

(5) Eleanor replied that the lottery case cuts both ways. Many people feel uneasy about flat-out asserting that a ticket will lose, precisely because they do not know it. The knowledge account explains this unease, whereas a truth norm or a justified-belief norm must explain why we hesitate at all. She added that if James's intuition is that lottery assertions are fine, he should explain why we so often hedge them: 'It is almost certain your ticket will not win.'

(6) James responded that the hedging is explained by Gricean considerations: we hedge because we want to avoid misleading our hearer about the strength of our evidence, not because assertion itself requires knowledge. He then raised a new case: suppose Maria has excellent evidence that her colleague is in his office — she saw him walk in ten minutes ago and the room has no other exit. She asserts, 'He is in his office.' Unbeknownst to her, he has just collapsed and been carried out by paramedics. Her assertion is false, so she does not know it. According to the knowledge norm, she has violated the norm of assertion. But this seems harsh; she did everything an epistemically responsible speaker would do.

(7) Eleanor answered that the knowledge norm does not entail that Maria is blameworthy — it only entails that her assertion was improper in the specific sense of violating the constitutive rule of assertion. Compare: a footballer who accidentally handles the ball when it is kicked at her arm from close range has still violated the handball rule, even though no one blames her. The rule is what it is. Moreover, we do criticise Maria's assertion ex post: we say 'You should not have said that — it turned out he was not there.' The knowledge norm captures this retrospective criticism; a truth norm would also rule it a violation, but a belief norm or a justified-belief norm would not.

(8) James made a final move: if the knowledge norm is a constitutive rule of assertion — like the rules of a game — then it should be impossible to assert something one does not know without thereby failing to assert at all. But we routinely describe people as asserting things they do not know; we do not withhold the word 'assertion' from them. So the knowledge norm, if it exists, is at most a regulative ideal, not a constitutive rule. And once we admit it is merely regulative, it is not clear why we should privilege knowledge over truth, justified belief, or any other epistemic standard.

At this point the group had to vacate the room, and the argument was never resolved.

For each of the eight moves above, assess whether the argument offered is sound, what it establishes or fails to establish, and which participant has the stronger position at that stage. Then give an overall verdict: which account of the norm of assertion — knowledge, truth, justified belief, or something else — emerges from the debate as the most defensible, and why.
