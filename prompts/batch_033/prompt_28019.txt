I'm a retired chemist, and after forty years in the lab I've started reading philosophy in earnest. Lately its been the deflationary theory of truth, mostly because I kept seeing the term and realized I couldn't say what it meant. A book I picked up (popular, not academic) summarized the view like this: deflationists deny that truth is a real property. On their account, to say that 'snow is white' is true is just to say that snow is white, and since 'is true' never adds anything, the concept of truth is basically idle — a bit of language we could drop without losing anything. Something about that summary bothers me, but I can't tell whether the problem is the book or my own gaps. I've since come across deflationists making a point of the truth predicate being useful for generalizations like 'everything the witness said is true,' which seems hard to square with calling it idle. Could you go through the book's summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful deflationist would accept, which are overstatements or conflatations, and whether the final claim — that truth does no real work — actually follows from the parts that are accurate? I'd rather not internalize a caricature.
