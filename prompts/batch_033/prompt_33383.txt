I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in formal epistemology, and I am grading a problem-set question on Bayesian updating. I would like an independent assessment of one student's answer before I finalize the mark. The question I set was:

"State Richard Jeffrey's rule of probability kinematics, and explain what it is a rule for. In doing so: (i) specify the assumptions the rule makes about the partition and about the new probabilities over it; (ii) state the rigidity condition and explain the role it plays; (iii) explain how Jeffrey conditionalization relates to ordinary conditionalization; and (iv) say whether the rule follows from the axioms of probability together with the new probabilities over the partition, or whether some further substantive assumption is needed."

One student wrote:

"Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics, is a rule for revising one's credences when the evidence is not a proposition learned with certainty but a change in how likely one takes the members of a partition to be. Let {E_1, ..., E_n} be a partition: the E_i are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive. Suppose experience moves my credences in the E_i to new values q(E_i), where q is a probability distribution over the partition. Then for any proposition H,

P_new(H) = Σ_i q(E_i) P_old(H | E_i).

The rigidity condition says that for every proposition H and each element E_i of the partition, my new conditional probabilities given E_i equal my old ones: P_new(H | E_i) = P_old(H | E_i). This is what licenses carrying the old conditional probabilities over into the weighted average.

The formula is simply the law of total probability applied to the new credence function. For any probability function, P_new(H) = Σ_i P_new(E_i) P_new(H | E_i); replacing P_new(E_i) with q(E_i) and P_new(H | E_i) with the old conditional probabilities yields the rule. Since the law of total probability is a theorem of the probability calculus, no substantive assumption beyond coherence is needed once the new probabilities over the partition are fixed.

A further consequence of rigidity is that the unconditional probabilities of propositions outside the partition remain unchanged through the update; the only propositions whose probabilities change are the partition elements themselves.

Ordinary conditionalization is the limiting case in which q assigns all the mass to a single E_j; the formula then reduces to P_new(H) = P_old(H | E_j). Jeffrey's rule is therefore a generalization of ordinary conditionalization, not a rival to it."
