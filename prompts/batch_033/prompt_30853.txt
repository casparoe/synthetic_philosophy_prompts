I chair the model-review panel of a national environmental regulator. About twice a month we receive a model submitted to support a permitting, quota-setting, or impact-assessment decision, and its authors openly concede that central assumptions are false — logistic growth, a representative agent, flat terrain, perfect mixing. We have two days per model, the panelists are domain scientists rather than modelers, and our ruling must survive administrative review. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the F-twist — the doctrine usually associated with Milton Friedman that the realism of an assumption does not matter if the model predicts well — that neither adopts that doctrine wholesale nor dismisses it. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with an accept or a reject, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the step is a judgment call rather than a checkable fact. The procedure must distinguish among uses of the same model — forecasting observables under roughly unchanged conditions, counterfactual evaluation of a policy change, and mechanistic explanation — and must explain how the same false assumption can be harmless in one use and disqualifying in another. It should handle the hard cases: assumptions the model's output is insensitive to, assumptions that do causal work in a counterfactual, and compensating errors that produce good fits for the wrong reason.

Then run the procedure on three submissions. (1) A fisheries stock-assessment model that assumes logistic growth, proposed for setting next year's quota under current conditions. (2) A general-equilibrium model with a representative agent and rational expectations, proposed to estimate the effects of a large structural change in the same economy. (3) An air-dispersion model that assumes flat terrain, proposed to site a new facility near a valley town. At least one of these should come out against what the submitting agency wants, and you should say which. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do — including where predictive success alone cannot settle the matter and where the panel is left with a decision that no checklist can make for it.
