I run a nonprofit that designs and facilitates citizens' assemblies and participatory budgeting processes for municipal governments. We handle eight to ten processes a year, each involving a few dozen residents over three or four weekends, producing recommendations that go to a city council for an up-or-down vote. The stakes are real: councils adopt our assemblies' recommendations more often than not, and a badly designed process can produce recommendations that are incoherent, easily dismissed, or worse than the status quo.

I've been reading Dewey — not systematically, but enough to recognize that his view of democracy as a method of collective experimental inquiry has implications for how we should structure these sessions. But I can't turn the philosophy into a facilitator's protocol myself, and I don't have the background to know which of Dewey's distinctions are load-bearing and which I can simplify for a facilitator who has never read a word of him.

Please write me a procedure — a step-by-step guide my facilitators can actually follow when designing and running a deliberative session — grounded in Dewey's experimentalist conception of democracy and inquiry. It should cover: how to frame the initial question so it functions as a genuine problem that opens inquiry rather than closing it; how to structure the presentation of evidence and expert input so it feeds collective hypothesis-formation rather than preempting it; how to guide the group through testing and revising proposals against anticipated consequences; and how to know when the process needs another round of inquiry rather than a vote. The procedure should be usable by a facilitator who has about fifteen minutes to review it before a session and no background in philosophy.

Then apply the procedure to two cases: (1) a participatory budgeting session where residents must allocate a fixed sum between funding a new library branch, expanding a youth sports program, and improving stormwater drainage — three proposals with incommensurable kinds of evidence behind them; and (2) a citizens' assembly on whether the city should adopt a rent stabilization ordinance, where the expert testimony is sharply contested and the group is polarized from the outset. For at least one of these, show me where the procedure produces a recommendation my team will not like — because it demands more time than we can give it, because it forces a conclusion the group is trying to avoid, or because it reveals that the question as framed by the city council is the wrong question and needs to be reopened.

Be explicit about where the procedure will break: where Dewey's ideal of inquiry assumes conditions that a three-weekend citizens' assembly cannot meet, where the procedure papers over a genuine tension in Dewey's own thought, and where a facilitator will have to make a judgment call that no procedure can eliminate. If you think the whole project of operationalizing Dewey this way is misguided — that his experimentalism is a regulative ideal, not something you can turn into a facilitator's checklist — say so plainly rather than giving me a procedure that misrepresents it.
