I work as a software engineer on a team that builds conversational AI systems for customer support. Recently we've been stuck on a recurring question: whether two of our systems — one is a standard large language model fine-tuned on our product catalog, the other is a retrieval-augmented generation system that pulls facts from a vector database at inference time — can be said to "believe the same things" about our inventory, pricing, and policies. The RAG system has completely different internal machinery: it stores knowledge as embeddings and retrieves relevant chunks on the fly, while the fine-tuned model has (presumably) encoded its knowledge directly in its weights during training. Our product manager wants to treat them as interchangeable for certain customer-facing tasks, but several engineers have pushed back, arguing that the systems are too different under the hood for that to be safe. The disagreement keeps resurfacing every time we consider swapping one system for the other in a new context, and we have no systematic way to resolve it.

I've been reading a bit about functionalism in philosophy of mind — the view that mental states are defined by their causal roles rather than by the physical stuff they're made of — and the related idea of multiple realizability, which says the same mental state can be implemented in radically different physical systems. This seems directly relevant to our problem. If functionalism is right, then two systems with different architectures could literally have the same beliefs, as long as those beliefs play the same causal role in producing behavior. But I have no idea how to operationalize this. What I need is a decision procedure: given two systems with different internal implementations, how do I determine whether they implement the same mental state — or, more practically, the same belief or intention — with respect to some specified domain? The procedure should be something my team can actually run. We have access to the systems' inputs and outputs and can design experiments, but we cannot meaningfully inspect the internals of the LLM (the weights are opaque to us). I need a step-by-step process: what to test, in what order, what counts as passing a given step, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it hands the decision to a human because the question turns on a philosophical judgment call rather than an empirical result.

Please ground the procedure in what functionalism and the multiple realizability thesis actually claim — don't just give me software testing best practices dressed up in philosophy jargon. I want to know which philosophical distinctions (for example, between wide and narrow content, or between functional isomorphism and mere behavioral equivalence) need to survive the translation into an engineering procedure, and which can be safely set aside. Then apply the procedure to two worked examples: (1) the two catalog systems I described above, and (2) a case where we compare a human customer service agent and an AI assistant, asking whether they can be said to "intend to help the customer" in the same sense. Flag any steps where the procedure forces a conclusion my team might resist, and be honest about what the procedure cannot settle — cases it was never designed to handle and cases where it gives an answer but the answer might be misleading. Please write the answer in American English.
