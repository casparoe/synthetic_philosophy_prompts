I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on philosophy of perception this term, and I'm working on a term paper about disjunctivism and the argument from illusion. Over the semester I've found myself drawn to a cluster of views that each seemed compelling when we read the relevant papers, but now that I'm trying to assemble them into a single position, I'm starting to worry that they don't all fit together. I'd like an audit before I commit to anything in writing.

Here are the commitments I've accumulated, roughly as I'd state them:

(1) Naïve realism about veridical perception: when I veridically perceive a tomato, the tomato itself and its redness are literal constituents of my conscious experience. Perception is fundamentally a relation of acquaintance with mind-independent objects and their properties.

(2) Disjunctivism about perceptual experience: veridical perceptions and subjectively indistinguishable hallucinations are mental states of fundamentally different kinds. There is no "common factor" — no mental core shared across the good case and the bad case.

(3) The argument from illusion has genuine dialectical force and cannot simply be dismissed as confused. Even if it doesn't ultimately succeed, it identifies a real explanatory burden that any theory of perception must discharge.

(4) If two conscious experiences are subjectively indistinguishable to the subject upon reflection, then they are of the same conscious type — that is, they share their phenomenal character. Indistinguishability is a criterion of type-identity for experiences.

(5) Introspection is a generally reliable guide to the character of one's own occurrent conscious experience. It is not infallible, but it provides strong prima facie evidence about what one's experience is like.

(6) The best explanation of why hallucinations can be subjectively indistinguishable from veridical perceptions is that the two share a common mental factor — something like representational content or qualia — that accounts for the indistinguishability.

I can see that some of these are in obvious tension (I'm not blind to the clash between (2) and (6)), but I suspect the picture is more complicated than just one contradiction. Some tensions might be only apparent and dissolve under the right distinctions; others might be genuine but surmountable with a small refinement; and some pairs might actually reinforce each other in ways I haven't noticed. I'd also like to know whether any of these commitments, once you draw out their implications, commit me to something I probably don't want to sign up for.

Please approach this as an expert philosopher of perception auditing a student's position before a paper deadline. Think through each pair of commitments carefully, distinguishing real incompatibilities from surface-level clashes that a well-drawn distinction can resolve.
