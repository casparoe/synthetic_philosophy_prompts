I work as a software engineer on a team that builds products around large language models. We use a standard pipeline: pretrain a transformer on a large text corpus with a next-token-prediction objective, fine-tune it with RLHF for helpfulness and accuracy, then evaluate it on benchmarks like MMLU and TruthfulQA that measure whether its outputs match real-world facts. When the model scores well, our documentation and marketing describe it as "knowing" things and "reasoning" about them. When it produces false statements, we call those "hallucinations" and treat them as bugs to be fixed through better training or retrieval augmentation.

I've been thinking about what this whole workflow presupposes about meaning and understanding. The model has never interacted with the world. It was trained only on text — strings of characters produced by humans — and its training signal was purely about predicting which token comes next in a sequence. It has no sensory access to the objects its words refer to, no ability to check a claim against reality, and no feedback loop that connects its outputs to states of the world. And yet we hold it to a standard of factual accuracy, as if its words are supposed to latch onto something outside the text.

I've come across the phrase "symbol grounding problem" and I gather it has to do with whether a system that manipulates symbols purely on the basis of their relationships to other symbols can ever be said to understand them, or to mean anything by them, or to refer to things in the world. But I don't know the philosophical terrain well enough to map my engineering practices onto it.

So here's what I want to understand: What theory of meaning — or reference, or understanding — does the standard LLM pipeline presuppose? When we train a model on text alone and then evaluate it for factual accuracy, what are we implicitly committing ourselves to about how symbols acquire meaning? Are there well-developed philosophical positions that would say our evaluation framework rests on a confusion — that a system trained this way cannot, in principle, "know" or "refer" to anything, and that treating its outputs as true or false is a category mistake? And if someone took those positions seriously, what would they have to change about how we build, train, evaluate, or talk about these systems?

I'm not looking for reading recommendations or citations. I want someone to reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning that our engineering practices commit us to, distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely happen to be compatible with, flag any internal inconsistencies (for example, between a training objective that treats language as distributional and an evaluation framework that treats it as referential), and tell me what the alternative would look like if we adopted a different theory of meaning — one that requires something beyond statistical patterns in text.
