I am presenting a paper at a modal epistemology workshop next term, and I want to arrive with a prepared Q&A strategy rather than discovering my audience's real objections during the discussion. The thesis I am defending is this: conceivability is a genuine but highly uneven guide to possibility. It earns its keep only when the conceived scenario is structurally detailed and the conceiver has actively ruled out the standard ways such scenarios go wrong — hidden contradictions, misdescribed identities, and imported impossibilities — and it is weakest exactly in the high-stakes metaphysical cases where philosophers lean on it hardest, such as zombies, ghosts, and claims about the separability of mind and body. My positive proposal is that the move from conceivability to possibility is best reconstructed as a defeasible inference to the best explanation from our own cognitive capacities and their track record, rather than as an a priori entailment or a merely psychological heuristic. The argument I will present runs roughly as follows: (1) no uniform principle from conceivability to possibility survives the standard counterexamples; (2) we nevertheless rely successfully on conceivability-like reasoning in engineering, planning, and everyday modal judgment; (3) the good cases differ from the bad cases because the good cases involve rich, constrained scenarios whose failure modes we know how to check, while the bad cases involve sparse scenarios where our checking procedures gain no traction; therefore (4) the rational attitude is not all-or-nothing but graded confidence keyed to the structure of the scenario.

The audience is a philosophy of mind and language reading group with a particular composition. Two members are committed two-dimensionalists who hold that, once conceivability is idealized and the primary/secondary and positive/negative distinctions are drawn, conceivability entails possibility, and who take the zombie argument to be sound; they will read my graded-confidence view as a failure of nerve. Two others are modal skeptics of an empiricist bent, who think conceivability is mostly a vivid image with a feeling of possibility attached and who will find my claim that conceivability is evidence at all far too generous. The remaining members are agnostics with no settled position. I expect about twenty minutes of questions.

Please produce a Q&A prep sheet with the following sections, in this order:

- "The objections, in order" — the six or seven objections I am most likely to receive, ordered by how damaging they are rather than by how likely, each 40–80 words and phrased the way the relevant member of this audience would actually put it, with a one-sentence note on who asks it and why it hurts.
- "The question I am hoping nobody asks" — one paragraph of 80–120 words stating, in the audience's voice, the objection that targets the weakest joint in my argument.
- "Two-minute replies" — for each objection, a reply of 200–250 words that concedes what should be conceded, holds what must be held, and could be delivered in about two minutes.
- "Where each reply is soft" — for each reply, one or two sentences on the point at which it is least secure and what a well-prepared interlocutor would say next.
- "The line I should not cross" — one paragraph of 100–150 words identifying the concessions or reformulations that would collapse my position into one of the two views represented in the room.
