You are a philosopher specialising in the metaphysics of property and the ownership of ideas. Please think through the following question systematically, starting by laying out the possible positions one could take about what it means to own an idea before evaluating which of them the actual legal practice commits to, rules out, or is simply silent about.

I am a journalist who covers science, technology, and intellectual property. Over the years I have become puzzled by something that seems to me to be a deep inconsistency in how the law treats independent creation — the situation in which two people arrive at the same idea, invention, or expression without any contact with one another's work. I want to know what theory of idea ownership, if any, the overall practice presupposes, and whether that theory is coherent.

Here is the practice as I understand it, stripped to its essentials. Consider three people — Alice, Bob, and Carol — each working in isolation.

Alice obtains a patent on a novel kind of hinge. Bob, who has never heard of Alice or her hinge, independently devises the same hinge and begins selling it. Bob infringes Alice's patent. His ignorance and his independent effort are irrelevant. Patent law treats the idea as belonging to the first to file (or first to invent, depending on jurisdiction), and everyone else is excluded, however they came by it.

Now suppose Alice writes a short story and holds the copyright. Carol, who has never encountered Alice's story, independently writes a story that is, by wild coincidence, word-for-word identical. Carol has not infringed Alice's copyright. Independent creation is a complete defence. Copyright law treats the expression as belonging to its author only against those who actually copied it.

And then there is trade secret law. If Alice keeps her hinge design as a trade secret, and Bob independently devises it, Bob is free to use it and even to publish it. Only if Bob obtained the design through breach of confidence, theft, or espionage would he be liable. Independent discovery is not merely a defence; it is not even a wrong.

So we have three regimes within the same broad legal family, and they give three different answers to the question of whether independently arriving at something already owned by another is actionable. Patent law says yes, and the owner's right is a right against the world. Copyright law says no, and the owner's right is only a right against copiers. Trade secret law says no, and the owner's right is only a right against those who obtained the secret wrongfully.

My question is: what theory — or theories — of what it means to own an idea does this pattern of practice presuppose? Is there a single coherent theory that makes sense of all three regimes, or does the practice presuppose inconsistent commitments? And if we were to replace whatever theory is implicit here with a different one — say, a Lockean labour-mixing view, or a personality-based view of the sort associated with Hegel, or a straightforward instrumentalist view that intellectual property exists only to incentivise creation and disclosure — which of these three regimes would have to change, and how?

Please be precise about what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with, and flag any points where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several rival theories would license the same rule. I am less interested in the doctrinal details than in the conceptual architecture that makes the doctrines feel natural or unnatural.
