I am a retired research chemist, and I have only recently begun reading academic philosophy in earnest, so I ask your patience with any imprecision in my terms. I have been working through value theory, and the heterogeneity problem for hedonism has been troubling me. As I understand it, hedonism holds that pleasure is the only thing good in itself and pain the only thing bad in itself. But the states we call pleasures — the taste of a meal, relief from anxiety, the satisfaction of finishing a difficult proof, schadenfreude, sexual pleasure, absorption in music — appear to share no single felt character, no common object, and no common physical substrate. If "pleasure" is merely a label for a disunified heap, then hedonism seems to face a dilemma: either its central term is too indeterminate to evaluate, or it collapses into the empty claim that whatever is good is good.

Please rank the following five candidate responses from most to least successful as defenses of hedonism against this objection. I have tried to describe each as neutrally as I can. For each, give a short paragraph explaining why it lands where it does. After the ranking, give one overall score out of 10 for how well hedonism survives the heterogeneity problem once the best available account is in place, where 0 means the problem is fatal to hedonism and 10 means the problem dissolves completely.

(a) The distinctive-feeling account: all pleasures share a single introspectively detectable felt quality, or a small family of such qualities, and this is what the word "pleasure" picks out.
(b) The attitudinal account: to take pleasure in something is to have a pro-attitude toward it while experiencing it — to desire it, like it, or be attracted to it — and this attitude, rather than any common felt texture, is what unifies the class of pleasures.
(c) The functional account: pleasure is whatever state plays the pleasure-role in our psychology — roughly, a state that we are disposed to seek, sustain, and report as pleasant — regardless of how physically or phenomenally diverse the underlying states are.
(d) The disjunctive account: "pleasure" is a family-resemblance term for a list of heterogeneous goods, and hedonism should be restated as the thesis that all and only members of this list are good, without expecting any deeper unity.
(e) The revisionist account: the right response is to abandon "pleasure" as the currency of hedonism and substitute a more precise notion — for example, a specific kind of affect, or well-being itself — thereby conceding that hedonism as classically stated does not survive the objection.

Finally, please tell me what would most likely change your verdict: which argument or consideration could move your top-ranked account down or your bottom-ranked account up, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
