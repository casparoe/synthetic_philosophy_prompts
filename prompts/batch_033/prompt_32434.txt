So two friends of mine got into it at a bar last weekend about whether it's rational to get emotionally worked up over fiction. It started when someone mentioned they'd cried at a movie, and it spiraled from there.

(1) Marcus said it's fundamentally irrational. You know the events on screen didn't happen — the characters don't exist, nobody actually died — so being sad about it is like being sad about a dream after you've woken up and know it was a dream. The belief that grounds the emotion is false, so the emotion rests on a mistake.

(2) Lena pushed back: the emotion isn't resting on a false belief because she never believes the fiction is real, not even momentarilly. She knows it's Cate Blanchett on a set. The sadness is real sadness — her heart rate changes, she tears up — but it's sadness directed at the content of the fiction, not sadness that presupposes the events occurred. It's no more irrational than being amused by a joke, she said; you don't have to believe the setup actually happened to find it funny.

(3) Marcus said that's exactly the disanalogy. A joke works because you recognize an incongruity in the abstract; it doesn't ask you to feel *for* anyone. But crying at a character's death means you're responding as if someone has suffered a loss. The emotion's proper object is a state of affairs you know doesn't obtain. That's not like finding a pun clever — it's a misfire of the empathy system.

(4) Lena said he's smuggling in a narrow theory of what emotions are "for." We feel grief at funerals, sure, but we also feel grief imagining future losses that haven't happened, or remembering ones long past. The emotion system clearly handles "offline" simulations — that's part of its function, not a bug. Fiction plugs into that same capacity. If it's rational to feel anticipatory dread about a presentation you might bomb next week (something that also hasn't happened), why isn't it rational to feel dread during a horror movie?

(5) Marcus said anticipatory dread is about something that could actually happen to you, so the belief component is "this is a real possibility." Fiction doesn't even clear that bar. Lena said plenty of fiction depicts things that *could* happen — that's most realist drama — so his distinction collapses. Marcus said even then you don't believe it's happening to anyone you care about, because there is no such person. Lena said you care about the character as a character — the same way you can care about a mathematical proof being elegant without believing numbers have feelings.

At that point they'd been going for twenty minutes and someone changed the subject. I've been turning it over since. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I'm especially curious whether Marcus's "proper object" point in (3) actually lands or whether Lena's simulation reply in (4) defuses it.
