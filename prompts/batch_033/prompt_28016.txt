I'm a high school student in an advanced philosophy seminar, and we've spent the last few weeks on split-brain cases and what they imply about the unity of consciousness. I've constructed an argument that seems to me to prove too much, and I can't figure out where it goes wrong — or whether it goes wrong at all. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of mind and help me diagnose the problem. Please take your time and think through the options carefully before settling on a diagnosis.

Here is the argument:

(1) In split-brain patients whose corpus callosum has been surgically severed, we observe behaviors that strongly suggest two independent streams of consciousness operating in the same skull: the left hand may unbutton a shirt the right hand is buttoning, a stimulus presented only to the right hemisphere can guide the left hand to select an object the patient verbally denies seeing, and so on.

(2) If we accept that these patients house two distinct conscious subjects (or two separate streams of phenomenal experience), then we need an account of what feature of neural organization normally secures the unity of consciousness — what is it about the intact brain that makes one subject rather than two?

(3) The natural answer is informational integration: in a normal brain, the hemispheres continuously share information through the corpus callosum, while in the split-brain patient this pathway is absent, so information is segregated into two relatively integrated but mutually isolated pools.

(4) But informational integration is a matter of degree. Many neural subsystems in a normal brain are only partially integrated with one another. The dorsal and ventral visual streams process information with considerable independence for extended stretches; subcortical structures operate with significant autonomy; even within a single hemisphere, specialized modules handle distinct tasks with limited intercommunication. If the split-brain case shows that reduced integration yields distinct subjects, then by parity of reasoning, shouldn't we find a proliferation of subjects — perhaps dozens or hundreds — within every normal human brain, wherever integration falls below some threshold?

(5) This yields a sorites problem: there seems to be no non-arbitrary degree of integration at which one subject becomes two. If we try to avoid the multiplication of subjects by setting the threshold very low (say, only complete disconnection counts), we seem to be making an ad hoc exception for the split-brain case. If we set it higher, we get an explosion of subjects.

(6) Alternatively, suppose we resist the initial move and insist that split-brain patients remain a single conscious subject, with the conflict behaviors explained away — perhaps as unconscious automatisms, or as the output of modules that aren't themselves conscious. But then we face a different problem: the behaviors we are dismissing in the split-brain case (purposeful object selection by the non-speaking hemisphere, conflicting manual actions that appear goal-directed) are considerably more sophisticated than many behaviors we routinely take as evidence of consciousness in other contexts — in infants, in non-human animals, or in patients with locked-in syndrome, where we infer consciousness from far less. If we dismiss these split-brain behaviors, what principled reason do we have not to dismiss those other behaviors too?

(7) So we face a dilemma: either we multiply conscious subjects beyond plausibility, or we adopt a standard for attributing consciousness so stringent that it threatens to rule out subjects we are independently committed to recognizing. Either way, something has gone wrong.

I find each step individually plausible, but the conclusion that our ordinary concept of a unified conscious subject is either incoherent or radically revisionary strikes me as too much to accept. I'd like you to identify which premise or step in this argument you think should be rejected, explain why it initially seemed plausible, and make clear what theoretical costs come with rejecting it — that is, what else you have to give up or revise if you take that route. Please also indicate whether you think the argument contains a subtle logical mistake (an equivocation, a scope shift, a modal slide) or whether it is valid and the real work is in choosing which premise to abandon. If there are standard positions in the literature that map onto different choices here, I'd appreciate knowing which positions reject which premises and what they pay for it.
