so i've been going back and forth on the paradox of deontology for a few weeks now — it came up in a conversation with a friend who's a hardcore consequentialist and i couldn't stop thinking about it. you know the basic setup: if you think there's a constraint against killing, presumably because killing is really bad, then why wouldn't you kill one person to prevent five murders? if it's the badness of killing that grounds the constraint, then more killings should be worse than fewer, and the constraint should point toward minimizing violations. but it doesn't. that's the paradox.

the view i've landed on is that this isn't actually a paradox once you go agent-relative. the constraint isn't about minimizing the total number of killings in the world; it's about *my* relationship to *my* actions. i have a reason not to kill, and your reasons not to kill are your business. the fact that five people will die if i do nothing is terrible, but it's not *my* killing that causes it. so there's no contradiction — the constraint never claimed to be about aggregate outcomes.

but i'm aware this could be a cope. what would actually count against this view? i want the real defeaters, not just the obvious stuff. what's the strongest case that the agent-relative move doesn't dissolve the paradox so much as rename it? are there edge cases or weird varaints of the scenario where the view starts to look genuinely inconsistent? and what would it mean for this to be wrong in a way that matters — not just a technical failure but something that should make me change how i think about moral reasons?
