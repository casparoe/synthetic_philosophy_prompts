I'm a third-year PhD student trying to pick a dissertation topic in early modern ethics, and I keep circling back to Leibniz. His moral philosophy feels oddly underworked relative to his metaphysics and logic — the theodicy has been done to death, sure, but the underlying moral theory seems full of live questions that people haven't really pushed on.

Here's the thing. I have about three years and my background is straight philosophy — I read the primary texts (in translation, my Latin is shaky) and the secondary literature, but I'm not an intellectual historian and I'm not doing archival work. So I need questions that are tractable with the texts we already have.

What I want from you: rank the open questions in Leibniz's moral philosophy by how worth pursuing they are for someone in my position, and defend the ordering.

To give you a sense of the terrain, here are some of the questions I've been turning over — but don't feel limited to these:

- The modal status of moral truths. Leibniz says moral truths are necessary and grounded in God's intellect, not God's will. But he also says God chooses among possible worlds, and different worlds have different moral properties. So in what sense are moral truths necessary? Is there a real tension here or is it just terminological?

- Moral necessity vs. metaphysical necessity. Leibniz carves out a special kind of modality — moral necessity — to explain why God must choose the best without being metaphysically determined. What kind of modality is this, actually? Does it do the work Leibniz needs it to do, or does it collapse under scrutiny?

- The tension between moral rationalism and psychological hedonism. Leibniz is a rationalist about moral knowledge — we discover moral truths through reason. But his moral psychology seems to say the will is always moved by the perception of pleasure or the increase of perfection. Can these two commitments be made consistent, or is there a genuine conflict?

- Supererogation. If the principle of sufficient reason plus the demand to maximize perfection governs all action, is there any room for going beyond what's required? Does Leibniz's framework collapse the distinction between obligation and supererogation?

- Moral considerability of non-human monads. If every monad has perception and appetition, does Leibniz's system generate moral obligations toward animals, or even toward plants and apparently inanimate things? And if so, what do those obligations look like?

For each question you rank, tell me: (1) why it's important — what else in Leibniz interpretation or in ethics more broadly turns on the answer; (2) how tractable it is given the available texts and the existing secondary literature; (3) what would change your ranking — what kind of new argument or evidence would bump it up or down.

If you think I'm missing a question that matters more than any of these, put it at the top and explain why.
