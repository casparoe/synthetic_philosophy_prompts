A friend of mine is really into Hofstadter and keeps saying consciousness is "just like Escher's Drawing Hands." His argument goes: the brain produces the self, but the self (as a pattern) can loop back and influence the brain — that's a tangled hierarchy, just like the hands each drawing the other. And the fact that we experience the self as real and causally effective is the "inviolate level" — the level where the strange loop is perceived as a stable thing, not a paradox. He uses this to push back against eliminativists who say the self is an illusion and there's nothing its like to be you.

I want to know how much work the analogy is actually doing. Give me the correspondence table: what in the brain is the left hand, what's the right hand, what's the paper, what's the act of drawing, and what corresponds to the fact that the picture is already fully drawn when you look at it — it's synchronic, not diachronic, and Escher himself drew both hands. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I especially want to know (a) whether the "inviolate level" in the consciousness case is doing the same work as in the Escher case, or whether the analogy is smuggling in a viewer that the consciousness system doesn't have, and (b) whether the fact that the hands are made of the same stuff (ink) while neurons and experiences aren't obviously the same stuff matters for the argument. Once you've laid out the disanalogies, tell me whether the conclusion — that the self is real, not an illusion, in the same kind of way the hands are real — survives, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole thing.
