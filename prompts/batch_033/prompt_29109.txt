I spent my career in molecular biology, and since retiring I've been working through the philosophical literature on personal identity — especially the mind-uploading and gradual-replacement debates. I've now read enough to notice a pattern that, as an experimentalist, I find unsettling.

The debate about whether you would survive a gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement with functionally equivalent artificial components has been running for decades with remarkably little movement. On one side, psychological-continuity theorists argue that you survive because the replacement preserves your memories, personality, dispositions, and all the psychological patterns that make you you — and since you are essentially a psychological entity, that is what survival consists in. On the other side, biological-continuity theorists (including animalists) argue that you do not survive — you are a biological organism, and replacing your brain piece by piece with silicon, no matter how functionally seamless, is a way of destroying that organism and creating a psychological duplicate. Both camps have heard each other's arguments. Both have access to the same thought experiments — Ship of Theseus variants, fission and fusion cases, spectrum arguments. And yet neither side moves.

What puzzles me is this: in science, when two theories agree on all the observable facts, we usually conclude that the disagreement is either verbal or that the question is underdetermined by the evidence. Here, both sides seem to agree on what the physical facts would be in a gradual-replacement scenario — which neurons get replaced when, what the system does at each step, what the person reports. The disagreement persists despite agreement on the physical facts. So what kind of disagreement is it?

My working hypothesis — and I offer it tentatively, since I am new to this — is that the two sides are simply using different criteria for personal identity, and that neither side's criterion can be justified without presupposing what is in dispute. If that is right, the impasse is not a failure of cleverness but a sign that the question, as ordinarily posed, does not have a determinate answer. But I worry I am missing something. Perhaps there is a hidden factual question I have not noticed. Perhaps there is a conceptual distinction that could break the deadlock. Perhaps a formal result shows that one side's position is incoherent in a way its proponents have not appreciated.

I would like you to approach this as a professional philosopher working on personal identity and the metaphysics of persons. Please take your time and think through the issue carefully. I suggest you begin by brainstorming the different ways one might classify philosophical disagreements — verbal vs. substantive, factual vs. conceptual, well-posed vs. ill-posed — and then apply that framework to this specific debate. Lay out what each side is actually committed to, charitably and in its own terms, not as caricatured by opponents. Then diagnose the structure of the disagreement. Is it verbal? Is it a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that could settle it? Are the two sides answering different questions under the same heading? And given your diagnosis: what, if anything, would count as progress? If the question is ill-posed, say so and explain why the illusion that it is well-posed has been so persistent. If progress is possible, tell me what kind of work — conceptual, empirical, formal — would actually move things forward, and what a resolution would look like.
