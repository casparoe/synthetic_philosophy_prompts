I've been going down a rabbit hole on money pumps and diachronic rationality, and there's a distinction I keep tripping over that I'd love to get sorted out. So the basic setup: you've got an agent whose preferences aren't quite standard — maybe they're intransitive, or they've got hyperbolic discounting, or they reverse preferences in some predictable way over time. A money pump is the argument that such an agent can be exploited: you offer them a series of trades, each of which looks good to them at the moment they make it, and they end up back where they started but with less money. The threat is supposed to show that the underlying preference structure is irrational.

Now, two classic responses to the threat keep coming up, and they seem related in a way I can't quite articulate. The first is the *sophisticated chooser*: this is someone who knows their future self is going to make dumb trades, so they pick a strategy now that ties that future self's hands — they route around their own predicted preference shifts like an obstacle course. The second is the *resolute chooser*: this person makes a plan and then just... sticks to it, even when the moment comes and they really *want* to deviate. They treat their earlier decision as binding in a way that overrides their current preferences.

Here's what's bugging me. On the surface they sound different — one is strategic and almost adversarial toward your future self, the other is about commitment and integrity. But when you actually try to model them, don't both require the same underlying capacity to represent your own future preference shifts and act against your current inclinations? Is the resolute chooser just a sophisticated chooser who's internalized the commitment device, or is there a deeper structural difference? And which one actually gives you a philosophically satisfying answer to the money pump — i.e., does either one rescue the rationality of the original preferences, or do they both just concede that your preferences are broken and you need a workaround?
