I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of deception, and I am preparing an upper-level philosophy-and-literature unit on Shakespeare's Othello. I want to use the play as a test case for the familiar claim that lying is morally worse than merely misleading. I would like a reading that takes both the play and the philosophical debate seriously, and that is willing to say where the play resists the distinction rather than serving as a mere illustration of it.

As background: on the standard view, a lie is an assertion the speaker believes to be false, made with the aim of getting someone to believe it, whereas misleading involves getting someone to acquire a false belief without asserting it — through implicature, presupposition, evasive or noncommittal speech, or the arranging of evidence. Many philosophers hold that lying is the greater wrong because the liar vouches for the false content in his own voice, while the misleader merely invites the hearer to form the false belief and so leaves the hearer some share of responsibility. A significant minority denies that the distinction carries moral weight, and some argue that misleading can be worse precisely because it hides behind deniability and makes the victim complicit in the deception.

Iago seems a natural test case. Much of his manipulation of Othello is misleading rather than lying: he lets Othello observe Cassio's unease and mutters enough to plant a suspicion; he arranges for the handkerchief to appear in Cassio's possession; he stages the conversation with Cassio about Bianca so that Othello, overhearing, takes it to be about Desdemona. But Iago also asserts things that are straightforwardly false, such as his invented account of Cassio talking in his sleep. I would like the reading to do the following.

First, go through Iago's central moves in the temptation of Othello and sort them into lies, misleadings, and cases that resist a clean classification; say what a theory of what is said or asserted would have to look like for those classifications to come out right, and flag the cases where the distinction starts to blur.

Second, assess the moral question the play seems to press. Does Othello treat misleading as the lesser wrong, or does it suggest that Iago's insinuation is worse than a direct false accusation would have been, because it makes Othello the instrument and co-author of his own error while leaving Iago deniable? Which scenes would you point to, and what does the distribution of responsibility between Iago and Othello imply for the philosophical debate?

Third, say where the philosophical lens genuinely illuminates the play and where an honest critic should concede that the play is not tracking the distinction — for instance, where Shakespeare's dramatic needs pull against the philosophical categories. I would also like a closing assessment of what the play adds to the debate that an exchange of examples does not.

Since I will check the text myself, please paraphrase scenes and speeches rather than quoting from memory, and do not worry about bibliography.
