I've been thinking about animal consciousness on my own, outside any academic setting. I've read a fair amount — people arguing about whether fish feel pain, whether octopuses have a sense of self, whether bees are just tiny robots or whether there's something it's like to be a bee. One thing I keep noticing is that the arguments often turn on unstated criteria: someone assumes that if you have a certain neurotransmitter, you're conscious; someone else assumes that consciousness requires a cortex, full stop; a third person says the only safe bet is to look at behavior. Nobody seems to agree on what the standards are.

So I want to step back and ask: what would a theory of the distribution of consciousness across animals actually have to get right? What are the desiderata — the requirements any adequate account must meet, the cases it can't get wrong, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts about evolution and neurobiology it can't contradict? With the reasoning behind each one.

I also want to know which of these requirements are genuine constraints and which are just the preferences of one camp — things like "consciousness requires language" or "only mammals have the right brain structures." And I want to know whether any of the requirements are in tension with each other — are there pairs that probably can't be jointly satisfied, given what we know? If the list itself rules out a whole class of theories, that's worth knowing.

I'm not asking you to score the existing theories against the list — I can do that myself once I have the criteria. But if there's a particular requirement that does most of the work of narrowing the field, I'd want to know which one.
