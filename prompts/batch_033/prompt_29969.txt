I'm taking a course on formal theories of democracy and I keep hitting the same wall: one person defends instant-runoff voting, another says it's garbage because it fails monotonicity, a third says none of that matters because the real issue is the spoiler effect, and everyone is just pointing at the one feature they happened to have a strong feeling about. Before I read another voting-system proposal I want the full spec on the table. What should a rule that takes individual ordinal rankings and returns a social ranking (or at least a winner) actually have to deliver? Give me the desiderata: the cases it has to get right (Condorcet cycles, the no-spoilers intuition, maybe preference intensity if you think it belongs), the distinctions it can't collapse (what a group would choose versus what's fair to its members, an input profile versus a rule, a tie versus a real indifference), and the formal results it cant contradict (Arrow's theorem, and Gibbard-Satterthwaite if you count manipulability as a constraint). For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and flag which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of a particular camp — independence of irrelevant alternatives, unrestricted domain, transitivity versus acyclicity, non-dictatorship, and so on.

The part I most want to see is the interplay: which subsets of this list are jointly unsatisfiable given Arrow's theorem, and which single requirement is doing the most work to knock out otherwise attractive rules. If the right lesson is that one of the "requirements" was never really a requirement — that IIA, say, was a technical convenience people mistook for a fairness condition — then say so and defend it. A table is fine.
