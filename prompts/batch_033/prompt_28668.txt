im a TA for an intro decision theory course and i have to grade this midterm answer. the question was: "State the St. Petersburg paradox and explain whether Bernoulli's expected-utility solution fully resolves it, or whether a residual problem remains for expected-utility maximizers." heres what the student wrote:

The St. Petersburg game is a fair coin tossed until heads comes up; if the first heads is on toss n, you get $2^n. Its expected value is (1/2)(2) + (1/4)(4) + (1/8)(8) + ... = 1 + 1 + 1 + ..., which is infinite, so expected-value maximization says you should pay any finite amount to play. But real people won't pay more than a few dollars, and that's the paradox. Daniel Bernoulli, who first posed the paradox, solved it by saying that people maximize expected utility rather than expected money, and that money has diminishing marginal utility. He proposed u(x) = log x. The expected utility is then Σ (1/2^n) log(2^n) = log 2 · Σ n/2^n = 2 log 2, which is finite, giving a certainty equivalent of $4. So the paradox is fully dissolved. The general lesson is that as long as a utility function assigns a finite value to every possible payoff, expected utility will be finite, so expected-utility theory has no residual problem here. The only thing left is the empirical question of what people's actual utility functions are.
