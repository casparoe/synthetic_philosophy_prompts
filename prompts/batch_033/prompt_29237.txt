I'm a PhD student in bioethics, and I've been working through the literature on the therapy/enhancement distinction. I find myself increasingly puzzled by why this distinction is treated as so philosophically intractable. If we adopt something like a biostatistical account of disease — where disease states are deviations from statistically typical species functioning relative to an appropriate reference class — then the distinction seems to fall out naturally: therapy aims to restore functioning to within the normal range, and enhancement pushes functioning beyond it. On this picture, the hard cases that dominate the literature — growth hormone for children who are short but not growth-hormone-deficient, modafinil for healthy students, SSRIs for the mildly dysthymic — look like they just need better empirical specification of the relevant reference classes and statistical cutoffs, not a fundamental rethinking of the concepts. Why, then, do so many philosophers regard the therapy/enhancement distinction as conceptually unstable or normatively unhelpful, rather than treating the boundary disputes as downstream of empirical questions about what counts as normal functioning for a given population? What exactly is the argument that the biostatistical approach fails to do the work I'm describing — and is the real worry that the distinction can't be drawn cleanly, or that even a cleanly drawn distinction wouldn't carry the moral weight people want it to?
