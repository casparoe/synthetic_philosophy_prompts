I've been reading about the symbol grounding problem in my spare time — the idea, as I understand it, is that a purely symbolic system just manipulates tokens according to rules, and none of those tokens are actually connected to anything in the world, so the system can't be said to really "mean" anything by them. It's syntax without semantics. The classic illustration is something like: you could build a system that manipulates Chinese characters perfectly according to formal rules, but if none of those characters are hooked up to the things they're supposed to be about, the system doesn't understand Chinese.

What I find puzzling is why this is still treated as an open problem. Modern machine learning systems — especially multimodal ones — are trained on enormous corpora of images paired with text, audio paired with transcriptions, even video with narration. A vision-language model doesn't just push around word tokens; it learns representations that are constrained by actual photographs of dogs, recordings of speech, real sensor data from robots interacting with physical objects. The symbol "dog" in such a system isn't defined solely by its relations to other symbols — it's causally coupled, through the training process, to patterns of pixels that were produced by actual dogs in the world. If the worry was that symbols were floating free of the world, haven't these systems closed the loop? What exactly is left of the symbol grounding problem once you've got a model whose internal states are shaped by terabytes of grounded perceptual data?
