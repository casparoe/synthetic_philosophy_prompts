I'm a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience, and I've been worrying about a methodological assumption that runs through both my field and clinical practice. In consciousness research, a subject's verbal report—"I saw the stimulus," "the image didn't reach awareness"—is treated as the primary datum for establishing when and what they consciously experienced. In the clinic, a patient's report of pain or of awareness during anesthesia is treated as authoritative in a way that no behavioral test or neural measure can override. But this practice seems to take for granted that the conscious experience itself plays a causal role in producing the report. If epiphenomenalism about consciousness were true—if phenomenal states have no causal powers whatsoever—then the report would be generated entirely by non-conscious neural processes, and the systematic match between report content and phenomenal character would be a correlation in need of explanation rather than something we can simply rely on as data. I want to get clear on what, exactly, the practice presupposes. Does it strictly require that consciousness causes reports, or could it be justified under epiphenomenalism by some other structure—say, a common cause in which the same neural processes produce both the experience and the report? And if epiphenomenalism turned out to be correct, what would have to change about how we collect and interpret first-person data?
