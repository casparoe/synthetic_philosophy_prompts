I'm in an epistemology seminar and the regress problem keeps coming up in a way that's driving me a bit crazy. The standard setup: if a belief is justified only when it's supported by another justified belief, then justification either stops somewhere, loops back on itself, or runs on forever. Foundationalists take the first option and say there are basic beliefs — the stock examples are beliefs about how things seem to you right now, like seeming to see a red patch, or simple logical truths — that are justified without being inferred from anything else. Coherentists say this just refuses the question and that justification is really a matter of mutual support among beliefs in a system, so the 'regress' is an artifact of picturing justification as a one-way chain instead of a web. Infinitists, on the other hand, accept the chain picture but say there's no need for it to stop: there's always a further reason available, and they answer the obvious "but you're a finite creature, you can't actualy have infinitely many reasons" objection by distinguishing between reasons you're currently aware of and reasons that are merely available or could be produced on demand. Every side has stock replies to the others' objections and, as far as I can tell, nobody has moved in decades. Wich makes me suspect the impasse isn't just that the problem is hard.

I don't want a survey of the three views. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a substantive dispute about the actual structure of justification, a verbal dispute about the word 'justified', or are the three camps answering different questions — one about what makes a belief justified, one about how we actually revise beliefs in practice, one about what the structure of reasons would have to be if someone kept demanding them? Do they differ about which cases count as data (ordinary inferential practice? skeptical scenarios? what we'd say if pressed forever?)? Is there a hidden empirical question about human cognitive capacities doing the real work in the finite-mind objection? And is the whole thing maybe ill-posed because 'justification' is being asked to do too many jobs at once? My working theory is that the three positions are each right about a different job the concept is supposed to do, and the debate stalls because nobody agrees on which job has priority. But I'm not at all sure — maybe it's a genuine structural disagreement after all.
