You are a professor of the philosophy of language who works on theories of interpretation, and you are designing a problem-set exercise for graduate students. Think through this carefully; begin by brainstorming two or three candidate mini-cases before settling on the one you present, and make sure the case earns its verdicts from its details rather than stipulating them.

Construct a thought experiment about the meaning of a literary text in which three standard views of interpretation come apart in an unusual pattern: actual intentionalism and anti-intentionalism assign the same meaning to the text, while hypothetical intentionalism assigns a different meaning. Work with these definitions. Actual intentionalism: the meaning of a text is what the author actually intended by it. Anti-intentionalism: the meaning of a text is fixed by the text's language together with the public conventions governing its use, with no appeal to the author's private mental states or biography. Hypothetical intentionalism: the meaning of a text is what a suitably informed reader, drawing on all publicly available evidence about the text, its context, and the author, would conclude the author most likely intended.

The case must be an ordinary literary scenario: an invented short passage of prose or verse, a minimal authorial situation, and whatever public evidence is needed. No real texts, no science fiction, no perfect interpreters. The scenario itself should be one paragraph. The engine of the case should be a gap between the author's actual intention and the best publicly warranted hypothesis about that intention: the author actually means what the words conventionally mean, but evidence external to the text's language — the author's known biography, earlier works, or public statements — would lead the suitably informed reader to infer something else.

After the scenario, do the following. State the passage, the author's actual intention, the conventional meaning of the passage, and the public evidence the hypothetical intentionalist's reader relies on. Then show for each of the three theories which meaning it assigns and why, making the reasoning explicit. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work — the feature you would have to remove to make the three views collapse back into agreement — and say how the case changes when it is removed. Finally, give the strongest objection a hypothetical intentionalist would press against the case: would they deny that a private, unrecoverable intention should determine meaning, dispute what the suitably informed reader would infer, or reject your characterization of the view?
