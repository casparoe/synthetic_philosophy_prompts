At a dinner party last week two friends of mine got into it about dirty hands in politics. The specific case that set it off: a governor authorized an undercover operation where an agent spent six months forming a genuine friendship with a suspected mass shooter. The op succeeded — it prevented an attack that would have killed dozens. But the agent later went public saying the friendship felt real and he's haunted by it.

Mark said the governor made the right call. You can't lead without getting your hands dirty sometimes. If the governor had balked and the attack happened, we'd say he failed his basic duty. The agent's torment is the price of preventing atrocity with ugly means.

Elena pushed back: So you're saying the governor did something both right and wrong at the same time? I've never understood that move. If authorizing the sting was the right decision all things considered, then it wasn't wrong. Calling it "dirty" is just a way of saying it felt bad but was correct. There's no genuine moral remainder — just emotional residue.

Mark: No, the governor genuinely wronged the agent — used him as a tool, damaged his psyche — and wronged the suspect by weaponizing a real human bond. Those are moral injuries, not just feelings. The governor should carry guilt about them; if he didn't, that itself would be a moral failure. But he still should have done it. That's the paradox: some acts are morally required and morally wrong at the same time.

Elena: That's not a paradox, it's a contradiction. Either the reasons in favor outweighed the reasons against, so the act was justified and not wrong — or the reasons against were decisive, so the act was wrong and he shouldn't have done it. You can't have both. If you're saying the governor ought to feel guilty for doing what he ought to have done, you're recommending irrational guilt.

Mark: So by your lights, a surgeon who amputates a leg to save a life has done nothing regrettable? The lost leg isn't a wrong, just a cost? That seems like failing to recognize that some necessary evils are still evils.

Elena: The amputation isn't wrong — it's justified. The loss is bad, but badness isn't wrongness. You're sliding between those two things.

Can you adjudicate this step by step? Who has the better of each exchange, and is there a way through the impasse they ended up at — or is one of them just confused?
