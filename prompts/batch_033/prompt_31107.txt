I teach a seminar called "Language and Perspective" in an MFA program — I'm a novelist by trade, not a philosopher, but the course draws on philosophy of language to explore how point of view operates below the sentence level. Last week we read Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language," and I gave my students a take-home question designed to test whether they understood the satirical mechanism rather than just the essay's surface. The question was:

"In 'A Person Paper on Purity in Language,' Hofstadter adopts a satirical strategy rather than arguing directly. Explain how the satire works — what is the mechanism of the analogy, and what philosophical point does it make about language and default assumptions that a straightforward essay might not capture as effectively? Be specific about the structure of the substitution and what it reveals."

One of my stronger students submitted the answer below. I have my own assessment, but I'd like a second opinion before I return it — I'm too close to the material and I want to make sure I'm not penalizing subtle misreadings that are actually defensible, or letting slide mistakes that are more serious than they look. Please grade this answer: give it a letter grade and provide point-by-point comments justifying the grade. Identify what the student gets right and where they go wrong. Here is the student's answer:

"Hofstadter's 'Person Paper' uses a satirical substitution: he writes a fictional letter complaining about 'race-neutral language' — the replacement of 'white' (the default term for people) with 'person.' The mechanism is an analogy: just as gender-neutral language reform replaces 'man' with 'person' in words like 'chairman,' the fictional reformers replace 'white' with 'person' in words like 'chairwhite.' The satire works by making the reader feel how absurd and heavy-handed race-neutral language seems, which is meant to reveal that our resistance to gender-neutral language stems from the same unexamined assumption — that the dominant group is the default human being. Hofstadter's philosophical point is that language encodes default assumptions so deeply that we don't notice them until someone challenges them; the satire makes the invisible visible. By framing the complaint from within the dominant group's perspective, he also shows that what feels like 'neutral' language to the dominant group is actually language that takes that group as the standard. The essay ultimately suggests that we should be more skeptical of language-reform projects generally, since they tend to replace one set of defaults with another rather than solving the underlying problem of markedness."
