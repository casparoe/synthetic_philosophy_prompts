I'm teaching an upper-level metaethics seminar and want to make a handout that helps students figure out where they actually stand on the Frege-Geach problem. The idea is a decision tree or flowchart where each branch node is a question about what it takes to solve the problem, and each leaf is a named metaethical position — so they get sorted by what they believe, not by what label they happen to like.

The positions I want to see as leaves include at least: traditional non-cognitivism (the kind Ayer and Stevenson held, which the problem was originally aimed at), Blackburn-style quasi-realism, Gibbard-style norm-expressivism, hybrid expressivism (like Ridge or Copp), realist cognitivism (where the problem is treated as a reason to just reject expressivism outright), and error theory (where the problem is a reason to reject expressivism but realism is also false). If there's a coherent "the problem is a pseudoproblem" or "the problem dissolves on closer inspection" leaf, include that too.

Here's what I want the tree to capture: the Frege-Geach problem says that if moral sentences express attitudes rather than beliefs, then we need to explain how they embed in complex sentences — conditionals, negations, modus ponens arguments — and how moral reasoning can be logically valid. The tree should separate positions by what they think is required to *solve* this, not by what label they use. So the branching questions should be about things like: whether a solution requires giving truth conditions for moral sentences, whether it requires showing that moral attitudes have a logic of their own, whether the whole framing of the problem secretly begs the question against expressivism, etc.

Phrase each node as a question a student could answer without already knowing the jargon. Build the tree from the logical space of possible answers first — map out the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — and then locate the actual, defended positions in that space. Flag any logical combinations that are empty, and say whether they're empty because the view is incoherent, just wildly unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Also flag any pairs of positions the tree can't cleanly seperate with the questions you've chosen — if two positions always give the same answers at every node, say so rather than inventing a fake distinction.

Keep it to something like 8–12 leaves. The audience is advanced undergrads who've read a couple chapters of a metaethics textbook and have seen the basic expressivist move but haven't read Blackburn or Gibbard directly yet.

Oh and please lead with the tree itself (as a text outline or flowchart description), no preamble or commentary, and then give the reasoning about why you built it that way after.
