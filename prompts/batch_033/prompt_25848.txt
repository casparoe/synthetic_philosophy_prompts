I've been reading a popular book on art forgery — one of those narrative nonfiction page-turners that moves through the big scandals (Van Meegeren, Elmyr de Hory, the Knoedler fakes) and then tries to draw some philosophical morals. In the chapter that steps back to ask what's really wrong with a forgery, the author offers what he presents as the settled philosophical view. He writes something to the effect of:

"The philosophical consensus on forgery is that our preference for originals over perfect forgeries is irrational. If two paintings are visually identical, then any pleasure or aesthetic experience you get from one you could get from the other, so paying millions more for the original is just snobbery dressed up as connoisseurship. Philosophers like Arthur Danto and Nelson Goodman have shown that the artworld's obsession with provenance is a kind of collective make-believe: we treat the original as special because we've been told it's a Vermeer, not because there's any real difference in the object itself. The only thing genuinely wrong with forgery is the deception — if a forgery were honestly labeled as such, there would be nothing left to object to."

Something about this feels too tidy to me. I know there's a substantial philosophical literature on forgery and the value of originals, and I suspect the author is collapsing distinctions that matter — maybe between aesthetic value and artistic value, or between different kinds of forgery, or between what Danto and Goodman actually argued and what the author says they showed. But I don't know the literature well enough to pinpoint where the summary goes wrong. Could you go through it and tell me what a philosopher working on this topic would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the conclusion — that our preference for originals is irrational — follows from anything accurate in the summary? And please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic; I don't want to come away thinking there's a consensus where there isn't one.
