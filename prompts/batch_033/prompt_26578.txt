I want to approach the problem of induction — and Goodman's "grue" challenge in particular — by first getting clear on what any adequate solution would have to deliver. Please write the desiderata for a theory of inductive inference: the full list of requirements it must satisfy to count as successful, with the case or problem that motivates each.

The list should include at least the following, but feel free to add others you think are genuine requirements: (1) It must explain why "all emeralds are green" is a legitimate inductive projection from observed cases while "all emeralds are grue" is not, without simply stipulating which predicates are projectible. (2) It must not rule out the kinds of inductions that actual scientific practice relies on — including inductions involving novel predicates and theoretical terms that lack a long history of past projections. (3) It must address the base Humean problem: any finite set of observations is logically consistent with infinitely many generalizations, so what licenses moving from observed to unobserved cases at all? (4) It must respect the distinction between lawlike regularities that support counterfactuals and accidental regularities that do not, or explain that distinction away convincingly rather than ignoring it.

For each requirement, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or reflects the commitments of a particular camp. Then identify any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — where meeting one forces you to fail another. That tension is what I most want to see mapped out. A brief scorecard of how the main families of response (Humean skepticism, reliabilism, Goodman's own entrenchment approach, and Bayesian approaches) fare against the list would be helpful but is secondary to the desiderata themselves.
