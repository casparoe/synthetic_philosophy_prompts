I'm in an upper-division moral philosophy course, and we recently spent a week on the paradox of deontology — the puzzle that a deontologist who refuses to violate a constraint (say, against killing) even to prevent several further violations of that same constraint seems committed to preferring more violations to fewer. In discussion, a classmate flatly dismissed the puzzle by invoking the maxim "Let justice be done though the heavens fall." The idea was that a deontologist is simply committed to doing the right thing regardless of the consequences, and that this commitment is not embarrassed by the paradox; it's just what the view amounts to.

I'm not convinced the maxim settles anything, because it can be read in several importantly different ways, some of which don't actually support a deontological response to the paradox, and some of which are themselves highly questionable. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this maxim could be expressing — from the trivial and uncontroversial to the radical and indefensible — and say, for each, whether it is plausible, whether it genuinely resolves the paradox of deontology, and whether it captures what someone who repeats the maxim probably intends? In particular, I want to know which readings my classmate would need to be relying on for the maxim to do the work he was using it to do, and which readings would leave the paradox entirely untouched or even support the opposite conclusion.

Present the readings as a numbered menu with a short verdict for each. Keep the answer to no more than 800 words, and write in American English.
