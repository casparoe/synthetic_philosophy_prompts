I am a philosophy professor, and I find myself in a personal situation that has exposed the limits of the way I ordinarily think about decisions. I have been offered a two-year fellowship at an institute that would put me in daily contact with the scholars whose work is closest to my own, in a country far from my family. In the same month, I learned that my mother's health is declining in a way that is unlikely to reverse, and that if I take the fellowship I will see her only briefly once or twice a year. Staying would mean a reduced teaching load in my home city and the chance to be present for her in a way that feels to me like an obligation of love rather than a mere preference.

The difficulty is not that I lack information or that the consequences are uncertain. It is that I cannot place the two possibilities on a common scale. The fellowship is not simply a career advantage I could offset with something else; it is the opportunity to do work I may not otherwise be able to do at all. The time with my mother is not simply a cost I could compensate by visiting more often; it is something I would not trade for any professional benefit, and yet I also find I would not simply give up the fellowship for it. When I try to weigh the options, neither strikes me as better, but they do not strike me as exactly equal either. Adding more to one side does not seem to close the gap, and adding more to the other side does not either.

I want to know whether there is a defensible way of understanding this kind of choice: one in which no option is better, and yet the choice is not arbitrary, so that I can choose well without there being a best option. Or should I conclude that I am avoiding a difficult trade-off, and that one of these options is in fact the better one whether or not I can see it clearly?
