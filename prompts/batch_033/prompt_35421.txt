I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, and I keep bumping into a pattern across different bits of metaphysics and philosophical logic that I don't have a proper name for. Here's the basic shape. You've got two things that are supposed to be connected in some way — say, an object and a property, or a subject term and a predicate in a sentence, or even just a pair of items standing in a relation. You want to explain what makes them actually hang together as a unified whole, rather than just sitting there as a bare list. So you introduce a third thing: a connector, a link, a bit of "glue." But now you've got three things: the original two, plus the connector. And you need to explain how the connector connects to each of the original two. So you introduce more connectors. But then those need their own connectors, and so on. It seems like any attempt to explain unity or combination by adding more elements just pushes the problem back one step, and you never actually get the connection you were after.

I know this looks like a regress, but it doesn't feel like just any infinite regress — it has a specific flavour, where the very thing you're trying to explain (connection, unity, combination) is the thing you keep presupposing in your explanation. It's like trying to explain how glue sticks by putting glue between the glue and the thing it's gluing, and then more glue between that glue and the thing, forever. I've seen something like this crop up in discussions of Frege and Russell on the unity of the proposition, and I think Bradley talks about it somewhere, but I'm not sure whether the pattern itself has a standard name across the literature, or whether it's been discussed as a general phenomenon beyond those specific contexts. I'm also not sure about its boundaries — when is it a genuine problem and when is it just a superficial similarity dressed-up to look deep? Part of what I'm trying to figure out is whether this is one problem that shows up in different guises, or a family of distinct problems that happen to share a structure. For instance, the version where you're worried about how a proposition manages to say something true rather than just being a list of names feels different from the version where you're worried about how a fact in the world manages to be a single fact rather than a scattered plurality of objects and properties. Are these the same difficulty or different ones?

If it does have a name, I'd love to know what it is, what the canonical statement of the problem looks like, and what the main lines of response are. If it doesn't, I'd be curious what you'd call it and how you'd distinguish it from nearby regresses — like the one in Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," or the third-man argument, or the kind of regress you get when you ask for justification of a justifying principle.
