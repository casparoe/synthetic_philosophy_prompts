I'm taking a course on rational choice theory this semester, and I need to submit a proposal for a term paper — about 5,000 words — by the end of the week. I want to write on L.A. Paul's argument from transformative experience: the idea that some major life decisions (whether to have a child, whether to change careers, whether to undergo a religious conversion) cannot be made rationally by standard expected-utility reasoning, because you cannot know the relevant subjective values before you have undergone the experience, and the experience itself changes your preferences in ways you cannot anticipate from the outside.

Here is my half-formed idea. Could an agent circumvent Paul's problem by appealing to second-order or meta-preferences? Suppose I do not know what it is like to be a parent, but I do know that I currently have a strong meta-preference — a preference about what sorts of first-order preferences I end up with — for being the kind of person who values parenting. If I can assign utilities to outcomes described at the level of preference-satisfaction (i.e., I value satisfying my meta-preference), perhaps I can make a rational decision without knowing the first-order payoff. The obvious worry is that this just pushes the problem up a level: if the experience is sufficiently transformative, my meta-preferences might shift too, and I cannot anticipate that shift any more than I can anticipate the first-order one.

Please turn this into a proper proposal. I need the following:

1. A precise statement of the question: can meta-preferences solve, or at least shrink, the decision problem posed by transformative experience? If so, under what conditions — for which kinds of transformative experience and which kinds of meta-preference — does the move work?

2. An explanation of why Paul's own discussion and the standard responses in the literature do not already settle this. For instance, Paul considers using testimony from others who have undergone the experience, but she argues that testimony does not supply the subjective knowledge needed. She also considers modeling the decision as a choice between whole lives rather than between outcomes with known utilities. Neither move, I want to argue, engages with the meta-preference strategy directly. Why not?

3. The method: what I would actually do in the paper. I would need to distinguish different kinds of meta-preference (e.g., a meta-preference grounded in my current values versus one grounded in a purely formal desire for coherence), construct a series of cases to test which kinds survive the transformative-experience challenge, and anticipate the strongest objections — especially the objection that the meta-preference itself is subject to transformation and the objection that the "distance" between first-order and second-order preferences collapses under scrutiny.

4. What would count as success and what would count as failure. If the meta-preference move works only for a narrow, uninteresting subclass of cases, is that still a worthwhile result? If it fails entirely, what would I conclude instead — that Paul's problem is even deeper than she claims, or that some other strategy (which one?) becomes more promising?

5. A realistic scope for a single-semester paper. I need to say what I can cover in 5,000 words and what I must explicitly set aside — for example, I should probably bracket the question of whether Paul's argument succeeds as a critique of revealed-preference theory in economics, and focus strictly on the normative decision problem for an individual agent.

Do not worry about citations; I will add those myself. I just need the conceptual architecture laid out clearly so I can fill in the argument.
