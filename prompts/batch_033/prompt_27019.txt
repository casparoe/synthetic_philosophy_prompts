In a reading group on philosophy of mind, I pressed the panpsychist in the room on the combination problem: if the fundamental constituents of reality each have some tiny form of experience, it is left entirely obscure how billions of such micro-subjects could compose into the single unified subject I take myself to be — a subject that does not present itself as a committee. The reply I received was "consciousness is fundamental," delivered as though that closed the matter. It didn't, and I want to be able to say precisely why.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that the sentence "consciousness is fundamental" could be used to express, from the nearly trivial to the radical. Separate at least two axes of variation: what "fundamental" means (unanalysable, irreducible, non-emergent, mereologically simple, present at the base level of nature, or something else) and what "consciousness" is being predicated of (the microphysical constituents, the cosmos as a whole, ordinary unified subjects, or something else). For each resulting reading, say whether it is close to trivial, a substantive and defensible thesis, or likely false. Most importantly, for each reading, say whether it would actually answer, dissolve, or even engage the combination problem I raised. I would like a menu of readings with verdicts rather than a single favoured interpretation.
