ok so a friend walked me through this and now i cant shake it. it seems to show that "uncountable" is just a trick of which model you're standing in, and i refuse to accept that, but i cant find the bad step.

(1) Suppose ZFC is consistent. By the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, ZFC then has a countable model — call it M.
(2) ZFC proves Cantor's theorem, so it proves the sentence "there is an uncountable set" (the powerset of the naturals does the job).
(3) Since M is a model of ZFC, M satisfies "there is an uncountable set."
(4) But M is countable: the whole domain of M can be listed against the natural numbers.
(5) So the very object M says is uncountable is a set that sits inside a countable list — which means it is countable after all.
(6) Therefore "uncountable" as used in set theory doesn't pick out an absolute property of any collection; it just reports what some model happens to think. The claim that there are uncountably many reals has no more absolute content than "some model says there are."
(7) And if that's right, ordinary mathematical talk about uncountable sets is just model-relative, which feels like it guts the whole idea.

I want to keep the ordinary picture where "there are uncountably many reals" is true full stop, not just true relative to a model. But i also don't want to give up either the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem or Cantor's theorem. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
