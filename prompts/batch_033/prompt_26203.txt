So I'm the person at my company who got stuck with "the welfare question," and I need to turn it into something we can actually use. I work for a salmon farming operation — we do Atlantic salmon, about 15,000 tonnes a year, and we've just started a small shrimp pilot that my boss is already regretting because the welfare standards for crustaceans are basically a philosophical minefield. Our retailers are starting to demand welfare certifications, and the certifiers all have different frameworks: one cares mostly about slaughter method, another is obsessed with stocking density and "natural behavior," a third just wants us to measure cortisol levels and call it a day. We get a proposal every few months — switch to electrical stunning, add shelters to the shrimp ponds, reduce density by 15%, try out this new feed that's supposed to reduce aggression — and right now we evaluate them by gut feel and whatever the most recent certifier audit flagged.

My CEO wants a decision procedure. Not a philosophy paper, not a literature review — a step-by-step thing that our ops team can run a proposal through in an afternoon and come out with a clear recommendation: do it, don't do it, or gather more evidence on X before deciding. The constraints: the people using it are biologists and engineers, they have zero patience for moral theory, and the stakes are real — we're talking decisions that range from a few grand to low six figures, four or five times a year. I want the procedure to handle at least three test cases: (1) switching from ice slurry to percussive or electrical stunning at harvest, (2) adding structural enrichment to the salmon net pens, and (3) lowering stocking density by 20%. For each step in the procedure, tell me what the diciest case is — the one where the step is most likely to misfire or give a nonsense answer. And here's the thing that keeps me up: for the shrimp, we don't even know if they feel pain in a way that matters. So the procedure needs to tell us what to do when the relevant science isn't settled, not just assume it is. If there's a point where the procedure has to say "a human needs to make a values call here and we can't automate that," mark it clearly — my CEO will hate it but I'd rather know.
