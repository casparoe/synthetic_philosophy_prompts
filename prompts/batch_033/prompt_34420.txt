ok so we read the twin earth thing in class and i thought i got it but now im confused about something. the idea is that if theres a planet exactly like earth except their clear drinkable liquid is XYZ not H2O, then when twin-earthlings say "water" they mean XYZ-water not H2O-water, even tho everything in their heads is identical to ours. so meaning isnt determined by whats in your head, its partly determined by the actual stuff in the world. fine.

but heres what bugs me. putnam also talks about the division of linguistic labor — like i can use the word "elm" without being able to tell an elm from a beech, and the meaning of "elm" in my mouth still depends on experts who *can* tell. so my question is: does the twin earth argument actually need the XYZ to be chemically different in a way *nobody* on twin earth has discovered yet? or does it work even if everyone on twin earth is in the same epistemic position as everyone on earth? because if the difference between H2O and XYZ is undiscovered, then the twin earth case is just a special case of the division of linguistic labor — the experts would eventually figure it out and the meaning was always fixed by the hidden structure. but if the difference *has* been discovered and everyone on twin earth knows their stuff is XYZ, then their heads arent identical to ours anymore (they have different beliefs). so it seems like the argument has this weird tension: either the heads are identical and the external difference is unknown (in which case its just the division of linguistic labor doing the work), or the external difference is known (in which case the heads arent identical). am i missing something about how the two parts of his paper fit together?
