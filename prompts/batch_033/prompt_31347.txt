I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a decision my protagonist must make. She runs a small clinic in a remote district, and at a moment of scarcity she must choose between using a limited supply of medicine on one patient whose need is acute and spreading it among several patients whose individual needs are less acute but whose combined prospects are better. A philosopher friend, defending her choice, put the underlying principle to me this way: each of us already makes exactly this kind of trade inside a single life, accepting a painful treatment now for the sake of one's later health or sacrificing a present comfort for a future good. Utilitarianism, he said, simply extends that same balancing to a group, treating the community as a single person spread across many bodies, so that one person's loss is weighed against another's gain just as one weighs one's own present losses against one's own future gains. Another friend objected that this analogy is doing the entire work, because persons are separate in a way that the stages of one life are not: there is no single subject who experiences both the cost and the benefit, and one person's suffering is not compensated by another person's flourishing.

I want to know how far the analogy actually carries the argument, since the moral center of the novel depends on it. Could you lay out what corresponds to what in the comparison, the present self, the future self, the single life, the pain, the compensating benefit, the act of choosing, and then say where the mapping breaks? In particular, which features of the single-person case does the analogy quietly import into the group case, such as a unified point of view, a single memory, consent, or the possibility of compensation within one life, and which of these disanalogies matter for the argument rather than being mere differences? Then tell me whether the utilitarian conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time, and what a better analogy would be if one exists. One complication I keep circling: my future self is not perfectly identical with my present self, so perhaps the single-life case is less unified than the objector assumes; I would like to know whether that thought repairs the analogy or only relocates the difficulty. I would be grateful for continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables.
