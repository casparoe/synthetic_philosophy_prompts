I've recently found myself in the middle of an argument about the foundations of deduction that I cannot quite shake. I contribute to a project that formalizes patterns of defeasible reasoning in a proof assistant, and the practical question I keep running into is this: when I write down a rule of inference — modus ponens, say — should I treat it on a par with the premises of an argument, or is it something of an entirely different kind, a license to move between statements rather than a statement itself? A philosopher on the team told me that Lewis Carroll had already laid out the problem with surgical precision in a short dialogue published in *Mind* in 1895, and that reading it carefully would save me from a category mistake I was making without realizing it. I've now read "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Vol. IV, Issue 14, April 1895, pp. 278–280) several times, and I think I grasp the broad shape of the regress it generates, but I keep getting stuck on one particular exchange. Here is the passage, which comes after Achilles has written down propositions A, B, and C in his notebook and has just asked the Tortoise to accept Z:

> "You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
> 
> "And why must I?"
> 
> "Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
> 
> "If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
> 
> "You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

I can feel that something important is slipping between the two characters here, but I am having trouble stating exactly what it is. My question is this: what does the Tortoise's move in this passage reveal about the difference between a rule of inference and a premise, and why does that difference matter? Specifically, is the Tortoise's demand unreasonable — and if so, on what grounds can we say it is unreasonable without simply begging the question against him? Or is the Tortoise making a perfectly legitimate point about the structure of justification, and if so, what does that imply about how a formal system can ever get off the ground?

I should add that I am not looking for a summary of the whole dialogue — I know how the regress continues. What I want is a careful reading of this passage in particular, one that helps me see why the Tortoise's conversion of the inferential link into a further premise is not just a clever trick but a genuine philosophical challenge. I would also ask you to indicate where your interpretation is most fragile: which alternative reading of the passage would most likely lead you to revise your account, or what consideration — perhaps from the philosophy of logic or from proof theory — might most seriously undermine your analysis.
