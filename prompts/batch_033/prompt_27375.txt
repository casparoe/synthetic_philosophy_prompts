I've recently started treatment for an anxiety disorder, and I've become preoccupied with something my therapist said: on bad days, the staircase in my building genuinely looks steeper to me. I've been reading about cognitive penetration of perception, and most of what I've found treats penetration as a threat to perception's standing as evidence — if fear gets inside the seeing, then the seeing is contaminated. I'm writing a short piece on the opposite possibility, and I need a case built to order.

Construct a single, ordinary-life scenario (no laboratory illusions, no drugs, no clinical population unless the mechanism would generalize) in which a current cognitive state — a belief, expectation, fear, or skill — penetrates a perceptual experience, and the penetrated experience leaves the subject epistemically better off than an unpenetrated experience would have: the resulting perceptual belief is more accurate, or better justified, than it would otherwise have been.

The scenario must meet three constraints. First, the effect must be genuinely perceptual: not a shift of attention, not a post-perceptual judgment or response bias, not a memory or imagery effect. Build the case so that a critic in the Pylyshyn or Firestone–Scholl line cannot simply redescribe it as attention, and say how the case blocks that redescription. Second, the epistemic benefit must not be stipulated: the penetrating state must not simply be assumed true and then allowed to bias perception in its own favor; the benefit should fall out of the structure of the case. Third, the penetrating state should be unremarkable, with no exotic mechanisms.

Deliver the result as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. The scenario itself.
2. Why it counts as cognitive penetration: the penetrating state, the altered experience, and the causal route.
3. The target perceptual belief, and why it is more accurate or better justified than it would have been without the penetration.
4. The strongest attention-or-judgment redescription a skeptic could offer, and the feature of the case that answers it.
5. The single feature that does the epistemic work, what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and one respect in which the general worry about penetrated perception survives even if the case succeeds.
