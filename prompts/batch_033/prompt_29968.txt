For some years now I have been puzzled by a pattern I observe among colleagues, students, and indeed in my own household. Many people who care about both climate change and animal welfare have shifted their consumption away from beef and toward chicken, on the grounds that beef has a far larger carbon footprint per kilogram of meat. The reasoning seems clear enough as far as it goes. But when one looks at the numbers, the shift is striking: producing a given quantity of edible protein from chicken requires killing vastly more individual animals than producing the same quantity from beef—a single steer yields hundreds of kilograms of meat, whereas a single broiler chicken yields perhaps two. If one takes animal lives or animal suffering to matter morally, the calculus appears to cut in opposite directions depending on whether the dominant consideration is greenhouse gas emissions or the number of sentient creatures harmed. And yet the environmentally motivated shift from beef to chicken is widely treated as an uncomplicated improvement, even by people who profess serious moral concern for animals.

I am trying to work out what theory of value this practice implicitly presupposes. Does it treat climate harm and animal harm as commensurable on a single scale, and if so, at what exchange rate? Does it presuppose that the lives or suffering of different kinds of animals have different weights—and if so, on what principle? Does it implicitly adopt a view on which the number of individuals harmed does not matter independently of the total quantity of harm, or one on which numbers do count but are simply swamped by the climate calculus? Or does the practice not strictly presuppose any particular theory, because it is driven by something other than consistent ethical reasoning—a kind of moral division of labour, or an unreflective ranking of environmental concerns above animal-welfare concerns? I would be grateful for a reconstruction of the implicit axiology, together with a clear statement of what would have to change in the practice if one replaced that axiology with a view that treats the number of individual animal lives as carrying significant moral weight.
