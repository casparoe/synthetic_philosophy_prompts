I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. In my work I often hear colleagues and collaborators say that a program, a model output, or a written specification means something, refers to something, or is about something. That has led me to a dispute I would like to understand more rigorously: the dispute over original and derived intentionality. I would like the two main positions in that dispute to be treated as parties to an adversarial collaboration, with Dennett's two-bitser example (a simple coin-operated device whose internal state is naturally described as registering the presence of a coin) as the shared focal case. On one side is the Searlean position: certain mental states, grounded in neurobiological processes, have original intentionality intrinsically, while the intentionality of artifacts such as sentences, programs, and model outputs is merely derived, existing only because an interpreter with original intentionality assigns content to them. On the other side is the Dennettian position: intentionality is a matter of adopting the intentional stance toward a system whose behavior is usefully predicted by treating it as having goals and information, and the original/derived distinction does not mark a genuine boundary between two kinds of aboutness.

Draft the joint statement that a committed Searlean and a committed Dennettian could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered agreed-claims section: substantive claims both sides can sign, including claims about what the two-bitser example establishes, what it leaves open, and what each side would need to show. Second, a disagreements table: each row should identify a topic, then give the Searlean formulation of their own view and the Dennettian formulation of their own view, with each formulation worded so that the side in question would accept it as a fair statement of its position. Third, a list of cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that a settled answer to any one of them would move at least one side. Fourth, for each crux, the concrete case, argument, or empirical finding both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you judge that some part of the dispute is verbal or terminological rather than substantive, identify that part and explain why.
