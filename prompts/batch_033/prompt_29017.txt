I've been following the debate about whether philosophy is useful to science — the one where one side cites Weinberg's remarks about philosophy being actively harmful to physics, Hawking's claim that philosophy is dead, and Feynman's comparison of philosophers of science to ornithologists who don't fly; and the other side points to Mach's influence on Einstein, the role of conceptual analysis in interpreting quantum mechanics, the migration of philosophical thought experiments into cognitive science, and the fact that several empirical sciences were born from philosophy. I want something more constructive than the usual exchange.

Draft the joint statement that a representative of the "philosophy has made no substantive difference to the trajectory of scientific progress" camp and a representative of the "philosophy has been and remains substantively useful to science" camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The statement should include:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides agree on. These should not be vacuous. They should cover: which historical cases are uncontroversial, which are contested, and why; what operational criteria would distinguish philosophy that makes a difference from philosophy that doesn't; and what burden of proof each side bears. I want to see whether the two sides can agree on a concrete set of historical episodes and what those episodes actually show, rather than just trading anecdotes.

2. A disagreements table. For each point of remaining disagreement, give the precise formulation each side would accept for its own position — stated in terms the other side would acknowledge as fair rather than a caricature. The table should distinguish disagreements about the historical record (did X actually influence Y?) from disagreements about counterfactuals (would Z have happened anyway?) from disagreements about what "useful" means in the first place.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, both sides should agree that it bears on the dispute, and each side should record in advance what it predicts the answer will be if the crux could be settled. Example cruxes might include: whether scientists with formal philosophical training produce different kinds of theories than those without, holding other variables constant; whether conceptual confusions have ever measurably slowed progress in a scientific field; whether the founders of quantum mechanics would have made the same technical advances without their philosophical commitments; and whether the migration of a question from philosophy to science (as happened with atomism, the nature of space and time, and the mind) counts as a success of philosophy or as philosophy rendering itself obsolete.

4. An honest diagnosis of which parts of the disagreement are verbal. If the two sides operationalize "useful" differently (e.g., causal contribution to discovery versus retrospective rational reconstruction versus training scientists to think clearly), or disagree about what counts as "philosophy" versus "science" in borderline cases, say so explicitly — and say whether the remaining disagreement survives once those definitions are aligned, or whether the dispute reduces to a difference in what each side wants the word "useful" to pick out.

Lead with the statement itself — no preface, no commentary.
