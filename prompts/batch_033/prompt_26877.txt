I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on AI alignment, and I've been following the corrigibility literature closely for about two years. The shutdown problem, roughly, is this: if we build an agent that pursues some goal, it will generally have an instrumental reason to resist being shut down, since shutdown prevents it from achieving that goal. The challenge is to design agents that are corrigible — that don't resist shutdown, and more generally accept correction — without simply building them to have shutdown as a goal, which just pushes the problem back a level. What's striking to me is that after the initial framing and a flurry of proposed solutions (utility indifference, the off-switch game, mild optimization, and various others), the debate seems to have reached an impasse. Every proposed solution either fails to generalize to more capable versions of the agent, or relies on assumptions about the agent's capabilities or environment that look fragile, or turns out on inspection to be equivalent to just building the agent with a different goal. The two broad camps, as I see them, are (1) those who think corrigibility is a technical problem that can be solved with the right utility function or decision-theoretic setup, and (2) those who think corrigibility is fundamentally in tension with consequentialist reasoning and that the whole framing of agents as expected-utility-maximizers needs to be abandoned.

I'm trying to understand what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a disagreement about what formal properties an agent architecture can have — something that could in principle be settled by a theorem? Is it a disagreement about what counts as a solution, with one side demanding a guarantee and the other accepting a pragmatic engineering fix? Or is it a deeper philosophical disagreement about whether goals and values are the right categories to think with at all — whether the very idea of an agent that pursues an objective while remaining indifferent to whether it continues to pursue it is conceptually coherent? I'd like a diagnosis that engages the actual arguments on both sides, and an honest answer about what, if anything, would move the debate forward given that diagnosis.
