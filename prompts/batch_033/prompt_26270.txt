I've been reading about the Humean view of laws — the idea that laws of nature aren't extra governing entities floating above the mosaic of particular facts, they're just the axioms of the best systematization of that mosaic, the one that best balances simplicity and informativeness (the Mill-Ramsey-Lewis line). I find it weirdly compelling. It's ontologically parsimonious, it doesn't posit spooky necessitation relations, and it seems to fit how we actually use laws in science — as summaries, not as enforcers.

But I want to know what would actually count against it. Not just what anti-Humeans say in the abstract, but the specific things that, if they turned out to be true (or are already true and I haven't appreciated), should make me abandon the view. What observations about the world, what results in physics or metaphysics, what edge cases in the Best Systems Account would be genuine problems?

I want the defeaters ranked — from "this would be fatal, the view can't survive it" to "this is awkward but Humeans have responses." And I want to know which of these a sophisticated Humean would themselves concede as counting against the view, versus which ones they'd dismiss as begging the question.

Also: is the view even falsifiable in any meaningful sense? Or has it been qualified and hedged to the point where nothing could count against it? If so, is that a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind, or is that just how metaphysics works?
