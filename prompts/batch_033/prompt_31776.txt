You run a decision-theory reading group for advanced undergraduates — about 12 students, one two-hour session. The group has just finished reading Newcomb's problem and the Prisoner's Dilemma and can state the differences between causal, evidential, and functional decision theory in those contexts. Next week they will tackle counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. Rather than present the scenarios as texts, you want to stage them as an in-class game so that participants feel the force of the dilemma — the pull toward paying in the mugging, and the unsettling structure of the XOR blackmail — before anyone names the problems or invokes a decision theory.

Design a game with two phases. In the first phase, participants should face a counterfactual-mugging-like structure: a predictor (played by the facilitator or built into the rules) commits to a policy before a random event, and after the random event is resolved, a subset of participants must decide whether to pay a cost that benefits only the branch they are not in. The rules should be arranged so that the tension between acting on the policy ex ante and acting on local causal information ex post arises naturally from the incentives, not from the facilitator's prompting. The second phase should introduce something like XOR blackmail: the predictor's message is structured so that receiving it is evidence of a bad state unless the participant commits to paying, but paying does not causally affect the state. These two phases can be run as a single continuous game (e.g., rounds) or as two separate exercises, but the through-line connecting them should be visible in the debrief.

Parameters: 12 participants, 120 minutes total (setup, gameplay, and debrief). No real money; use tokens, points, or a scoring system that participants can care about within the session (e.g., points redeemable for choosing next week's snacks). The predictor role should not require actual superhuman prediction — design the protocol so that the logic of prediction or commitment is enforced mechanically (e.g., pre-written envelopes, a trusted third party, or a rule that makes certain outcomes logically impossible).

Your deliverable should include:

- Materials needed and how to prepare them.
- Rules for both phases, stated clearly enough for a facilitator to read aloud.
- The exact sequence of what happens when, with a timeline.
- The payoff scheme (what participants stand to gain or lose at each choice point, and how the overall score is determined).
- A debrief script that maps what happened in the game to the formal structure of counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, and then to the commitments of CDT, EDT, and updateless/functional decision theory. The debrief should name the ideas only after the game is over.
- A prediction of what will go wrong the first time the activity is run (e.g., participants finding loopholes, misunderstanding the rules, or the payoff structure failing to produce the intended tension), and how the facilitator should handle each failure.
- An honest note on which feature of the real decision-theoretic problems the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, perfect prediction, logical omniscience, or the single-shot versus iterated nature of the commitment — and what the facilitator must supply in the debrief to close the gap.

The activity should make participants feel, before they theorize, that paying $100 in the tails branch is not obviously stupid, and that receiving the XOR message is not obviously a reason to pay — and that these two verdicts are in tension with each other in a way that a single decision theory struggles to capture. The debrief should show that the tension is the point.
