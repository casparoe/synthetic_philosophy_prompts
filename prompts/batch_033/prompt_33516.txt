Question: "Scheffler argues that human extinction would be bad in a way that goes beyond the deaths of the people who die in the extinction event. Explain his central argument for this claim, making sure to clarify the role of his doomsday and infertility thought experiments."

Student's answer:

"Scheffler's central claim is that the value of many of our projects and activities depends on the assumption that humanity will continue to exist after we die. He calls this the 'afterlife conjecture' -- the afterlife here isn't about personal survival after death, but about the survival of the human species. His argument is that if we knew humanity would end shortly after our own deaths, this would undermine our confidence in the value of our current pursuits. The doomsday scenario illustrates this: suppose you learned that thirty days after your death, an asteroid would wipe out all human life. Scheffler thinks this knowledge would make many of your current activities seem pointless -- things like cancer research, writing a novel, or even maintaining friendships would lose their meaning because they depend on the existence of future generations who can benefit from or carry forward what you've done. The infertility scenario makes a similar point but from the other direction: imagine everyone became infertile so that no new people would be born after the current generation dies. This too would drain much of our activity of its significance, even though no one dies prematurely. The key insight is that our reasons for action are essentially forward-looking: we need there to be people in the future for our actions to matter. So the badness of extinction isn't just about the people who would have existed but won't; it's about the loss of meaning and value for those of us who are alive now, whose projects and commitments would be robbed of their point."
