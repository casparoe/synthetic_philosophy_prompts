I'm taking a graduate seminar on metaphilosophy, and we've spent three weeks on the question of whether philosophy makes progress. The discussion has become increasingly frustrating — not because people disagree, but because I can't figure out what kind of disagreement we're even having. Here's the shape of it.

One camp (call them the pessimists) says: look at the big questions — free will, consciousness, the nature of justice — and you'll find the same live options that were on the table centuries ago, with no decisive resolution. If philosophy made progress, we'd have settled some of these. The other camp (the optimists) replies that this sets an impossibly high bar: physics hasn't settled the interpretation of quantum mechanics, mathematics hasn't settled the continuum hypothesis, and nobody says those fields don't make progress. Progress in philosophy, they say, consists in mapping the logical space, ruling out dead ends, sharpening distinctions, and achieving convergence on at least some questions — look at the PhilPapers survey, where you find substantial majorities on a range of positions.

The pessimists counter that this is question-begging: the optimists are defining "progress" as whatever philosophy happens to produce, and by that standard any ongoing activity counts as progress. The optimists counter that the pessimists are the ones begging the question, by defining progress in a way that only empirical sciences can achieve and then acting surprised that philosophy doesn't meet it.

And here's where I get stuck. This back-and-forth looks to me like a debate about the word "progress" — but both sides seem to think something substantive is at stake. Moreover, the debate about philosophical progress is itself a piece of philosophy. If the pessimists are right that philosophy doesn't make progress, then their own position is just another philosophical view that will never be settled — which doesn't make it false, but does raise the question of why they're arguing for it. If the optimists are right, then the fact that this debate keeps cycling should itself be evidence against their view, or at least a puzzle for it.

I want you to approach this as a philosopher who works on disagreement and conceptual engineering. Think carefully: start by laying out the possible diagnoses — verbal dispute, different standards of progress, different questions being asked under one heading, an ill-posed question, a substantive disagreement where one side is simply wrong, or something else — then commit to one. I want a definite answer about what kind of disagreement this is, supported by close attention to what each side actually argues, and an honest account of what, if anything, would move the debate forward given your diagnosis. If your diagnosis implies that the debate itself cannot make progress, say so and explain why that conclusion does not undermine your own answer.
