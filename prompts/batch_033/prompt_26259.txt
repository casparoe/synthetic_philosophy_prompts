I'm a software engineer, and recently a colleague forwarded me an article about METI International, an organization that actively beams high-powered radio messages toward nearby star systems in the hope of making contact with extraterrestrial intelligence. What gave me pause wasn't the astronomy — it was the governance situation, or rather the absence of one. As far as I can tell, there is no binding international framework that regulates who gets to transmit, at what power, toward which targets, or after what kind of consultation. A small group of scientists and private funders can effectively decide, on their own, to speak for the entire human species — and for every generation that follows — in an interaction whose downside includes, at least in principle, existential catastrophe. That is the claim at the heart of the dark forest hypothesis and related arguments: that broadcasting our existence to an unknown galactic environment is an irreversible gamble with the worst possible stakes.

Normally, when a domain involves tail risks of civilizational scope — nuclear weapons, climate engineering, gain-of-function research — we build treaties, oversight bodies, and precautionary defaults, even when the probabilities seem remote. Here we don't. The practice, as it exists, is a kind of de facto laissez-faire: individual actors transmit, and the rest of us inherit whatever consequences follow. I'm trying to understand what philosophical theory of rational choice under extreme uncertainty this institutional posture implicitly presupposes. Does it assume that the probability of catastrophic contact is strictly zero, not merely very small? Does it presuppose a particular view about the nature of intergenerational obligations — perhaps that future people have no standing in decisions made today? Or is it implicitly relying on a principle that unquantifiable uncertainties simply cannot be acted on, which would itself be a substantive philosophical commitment? And if we made those presuppositions fully explicit, would the practice remain defensible, or would it start to look philosophically incoherent next to the precautionary logic we already apply in other existential-risk domains?
