For an advanced undergraduate seminar on moral philosophy, I am designing a diagnostic instrument that sorts students into positions on supererogation — roughly, the category of acts that are morally good without being morally required. The point is to sort people by what they actually think about concrete cases, not by which label they endorse, so no question should use the word "supererogation" or ask directly whether the respondent believes in it.

I would like eight to ten questions. Each should present a concrete moral scenario and ask the respondent to choose among options such as: the agent acted as duty requires; the agent went beyond what duty requires; the agent did something good but not required; the agent did something merely permissible; or the agent did something there was reason not to do, though it turned out well. The cases should vary along dimensions that separate the main positions: whether the act involves significant sacrifice, whether it benefits strangers or intimates, whether it is an action or a forbearance, whether the cost to the agent is in the same currency as the benefit to the recipient, and whether the act is one-off or sustained over time.

The positions I want the quiz to separate include at least these: the classical view (supererogatory acts exist and are praiseworthy to perform but not blameworthy to omit); the Kantian or rigorist view (every morally good act is a duty, so the category of supererogation is empty); the imperfect-duty view (supererogation is real but is grounded in duties of beneficence that leave latitude, so the line between required and supererogatory is a matter of how much one does within an open-ended obligation); the conceptual-instability view (the very idea of a good act that is not required is incoherent — if it is good, why would morality not demand it?); the heroism-only view (supererogation is a genuine category but its extension is far narrower than commonly supposed, covering only acts of extreme self-sacrifice or saintliness); and the impartial-spectator view (what we call supererogatory is really what a fully informed, impartial spectator would recommend but not demand, with the demand/recommend distinction doing the work that duty/supererogation does on the classical picture).

Please provide the questions, the answer options for each, and a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions. Also flag any pair of positions your quiz cannot reliably pull apart, and note any logically possible positions in the space that no one (to your knowledge) defends — with a brief diagnosis of why each is unoccupied.

Finally, indicate how confident you are in the taxonomy as a whole, and single out the specific branch-point or diagnostic question you are least sure about — whether because the philosophical landscape is contested, because the cases pull in conflicting directions, or because you suspect there is a position you have missed.
