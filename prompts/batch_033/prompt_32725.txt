I've been working through the two-envelope paradox and have constructed a version of the switching argument that I cannot find the flaw in, though the conclusion is clearly impossible. I would appreciate a careful diagnosis.

Here is the setup. Two envelopes contain money; one contains exactly twice as much as the other. You are given one envelope at random, open it, and observe the amount X inside. You may keep X or switch to the other envelope.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Given that you observed X, the other envelope contains either X/2 or 2X.

(2) Since you chose the envelope at random, the unconditional probability that your envelope is the larger one is 1/2, and the probability that it is the smaller one is 1/2.

(3) Observing X does not change these probabilities, because X gives you no information about whether you hold the larger or smaller envelope. Whatever amount you see, it is equally consistent with either possibility, so the conditional probabilities remain 1/2 each.

(4) Therefore, the expected value of switching is (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = (5/4)X.

(5) Since (5/4)X > X, you should always switch.

(6) But the argument makes no use of the specific value X, so you should switch even before opening the envelope.

(7) And once you switch, the same reasoning applies to the new envelope: you should switch back.

(8) So rational choice would oscillate indefinitely, which is absurd.

I can see that (8) is unacceptable, so something must be wrong earlier. My instinct, shaped by a career in the natural sciences, is that the problem lies somewhere around step (3) — that the uniform distribution over "larger" and "smaller" cannot be coherently combined with the observed value X in the way the argument assumes. But I cannot articulate the precise error, and I want to know whether my instinct is right. If the problem is that there is no probability distribution over the possible amounts in the envelopes that makes (3) true for every X, then I would like to understand what the hidden assumption about the prior is, and what happens to the argument when we specify a particular, well-behaved prior. Does the paradox simply dissolve, or does it reveal something deeper about the relationship between observation and conditional expectation? And if I am wrong about step (3), which step should I reject instead, and what does rejecting it cost?
