I serve on the ethics committee of a university teaching hospital, and we must issue a written opinion before our meeting next Friday. The case has split us in a way that cuts deeper than the usual disagreements about futility or substituted judgment.

The patient, LB, is fifty-eight years old and underwent complete corpus callosotomy at twenty-three for otherwise intractable epilepsy. The surgery was successful by its own lights: seizures stopped, and LB has lived independently ever since, holding a job and maintaining a long-term partnership. But family members and the neurologists who have followed LB over the decades have long noted the familiar disconnection phenomena — the left hand unbuttoning a shirt the right hand is buttoning, the left hand selecting a food LB's speech center claims to dislike, and so on. These have been treated as curiosities, not as crises of identity.

LB now has pancreatic cancer with a grim prognosis. The oncologist estimates six to twelve months without chemotherapy, perhaps eighteen to twenty-four months with it. The regimen would be punishing — severe nausea, neuropathy, fatigue — and would not be curative. It would extend life, but it would impose a heavy cost on whatever life remains.

Here is the dilemma. When LB is asked verbally whether to proceed with chemotherapy, LB says yes, firmly, with apparent comprehension of the risks and the limited benefit, and with reasons that reference specific future events LB wants to live to see. Standard capacity assessments — all of them verbally administered — yield normal scores. But over the past two weeks, LB's left hand has repeatedly and with unmistakable purpose taken a pen and scrawled "NO," "STOP," and on one occasion "let me go" on whatever paper is nearby. LB's partner reports that at home the left hand has pulled the chemotherapy consent forms off the kitchen table and shoved them behind a bookshelf. When the attending physician asks LB about these actions, LB (speaking) dismisses them: "That's not me. I want the treatment. That hand has always done its own thing." LB exhibits no signs of clinical depression and has no psychiatric history.

The advance directive is silent on how to adjudicate interhemispheric conflict.

The committee is divided into roughly two camps. One camp holds that LB is one person who is ambivalent — the left-hand actions are expressions of fear or a submerged wish to refuse treatment, but LB remains a unified agent whose verbally articulated, reflectively endorsed preference should govern. On this view, the left hand is not a second person; it is, at most, a dissenting part of one person, and it would be perverse to let a hemisphere that cannot speak, justify its position, or engage in dialoge override the hemisphere that can do all three. The other camp holds that the standard interpretation of these cases is question-begging: we treat the speaking hemisphere as "the" person only because speech is our privileged medium for attributing agency, and we have no non-arbitrary reason to privilege the left hemisphere's preferences over the right hemisphere's simply because the left hemisphere controls the mouth. On this view, we are faced with something closer to two centers of will with incompatible preferences about continued existence, and the committee cannot simply defer to the one that talks.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. It must include: (1) a clear recommendation — proceed with chemotherapy, withhold it, or pursue some specified third course — together with a concrete protocol and a timeline for implementation or revew; (2) an honest and serous engagement with the strongest argument for whichever position you recommend against; and (3) a brief statement of what, if anything, this case implies for how the hospital should handle future split-brain patients who exhibit interhemispheric conflict over treatment decisions, including whether our standard capacity-assessment protocols need revison.
