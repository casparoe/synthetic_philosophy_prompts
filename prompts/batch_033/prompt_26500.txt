My philosophy teacher has spent the semester distinguishing what she calls "merely verbal" disputes from genuine ones, and she's handed down verdicts on quite a few cases in class. I've been keeping a list. Here is what she's said so far:

- Two biologists arguing about whether a virus counts as "alive," where they agree on all the underlying biochemistry: merely verbal.
- The ship of Theseus — whether it is the same ship after every plank has been replaced: genuine.
- Two mathematicians arguing about whether zero is a natural number: merely verbal.
- Whether a hot dog is a sandwich: merely verbal.
- Whether the subject in a standard Gettier case — true belief, strong justification, but the justification depends on a false intermediate belief the subject has no reason to suspect — actually knows the proposition: genuine.
- Two people arguing about whether a particular act is morally wrong, having agreed on every descriptive fact about what was done, by whom, and with what consequences: genuine.

Next week we are taking up a case she has not yet ruled on: whether a corporation should count as a "person" for purposes of a particular statute, where everyone agrees on all the factual features of corporations — their internal structure, how they operate, their causal powers — and the disagreement is purely about whether the legal term properly applies. She is going to give her verdict.

I want to predict what she is going to say, but I am more interested in reverse-engineering the principle she is actually running. The verdicts so far are jointly consistent with at least two quite different rules for drawing the line between verbal and genuine disputes. What are those candidate rules? What does each rule predict about the corporation case? Which rule do you think she is most likely using, and why? If you think the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine it — if both rules fit them equally well — then what further case could I propose that would settle which principle she holds?
