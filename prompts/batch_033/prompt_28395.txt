I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the sunk cost fallacy — specifically, whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational or whether there are principled exceptions. I've collected my intuitive verdicts on a range of cases, and they don't seem to fit a single simple rule. I'd like you to help me find the principle (or small set of principles) that best captures my pattern of judgments, tell me what it predicts for some further cases, and be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. **The movie ticket.** I've paid $15 for a non-refundable movie ticket. Twenty minutes in, I realize the film is terrible and I'm getting no enjoyment from it. I stay anyway because I paid. Verdict: irrational. The $15 is gone either way; staying only adds the cost of a wasted evening.

2. **The bad meal plan.** I've prepaid for a semester of dining-hall meals. The food is genuinely poor — badly cooked, repetitive, unappealing. I could afford to eat elsewhere, but I keep eating at the dining hall because I've already paid. Verdict: irrational. I'm adding the harm of bad meals to the harm of wasted money.

3. **The PhD near the end.** A friend is in the fifth year of a six-year PhD program. She has come to dislike the field and doesn't want the academic career the degree leads to. But she has only one year left, and the degree would significantly improve her non-academic job prospects. She decides to finish. Verdict: rational. The four years already spent aren't just a psychological weight — they're a genuine reason to complete the final year, because the credential's value is unlocked only by finishing.

4. **The PhD early on.** Another friend is one year into a five-year PhD and realizes he hates it. He stays because he doesn't want the first year to have been wasted. Verdict: irrational. One year is a reasonable cost for discovering a path isn't right; adding four more years of misery to justify one bad year is a poor trade.

5. **The failing product launch.** A company has spent $50 million developing a new software product. User testing shows it solves a problem nobody has. The team argues they should launch anyway because of the money already spent. Verdict: irrational. The $50 million is gone; launching will only add marketing costs and reputational damage.

6. **The long-term relationship.** A couple has been together for eight years. The relationship isn't abusive or toxic, but it has grown stagnant and neither person is happy. One partner thinks they should stay together because of the eight years they've invested. Verdict: I'm torn. Part of me says the eight years are sunk and irrelevant. But another part says that an eight-year history isn't merely a cost — it's a shared history that partly constitutes the relationship's value, and walking away discards something real that a new relationship wouldn't have. I'm not sure this is the sunk cost fallacy at all.

7. **The war.** A country has been fighting a war for three years. Thousands of soldiers have died. The original objective is clearly unachievable, but political leaders argue that withdrawing now would mean those soldiers died for nothing. Verdict: this reasoning feels deeply wrong to me — it compounds the tragedy by adding more deaths. But I also feel its emotional force in a way I don't feel for the movie ticket. I want to say it's irrational, but I'm less confident here than I am about the consumer cases.

8. **The hour-long line.** I've been waiting in line at a restaurant for an hour. The line has barely moved. I consider leaving, but I've already invested an hour. Verdict: the wait time is actually relevant, and not fallaciously so. The hour I've waited gives me information about how much longer I'll probably wait, and it also means I'm now closer to the front. This isn't like the movie ticket — the sunk cost has changed my objective situation.

9. **Finishing a book.** I've spent eight months writing a book manuscript. The first two-thirds are strong, but I've run out of ideas and the remaining chapters would be mediocre. I could publish it as-is with a weak ending, or abandon it. I decide to push through and finish it. Verdict: rational, or at least not clearly irrational. An almost-finished book with a weak ending is still a publishable book; an unfinished manuscript is nothing. The sunk investment creates a real option that abandoning would forfeit.

10. **The gym membership.** I pay $80 a month for a gym I never go to. I keep the membership because canceling would feel like admitting I wasted the money I've already paid. Verdict: irrational. The past months' payments are gone; the only question is whether I'll use the gym going forward.

What I'm noticing is that I seem to have at least three different reactions: (a) clear cases where honoring sunk costs is straightforwardly irrational (movie ticket, meal plan, gym, failing product); (b) cases where the past investment seems genuinely relevant because it has changed the objective structure of the choice (the line, the PhD near the end, the book manuscript); and (c) cases where the investment feels relevant in a way I can't easily dismiss but also can't easily defend (the relationship, the war).

Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts, or at least most of them? If not, what's the best candidate principle, and which of my verdicts does it require me to revise? I'd also like to know what your preferred principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on: a parent who has spent years pushing a child into a particular sport or instrument, the child clearly dislikes it and has no aptitude, but the parent insists on continuing because of the years already invested in lessons and equipment. Is this like the movie ticket, like the PhD, or like something else?
