I've been trying to get clear on what I think about confirmation and old evidence, and I'm hoping you can help me extract a principle from my case judgments. I've read the Bayesian literature on and off for years — Glymour, Earman, Howson and Urbach, the back-and-forth in the journals — but I keep finding that my intuitive verdicts pull in different directions, and I'd like to know whether there's a single coherent account behind them or whether I need to abandon something.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

**1. Mercury and General Relativity.** The anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion was known for over half a century before Einstein proposed GR. GR wasn't constructed to explain it — the theory was motivated by considerations about equivalence and general covariance — but it entailed the anomaly precisely, with no free parameters left to tune. I judge that the perihelion data strongly confirms GR. If anything, the fact that the data was sitting there unexplained for decades makes the confirmation feel stronger, not weaker. This seems like a clear case of old evidence confirming a theory.

**2. The Ptolemaic Epicycles.** An ancient astronomer notices that Mars sometimes appears to move backward against the fixed stars. He constructs a geocentric model with epicycles specifically designed to reproduce this retrograde motion, adjusting the radii and periods until the fit is good. I judge that the retrograde motion data does not confirm his model in any interesting sense. The model was built to fit the data, so the fit can't count in its favor — or if it does, the confirmation is negligible. The data isn't doing any work beyond what went into the model's construction.

**3. The Novel Prediction.** A cosmologist proposes an inflationary theory that predicts a specific value for the scalar spectral index, one that differs from the then-standard Harrison–Zel'dovich value of exactly one. The prediction is precise and surprising. A few years later, the Planck satellite measures the spectral index and finds it within the predicted range. I judge that this strongly confirms the theory. This is the textbook case of novel prediction, and I have no hesitation about it.

**4. The Forgotten Measurement.** A physicist proposes a theory that makes a surprising prediction about the anomalous magnetic moment of the muon. Unbeknownst to her, a lab measured this quantity five years earlier and published the result in a journal she doesn't read. The measurement matches her prediction. The broader community knows about the measurement, but she doesn't. I find myself wanting to say that the measurement confirms her theory — it would be bizarre to deny that — but that it confirms it less than if the measurement had been made after her prediction. The timing of her knowledge seems like it should matter, but I'm not sure why or how much.

**5. The Unappreciated Evidence.** In the decades before Darwin, naturalists had documented that species on islands resemble species on the nearest mainland in systematic ways, that similar climates on different continents host dissimilar species, and that fossils in a given region often resemble the living species found there. Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection wasn't constructed specifically to explain these biogeographic patterns — he drew on many lines of evidence — but the theory explains them beautifully. I judge that the biogeographic data confirms the theory, and fairly strongly, even though the data was collected before the theory was proposed. This feels structurally like the Mercury case, but I'm less confident about the strength of confirmation here.

**6. The Ad Hoc Rescue.** A theory predicts a new particle at a specific mass. A collider search finds nothing at that mass, with enough sensitivity that the particle should have been seen. The theorist then adds an auxiliary hypothesis — a new symmetry that hides the particle from detection at that energy but makes it visible at a higher one, conveniently just beyond current reach. I judge that the original theory has been disconfirmed by the null result, and the patched version starts from a lower credence than the original theory had before the null result. The null result doesn't confirm the patched theory; the patched theory merely survives. But I'm not sure how to formalize the difference between "surviving" and "being confirmed by" old evidence.

**7. Two Theories, Same Old Evidence.** Two competing theories T₁ and T₂ both entail some old evidence E. T₁ was developed with full knowledge of E and was shaped by it — the theorist worked backward from E to ensure the theory accommodated it. T₂ was developed independently to solve a different problem, and only later was it noticed that T₂ also entails E. I judge that E confirms T₂ more than it confirms T₁, even though both entail E and E was known all along. But I worry I'm importing considerations about how the theories were constructed — the context of discovery — that shouldn't matter to confirmation itself, which is supposed to be a logical relation between theory and evidence.

What I'm after is a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. I want the principle stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases. In particular, I'd like to know:

- Is there a version of Bayesian confirmation theory that handles all of these, or do I need to go beyond Bayesianism?
- Which of my verdicts, if any, does the best principle force me to abandon?
- What does the principle say about a case I haven't settled: a theory that was constructed to explain old evidence E₁, but also entails old evidence E₂ which the theorist didn't know about and which played no role in the theory's construction? Does E₂ confirm the theory, and if so, how much compared to a novel prediction?

Please give the answer in American English.
