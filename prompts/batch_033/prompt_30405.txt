I'm trying to get clear on what a theory of comprehension is even supposed to do before I read another page of Dennett, because every account I look at seems to be answering a different question. The setup I care about is the double inversion he keeps coming back to: Darwin showed you can get design and adaptive competence out of a process — natural selection — that itself understands nothing and aims at nothing, and Turing showed a machine can be competent at a task, doing arithmetic or playing chess, without comprehending what it is doing. Dennett's gloss is that this inverts the commonsense order. Normally we think comprehension comes first and competence flows from it; on the inverted picture, comprehension is something that has to be built up out of pieces that don't comprehend, and it shows up late as a product rather than as the original source. I'm convinced enough to take that seriously, but I dont yet know what a theory that takes it seriously has to deliver.

So: write the desiderata. What does an adequate theory of how comprehension arises from non-comprehending processes have to do? I want the list of requirements with the case or consideration behind each one — for instance, what it has to explain about why comprehension looks like the cause of competence when (on this picture) it isnt, or which distinctions it can't collapse (competence vs comprehension, reasons that are represented vs reasons that are merely free-floating in the system's history). Also say which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are more like preferences of one camp: does the account have to say anything about phenomenal consciousness, or can it bracket that? And I especially want to know which single requirement does the most work in excluding the usual views — dualism, intelligent design, an original comprehender, Searle-style original intentionality — and whether you think any pair of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied. That last part is what I'd find most usefull.
