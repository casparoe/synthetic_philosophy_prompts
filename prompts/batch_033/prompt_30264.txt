I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist, a former graduate student in philosophy now drifting through a sequence of short-lived relationships, finds she cannot stop tracing every conviction back to its grounds — and then to the grounds of those grounds — until the chain either bottoms out in something she cannot justify, circles back on itself, or simply never ends. The novel is organized around the epistemological regress problem: foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism each receive a section, and her romantic attachments in each section mirror the structure of the corresponding position.

I would like a villanelle to appear in the third section (infinitism), written in the protagonist's voice. The two refrains should embody the tension between the demand that justification never stop — that every reason given can be asked for its reason, and that stopping is arbitrary — and the psychological cost of that demand: the vertigo, the sense that nothing ever settles, the exhaustion of an endless chain of because-clauses. The form itself, with its refrains returning and never quite resolving, should perform the very regress it describes. The reader should feel, by the end, why someone might find infinitism philosophically honest and humanly unsustainable in the same breath.

Please make the poem accurate enough that an epistemologist would recognize the three positions and see how the villanelle form maps onto the regress — the way each return of a refrain mimics the demand for one more reason, the way the refrains circle past each other like justifications that never quite meet. I would also appreciate a brief note on where the poem leans on a contested reading of any of the positions, and which aspect of the rendering you are least confident about.
