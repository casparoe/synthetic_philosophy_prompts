Analyse the American Psychiatric Association's 1973 decision to remove homosexuality from the second edition of its Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders as a case study in the debate between naturalism and normativism about health and disease.

For the purposes of the analysis, take naturalism to be the view that disease is a value-free departure from normal biological functioning, with Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory as its most developed version: health is statistically normal functioning for a reference class of the same age and sex, and disease is a statistically subnormal part-function that impairs survival or reproduction. Take normativism to be the view that judgements of health and disease are ineliminably evaluative, and that a condition counts as a disease only relative to judgements about harm, dysfunction, or the kind of life a person should be able to lead.

The salient facts of the episode, as I understand them, are these. Homosexuality had been listed as a mental disorder in the DSM. In 1973, after years of pressure from gay and lesbian activists and from psychiatrists sympathetic to them, the APA's Board of Trustees voted to remove the diagnosis, and the membership subsequently ratified the decision. The APA did not simply drop the category: it replaced it with a narrower one tied to distress about one's sexual orientation, so that a person whose homosexuality caused them no distress would no longer be diagnosable on that basis alone. The decision was widely understood at the time to be partly scientific and partly political, and the episode is still cited as a paradigm of how social values enter psychiatric classification.

Give me a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. Specifically: (1) By the lights of a thoroughgoing naturalist, was homosexuality a disease before 1973, and did the APA have scientific grounds for the change? (2) By the lights of a normativist, what was actually happening in the reclassification, and does the retention of a distress-based category show that values were still doing the work? (3) Which features of the episode does each view explain well, and which does each misdescribe or have to strain to accommodate? Pay particular attention to whether a naturalist can justify the 1973 outcome on its own terms, and whether a normativist can explain why the APA insisted on retaining a residual category rather than simply declaring the whole matter non-psychiatric. (4) Say what the episode, so analysed, shows about the two positions: does it refute simple naturalism, force a refinement, or reveal that normativism has its own problem with demarcating medicine from politics?

Do not end on a survey of possible readings. Take a definite position on what the case establishes, defend it against the strongest objection you can think of, and flag the point at which your own verdict rests on a contestable assumption about what disease concepts are for. Write the answer in British English.
