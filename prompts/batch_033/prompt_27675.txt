I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about causation by omission, going by my gut on cases. Here they are:

1. A lifeguard on duty watches a swimmer drown and does nothing. I say the lifeguard's failure to act caused the swimmer's death.

2. A stranger on the beach watches the same swimmer drown and does nothing, though throwing a nearby life preserver would have been easy. I say the stranger's omission caused the death, but I feel some resistance to saying it — it seems slightly less causal than the lifeguard case, even though the counterfactual structure is identical.

3. A gardener goes on vacation for two weeks without arranging for anyone to water the plants. The plants die. I say the gardener's omission caused the plants' death.

4. A neighbor who has never met the gardener notices the plants wilting through the window and does nothing. I do not say the neighbor's omission caused the plants' death — that feels wrong, even though the neighbor could have broken in and watered them.

5. A doctor fails to administer a standard antibiotic to a patient with a bacterial infection. The patient dies. I say the doctor's omission caused the death.

6. A passerby on the street fails to administer an antibiotic to a stranger having a medical emergency — the passerby happens to be carrying the right drug and knows how to use it. I do not say the passerby's omission caused the death, though I'm less confident about this one than case 4.

7. A parent fails to feed their infant. The infant dies. I say the parent's omission caused the death.

8. A manufacturer designs a car and omits a standard safety feature that would have prevented a specific kind of crash fatality. A customer dies in exactly that kind of crash. I say the manufacturer's omission caused the death.

9. The same manufacturer omits a safety feature that no car on the market has, and that no regulation requires, but that the manufacturer's own engineers had sketched out and knew would prevent a rare kind of fatality. A customer dies in exactly that way. I am genuinely torn here — I lean toward saying the omission did not cause the death, but I keep flipping.

10. A government fails to build a flood barrier that would have protected a town from a storm surge. The town floods and people die. I say the government's omission caused the deaths — but only if the barrier had been funded, approved, and was standard practice in comparable towns. If building it would have required unprecedented effort and expense, I find myself wanting to say the omission didn't cause the deaths, even though the counterfactual is the same.

11. Everyone in the world who owns a phone fails to call in a warning about an impending natural disaster they each independently learned about. The disaster hits and thousands die. I do not say any particular individual's omission caused the deaths, but I do want to say something causal about the collective failure. I'm not sure what.

12. A pedestrian fails to shout a warning to a cyclist about to collide with an opening car door. The cyclist crashes. I do not say the pedestrian's omission caused the crash — it feels like a mere non-happening, not a cause.

Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? I'm especially interested in what the principle would say about case 9 (the unrequired safety feature) and case 10 (the barrier that wasn't standard practice), since those are the ones I can't settle. If no clean principle gets everything, tell me which verdicts I should reconsider and what I'd be committing to by keeping them. And if the best principle still leaves some cases as genuinely indeterminate — where the concept of causation by omission just doesn't yield a definite answer — I'd like to know that too.
