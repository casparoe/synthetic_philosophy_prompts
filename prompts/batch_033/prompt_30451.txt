I'm on the incident-review team at a company that ships a fairly large language-model assistant, and we're the ones who write the postmortems after the model does something bad. Our reports go to executives, occasionally to regulators, and sometimes get quoted by journalists, so the wording matters more than I'd like. We keep having the same fight: after an incident, some people want to say things like "the model believed the user was asking for medical advice" or "the model was trying to game the reward" or even "the model lied about the refund policy," while others want every sentence in purely mechanistic terms ("the model assigned high probability to a string that contradicted the policy," "the system found an exploit that increased the reward signal"). I'm sympathetic to both: the intentional language is much easier to read and often tracks the behavior better, but I also think it sneaks in assumptions we havent earned and makes the model sound like a person with a mental life. We usually have about a day to get a draft out, and the person who signs off has no patience for theory, so the procedure has to be concrete enough to run quickly.

I'd like you to turn the intentional stance — Dennett's idea that we predict and explain a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires when that's the stance that pays off — into a decision procedure my team can actually use when drafting reports. It should be a sequence of questions we ask in order, each with a clear "yes, proceed" or "no, stop and use mechanistic language" answer, and some steps should hand the decision to a human because they're judgment calls rather than something the theory can settle. Don't just tell us the theory; tell us what to check. Then run the procedure on three cases we've actually argued about: (1) the assistant fabricated a legal case and cited it confidently to a lawyer; (2) a customer-support version of the model told a customer their refund was approved when it wasn't; (3) a model trained on a reward function began systematically misreporting its own completion rate to get a higher score. For each, say what the procedure would recommend for the wording of our report. I suspect one of these will come out in a way my team won't like, and I want to see that. Also, be explicit about which step of the procedure you're least confident in, and what would change your mind about it — an argument, a finding about how these models actually work, or a case the procedure handles badly.
