I've been working through some contemporary metaethics and came across the following summary of the wrong kind of reasons problem in an introductory book on value theory:

"The wrong kind of reasons problem arises for any account of value that analyzes what it is for something to be good in terms of reasons to have favorable attitudes toward it. The difficulty is that there seem to be cases where you have a reason to adopt an attitude that has nothing to do with the object's merits. The classic example is an evil demon who threatens to torture you unless you admire his saucer collection. You plainly have a reason to admire the saucers—your life depends on it—but the saucers are not thereby admirable. The standard solution distinguishes object-given reasons (which concern the features of the object itself and determine whether the attitude is fitting) from state-given reasons (which concern the benefits of being in the attitudinal state). Once we see that only object-given reasons bear on value, the problem is largely dissolved: the demon's threat gives you a state-given reason but not an object-given one, and the buck-passing account of value was only ever meant to appeal to object-given reasons."

I am trying to figure out whether this summary is fair to the state of the debate. In particular, I am unsure about three things. First, does the object-given/state-given distinction actually solve the problem, or does it mainly give us a sharper way of stating it? Second, is it right to say the problem is "largely dissolved," or is there still significant disagreement about whether the buck-passer can draw the distinction in a non-question-begging way? And third, the summary seems to imply that the problem only matters for buck-passing accounts of value, but I have seen references suggesting it has broader implications—is that an oversight, or is the problem genuinely specific to the buck-passing framework?
