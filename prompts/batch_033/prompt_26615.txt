I spent the better part of four decades working in population genetics, and since retiring I have turned to philosophy of science — Popper in particular, whose work I had always meant to read properly. I have been struck by how often he reaches for an analogy between Darwinian evolution and the growth of scientific knowledge. Both, on his account, proceed by something like conjecture and refutation: the organism throws up a variation, the environment eliminates the unfit; the scientist proposes a hypothesis, the critical community eliminates the false. He goes so far as to call the growth of knowledge 'an evolutionary process' and builds a whole programme — evolutionary epistemology — on the parallel.

I find the analogy intuitively appealing, but my instincts as a biologist also make me wary of it. Natural selection is not the only evolutionary force, for one thing, and the analogy seems to treat 'the environment' as a kind of monolithic referee in a way that real ecologies resist. I would be grateful if you could work through the mapping systematically. What in the scientific process corresponds to mutation, to recombination, to drift, to the selective environment, to fitness, to the population, to generation time? Where does the analogy genuinely illuminate, and where does it strain or break? In particular, I want to know whether features of biological evolution that lack any obvious scientific analogue — say, the absence of a separation between germ line and soma, or the role of historical contingency — matter for the epistemological conclusions Popper wants to draw, or whether they are merely disanalogies of detail. And if the analogy turns out to be carrying more weight than it can bear, is there a better one?
