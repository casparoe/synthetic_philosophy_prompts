So I'm in this philosophy of mind class this semester and we just spent a week on Dennett's stuff about the Sphex wasp — you know the one, where the wasp does this whole elaborate routine of dragging a paralyzed cricket to the entrance of its burrow, then goes inside to "inspect" the burrow, comes back out, and if some meddling experimenter has moved the cricket a couple inches away while the wasp was inside, the wasp just drags it back to the threshold and goes inside to inspect again, and you can do this like forty times and it never catches on. It looks thoughtful, even ritualistic, but it's totally rigid. Dennett calls this "sphexishness" and uses it to talk about the difference between merely complicated behavior and genuinely flexible, intelligent behavior.

Anyway my friend Jake is a neuroscience major and we got into this huge argument after class. He thinks sphexishness is basically a preview of what neuroscience is eventually going to reveal about us — that we're just running way more elaborate versions of the same kind of program, and that free will is an illusion we tell ourselves because we can't see our own source code. I kept pushing back but honestly I couldn't quite land the counterargument in a way that satisfied me. Like, I think there's something importantly different about human flexibility, but I'm not sure how to draw the line without just asserting it.

For my final paper I want to write a dialogue between two characters who take these positions and really go at it — one who thinks sphexishness shows that all behavior is ultimately mechanical and the appearance of genuine flexibility is just a matter of complexity we haven't yet explained, and one who thinks sphexishness actually helps us see what real agency would consist in and why humans might have it. Can you write that dialogue? Make sure both sides are genuinely smart about it — no strawmen, no cheap shots.
