In the course of researching a novel whose protagonist is a linguist, I've been reading around in philosophy of language. A literary studies colleague recommended a widely cited essay that includes the following summary of Wittgenstein's later philosophy:

"Wittgenstein abandoned the idea that words have fixed meanings and replaced it with the notion of language games. Just as the rules of chess determine what counts as a valid move, the rules of a language game determine what counts as a meaningful statement. If you're not playing the game, you're not making sense. Since each community has its own language games with their own internal rules, truth is always relative to a game, and there's no standpoint outside all games from which to judge one as better than another. Wittgenstein's point about family resemblance reinforces this: there's no essence to concepts like 'game' or 'language,' just overlapping similarities, so any attempt to define them is a mistake."

I'm not a philosopher, but I've read enough Wittgenstein to suspect this elides some important distinctions. Could you go through this summary and tell me what a careful reader of Wittgenstein would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the relativist conclusion the author draws actually follows from Wittgenstein's views?
