I've been thinking about Zeno's paradoxes on my own for a while now — the Dichotomy, Achilles and the Tortoise, the Arrow, the Stadium — and I keep running into the same problem. People who agree that the paradoxes are worth taking seriously still end up in completely different places. Some say calculus settled it. Some say calculus missed the point entirely. Some say we need to give up on the idea that space is infinitely divisible. Some say Zeno was actually right and motion is an illusion.

I'd like to build a taxonomy of the positions on Zeno's paradoxes — a decision tree where each branch is labeled with a question that genuinely separates the views, and each leaf is a named position. The goal is to sort people by what they believe about the paradoxes, not by which label they prefer. So every question should be a concrete claim about infinite tasks, the structure of space and time, or what a mathematical proof can settle — phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position their answer points toward.

Please make sure the taxonomy includes at least these leaves: the standard mathematical solution (limits and convergent series), the Aristotelian response (the distinction between potential and actual infinity), spatial atomism (space and time are discrete), the super-task objection (that completing infinitely many distinct tasks is impossible regardless of convergence), and the Eleatic position (that Zeno's argument succeeds and motion is illusory). If there are other genuinely distinct positions — I've come across Bergson's, Whitehead's, and intuitionist approaches — include them, but only if they separate cleanly from the others on the diagnostic questions you're using. If two positions give the same answers to every question you can think of, flag them as positions the tree cannot separate and explain why.

Also note any combination of answers that has no defenders: is it empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has explored it?

I don't have a formal background in philosophy — I've just been reading and thinking about this on my own — so the taxonomy should be clear enough for someone in my position, but I'm not asking for a beginner's introduction. I want the diagnostic structure. Please write the answer in American English, and I don't need a bibliography or reading list.
