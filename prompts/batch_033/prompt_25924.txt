I've been exchanging letters for the past year with a friend about moral dilemmas — the kind where you have to choose whether to harm one person to save several others. Neither of us has studied philosophy formally; we simply find the questions gripping and have been working through cases together. Over time she has settled into a set of verdicts that feel consistent to her, though she has never stated the principle behind them in so many words.

Here are the cases we have discussed and her settled views on each:

1. **The standard switch.** A runaway tram is heading towards five people on the track. You can pull a lever to divert it onto a siding where one person is standing. She says it is permissible to pull the lever.

2. **The footbridge.** The same tram is heading towards five people. You are on a footbridge above the track, next to a large man. If you push him off, his body will stop the tram, killing him but saving the five. She says it is impermissible to push him.

3. **The hospital wing.** A runaway hospital trolley is heading towards five patients in a ward. You can press a button to divert it into a side room where one patient is recovering, killing that patient but saving the five. She says it is permissible to press the button.

4. **The transplant.** A surgeon has five patients who will die without organ transplants. A healthy visitor arrives for a check-up. The surgeon could kill the visitor and distribute his organs, saving the five. She says this is impermissible.

We have just started discussing a fifth case, and I want to know what she would say before I hear her answer:

5. **The loop.** The tram is heading towards five people. You can pull a lever to divert it onto a loop of track that rejoins the main line before the five. On the loop there is one person. If the loop were empty, the tram would simply go round and hit the five anyway. But because the one person is there, the tram strikes them and stops before reaching the five.

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the reasoning behind it. What rule or principle is she implicitly running that fits her first four verdicts? What other rules would fit those four just as well but give a different answer on the loop case? Which rule do you think she is actually using, and why? And if you had to design one further case — not the loop — that would most cleanly tell those rival rules apart, what would it be?

Please write your answer in British English.
