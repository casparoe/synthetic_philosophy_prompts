I've been reading Scheffler's work on the afterlife conjecture — the claim that the continued existence of humanity after our deaths is a non-instrumental condition of many of our current projects and values having the significance we take them to have. His central thought experiment asks us to imagine learning that humanity will go extinct shortly after we die from natural causes; the intuition is that this knowledge would drain much of what we care about of its point, even though our own lifespan is unaffected.

I want to go further than Scheffler does and argue that the badness of human extinction identified by the afterlife conjecture is not merely an additional harm layered on top of the harm of all those individual deaths, but is in an important sense conceptually prior: the deaths are bad partly *because* they foreclose the collective future that gives individual lives their meaning, not just because each death is a separate tragedy. If this is right, it has implications for how we weigh extinction risk against other large-scale harms — in particular, it suggests that standard person-affecting frameworks systematically understate what is at stake.

Please make the case for this position. Engage seriously with the strongest objection: that it seems morally perverse to claim that the destruction of meaning is more fundamental than the destruction of the lives themselves, and that this looks like valuing abstractions over concrete suffering. Your argument should address whether this objection succeeds or whether the position can absorb it.
