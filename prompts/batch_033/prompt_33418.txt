I've been going down a bit of a rabbithole with Bayesian epistemology recently — I'm not a philosopher by training, I'm in cognitive science, so I'm coming at this sideways — and I keep tripping over this thing called Van Fraassen's Reflection principle. The way it was explained to me, it says that if you're rational, your current credence in a proposition should equal your expectation of your future credence in that same proposition. So if you're certain that tomorrow morning you'll have credence 0.8 that it'll rain next Tuesday, then right now you should also assign 0.8 to rain next Tuesday. The idea is that you should treat your future self as an expert, at least absent a reason to think you'll lose evidence or go mad in the meantime.

I mentioned this to my supervisor and she's fully on board — she thinks violating Reflection is just a diachronic Dutch book waiting to happen, and that it's as fundamental as the Kolmogorov axioms. But I've been chewing on it and I honestly can't see why it's supposed to be a *requirement* of rationality rather than, at best, a rule of thumb. If I know I'm about to pull an all-nighter and my judgement is going to be shot, or I'm going to get drunk at a party and become wildly overconfident about everything, or — less dramatically — I'm simply going to *forget* some evidence that I currently possess, then it seems not just permissible but *correct* to say: my future credence is going to be 0.9, but I'm not moving from my current 0.3, because I know my future self will be epistemically compromised. That doesn't strike me as a rational failing. If anything, deferring to my sleep-deprived future self would be the irrational move.

So here's what I'd like: can you make the strongest possible case *against* Van Fraassen's Reflection principle as a normative requirement of rationality? I'm not after a neutral survey — I want you to actually argue the position. Engage with the main defences of Reflection (the Dutch-book argument, the "if you foresee a rational shift you should just make it now" move, and whatever else is out there) and explain why they fail. Also, I'd be interested in whether you think there's a salvageable restricted version of the principle — something like "you should defer to your future self only when you're confident they'll be at least as epistemically virtuous as you are now" — and whether that restricted version is too weak to do the work Reflection was originally meant to do. If the restriction basically guts the principle, say so.
