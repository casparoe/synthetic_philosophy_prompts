I am currently taking a course on free will and moral responsibility, and our professor has been working through a series of hypothetical cases about the principle of alternative possibilities and Frankfurt-style counterexamples. I would like you to act as an expert philosopher of action and moral responsibility, think carefully, and help me work out what principle she is implicitly applying. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that could fit her track record before you commit to a prediction.

So far she has given verdicts on three cases:

1. The plain case. A woman deliberates about whether to embezzle money from her employer, decides to do it, and does it. Nothing prevents her from refraining, and she could easily have done otherwise. The professor says she is morally responsible.

2. The standard Frankfurt case. Unbeknownst to a woman, a neuroscientist has implanted a device that will detect a certain neural pattern—the earliest sign that she is about to decide to refrain from embezzling—and that will directly cause her to embezzle if the pattern occurs. The world of the case is not deterministic. In the actual sequence, the woman decides on her own to embezzle; the pattern never occurs, though it could have, and the device stays idle. The professor says she is morally responsible.

3. The direct-manipulation case. A neuroscientist uses an implanted device to directly produce in a woman the decision to embezzle, bypassing her ordinary deliberation altogether; the manipulation leaves her no ability to do otherwise and no alternative mental act open to her, though she experiences the resulting decision as her own. The professor says she is not morally responsible.

The professor has said only that she takes the standard Frankfurt case to show that the simple principle of alternative possibilities needs refinement; she has not told us which refinement she accepts.

Next week she will present this new case. The scenario is stipulated to be deterministic: a woman's deliberation about whether to embezzle is the deterministic product of her own settled character and values, which she endorses. A neuroscientist has implanted a device that, had any alternative decision—or any neural precursor of one—begun to form, would have directly caused her to embezzle. Because the world is deterministic, no such alternative ever begins to form, and the device never activates. The woman deliberates and decides to embezzle, and the decision issues from her own character and values. Given determinism, there was no alternative possibility of any kind—not even a different neural sign or a different decision.

What verdict is the professor most likely to give in this new case? More importantly: which principle about alternative possibilities and moral responsibility is she implicitly running, which other principles fit her earlier verdicts just as well, and what does each candidate principle predict about the new case? Give your best guess with your reasons, and then describe a further case that would most cleanly distinguish the remaining candidate principles from one another.
