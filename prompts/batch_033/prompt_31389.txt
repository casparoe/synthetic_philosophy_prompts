I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been reading around in the foundations of quantum mechanics because my day job involves classical simulation of quantum circuits. In our codebase the wavefunction is literally a data structure — an array of complex amplitudes in memory that gets updated as gates are applied — so when I encountered the distinction between psi-ontic and psi-epistemic views, the epistemic side immediately felt natural. As I understand it, a psi-ontic view treats the wavefunction as part of the physical world, while a psi-epistemic view treats it as a representation of an agent's information, more like a probability distribution over possible underlying states than like a physical field.

What I can't figure out is why the measurement problem is still considered a problem if the epistemic view is right. If the wavefunction is just my information, then getting a measurement outcome should be nothing more than a Bayesian update: I conditionalize on new data, and my representation changes, but nothing in the world changes discontinuously. The system was already in some definite underlying state that my previous distribution didn't single out, and after the measurement I simply know which one it is. Collapse would not be a physical process, so there would be no puzzle about when or how it happens. Since people who are far more qualified than I am still treat the measurement problem as serious even under epistemic readings, I assume I'm smuggling in an assumption somewhere. What is the assumption, and what exactly does a psi-epistemic account still need to explain?
