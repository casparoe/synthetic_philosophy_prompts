You are a professional philosopher of emotion who is also fluent in computational neuroscience, and you are writing for an audience of one: me. I am a computational neuroscientist who works on reinforcement learning models of the brain. I think natively in terms of policies, value functions, reward prediction errors, model-based and model-free control, latent-state inference, and interoceptive predictive coding. I can follow philosophical distinctions when they are explained to me, but I want them translated into my vocabulary, not merely illustrated with loose analogies.

The subject is the old dispute about what emotions are. I want you to restate the three main families of theories in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report: what did not survive the translation.

The three families, as I understand them:
- Judgment theories: an emotion is, or essentially involves, an evaluative judgment or appraisal — fear is judging that something is dangerous in a way that matters to me.
- Perception theories: an emotion is a perception of something, most often a perception of bodily changes or a perception of value through bodily changes — fear is a readout of a body already preparing to act.
- Feeling theories: an emotion is the felt experience itself — fear is what it feels like, and the bodily changes and judgments are causes, accompaniments, or consequences rather than the emotion.

I have a standing suspicion I want you to test. I suspect that part of this dispute is a levels-of-analysis artifact — computational, algorithmic, implementational — and part of it is a genuine disagreement about whether emotion is essentially cognitive, embodied, or conscious. I want to know which part is which, and whether the residue after translation points to a real limitation of my vocabulary or to confusion inside the philosophical positions themselves.

Before you write the final answer, think carefully about the cleanest honest mappings and where they will break. Do not force a translation where none exists; the failures are the point. Do not quietly import assumptions from my vocabulary and present them as neutral restatements — flag it whenever you do.

Produce the response under exactly these headings, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. Groundwork (150 words). State the three positions more precisely and lay out the rules of a faithful translation.
2. Vocabulary map (200 words). A table mapping the key notions — appraisal, bodily change, feeling, intentionality, valence, and any others you need — to their best counterparts in reinforcement learning and predictive processing, with a one-line note on how faithful each mapping is.
3. Judgment theories in the target vocabulary (250 words).
4. Perception theories in the target vocabulary (250 words).
5. Feeling theories in the target vocabulary (250 words).
6. Residue report (350 words). Separately list what translated cleanly, what translated only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. For each residue, say whether it is a blind spot of the reinforcement-learning vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked.
7. Verdict (150 words). Give me your honest assessment of which parts of the judgment-perception-feeling dispute are substantive and which are artifacts of level of analysis, and state the remaining question that my vocabulary would need to grow in order to express.
