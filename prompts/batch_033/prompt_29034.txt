I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm working on a book with a long courtroom subplot. The pivotal scene turns on whether a jury will say a character acted intentionally or just negligently, so I've been skimming pop philosophy and psychology books to get the psychology right. One book sums up the Knobe effect like this:

"Knobe's experiment goes like this. Subjects read about a corporate chairman who approves a new program that will make money and, as a side effect, either damage or help the environment. In both versions he says he doesn't care about the side effect one way or the other; he only cares about profit. Asked whether he brought the side effect about intentionally, subjects say he intentionally harmed the environment but did not intentionally help it. The usual explanation is that people's moral evaluation of the outcome bleeds into their judgment of whether the act was intentional: because harming is bad and helping is good, we blame him in the first case and praise him in the second, and then we report the blameworthy act as done 'on purpose.' The upshot is that the concept of intentional action is not a neutral description of what was in the agent's head; it's driven by whether we morally approve or disapprove of what happened. That's why, when a jury decides whether a defendant acted intentionally, what it's really doing is deciding whether to condemn the defendant — the legal distinction between doing something on purpose and doing it by accident is just moral judgment in disguise."

I'm inclined to believe the experiment and the asymmetry, but the jump to "jury instructions are moral judgments in disguise" feels too tidy, and I don't want to build a whole scene on a misreading. Could you go through that summary and tell me what's fair, what's overstretched, and whether the courtroom conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts? Also, what would a defender of the Knobe effect or a philosopher working on intention and moral judgment object to in the way the book puts it? No citations needed — I just don't want to embarass myself in front of readers who know this stuff.
