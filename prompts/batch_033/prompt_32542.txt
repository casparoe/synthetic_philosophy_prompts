I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar, and last week the discussion turned to Daniel Dennett's notion of free-floating rationales — the idea that evolution by natural selection generates reasons for the traits and behaviors of organisms that exist independently of any mind that represents them. Two classmates, Elena and Marcus, ended up in a protracted argument after class, and several of us stayed to listen. Neither of them seemed to get the better of the other, and I'd like to understand who was right about what. Here is how the exchange went, as best as I can reconstruct it.

Elena opened by saying she found the whole idea of free-floating rationales close to incoherent. Reasons, she said, are considerations that count in favor of something — an action, a belief, an attitude. That counting-in-favor relation is inherently normative, and normativity presupposes a perspective from which something matters. Natural selection, by contrast, is a blind causal process. It doesn't track reasons; it just differentially preserves whatever happens to reproduce. Calling the outcomes reasons is a façon de parler that Dennett has mistaken for an ontological discovery.

Marcus replied that this was too quick. Consider the broken-wing display in ground-nesting birds. The bird feigns injury to lure a predator away from its nest. There is a genuine explanatory relationship between the display and the survival of the chicks, and that relationship — the fact that the display works because it exploits the predator's foraging instincts — is real whether or not the bird understands it. That relationship is the reason the trait exists. It's free-floating because it wasn't formulated by any mind before it was instantiated, but it's not a fiction. Dennett's analogy, he said, is to centers of gravity: an asteroid has a center of gravity before any physicist calculates it. The center of gravity isn't a physical object, but it's not a ghost either.

Elena answered that the center-of-gravity analogy papers over exactly the problematic feature. A center of gravity is a descriptive mathematical abstraction — it summarizes a mass distribution. A reason is normative — it doesn't just describe how things are; it bears on how things ought to be. The broken-wing display exists because it worked, but "it worked" is a retrospective causal judgment, not a reason that was there to be discovered. If the same bird had a different mutation that happened to produce the same effect, that mutation would have been selected too, and we'd call that the reason. But that just shows we're telling a causal story backwards and calling it a rationale.

Marcus pushed back: the retrospective character doesn't make it unreal. The reason a trait was selected is the selection pressure itself — the fact that organisms with the trait outreproduced those without it. That fact obtained at the time of selection, even if no one was around to articulate it. The broken-wing display didn't become a good solution to the nest-defense problem only once someone noticed. It was a good solution all along, and that's why it spread. The goodness of the solution is the free-floating rationale.

At this point Elena shifted ground. She said that even if you grant that selection pressures can be retrospectively described as reasons, the term rationale smuggles in more structure than is actually there. A rationale, she said, is an articulated structure of considerations — premises leading to a conclusion. Natural selection doesn't operate on anything like premises. It operates on differential survival. The "reason" that the broken-wing display exists isn't really a reason; it's a causal explanation of the trait's persistence, dressed up in intentional language. The intentional idiom is useful for prediction, as Dennett's own intentional-stance framework acknowledges, but usefulness doesn't confer reality.

Marcus responded that this conflates representing a reason with there being a reason. Dennett's point is precisely that there were reasons before there were reason-representers. The intentional stance is a way of detecting patterns that are really there — real patterns, in Dennett's phrase. The broken-wing display really does solve a problem, and the problem-solution structure is part of the fabric of the world, not projected onto it by us. If we encountered a thoroughly alien biosphere on another planet, we could still ask what a given trait is for, and there would be a fact of the matter, even if neither we nor the organisms had ever represented it.

Elena came back with a final objection. She said Marcus's position seemed to require that selection pressures be mind-independent in a way that is hard to sustain. Which selection pressures explain a trait depends on how we carve up the environment, and that carving already reflects our interests and explanatory purposes. Is the reason for the broken-wing display that it distracts predators, or that it reduces the probability of nest discovery by 37 percent, or that it exploits a specific neural pathway in foxes? All of these are true descriptions of the same causal process, but they pick out different "reasons." If the rationale is free-floating, which one is it? The answer seems to depend on what we care about explaining, which suggests the reasons aren't floating free of us after all.

Marcus replied that this is just the familiar problem of explanatory selection, which arises for any causal explanation and doesn't specifically threaten Dennett's view. When we ask why the window broke, we can cite the throw of the rock or the fragility of the glass — both are causes, and which we cite depends on our interests. That doesn't mean the rock didn't break the window. Similarly, the fact that we can describe the selection pressure at different grains doesn't mean there is no fact of the matter about what the trait was selected for.

They left it there, and I've been turning it over since. I'd like you to go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move: who is right about which specific claims, where each argument succeeds or fails, and whether either of them is missing something important that would clarify the dispute. Please also say who, in your view, has the stronger overall position.
