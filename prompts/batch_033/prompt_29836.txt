My father passed away three months ago, and I've been spending a fair amount of time with a close friend from university who is a devout Christian. She has been remarkably kind to me during this period, and I'm genuinely grateful for her presence. But I find myself increasingly puzzled by the things she says. She tells me my father is "in a better place," that his suffering has meaning within a larger plan, and that she knows we will be reunited one day. When I asked her once, gently, whether she meant these things the way one means that it will rain tomorrow, she seemed almost offended by the question. She said something like: of course she doesn't mean it that way, but that doesn't make it less real.

Last week I had a long, somewhat heated argument with another friend — a philosophy graduate student — who insisted that statements like hers are simply false or meaningless because there is no evidence for them and no way to test them. I pushed back, because I could tell that my religious friend was not making ordinary factual claims that happen to be ill-supported; she wasn't, for example, citing scripture as though it were a laboratory report. But I couldn't articulate what she was doing instead. The argument left me frustrated on both sides: the philosopher seemed to miss something essential about how my religious friend's words actually function in her life, and yet I don't know how to describe that "something" without sounding like I'm granting the truth of theological claims I don't myself believe. My religious friend's statements seem to guide her conduct, shape her emotional responses, and anchor her sense of what matters in a way that feels categorically different from, say, her belief that the bus arrives at 8:15. So my question is: how should I understand what my religious friend is doing when she makes these statements? Is there a coherent way to see them as meaningful without treating them as empirical hypotheses, and if so, what does that imply for how I should respond to her going forward — both for my own sake and for the sake of our friendship?

Please structure your response in the following sections:

1. Diagnosis (about 300 words): Identify the philosophical structure of my confusion — what assumptions about meaning and belief are in tension here, what picture of language makes the philosopher's objection seem decisive, and what thinkers or traditions have tried to resist that picture.

2. The Positive Account (about 400 words): Lay out a coherent way of understanding religious utterances that treats them as genuinely meaningful without reducing them to empirical claims. Be precise about what kind of "meaning" and what kind of "belief" are involved, and explain how this account makes sense of the role my friend's statements play in her life.

3. The Cost (about 200 words): What does this account require me to give up? Does it, for example, commit me to saying that the philosopher friend is simply mistaken about the nature of meaning, or does it instead show that both friends are partly right in different respects? Does it imply anything uncomfortable about my own non-religious convictions?

4. Practical Guidance (about 200 words): Given this account, how should I think about my conversations with my religious friend going forward? Should I say "I believe that too" when I don't? Should I stay silent? Is there a way to engage with what she is actually doing without either pretending or condescending?
