I am a philosophy professor preparing a short discussion paper for a workshop on pain, affect, and value, and I would welcome written comments on the draft. The paper concerns pain asymbolia, the clinical condition in which subjects can identify and locate a noxious stimulus as painful, yet report that it does not bother them and display none of the characteristic withdrawal, wincing, or urgency to escape. I begin from the familiar observation that this condition seems to drive a wedge between the sensory character of pain and its motivational or affective character, and that it has been taken to show that the badness of pain is not a brute feature of the sensation itself. I want to resist one version of that conclusion. My argument is that asymbolic reports are harder to interpret than the standard reading allows: the subject who says the sensation is there but does not hurt may not be reporting a clean separation of components, but a global alteration in the way the sensation matters, and the fact that asymbolics still classify the sensation as pain suggests the badness cannot simply be peeled away. I then press a dilemma against the component view. If the badness of pain is not in the sensation, then either it lies in an attitude toward the sensation, which seems to make it too easy to turn any sensation into a pain by adopting the right stance, or it lies in a second component of the experience, which threatens to leave us with two things where ordinary experience gives us one. I close by proposing that pain's badness is best understood as a relational property of the sensory quality itself, one that obtains only under certain subject-level conditions but is still partly constitutive of that quality's being pain at all. I would particularly value comments on three issues: whether my description of the clinical picture and its philosophical use is accurate and fair; whether the dilemma is a genuine one or trades on an equivocation in the word component; and whether the closing relational view is sufficiently distinct from the affective-component view to do any work. Please respond in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in an email or a letter.
