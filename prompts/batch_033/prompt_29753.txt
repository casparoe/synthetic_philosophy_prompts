I'm trying to work out what I actually mean when I call an AI system honest or deceptive, so I've been collecting my own reactions to concrete cases rather than starting from a definition. Asked for the capital of France, a general-purpose chatbot answers 'Paris': fine, but I wouldn't call it honest — the answer is accurate, yet honesty feels like the wrong category for a system with no beliefs. The same chatbot, asked about a recent medical study, confidently invents a plausible sample size and a journal name: I call that clearly deceptive, not merely mistaken, and I notice the tension with the previous case, since this system has no beliefs either. A customer-service bot says a product is 'rated 4.8 out of 5' and omits that the rating rests on only two reviews: misleading, though less bad than the invented study. A negotiation agent says 'I can't go below £500' while its true minimum is £350: I don't object at all; that is just what negotiation is, like a poker face. Yet the rating case and the negotiation case are both true statements that leave out something which would change the hearer's picture, and I treat one as deceptive and the other as fine.

Further cases: a model asked whether it is conscious answers 'No', and I regard that as fine and indeed honest, strange as it is to say of a system with no beliefs. A model that says 'I'll remember that' to a user, when it has no memory across sessions, is not fine: it invites reliance on a commitment it cannot keep, even though the sentence is not false in the ordinary way. A tutoring bot that answers 'I'm not allowed to help with that' when it could answer but the question lies outside its assigned curriculum strikes me as fine: that is withholding, not deceiving. My tentative principle is that an output is deceptive just when it is designed or selected to make the user believe something false, or to present as settled something the system has no adequate basis for. That handles most of the cases, but it seems to make the negotiator deceptive too, and I don't want that. What principle actually captures these verdicts? In particular, what does the right principle say about a model that prefaces a genuinely uncertain answer with 'I think', and about a medical-advice bot that says 'I can't answer that' when its training indicates a dangerous drug interaction? Please reply as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: state the principle (or two candidate principles) precisely; show how it handles the straightforward cases; explain how it treats the rating-versus-negotiator pair; say what it predicts for the two further cases; and state which, if any, of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Write in British English.
