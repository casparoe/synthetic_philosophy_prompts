I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been writing a book about an art conservator restoring a seventeenth-century terracotta statue that was shattered in a flood. The plot turns on a question I took to be simple: after she replaces the missing arm and re-fires the broken fragments, is the object in her studio the same statue that stood in the church for three hundred years? A philosopher friend read my manuscript and gave me an argument that seems to show the question is not merely difficult but unanswerable, because our ordinary way of counting material things is incoherent. I cannot find the exit, and I need to know whether my novel's central question is a real one.

Here is the argument as he put it. (1) A sculptor shapes a statue from a lump of clay. The lump can survive being squashed into a ball; the statue cannot, since squashing destroys the statue but leaves the clay. So the lump has a property the statue lacks, and by Leibniz's Law — if two things differ in their properties, they are distinct — they are distinct objects. (2) Yet the statue and the lump are made of exactly the same matter at exactly the same place and time. (3) If two distinct objects can coincide in that way, there is no principled reason to stop at two: the same matter also composes a thing that would survive losing its left hand, a thing that would not, a thing that would survive being painted, a thing that would not, and so on without end. (4) So every piece of matter at every moment coincides with an indefinite number of objects, each with its own persistence conditions. (5) If that is right, ordinary counting is meaningless — there is no fact of the matter how many objects are in a room — and the world is populated with an endless zoo of gerrymandered things. (6) That conclusion is absurd. (7) So premise (1) must be false: the statue and the lump are identical after all. (8) But if they are identical, then the difference cited in (1) was never a real difference in the object; it was a difference in how we describe the same thing. So Leibniz's Law either fails, or it applies only to our descriptions rather than to the world. (9) That threatens every identity claim we make, including the conservator's claim that the restored statue is the same statue as the one that stood in the church. (10) So either the world is an endless zoo of coinciding objects, or identity is a matter of description. Both seem unacceptable, and my novel's question has no determinate answer.

I would rather keep the statue and the clay distinct than accept the zoo, and I would rather keep Leibniz's Law than accept that identity is a matter of description; but I want to know exactly which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.
