I'm a retired physical chemist who's gotten into decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice over the last few years. The position I've ended up holding is built on Good's theorem on the value of information: as I understand it, for an expected-utility maximizer with fixed preferences, the expected value of costless information is never negative, because you can always just ignore the signal and take the action you would of taken anyway. From that I've drawn the broader conclusion that there is no rational reason to avoid free information, and that people who'd rather not know are either making a mistake or smuggling in a cost they haven't owned up to. I want to know what would count against this. Not the easy stuff like "the news would make me miserable" — I can see that's a cost, not free information. What I'm after is: which assumptions of the theorem do the real work, what would an actual case have to look like for a coherent agent with stable preferences to strictly prefer ignorance, and how much of my broader conclusion survives once we add bounded rationality, preferences that shift when you learn things, or the fact that other people know what you know? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.
