In a seminar on political ethics we have been reading about the problem of dirty hands, and the discussion turned to a case in which a minister authorizes a drone strike on a compound where a terrorist leader is meeting, knowing that several civilians in the building will be killed; the strike prevents an attack that would plausibly kill hundreds. A classmate defended the minister by saying that in politics 'the ends justify the means,' and added that anyone who hesitates is refusing to accept what responsibility requires. I found this too quick, but I do not want to respond with the opposite slogan that no end ever justifies a harmful means, because that seems false as well. Can you lay out the distinct precise principles 'the ends justify the means' could be expressing, and say for each whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or outright false, and which of them would actually license the minister's decision? In particular, I would like the readings distinguished along at least these dimensions: what makes something count as an 'end' (any good consequence, an averted catastrophe, a prevented rights violation), whose good is counted (the public, all affected people, the agent's own cause), how certain and how large the expected good must be, whether the means must be necessary or merely effective, whether the principle is a claim about what is right, what is excusable, or what the agent should feel afterward, and whether the agent is left with any moral remainder, guilt, or remorse. I want a menu of readings I can take back to the seminar and use to show where the slogan is doing real argumentative work and where it is simply being waved around.
