I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a longform piece about what philosophy can tell us about the value of mortality — and whether the fact that we die is genuinely bad for us or merely something we're conditioned to fear. I want to anchor the piece in a reading of Harold Ramis's *Groundhog Day* through the lens of the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies.

The film gives us a protagonist who cannot stay dead: Phil Connors attempts suicide multiple times — electrocution, jumping off a building, driving off a cliff — and wakes up each time to the same February 2nd. The Epicurean position, as formulated by Epicurus in the Letter to Menoeceus and elaborated by Lucretius, holds that death is nothing to us because when death is present, we are not, and when we are present, death is not; Lucretius adds the symmetry argument that if we do not regret the eternity before our birth, we should not regret the eternity after our death. Thomas Nagel, Bernard Williams, and others have pushed back, arguing that death deprives us of goods we would otherwise have enjoyed, and that this deprivation is bad for us regardless of whether we experience it.

I'd like a committed reading of what the film shows us about this debate. Does *Groundhog Day* dramatize a counterexample to the Epicurean claim — by showing us a man for whom the removal of death is first liberating, then existentially horrifying, and by suggesting that mortality is precisely what gives our choices their weight and our days their narrative shape? Or does the film, perhaps unintentionally, vindicate the Epicurean point from a different direction: that what matters is not death itself but how we live, and that Phil's eventual escape from the loop through moral growth and genuine connection to others suggests that the alleged badness of death was never really the issue? I want a reading that takes both the philosophy and the film seriously, that identifies specific scenes and structural features of the plot that bear on the argument, and that is willing to say where the film complicates or resists a clean philosophical moral. Most importantly, I want to know whether the film adds something to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone don't capture — something a reader who has only read the Epicureans and their critics would miss.
