Two members of a cause-prioritization working group I sit in on spent an evening arguing about the standard framework that compares causes by importance, tractability, and neglectedness, and I was asked to referee. I couldn't, so I'm bringing the exchange here. I've reconstructed it as faithfully as I can from my notes, with the speakers labeled Dana and Eli:

(1) Dana: The framework treats importance, tractability, and neglectedness as three separate criteria, but neglectedness isn't a fundamental reason to prefer a cause. If a cause is important and tractable, crowding should already show up in tractability: the more effort a cause has absorbed, the less the marginal unit of effort accomplishes. So neglectedness is just a heuristic for diminishing marginal returns, not an independent factor.

(2) Eli: Diminishing returns are only one mechanism. A cause can be neglected because it is stigmatized, invisible, or has no constituency to lobby for it. Those causes are neglected even before returns diminish, and a paper estimate of importance and tractability may miss what is actually available in practice precisely because nobody is looking. Neglectedness carries information the other two factors don't.

(3) Dana: But the cash value of that information is still about marginal impact. If stigma makes a cause neglected, the significance of that fact is that the marginal unit of effort there does more good than it would elsewhere. That is what tractability, understood marginally, already captures. Adding neglectedness as a separate third criterion counts the same consideration twice.

(4) Eli: You're talking as if marginal tractability were a quantity we can just read off. It isn't. Our estimates of importance and tractability are coarse and unreliable. Neglectedness is an independent, observable proxy that lets us correct those estimates. Its role is epistemic — a check on our other judgments — not a separate channel of value. That's calibration, not double-counting.

(5) Dana: If its role is purely epistemic, then it shouldn't be weighted as a third criterion at all. A calibration check belongs inside the estimation of the other two factors. If we had good direct evidence about marginal impact, neglectedness would add nothing. A genuine factor doesn't vanish when your information improves.

(6) Eli: That's too quick. Neglect can alter the situation itself, not just our estimates. A neglected field has less competition for talent, funding, and attention, so a given unit of effort can get more traction there. That is a causal feature of the cause's situation — related to tractability, but distinct from the technical solvability that tractability is usually taken to mean. Even with perfect information, neglect would still matter through this channel.

(7) Dana: Then what you're calling neglect is just one input to tractability — the receptiveness of the environment to a unit of effort. You've conceded my point under another name: everything neglectedness does is either an input to marginal tractability or a fallible clue about it. There is no third thing left over.

(8) Eli: That may be the right taxonomy, but a framework is a tool for choosing under uncertainty, not a piece of metaphysics. In that setting, keeping neglectedness separate earns its keep: it is comparatively easy to observe, it is the factor people most systematically underweight, and it corrects the bias toward salient, prestigious causes. Fold it into tractability and it will get lost, and the framework will do its job worse. The question is which framework helps us choose better, not which is more parsimonious.
