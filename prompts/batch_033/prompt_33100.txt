I'm writing a paper on the normative foundations of concern about suffering risks and I want to check whether my commitments are jointly tenable before I build an argument on top of them. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Longtermism about expected value. The expected value of the far future is astronomically large, so the most important feature of any action or policy is its expected effect on the long-run trajectory. Near-term effects matter, but only insofar as they propagate into or constrain the far future.

(2) Suffering-happiness asymmetry. There is a fundamental asymmetry between suffering and happiness, and this asymmetry is not merely a matter of diminishing marginal value. Preventing extreme suffering matters more than creating comparable amounts of happiness, and this becomes decisive when we are comparing vast quantities of each — the kind of quantities that become relevant on a civilizational or cosmic scale.

(3) S-risks as a distinct category. S-risks — scenarios in which the far future contains astronomical amounts of suffering — deserve to be treated as a distinct category of risk, separate from extinction risks. Extinction is not the only way the future can go badly, and treating s-risks as a subcategory of extinction risk obscures the distinctive reasons we have to prevent them.

(4) Consequentialist structure. Our reasoning about s-risks should be consequentialist in structure: we evaluate actions and policies by their expected consequences, without appealing to independent deontological constraints that would preemptively block the consideration of certain consequences.

(5) Centrality of digital minds. The possibility of artificial sentience and digital minds is central to s-risk reasoning. Digital minds could be instantiated in vastly greater numbers than biological minds and could be subject to forms and intensities of suffering that biological minds cannot experience. This changes the scale of the problem qualitatively, not just quantitatively.

(6) Side-constraints on intervention. Despite the astronomical stakes, we should not support or implement measures to reduce s-risks that would involve significant violations of individual rights, the infliction of large near-term harms, or the preemptive restriction of technological development in ways that would foreclose important opportunities.

(7) Fanaticism about tiny probabilities. When an intervention has even a tiny probability of positively influencing the long-run trajectory, that tiny probability multiplied by the astronomical stakes yields an expected value that swamps any near-term considerations. A very low probability of success is therefore not, by itself, a reason against pursuing an intervention aimed at the long run.

I want to know whether this set of commitments hangs together. Specifically:

- Which pairs — or larger subsets — are in genuine tension with each other?
- Which pairs only appear to be in tension but dissolve on closer inspection?
- For each genuine tension, how severe is it: does it force a revision, or can it be managed with a refinement of one or both commitments?
- What is the least costly revision that would restore consistency across the whole set, and what does that revision cost me in terms of the overall view?

Please separate what is settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these topics, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
