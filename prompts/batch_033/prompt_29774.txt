For a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory, write a decision tree that takes a reader from their reaction to a pair of concrete gambles to a named position on how decision theory should treat games with undefined or conditionally convergent expected values.

The tree must use the following three cases as diagnostic material:

1. The St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on the nth toss, you receive $2^n. (Expected monetary value: infinite.)

2. The Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on the nth toss, you receive (−1)^(n−1)·2^n/n dollars — that is, the payoffs alternate in sign and grow without bound in magnitude. The expected monetary value is the conditionally convergent series Σ (−1)^(n−1)/n, whose terms can be rearranged to sum to any real number, or to ±∞.

3. The Altadena game: identical to the Pasadena game except that the payoffs are rearranged (by some specified permutation of the natural numbers) so that the partial sums of the expected-value series converge to a different limit — say, +∞ instead of ln 2. The two games have exactly the same outcomes with exactly the same probabilities; they differ only in which outcome is paired with which number of coin tosses.

Each branch node in the tree should be a question that a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to — no node should simply ask "do you hold position X?". The questions should track the genuine fault lines in the debate: for instance, whether an agent must have a real-valued expected utility for every well-formed gamble; whether the value of a gamble is invariant under rearranging which outcome is paired with which state; whether dominance reasoning applies when expectations are undefined; whether the problem is a reason to restrict the domain of decision theory or to revise its formalism.

Leaves should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody has defended it in print) and a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. The tree should be built from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a pre-existing list of names: first identify the key claims and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate actual positions in that space. Unoccupied combinations should be noted and briefly diagnosed — is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

The tree must include as leaves at least the following positions (you may add others if the logical space demands them):

- The bounded-utility view: utilities must be bounded, which dissolves both the St. Petersburg and Pasadena problems because all expectations become finite and absolutely convergent.
- The domain-restriction view: unbounded utilities are permissible, but games whose expectations are conditionally convergent are simply outside the scope of expected utility theory; the theory is silent about them.
- The dominance-extension view: where expected utility is undefined, statewise dominance or weak dominance still constrains rational preference, and this settles many (though not all) comparisons between Pasadena-type games.
- The ordering-sensitivity view: a Pasadena game and its Altadena rearrangement are different games, not the same game; the value of a gamble is sensitive to the mapping from states to outcomes, so the rearrangement changes the gamble's identity.
- The comparativist view: the absolute expected value of a Pasadena game is undefined, but differences between Pasadena-type games may have well-defined, absolutely convergent expectations, so pairwise preferences can be recovered even when individual values are not.
- The permissivist view: where expected utility is undefined, rational agents may permissibly adopt any of several preference orderings, provided they respect dominance and other uncontroversial constraints; there is no fact of the matter about which is correct.

Flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot separate (for example, because they agree on all verdicts about the three diagnostic cases but disagree on the underlying normative story). If two positions coincide on all concrete cases the tree uses, note this and explain what additional case or question would be needed to split them.

The tree should have between six and ten leaves and between four and seven branch nodes. Target an audience that has completed one course in formal epistemology or decision theory and is familiar with expected utility theory, the St. Petersburg paradox, and the basics of infinite series, but has not encountered the Pasadena paradox before.
