I have been teaching a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory, and we recently spent two sessions on the absent-minded driver problem, originally due to Piccione and Rubinstein. The problem is deceptively simple, but it has generated a surprising amount of controversy — and I have constructed an argument, drawing on that controversy, that seems to show that the standard principle of expected utility maximization is either incomplete or self-undermining when the agent has imperfect recall. I am fairly confident the argument goes wrong somewhere, but I want to know exactly where and what the fix costs. Here is the argument, cleaned up.

The setup: A driver is on a highway with two identical-looking exits. Exit 1 leads to a bad neighborhood (payoff 0). Exit 2 leads home (payoff 4). Staying on past both exits leads to a dangerous area (payoff 1). The driver is absent-minded: whenever he reaches an exit, he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. He knows all of this in advance, including the fact that he will be absent-minded.

(1) A rational agent, at each decision node, chooses an action that maximizes expected utility relative to his credences at that node.

(2) At the planning stage, the driver can choose a probability p of exiting at each exit he encounters. His expected payoff is p·0 + (1−p)p·4 + (1−p)(1−p)·1 = 1 + 2p − 3p², which is maximized at p = 1/3, yielding expected payoff 4/3.

(3) At an exit, the driver's credence that he is at the first exit depends on his strategy. If his strategy is to exit with probability p, then the probability of reaching exit 1 is 1, and the probability of reaching exit 2 is 1−p. So his credence of being at exit 1, conditional on being at some exit, is 1/(2−p).

(4) Given these credences, the expected utility of exiting is 4(1−p)/(2−p), and the expected utility of continuing is 1 + 3p/(2−p).

(5) For the strategy to satisfy (1) at the exit, the driver must be indifferent between exiting and continuing — otherwise he would strictly prefer one action and would not randomize. Setting the two expressions equal gives p = 1/3, with common expected utility 8/5.

(6) So the unique strategy consistent with (1) is p = 1/3. At the exit, the driver is indifferent, so he can randomize accordingly.

(7) But now the driver notices something. The expected utility of continuing, computed in (4), takes as given that at any future exit he will continue to follow the strategy p = 1/3. But he is not bound by that assumption. He is at the exit now, deciding what to do. He can ask: what if I continue now, and then, at the next exit (if I reach it), I re-optimize rather than mechanically following the plan?

(8) If he continues now and re-optimizes at the next exit, he will face the same calculation. But he will also know that he has already passed one exit. He might reason: "I am at an exit. I continued at the last exit. But I don't know whether that was the first or the second exit. So I am in exactly the same epistemic situation as before." If he is in the same epistemic situation, he will again be indifferent, and again he might continue. This regress suggests he will always continue, yielding payoff 1.

(9) The driver might try to circumvent the problem by precommitting to the randomization device. But precommitment is not rationally binding if, at the moment of action, the agent can see that the precommitted action is not uniquely optimal. And the driver can always reason: "I am indifferent between continuing and exiting. If I am indifferent, I can choose either. If I choose to continue, I am not violating expected utility maximization — I am merely selecting one of two equally good options."

(10) The problem is that if the driver chooses to continue whenever he is indifferent, his effective strategy is not p = 1/3 but p = 0 (always continue). But then, by (3), his credence of being at the first exit is 1/2, and the expected utility of continuing is (1/2)(4) + (1/2)(1) = 2.5, while the expected utility of exiting is (1/2)(0) + (1/2)(4) = 2. So continuing is now strictly better, and p = 0 is consistent with (1).

(11) But the ex ante expected payoff of p = 0 is 1, which is worse than the ex ante expected payoff of p = 1/3, which is 4/3. So the driver, by following (1) at the exit, has reasoned himself into a strictly worse outcome than he could have achieved by committing to the mixed strategy.

(12) The principle in (1) therefore seems to be self-undermining: it tells the driver to randomize at the exit, but the very act of being indifferent and choosing to continue leads to a strategy that is worse by the driver's own lights.

(13) The conclusion is that either (1) is false — expected utility maximization is not the right principle for agents with imperfect recall — or there is a mistake in the reasoning from (7) to (12).

I suspect the flaw is somewhere in the move from indifference to a shift in strategy, but I want to know exactly what principle is being violated and whether rejecting it requires us to revise decision theory in a way that has consequences elsewhere. The argument seems to exploit a tension between the ex ante perspective and the perspective of the agent at the decision node — a tension that does not arise for agents with perfect recall. If the argument is unsound, I want the diagnosis. If it is sound, I want to know what a repaired decision theory would look like. Please identify the step that goes wrong, explain why it goes wrong, and say what it costs to fix it.
