You're a philosopher of science who works on natural kinds and biological classification, and you're good at turning theory into something a working biologist can actually use. I want you to think carefully and start by brainstorming a bit before you write the final procedure.

I'm on the scientific advisory committee of a state wildlife agency. Four or five times a year we have to rule on proposed changes to how we classify organisms: usually a proposal to split one recognized species into two, or to lump two into one, sometimes to recognize a subspecies or a population-level management unit. These decisions feed directly into endangered-species listings, hunting and fishing rules, and land-use permits, and they get challenged in court alot. We're mostly field biologists and ecologists; nobody on the committee has formal philosophy training, and we've been deciding case by case with no shared framework, which is starting to feel indefensible. Our director has to sign off on whatever we recommend and she wants a written rationale she can defend to a judge.

I want you to write us a decision procedure we can actually run on a proposed split or lump. It should be grounded in what the philosophy of natural kinds has to say about when a category tracks something real in nature rather than just being a convenient filing system, but it has to be usable by smart non-philosophers over two or three meetings. It should be a checklist or sequence of questions to ask in order, and it should say clearly at each step what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is a values call rather than a scientific one.

Before you write the procedure, brainstorm the relevant considerations: whether the candidate group supports induction, whether knowing something belongs to the group lets you predict other things about it that tend to pay off, whether the members share a stable cluster of properties held together by some underlying mechanism rather than just by our sorting, whether the thing is more like an individual with a history than a kind, whether recognizing it would force us to recognize a thousand other groups, and anything else you think matters. Then turn that into the procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases:
(1) a proposal to split a widespread bird species into an eastern and a western species based on a small genetic difference, a difference in song, and no known ecological or morphological difference;
(2) a proposal to recognize a fish population in a single watershed as its own management unit, where the population is isolated and locally adapted to water temperature but still reproductively compatible with the next watershed over;
(3) a proposal to keep recognizing a traditional species that turns out to be a grab-bag of superficially similar but not closely related populations.

I want the third one to be the case where your procedure gives an answer I'm not going to like, and I want you to be honest about where the procedure will misfire, what it can't settle, and whether the idea of natural kinds is doing real work here or just giving us a fancier way to describe our hunches. Don't give us a bibliography or a reading list; we need the procedure itself.
