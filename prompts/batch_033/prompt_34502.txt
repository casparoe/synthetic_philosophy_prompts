I've been turning over counterfactual mugging in my head and I think I've found an argument that shows the whole thing is busted, but I'm not totally confident and want someone to check my reasoning.

Quick setup for anyone who hasn't seen it: Omega (a perfect predictor) flips a fair coin. If heads, Omega gives you $10,000 — but only if you would have given Omega $100 in the tails case. The coin comes up tails, and Omega asks you for $100. The standard argument says you should pay: if you're the kind of agent who pays on tails, you get $10,000 on heads (expected value $5,000, minus the $50 expected payment on tails, net $4,950). If you're the kind who doesn't pay, you get $0. So paying dominates.

Now here's my argument that this reasoning can't be right:

(1) If you should pay in the original counterfactual mugging, it's because a decision procedure should be evaluated by its expected value across all branches, including counterfactual ones where the predictor's behavior depends on the procedure.

(2) The exact same reasoning applies no matter what the predictor conditions on. If Omega had conditioned the $10,000 on whether you'd destroy your smartphone, the expected value of the "destroy phone" policy would be $5,000 minus half the value of your phone, and the "keep phone" policy would be $0.

(3) So if you accept (1), you're committed to accepting: in any situation where a perfect predictor tells you it flipped a coin that came up tails, and it would have given you $10,000 on heads iff you do X on tails, you should do X — provided that the expected value calculation (0.5 × $10,000 − 0.5 × cost(X)) comes out positive.

(4) Now consider a sequence of such predictors. On Monday, Predictor A tells you the coin came up tails and asks you to smash your phone. On Tuesday, Predictor B tells you the coin came up tails and asks you to smash your laptop. On Wednesday, Predictor C asks for your television. Each predictor is genuinely perfect and independent. Each individual decision has positive expected value. So by (3), you should smash your phone, then your laptop, then your TV.

(5) By the end of the week you've destroyed everything you own, having received not a single dollar in the actual world, because every coin came up tails. But you were supposedly "rational" to do this, because each individual choice maximized expected value counting counterfactual branches.

(6) A decision procedure that tells you to destroy all your possessions for purely counterfactual gains is not a rational decision procedure. The fact that the coin "could have" come up heads doesn't change the fact that it didn't, and you've ruined your life for nothing.

(7) Therefore (1) is false. The original counterfactual mugging argument is unsound.

I can see a couple replies. One is that the iterated case is different because the predictors' predictions might interact — once you smash your phone, Predictor B updates its model of you — but you could just stipulate that the predictors are independent and made their predictions before the sequence started. Another reply is that the sequence of all-tails is just tremendously bad luck, and the procedure is still correct ex ante, the same way a casino with a house edge can lose money on a particular night. But that feels like it misses the force of the objection: the problem isn't that you got unlucky, it's that the reasoning tells you to take actions that are guaranteed to harm you in the actual world, and the only justification is "but in a different world you would have gotten a reward." At some point that stops being a justification.

Which step of my argument actually fails, and what do you have to give up to reject it? Also, when you answer, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this topic — don't present a contested view as the consensus.
