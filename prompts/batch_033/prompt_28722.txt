So I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been TAing this intro course where we just did the conceivability argument — Chalmers, zombie world, the whole thing. As a treat I showed my sections Blade Runner (Final Cut, natch) and now I can't stop thinking about what the film actually *does* to the argument, and I'm not sure whether it's a genuine challenge or just a vibes-based distraction.

Here's the thing. The zombie argument needs us to conceive of a being physically identical to a human but with no phenomenal consciousness — all dark inside, no what-it's-like. Blade Runner never gives us that. The replicants aren't physically identical to humans; they're bioengineered, have implanted memories, and you can detect them with the Voight-Kampff test if you ask enough questions. So strictly speaking the film isn't about philosophical zombies at all. And yet. By the end, we're convinced Roy Batty has an inner life — the "tears in rain" monologue lands precisely because we take him to be describing genuine experience, genuine loss, a subjective perspective on his own memories that he knows will vanish. But the film hasn't given us any metaphysical argument for this; it's given us narrative and performance and Rutger Hauer's face. If our judgment that a being is conscious can be swayed that easily by a story — by whether we *like* the character, basically — what does that say about our supposed ability to conceive of zombies from the armchair? It feels like the film is a kind of methodological critique of the conceivability argument, but I can't quite articulate it without overclaiming.

I'd love a reading of Blade Runner through the lens of the zombie conceivability argument. Where does the film genuinely challenge the argument's methodology — our reliance on modal intuitions, the move from conceivability to possibility — and where does it just change the subject? Does the fact that we come away convinced the replicants are conscious actually tell us anything about whether zombies are conceivable, or is that a category mistake (we're making an epistemological judgment about a fictional character, not a metaphysical judgment about a possible world)? And if you were teaching this stuff — would you show the film, or does it just muddle things?
