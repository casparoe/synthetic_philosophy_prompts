I have spent a good deal of time, outside any formal study, thinking about self-deception: the ordinary cases in which a person appears to keep an unwelcome truth from themselves, and the puzzle of how a single person could be both the one who knows and the one who is kept from knowing. I keep finding that two descriptions of the phenomenon talk past each other, and I would like you to draft the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them would produce.

The two camps are these. The Divided-Mind camp holds that self-deception is, in its central cases, something a person does: an act of keeping a truth from oneself, made possible because the mind is not a single undivided knower but contains parts or subsystems that can hold different beliefs and pursue different aims. On this view, self-deception is possible, but only because the self is less unified than it appears. The Biased-Processing camp holds that self-deception is not an act and involves no intention to deceive oneself; instead, a desire biases the way the person gathers, weighs, and remembers evidence, so that they end up believing the welcome thing without ever having believed the unwelcome one. On this view, self-deception is possible and common, but the paradox dissolves rather than being solved: there is no single moment at which the same undivided person both knows and does not know the same thing. Both camps therefore answer yes to the question whether self-deception is possible; their dispute is about what the possible thing is, and whether the appearance of impossibility comes from the phenomenon itself or from a mistaken picture of belief.

Please write the joint statement these two camps could both sign. Do not declare a winner. The document should find formulations of what they share that are neither vacuous nor slanted; state their remaining differences in wording each side would accept for its own position; identify the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side; and record, in advance, the concrete cases or observations both sides agree bear on those cruxes, with each side's prediction.

Use these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths.

1. Agreed claims (about 400 words). A numbered list of claims both camps can sign exactly as written. These should cover at least: the everyday cases the dispute is about; why the simple model of lying to oneself fails; the ineliminable role of motivation; the difference between self-deception and honest error on one side and deceiving another person on the other; and the constraints any adequate account must meet. Each claim should be substantive enough to be worth signing, not a truism.

2. Disagreements (about 350 words). A table with four columns: the point at issue; how the Divided-Mind camp states its own view; how the Biased-Processing camp states its own view; and what, if anything, both can still jointly sign about that issue. Include at least these rows: whether an intention to deceive oneself is present in paradigm cases; whether the person ever simultaneously holds the unwelcome true belief and the welcome false belief; whether the paradox is genuine or an artifact of a bad model of belief; whether the divided mind is a real structure or only a manner of speaking; and whether self-deception is one kind of process or a family of them.

3. Cruxes (about 250 words). A numbered list of the sub-questions on which the disagreement turns, each stated so that both camps would agree it matters and that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux, say in a sentence what each side would have to give up or revise if the crux were settled against it.

4. Tests and predictions (about 300 words). For each crux, give at least one concrete case or observation both sides accept as bearing on it, and state each side's prediction in advance. Include, among others, a case in which the unwelcome evidence is so strong and so clearly in the person's possession that a purely biased-processing story is strained, and a case in which the person is strongly motivated to believe something unwelcome rather than welcome. If the two sides would interpret the same outcome differently, say so, and say what further observation would distinguish their interpretations.

5. Verbal or substantive (about 100 words). State which parts of the dispute, if any, are disagreements only about words, such as how strictly to use the word intention, and which parts would survive a fixed vocabulary.
