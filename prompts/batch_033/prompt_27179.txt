In my seminar on Descartes, we have been working through his views on which beings possess a mind—or, in his terms, a rational soul. Our professor gave us a handout that distilled the following verdicts from the primary texts, each representing Descartes' own settled judgment about a particular kind of being.

1. **A healthy adult human being.** Possesses a mind. Thinks, reasons, and uses language in a creative, open-ended way that is not triggered by any fixed set of stimuli.
2. **A human infant.** Possesses a mind, even before it can speak or reason competently. The rational soul is present from the beginning, though its operations are initially confused.
3. **A dog.** Does not possess a mind. All of its behavior—yelping, running, seeking food—can be explained purely mechanically, without supposing any inner thought or feeling.
4. **A parrot that has been trained to utter phrases.** Does not possess a mind. The parrot’s words are not expressions of thought; they are bodily motions produced by habit, like the sounds a mechanical organ might make.
5. **A hypothetical automaton built to resemble a human being and pre-programmed to speak a few stock sentences in response to certain triggers.** Does not possess a mind. Even if the machine produced sensible words, it would not be able to arrange them appropriately in answer to everything said in its presence, which even the dullest human can do. Its lack of genuinely flexible language betrays an absence of reason, and therefore of a rational soul.

Our professor then posed a question she wants us to address in a short paper: consider a being that is not biologically human but that, in long-term, unconstrained conversation, uses language with exactly the same flexibility, appropriateness, and creativity as a typical adult human—adapting to novel topics, making jokes, asking original questions, and displaying clear understanding. (Imagine something like a highly advanced language model or an extraterrestrial person.) What would Descartes say about whether such a being has a mind? More importantly, what rule or principle best captures Descartes’ implicit criterion for mindedness, given the five verdicts above? Are there distinct candidate rules that both fit those verdicts equally well but that diverge on this new case? If so, give your best prediction about Descartes’ own verdict, with reasons for preferring one candidate over the others, and name one further case—real or imagined—that would definitively separate the rival interpretations.
