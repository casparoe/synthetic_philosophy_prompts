I'm putting together a grad seminar on democratic theory and I want to open with the boundary problem — the question of who should count as the demos, i.e., who gets a say in a given collective decision, before we even talk about how decisions get made. I keep seeing people gesture at the all-affected-interests principle or the all-subjected-to-the-laws principle and then immediately poke holes with kids, tourists, expats, permanent residents, future people. Before I assign any positive view, I want the full spec of what an adequate answer to the boundary problem actually has to do.

Write me the desiderata: the cases a theory has to classify correctly, the distinctions it isnt allowed to collapse, the constraints it has to respect, and the reason each one is a requirement. Cases I want to see at least touched: long-term noncitizen residents, citizens abroad, children, convicted felons, tourists, future people, stateless people, and people outside a state who are affected by its environmental policy. I also want you to say whether the theory owes any answer about nonhuman animals, or whether that's a different question.

On the distinctions: make clear whether a theory has to keep apart being affected by a decision, being subject to a law, and having a stake in the outcome; inclusion in the demos vs how much weight your voice gets; and constituting the demos vs the decision rule used within it. Flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste. Then tell me which pairs of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — my money's on determinacy plus the all-affected idea, but argue it — and which single requirement does the most to rule out the standard principles. Also address the bootstrapping worry directly: is it a real constraint that a theory cant just say the demos decides its own boundaries because that presupposes a demos, or is that overblown? I want the list to do the work; skip the scorecard of existing views except where it makes a requirement vivid.
