I teach an upper-level ethics seminar and I'm setting the final. I want one essay question on moral worth and acting for the right reasons. It should be answerable in about 75 minutes by someone who has done the reading, but it should not be answerable by reciting the standard views. The crux I want students stuck on is this: moral worth seems to require acting for the reason that makes the act right, but it is much less clear whether the agent's motivating reason must be identical to the right-making reason, whether it can merely coincide with it, and whether the agent has to see that reason under a moral description. The question should make them keep three things distinct: the reason for which the agent acts, the reason that actually makes the action right, and the agent's own view of why the action is right. It should also make them take a stand on a hard case: someone who does the right thing for a reason that is in fact a right-making reason, but who sincerely believes she is doing something wrong overall. Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain. For each of the five must-hit points, give the marks it is worth (four marks each) and a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong.
