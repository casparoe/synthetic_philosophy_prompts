I've been mulling over population ethics — basically, when is one world better than another given how many people exist and how well off they are. I don't have a philosophy background, I just find myself going in circles on these cases. Here's where my gut lands:

Start with a world of a billion people all flourishing. Compare it to a world with a hundred trillion people where every life is just barely above neutral — mild boredom, ocassional small pleasures, nothing awful but nothing great. My gut says the first world is better, no contest.

But now take a world of a million people living great lives. Add a hundred thousand more people whose lives are genuinly good, just not as good as the original million. The original people are unaffected. I don't think the new world is worse. It seems at least as good.

Now take a world where everyone is at wellbeing 80 out of 100. Compare it to a world with twice the population, everyone at 79. I think the second world is better — the tiny per-person drop seems worth it.

Here's the problem. If I keep making moves like that last one — slightly lower everyone's wellbeing, add more people at that level — I eventually reach the hundred-trillion barely-worth-living world from the first case. But I said that world was worse than the flourishing billion. So somethings gotta give.

Final case: ten billion happy people, versus that same world plus one extra person with a life of pure suffering. I say the world with the suffering person is worse.

I want to know what principle could be driving these verdicts. Give me exactly five different principles that could each capture a different subset of my verdicts. For each one, say which verdicts it gets right and which ones it has to reject. One sentence describing the principle, one sentence on what it saves and what it drops. No intro, no conclusion — just the five numbered items.
