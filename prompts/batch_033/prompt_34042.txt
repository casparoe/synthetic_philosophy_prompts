Question:

Explain the central disagreement between reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimonial justification. Then identify one objection that each side faces, and briefly say how a proponent of that view might reply.

Student's answer:

The reductionism/anti-reductionism debate concerns whether testimony is a basic source of epistemic justification. Reductionists, following Hume, hold that testimonial justification is reducible to other sources: a hearer is justified in believing a speaker's testimony only if they have independent reasons — from perception, memory, or inference — to think that the speaker is reliable and that the particular testimony is likely true. Anti-reductionists, following Reid, hold that testimony is a basic source of justification on a par with perception and memory: a hearer is prima facie justified in believing what they are told simply in virtue of understanding it, without needing positive reasons for the speaker's reliability.

One important nuance is that reductionism comes in global and local versions. Global reductionists hold that a hearer must have non-testimonial evidence that testimony in general is reliable. Local reductionists hold that a hearer only needs evidence about the reliability of the particular speaker and the particular circumstances. Both versions face the problem that young children clearly acquire justified beliefs through testimony yet lack the cognitive sophistication to have the required evidence. This is a serious objection to reductionism. An anti-reductionist objection is that the view licenses too much: if we are prima facie justified in believing whatever we are told, we would be justified in believing the testimony of known liars, which is absurd. The anti-reductionist can reply that the prima facie justification is defeasible — it can be defeated by evidence that the speaker is dishonest — so the objection overstates the problem.

For the anti-reductionist, the main objection is the problem of reductionism's plausibility at the individual level: anti-reductionists struggle to explain how we can rationally discriminate between trustworthy and untrustworthy speakers without appealing to background evidence, which looks like a concession to reductionism. The anti-reductionist can reply that the initial justification is default but that background evidence plays a filtering role in determining whether that justification is defeated, which is a different role than the one reductionists assign to it.
