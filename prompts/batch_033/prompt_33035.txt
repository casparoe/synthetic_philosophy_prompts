I spent my career in condensed matter physics, mostly thinking about open systems, entropy flows, and the thermodynamics of computation. I retired a few years ago and, on a whim, picked up a copy of Clark and Chalmers's paper on the extended mind thesis. I have now read it four times, and each time I swing between thinking it is obviously true and thinking it is a category mistake dressed up as a radical claim. The core idea, as I understand it, is that cognitive processes are not confined to the skull; when an external resource—a notebook, a smartphone, a set of physical symbols—is reliably coupled to an agent and plays a functional role that we would unhesitatingly call cognitive if it were implemented in neural tissue, then that resource is literally part of the cognitive system. The parity principle is the engine of the argument: if a process counts as cognitive when it happens inside the head, it should count as cognitive when it happens outside, provided it does the same work.

Here is what I want. I would like you to restate the extended mind thesis, and the parity principle in particular, using the vocabulary of thermodynamics and information theory as a physicist would deploy it: talk about system boundaries, coupling, entropy production, free energy landscapes, mutual information, coarse-graining of phase space, and the like. I want a translation that is faithful to the philosophical claims, not a parody. Then, and this is the part I am most interested in, give me the residue report. Which claims of the extended mind thesis translate cleanly into the thermodynamic vocabulary, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression at all in that vocabulary? I am especially curious about whether the thesis's reliance on functional equivalence—the parity principle—survives the translation, or whether a physicist's notion of a system, defined by the boundaries across which we track entropy and work, forces a different and perhaps sharper way of drawing the line between the agent and its environment. I want to know whether the residue points to something the philosophers are seeing that my physics vocabulary cannot capture, or whether the physics vocabulary reveals that the thesis is less precise than it appears when it is stated in the language of functional roles and cognitive kinds.

Please write your answer in continuous prose, the way you would write me a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold text.
