I am currently taking a philosophy of cognitive science course, and we have been discussing whether the internal states of trained neural networks should be understood as genuinely representational. I would like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments of a practice our readings keep alluding to but never examine directly: the methodology of contemporary neural network interpretability.

The practice looks roughly like this. Researchers train linear classifiers, called probes, on a model's hidden activations to recover properties such as truthfulness, sentiment, or syntactic category. They use activation patching, replacing or zeroing out activations at particular layers and attention heads, to see whether the model's behavior changes in systematic ways, and on that basis they report that certain components form a circuit implementing a task. They also routinely speak of directions in activation space as encoding concepts.

What theory of representation does this practice presuppose, if any? Does it require realism, the view that the model's hidden states carry genuine semantic content, or is it fully compatible with an instrumentalist account on which encoding means only that the states support reliable prediction and intervention? Reconstruct the implicit theory of representation, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the probing, patching, and circuit claims presuppose the same thing or sit uneasily together. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the methods and reporting conventions of interpretability research if the field replaced realism with a purely instrumentalist account: would a probe still be evidence of anything, and would circuit language need to be revised or abandoned?

Please write the entire response as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
