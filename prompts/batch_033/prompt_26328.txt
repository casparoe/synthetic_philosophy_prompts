A friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about chance and credence, and by the end he had me pinned with an argument I still cannot find the exit from. He started from two claims I thought I could comfortably hold together — that rational credence should defer to objective chance, and that objective chance is not some primitive metaphysical extra but is grounded in the actual pattern of events — and walked me step by step to the conclusion that you cannot consistently hold both. I have been turning it over since and I am stuck. Here is the argument as he laid it out, with the premises numbered.

(1) The Principal Principle: If you know that the objective chance of some event E is x, and you possess no inadmissible information, then your rational credence in E ought to be x.

(2) Humean Supervenience: The objective chances are not fundamental; they supervene on the total mosaic of particular, non-modal facts — roughly, the complete pattern of what actually happens throughout all of space and time.

(3) From (2): If you were to know the total mosaic, you would thereby know the objective chances.

(4) But if you know the total mosaic, you know whether E occurs, and so your rational credence in E should be either 0 or 1.

(5) Now take any case where you take yourself to know the objective chance of E. By (2), that chance is fixed by the total mosaic. So your knowledge of the chance must, it seems, ultimately rest on the total mosaic — even if only indirectly. But if your epistemic situation is ultimately grounded in facts that, if known directly, would force your credence to 0 or 1, then your credence cannot rationally be x. The Principal Principle never gets a grip.

(6) Therefore, the Principal Principle is either inapplicable in every real case (because whenever you know the chance you are in effect relying on facts that, if fully known, would be inadmissible), or it demands something impossible: that your credence be both x and 0 or 1.

(7) But the Principal Principle is not useless. We apply it routinely: I know a fair coin has chance 1/2 of landing heads, I set my credence to 1/2, and this is paradigmatically rational.

(8) So Humean Supervenience and the Principal Principle cannot both be true. One of them must be abandoned.

My friend is prepared to abandon Humean Supervenience and go primitivist about chance. I am not. I think the argument must contain a mistake, but I cannot locate it precisely, and I cannot tell whether the standard moves that save both principles — if there are any — require me to pay a price I would rather not pay. Which step or inference is the problem, why did it look compelling, and what are the main strategies for resisting the argument without giving up on either Humean Supervenience or the Principal Principle? If one of those strategies is to reformulate the Principal Principle itself, I would like to know what the reformulation says and what it sacrifices.
