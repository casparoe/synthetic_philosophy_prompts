I've been working through an argument that seems to show that expected value reasoning, if followed consistently, recommends fanatical behavior — and I cannot find the flaw. I'd like you to tell me which step is wrong and what it costs to reject it.

(1) When you face a choice under uncertainty, you should pick the action with the highest expected value: for each possible outcome, multiply its value by its probability, sum across outcomes, and choose the action with the largest sum.

(2) For any action whose expected value is finite, there exists another action with a far smaller probability of success but a far larger payoff if it succeeds, such that the second action's expected value exceeds the first's — provided only that payoffs are not bounded above.

(3) Concrete case. You have $5,000 to give. Option A: donate it to a well-run malaria charity where the evidence says it reliably saves one life. Expected value: one life saved, near-certain.

(4) Option B: spend the $5,000 on an intervention that has a one-in-a-trillion chance of preventing an engineered pandemic that would kill a hundred trillion sentient beings. Expected value: (10⁻¹²) × (10¹⁴) = 100 lives saved. Expected value reasoning says you should prefer B to A.

(5) But now iterate. Option C: a one-in-a-septillion chance of preventing a catastrophe that kills a nonillion beings. Expected value: again 100 lives saved, or more, depending on the numbers you plug in. Option D dominates C, and so on. The chain has no endpoint; for any intervention you pick, there is a more speculative one that expected value reasoning tells you to prefer.

(6) So expected value reasoning, applied consistently, directs you away from interventions with high probabilities of modest success and toward interventions with vanishingly small probabilities of incomprehensibly large success.

(7) But a decision procedure that tells you to ignore the suffering you can actually prevent, right now, with high confidence, in favor of chasing astronomically improbable cosmic payoffs is not a sane guide to action.

(8) Therefore, expected value maximization is not the correct theory of practical reason — or it must be constrained in some way that blocks the slide from (4) to (5).

I am stuck. If I reject (1), I lose the most powerful framework I have for deciding under uncertainty, and I do not know what to put in its place. If I reject (2) by imposing an upper bound on possible payoffs, I need to justify the bound without it looking arbitrary, and I need to explain why a bound should exist at all. If I reject (4) by denying that probabilities that small are meaningful, I need a principled threshold, and every threshold I can think of looks like I drew a line in the sand. If I accept (7) and bite the bullet, I end up with a decision theory that seems to license paralysis or fanaticism. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
