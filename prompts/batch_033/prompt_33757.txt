In jurisdictions with physician-assisted dying laws—Oregon's Death with Dignity Act is the canonical example—a terminally ill patient may receive a lethal prescription but must self-administer it; the physician may not administer the drug. Meanwhile, in those same jurisdictions, a physician may withdraw life-sustaining treatment (a ventilator, dialysis) knowing the patient will die, and may also administer high doses of opioids for pain relief under the doctrine of double effect, even when those doses are likely to hasten death. So we have three practices sitting side by side: (1) a prohibition on physician-administered lethal injection, (2) permission for patient self-administered lethal medication, and (3) permission for physician-conducted withdrawal of life support and palliative sedation that may hasten death. What theory of action, responsibility, or moral significance does this pattern of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—what distinctions does it need to draw, and why? Is the theory coherent across the three practices, or do they embed inconsistent commitments? And what would the law have to change if we replaced the presupposed theory with a straightforward consequentialist one, or with one that rejects the act/omission distinction entirely?
