So at an EA meetup last week two people got into it about population ethics and I've been chewing on it since. The argument went roughly like this:

Sam kicked it off by saying total utilitarianism is dead on arrival because it implies the repugnant conclusion — that a vast population of people with lives barely worth living is better than a smaller population of people flourishing, just because the total sum of welfare is larger. He said anyone who doesn't find that repugnant has lost the plot.

Mira pushed back: "What exactly is supposed to be repugnant about it? If each of those lives is net positive — and 'barely worth living' means exactly that — then adding more net-positive lives is making the world better. I think people are just squeamish about huge numbers of unglamorous lives, but that's an aesthetic reaction, not a moral objection."

Sam said no, the point is that lives barely worth living involve a ton of suffering barely offset by just enough pleasure to scrape above zero. The claim that we should prefer a world saturated with that kind of existence over a world of genuine flourishing is what's repugnant — not the big number itself.

Mira said: "Fine, but then you need an alternative theory. If you switch to average utilitarianism to avoid it, you get the conclusion that two people in ecstasy is better than billions of very happy people, which is arguably even weirder. What's your view?"

Sam said he's drawn to critical-level utilitarianism: there's a threshold of well-being, and only lives above it count positively toward the total. Lives below the threshold don't count, even if they're net positive for the person living them. That blocks the repugnant conclusion without collapsing into averaging.

Mira said that just moves the problem: critical-level views imply that adding a life just below the threshold makes the world worse from the impartial perspective, even though the person would be glad to exist. "You're saying their life is net-negative from the universe's point of view. That's a huge bullet to bite."

Sam said every population axiology bites some bullet somewhere, and the repugnant conclusion is the one bullet he's least willing to bite. Mira said she honestly finds the repugnant conclusion less bullet-like than Sam's critical-level implications, and the whole thing stalled out.

I couldn't sort out who had the better of it. Can you adjudicate move by move — say who's right about what, flag any moves that are clearly mistaken, and give an overall score? Also, is there a view neither of them brought up that handles the repugnant conclusion better than either total utilitarianism or critical-level views?
