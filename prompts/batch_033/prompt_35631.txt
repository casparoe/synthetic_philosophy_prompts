I teach a course on care ethics, and a student of mine keeps pushing this analogy: moral reasoning, she says, is basically like a parent attending to an infant — you're not applying a rule, you're perceiving a need, and the right response is whatever fits that particular relationship in that moment. From this, she draws the conclusion that ethics is fundamentally about cultivated attention to particular others, and that universal principles (Kantian, utilitarian, whatever) are at best crude shortcuts and at worst distortions that blind you to what's actually morally salient.

I'm sympathetic, but I worry the analogy is doing too much of the argument for her. I'd like you to audit it properly. You're a professional philosopher who works on care ethics — think it through carefully, maybe start by brainstorming what the analogy is supposed to buy you, then lay out the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to the parent, the child, the history of the relationship, the kind of attention involved, the asymmetry of need, and so on. Then walk me through where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports features that don't generalize to the moral case — the radical dependency of the infant, the fact that the parent has near-total authority and responsibility, or the way the parent's own interests are largely set aside. And give me the bottom line: once the disanalogies are on the table, does her conclusion about universal prinicples survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If there's a better analogy that captures what she's after without the baggage, I'd like that too.
