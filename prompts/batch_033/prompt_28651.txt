Had a long argument with my roommate about utilitarianism the other day — she took a moral philosophy class and came back convinced Williams basically demolished the whole tradition. I asked her to explain his objection, and this is roughly what she said (I wrote it down after, so the wording is mine but the claims are hers):

"Williams's integrity objection is about the drowning wife case. A guy sees two people drowning, his wife and a stranger, and he can only save one. A utilitarian has to think about it — he has to caluclate whether saving his wife really produces the most overall utility. And Williams says that calculation is 'one thought too many.' If you actually love your wife, you shouldn't even consider the stranger as a serious option; the fact that utilitarianism requires you to run the numbers at all shows it can't handle genuine love and commitment. That's the integrity objection: utilitarianism alienates you from your own feelings, because it makes you treat your wife and a stranger as interchangeable units in a cost-benefit analysis."

I buy that this is directionally right, but something about it feels a little too clean. I've heard that Williams's point isn't exactly about calculating, and that the integrity objection is about something bigger than romantic love. What in her version would Williams accept, what would he push back on, and does her conclusion — that utilitarianism can't handle genuine love — actually follow from the parts that are accurate?
