At a recent department colloquium, two of my colleagues became entangled in a dispute about G.E. Moore's 'proof of an external world' that lasted through the drinks reception and showed no signs of resolving itself by the time I left. I have my own views, but I found myself genuinely unable to adjudicate several of their exchanges, and I should welcome a careful analysis.

The argument began when Hendricks remarked that Moore's proof — holding up one hand, then the other, and concluding that external objects exist — is not a proof at all but a piece of philosophical theatre. Carroway objected that this dismissal is too quick: Moore satisfies the formal conditions of a proof (a valid deduction from premises he knew to be true), so if it fails, the failure must be subtler than Hendricks was allowing.

(1) Hendricks: The proof is question-begging. The premise 'here is a hand' already asserts the existence of an external object, which is precisely what the sceptic denies. You cannot settle a dispute by simply reasserting one side of it in the premise.

(2) Carroway: That conflates two things. A premise can entail the conclusion without the argument being question-begging — otherwise every valid deduction would beg the question. Moore's premise does not contain the conclusion as a constituent; it entails it only together with the suppressed premise that a hand is an external object. That suppressed premise is a conceptual truth, not what is at issue. The sceptic does not deny that hands, if they exist, are external objects; the sceptic denies that we know hands exist at all. So Moore is not assuming the very thing in dispute.

(3) Hendricks: That merely relocates the problem. If the sceptic grants the conceptual claim that hands are external objects, then accepting Moore's premise just is accepting that at least one external object exists. You cannot rationally accept 'here is a hand' while withholding 'external objects exist' — they are not psychologically separable in the way Carroway's distinction requires. So the proof offers no leverage: either you already accept the conclusion, or you reject the premise. There is no neutral ground from which the argument can move you.

(4) Carroway: That is a psychological claim, not a logical one. But set that aside. The real point, which Moore himself made, is that the proof displays the structure of our epistemic situation: we are more certain of particular propositions like 'here is a hand' than we are of any philosophical premise that could be adduced in favour of scepticism. The proof is not meant to persuade the sceptic by his own lights; it is meant to show that the sceptic's lights are dimmer than ordinary ones. The argument works in reverse, as it were: it invites us to see that if a valid argument from a premise we know to its conclusion fails to transmit warrant, the blame lies not with the premise but with some defective theory about what knowledge or proof requires.

(5) Hendricks: Then it is not a proof; it is a refusal to engage. If the only criterion for 'knowing the premise' is that we ordinarily take ourselves to know such things, then the entire dispute has been settled by fiat in Moore's favour. The sceptic, by definition, questions whether we know such things. Telling him we are more certain of them than we are of his arguments is not an answer — it is simply restating the disagreement. A 'proof' that only convinces those who already accept its conclusion on other grounds is exactly what a question-begging argument is.

At this point they began going in circles, and I excused myself. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it is substantially correct or mistaken, and why. Where a move contains both insight and error, separate them. Finally, say whether there is a way of understanding Moore's proof that escapes both of their criticisms, or whether the exchange reveals that the proof is, in the end, a failure.
