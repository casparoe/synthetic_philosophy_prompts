I'm not a philosopher, but I've been reading around in moral philosophy lately because of a decision I had to make, and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for. Last month I was choosing between two jobs: one teaching high school in a city I love, the other a well-paid remote position that would let me stay close to my aging parents. After weeks of going back and forth, I realized I had all the facts I was ever going to get, and it still didn't seem true that either job was better than the other. But saying they were equally good also felt wrong. If one of the jobs had been improved just a little — a slightly higher salary, say, or a shorter commute — I don't think I would have suddenly judged it the better choice. It would have just been a slightly different version of the same option, still not ranked above the other one. I've since noticed the same thing in arguements among friends about whether to have kids, and in public debates about whether to spend limited money on healthcare or on education. It doesn't feel like people are simply missing information or being bad at weighing; it feels like the options don't sit on a single scale in the first place. I did eventually choose, but I still can't say whether the choice was justified, and that bothers me.

Is there an established concept for this, and is it really a different thing from ordinary uncertainty? I don't just want a label — I want to know how philosophers carve this up: is there a third relation besides better, worse, and equal, or is that idea confused? And if there is, what would it mean for how we should actually choose in situations like this? Please write your answer in American English.
