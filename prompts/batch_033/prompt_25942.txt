I keep seeing people in econ and policy circles use the Ellsberg paradox to argue that ambiguity aversion is irrational — the idea is that if you'd pay a premium to avoid the unknown-proportion urn over the 50/50 urn, you're making a mistake, and by analogy, real-world decisions that favor known risks over unknown ones (investing domestically vs. abroad, sticking with familiar tech, etc.) are similarly irrational. I want to know how far the urn analogy actually carries. What maps cleanly from the Ellsberg setup to real-world ambiguity, and where does the mapping break? In particular, I'm interested in whether the features that make ambiguity aversion look irrational in the lab — like the fact that the unknown urn is objectively no worse in expected value — are actually present in the real-world cases the analogy is being used to criticize. And if the analogy breaks in ways that matter, does the conclusion (that real-world ambiguity aversion is a bias) survive, or was the analogy doing all the work?
