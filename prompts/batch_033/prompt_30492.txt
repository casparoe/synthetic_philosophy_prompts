I've been teaching a unit on epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment, and I've noticed something frustrating about the literature: nearly every case that's supposed to show these two views coming apart works by giving the subject and the attributor different practical stakes. The subject is in a low-stakes situation while the attributor is in a high-stakes one, or vice versa, and the theories diverge because pragmatic encroachment tracks the subject's stakes while contextualism tracks the attributor's. But that is not a very deep difference—it just shows that the two theories locate the "practical pressure" on different people. What I want is a case where the real structural difference between the views does the work: the fact that contextualism makes knowledge ascriptions sensitive to conversational factors (which error possibilities have been raised, what is salient in the discourse) while pragmatic encroachment cares only about the subject's practical situation, full stop.

So here is the specification. Construct a thought experiment in which pragmatic encroachment and attributor epistemic contextualism deliver opposite verdicts about whether an attributor's utterance of "S does not know that p" is true. Specifically, I want pragmatic encroachment to say that S does know that p, while contextualism says the attributor speaks truly in denying that S knows. Constraints: the scenario must be an ordinary workplace or domestic setting—a lab, a kitchen, a workshop, nothing involving courtrooms, surgeons, or ticking bombs—no longer than a single paragraph, and, this is the key constraint, the subject S and the attributor must face identical practical stakes. The divergence must come from a conversational or salience asymmetry, not from who has more to lose.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. First, walk through what pragmatic encroachment says about whether S knows and why, making explicit reference to the subject's stakes and evidence. Second, walk through what attributor contextualism says about whether the attributor's denial of knowledge is true and why, referencing the attributor's conversational context and the epistemic standards it sets. Third, identify the specific feature of the case that makes the two views peel apart—be precise about which element of the scenario each view is keying on, and why that element registers for one theory but not the other. Fourth, remove that feature and tell me what happens: does the case collapse into a standard stakes-driven divergence between the two views, or does some other structural difference remain? Fifth, take the side of whichever view comes out looking worse in your case and mount the best defense you can: how would a proponent of that view resist your analysis, and what would they need to add or amend in their view to handle the case?
