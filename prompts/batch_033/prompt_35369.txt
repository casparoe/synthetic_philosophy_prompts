I'm a novelist working on a character study, and I keep noticing a pair of patterns in how people's traits play out — or fail to — that I don't have good language for. I'm hoping philosophy has mapped this terrain.

First pattern: a person has a stable disposition — say, a genuine short temper. The usual trigger arrives (an insult, a frustration), but instead of the disposition manifesting, the trigger itself removes the disposition, so there's nothing to manifest. Not suppression, not self-control; the disposition is simply absent in that moment, though the person hasn't changed. A concrete case: a man is about to lose his temper at a colleague, but the colleague, sensing it, says something so unexpectedly generous that the anger evaporates — not overridden, but gone, as though the trigger destroyed the disposition it was supposed to activate.

Second pattern: the disposition is present, the trigger is present, but something in the environment blocks the manifestation without touching the disposition. The glass is fragile, it's struck, but it's wrapped in packing material and doesn't break. The fragility is still there; it just can't express itself.

I want to know: do these two patterns have established names in the philosophical literature on dispositions? How are they distinguished from each other and from the simpler case where the disposition is merely absent? And — this is the part I most want to get right for my character work — what is the correct way to think about whether the disposition exists during the interval when it's blocked or removed? I'm not satisfied with bare labels; I want the carving: what each phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where the boundary cases lie. Please also say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
