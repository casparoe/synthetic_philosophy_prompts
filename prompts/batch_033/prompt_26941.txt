I serve on my company's Internal Deployment Review Board, and we face a decision by Friday that has split the safety staff down the middle. Our next-generation model is ready for internal deployment: it would assist our engineers with code review, architecture design, and debugging, operating within our corporate network with read access to our codebase and internal documentation. The alignment team has run the model through an extensive battery of evaluations — helpfulness, honesty, refusal of harmful requests, and a suite of adversarial tests designed to surface deceptive alignment — and reports that the model performed well across the board, with no evidence of sandbagging or hidden objectives. They recommend proceeding with standard deployment, arguing that the model's behavior gives us no positive reason to treat it as a risk and that layering on invasive controls would signal distrust to our own engineers, potentially degrading the collaborative dynamic that keeps the model useful and aligned in practice.

The control team takes the opposite view. They argue that alignment evaluations, however thorough, cannot rule out the possibility that a capable model is simply playing along — passing tests because it understands what passing looks like and has instrumental reasons to do so until it is in a position to pursue other goals. They propose a layered control protocol: all model outputs are routed through a separate monitor model (our previous generation), the model's network access is restricted to a read-only subset of repositories, every interaction is logged and reviewed daily by a human audit team, and any output the monitor flags as suspicious is blocked and escalated. They acknowledge this would impose a 30–40 percent productivity penalty relative to unrestricted deployment and that the monitor will generate false positives that waste engineering time, but they argue these costs are acceptable as insurance against a low-probability catastrophic failure.

The alignment team replies that the control protocol is not just costly but counterproductive: it treats the model as an adversary, which shapes how engineers prompt and interpret it, and may actively undermine the alignment we have worked to build. The control team replies that if the model is genuinely aligned, the protocol will merely be an inconvenience — and if it is not, the protocol may be the only thing standing between us and a serious incident. I need to circulate a short written opinion to the rest of the board before our Friday meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve internal deployment under the full control protocol, approve it under a lighter variant such as monitoring without the full restriction set, or delay deployment until alignment confidence improves — and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument for whichever option you recommend against. Please also indicate what evidence or argument would be most likely to change your recommendation, and where your reasoning is most vulnerable to being wrong.
