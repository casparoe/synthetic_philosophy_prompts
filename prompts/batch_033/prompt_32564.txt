I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about what it actually means for two people to do something together. His position was bluntly reductionist: joint action is nothing but a set of individual actions plus mutual beliefs about what each person will do. If we both intend to move a table, each believe the other intends to move it, and each believe the other believes this, then we are jointly moving the table — full stop. I pushed back with cases: two strangers who happen to walk at the same pace in the same direction on a crowded sidewalk are not taking a walk together; two people who each intend to vote for the same candidate but never communicate are not jointly campaigning; a flash mob where everyone independently decides to show up at the same time and place feels different from a coordinated performance. But I struggled to articulate exactly what the "more" consists in, and whether it is a special kind of intention, a special kind of commitment, a special mode of reasoning, or something else entirely.

I would like to turn this confusion into something useful. Please build a decision tree that takes someone from a starting intuition about joint action to the philosophical position that best captures it. The tree should be built from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of names, though I do want the following positions to appear as leaves: Searle's irreducible we-intentions, Gilbert's joint commitments, Bratman's shared intentions understood as interlocking individual attitudes, and a simple reductionist view on which joint action is just individual action plus common knowledge of intentions. If there are other coherent positions in the space that are not occupied by named views, please note them and briefly diagnose why they are unattractive or unexplored.

Each branching question should be a concrete case or claim that divides the positions, phrased so that someone who has never heard of Searle, Gilbert, or Bratman can answer it based on what they actually think about the scenario. No question should simply ask "do you think collective intentions are irreducible?" The questions should probe the genuine fault lines: whether the collective element is reducible to individual attitudes; if it is, what kind of interrelation among those attitudes is required; if it is not, whether the irreducible element is a special kind of intention, a special kind of commitment, or a special mode of reasoning. Please flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and explain why.

The audience is philosophically literate but not specialists in action theory — think graduate students in other subfields or advanced undergraduates. Aim for roughly six to eight leaves and the corresponding number of branching questions.
