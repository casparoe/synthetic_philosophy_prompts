Having spent my career in molecular biology, I have only recently turned my attention to philosophy of mind, and I find myself both fascinated and unsettled by the eliminative materialist position advanced by Paul and Patricia Churchland. The claim, as I understand it, is that our commonsense psychological framework—the network of beliefs, desires, hopes, fears, and intentions we use to navigate social life—is not a source of fundamental insight but rather a stagnant, empirically inadequate theory, one that will eventually be displaced by a mature neuroscience in much the way that phlogiston theory was displaced by oxygen-based chemistry. When I first encountered this view, I confess I found it almost self-refuting: surely the very act of arguing for eliminative materialism presupposes that one holds beliefs and intends to persuade. But as I have read further, I have come to appreciate that the eliminativist has resources to deflect this charge, and that the deeper question is whether folk psychology exhibits the kind of explanatory and predictive success we should expect of a theory that carves nature at its joints—or whether it systematically fails in ways that suggest its central posits will go the way of caloric fluid and the luminiferous ether.

I would be grateful for a dialogue between an eliminative materialist and a defender of folk-psychological realism that explores this tension in a substantive way. I am particularly interested in seeing them grapple with the following: the eliminativist's claim that folk psychology has made no significant theoretical progress in over two millennia, in stark contrast to the sciences; the realist's rejoinder that folk psychology is not a scientific theory at all but a practical framework whose success lies in navigating everyday social interaction, not in explaining the neural substrate; the eliminativist's further point that folk psychology fails to explain mental illness, sleep, memory consolidation, and a host of other phenomena that a mature neuroscience handles with increasing precision; and the realist's concern that if we abandon belief-talk, we lose the very conceptual resources needed to make sense of rational argumentation, deliberation, and even the scientific enterprise itself. Both characters should be equally competent and well-read, and neither should serve as a strawman for the other. I would like to see genuine back-and-forth in which each interlocutor responds directly to the other's actual points, rather than a pair of monologues that happen to be interleaved.
