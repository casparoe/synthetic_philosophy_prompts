I am trying to get my views on Bayesian confirmation into a coherent shape, and I would like a consistency audit rather than a survey. Here is the package I currently hold:

1. Subjective Bayesianism: rational degrees of belief are probabilities, and updating proceeds by conditionalization.
2. Positive relevance: a piece of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H just in case P(H | E) > P(H).
3. Old evidence sometimes confirms: a known fact can genuinely confirm a newly introduced theory — the long-known anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, for example, confirmed general relativity when Einstein proposed it.
4. Anti-counterfactualism about old evidence: I do not think the right response is to imagine a prior state in which the evidence was not known and ask what it would have confirmed there. Conditionalizing on a proposition one already assigns probability 1 is a no-op, and I doubt there is any principled way to set aside a known fact and recover the credence one would have had without it.
5. The logical-connection diagnosis: in cases of old evidence, what actually does the work is that the scientist learns something new — typically that the theory entails the old evidence — and it is this newly learned relation, not the old evidence itself, that bears the confirmatory weight.

My question is whether (1)–(5) form a stable package. Please go through the commitments and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and say in each case what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. I cannot tell where the strain is, so do not assume I already know which claim should go.
