I'm TAing an epistemology course this semester and we just spent two lectures on Fitch's paradox of knowability. The students can follow the proof fine — they get that if you assume all truths are knowable and theres at least one unknown truth, you can derive a contradiction using the sentence "p and no one knows that p." But I don't think they've really *felt* the paradox, you know? The way two totally reasonable-sounding claims just can't coexist, and the proof feels almost like a trick but isn't.

For the last discussion section I want to give them something different, something that gets at the shape of the thing rather than the formalism. Could you write a villanelle about Fitch's paradox? Here's what I'm picturing: the two refrains should be the two claims that generate the contradiction — something like "all truths are knowable" and "some truth remains unknown" (you can phrase them more poetically, obviously). The villanelle form is perfect for this because the refrains keep circling back and taking turns, just like the paradox itself — the two claims seem to peacefully alternate through the stanzas until the final couplet forces them together and you realize they can't both be true. The turn, where the refrains finally meet in the last two lines, should do the structural work of showing the contradiction.

It doesn't need to teach the proof — they already have that. But someone who knows the paradox should recognize exactly what's happening in the poem's form, and someone who doesn't should still find it a decent poem. Don't just name-drop Fitch or the paradox; let the form carry the idea. Oh and I'm not a poetry person so I don't have strong opinions about meter, but try to make it scan alright.
