I've been working through Amartya Sen's impossibility result on the Paretian liberal for a political philosophy seminar, and I've reconstructed the argument in a way that strikes me as deeply troubling — not just because the result is surprising, but because the chain of reasoning seems airtight and yet the conclusion feels like it proves far too much. I'm hoping someone can identify exactly where the argument goes wrong, or else tell me which premise I should be prepared to abandon and what the collateral damage is.

Here is the argument as I understand it, laid out step by step. (1) A social choice rule should respect at least a minimal form of individual liberty: there should be at least one person and at least one pair of social alternatives — say, whether that person reads a certain book or not — such that the person's preference between those two alternatives is decisive for the social ranking, regardless of what anyone else wants. Call this Minimal Liberalism. (2) A social choice rule should also respect the Weak Pareto Principle: if every individual in the society strictly prefers alternative X to alternative Y, then the social ranking should rank X above Y. (3) Social preference should be transitive — if society ranks A above B and B above C, it must rank A above C — since otherwise social choice can cycle and no stable outcome is guaranteed. (4) Sen constructs cases (the famous Lady Chatterley's Lover example, or the one with the prude and the lewd) where Minimal Liberalism assigns decisive rights to two different individuals over two different pairs of alternatives, and where the individuals' preferences happen to align so that, when both exercise their rights, the resulting social ranking — combined with the Weak Pareto Principle — yields a cycle: A is ranked above B (by one person's right), B above C (by Pareto), and C above A (by the other person's right). (5) So any social choice rule satisfying Minimal Liberalism, Weak Pareto, and transitivity leads to contradiction when preferences take a certain (perfectly possible) configuration. (6) Therefore at least one of the three must go. (7) Weak Pareto seems almost definitional of respecting individual preferences — it just says unanimity should prevail. (8) Transitivity seems definitional of rational choice — without it, the very idea of a "social preference" unravels. (9) That leaves Minimal Liberalism as the one to abandon. (10) But Minimal Liberalism is already about as thin a conception of individual rights as one could imagine — it only asks that at least one person get to decide at least one personal matter. If even that must be sacrificed, then the very idea that a society can guarantee any sphere of personal liberty while also being responsive to unanimous preferences and rationally consistent appears to be a kind of mirage.

The conclusion seems to prove too much. It suggests that no coherent social decision procedure can simultaneously respect even a shred of individual rights, respect unanimous preferences, and be rational. But that can't be right — or if it is right, it's a much more radical result than most people who cite Sen seem to acknowledge. I want to know: which step in this argument is the one to reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Is there an equivocation somewhere in how "rights" or "preferences" are being formalized? Is transitivity being asked to do too much? Or is the Weak Pareto Principle less innocent than it looks once we notice that the "unanimous" preferences in Sen's cases are other-regarding preferences — the prude wants to censor what the lewd reads, and vice versa — and perhaps Pareto should only apply to self-regarding preferences? If that's the fix, does it carve back the Pareto principle so far that it loses its intuitive force, or does it open a different can of worms about how to classify preferences as self- versus other-regarding? I'm less interested in which premise people happen to find least intuitive and more interested in a precise diagnosis of the argument's structure: which move, exactly, is the mistake, and what else breaks when you fix it.
