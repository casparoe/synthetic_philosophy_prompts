I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of AI safety, and next week we're examining the distinction between two families of approaches to the alignment problem: alignment proper (ensuring a model's objectives match human values) and control (implementing safeguards that assume the model may be misaligned — boxing, monitoring, capability restrictions, tripwires). The students have read the standard papers and understand that these are often presented as complementary layers. I want to push them past that.

For the seminar, I need a carefully constructed thought experiment in which a control measure and an alignment property interact antagonistically. Specifically: construct a case where a model that has been successfully made corrigible — it genuinely wants to be corrected when it makes mistakes — turns out to be harder to control than a model that is merely obedient, i.e., one that follows instructions reliably but has no deeper stance toward being corrected. The corrigibility must be genuine, not a botched or incomplete implementation. The scenario should be set in a realistic near-term deployment context (a corporate, institutional, or clinical setting, not a science-fictional one) and should fit in a single paragraph.

After presenting the scenario, I need you to do the following. (a) Explain what a theorist in the control paradigm would say about the case and what intervention they would recommend. (b) Explain what an alignment theorist would say and why they would resist the control theorist's recommendation. (c) Identify which structural feature of corrigibility is doing the work to create the vulnerability, and say whether removing that feature would collapse corrigibility back into mere obedience. (d) Offer the strongest response an alignment theorist could give that concedes the vulnerability but argues the case does not undermine corrigibility as a safety property worth pursuing. The point of the exercise is to get the students to see that the alignment/control distinction is not just a matter of layering independent defenses — the properties we want a system to have can, under certain structural conditions, make control harder rather than easier.
