I've been thinking about a puzzle in how we judge whether evidence supports a theory, and I'd like to see it turned into a concrete scenario.

Here's the issue. Suppose you have a mathematical rule for updating your beliefs: you take your prior confidence in a hypothesis, multiply by how probable the evidence would be if the hypothesis were true, and divide by how probable the evidence is overall. That rule seems to imply that if you already knew the evidence before you ever formulated the hypothesis, the evidence can't shift your confidence at all — because the probability of something you already know is 1, and multiplying and dividing by 1 leaves you where you started. But that doesn't match how we actually reason. Some of the best-confirmed theories in history earned their status by explaining facts we'd known for decades or centuries.

I want a thought experiment built to isolate this tension. Construct a case with two investigators who agree on three things: how plausible the hypothesis is before considering the evidence, how likely the evidence would be if the hypothesis were true, and how likely the evidence would be if the hypothesis were false. Despite this agreement, one investigator concludes the evidence strongly confirms the hypothesis, while the other concludes it provides no confirmation whatsoever. The only source of their disagreement should be that the evidence was already known before the hypothesis was proposed.

Constraints: use an ordinary setting — a medical diagnosis, a criminal investigation, a factory quality-control problem, or something similar. No exotic physics, no science-fiction elements. The hypothesis and the evidence should be understandable to someone with no background in statistics or formal logic.

After presenting the scenario, walk through each investigator's reasoning step by step, showing exactly where their treatments come apart. Identify which feature of the standard updating rule creates the divergence. Then tell me what someone who thinks this whole problem is a confusion — someone who believes the updating rule handles old evidence without any special modification — would say is the mistake in the investigator who finds no confirmation. Does that reply actually resolve the tension, or does it just relocate it?
