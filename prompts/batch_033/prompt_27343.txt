I have no formal training in any of this, but for a while I have been working through an idea on my own: that people with deeply opposed value systems can still make deals that leave everyone better off, not by compromising on their values, but by trading concessions — each side gives ground on what it ranks lower in order to gain what it ranks higher, so that the outcome is better for each by its own lights rather than a midpoint between them. I want to stress-test that idea against the Oslo peace process of the 1990s, because Oslo looks like a case where such a trade was actually attempted and then unraveled, and I cannot tell whether that counts for the idea or against it.

Here is the record as I understand it. In 1993, Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization exchanged letters of mutual recognition and signed an interim agreement, the Declaration of Principles. The PLO accepted Israel's right to exist and renounced violence, committing to resolve the conflict by negotiation; Israel recognized the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people. The agreement created a Palestinian Authority with limited self-government, beginning in Gaza and Jericho and later extending over parts of the West Bank, while the hardest issues — Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, and final borders — were deferred to final-status talks. Over the following years the process collapsed: Rabin was assassinated in 1995 by a right-wing Israeli opposed to the accords, the Camp David summit in 2000 failed, and the Second Intifada broke out shortly after.

Analyze Oslo through the gains-from-trade picture of moral compromise. Before reaching any verdict, say what that picture claims the parties faced and should have done: what each side stood to gain and lose, which values each had to rank lower, and what a rational deal would have contained. Then do the careful mapping. Who were the parties whose value rankings actually constrained the outcome — the two leaderships, their domestic constituencies, the settler movement, the Palestinian diaspora, the armed factions? What did each actually concede, and did those concessions really trade lower-ranked values for higher-ranked ones, or is that description forced? Then say where the picture explains the shape of the process well and where it has to distort or omit: the phasing and security coordination built on monitoring rather than trust, the spoilers on both sides who worked to kill the deal, and the possibility that some stakes, such as control of Jerusalem or the refugee right of return, were treated as beyond exchange rather than merely ranked low. Finally run the analysis in the other direction. Does Oslo confirm that moral compromise as gains from trade is a real possibility in conflicts of this kind, or does the failure show that the picture mistakes a temporary alignment of interests for a genuine compromise, or that it is silent about exactly the things that made the process fall apart? Where precisely does the model break, and what would a better account of Oslo need to include that this one cannot?
