I help run a small grantmaking program at an animal-welfare organization focused on farmed aquatic animals, and I am preparing our next two-year research funding round. I have spent years reading rationalist and effective-altruism discussion of invertebrate sentience, welfare ranges, and cause prioritization, so I am comfortable with expected-value reasoning; after one too many conversations in which the question whether shrimp feel pain dissolved into citation-slinging, I have come to suspect that at least part of the bottleneck is conceptual rather than purely empirical. Deciding what should count as evidence of valenced experience in animals with nervous systems unlike a mammal's, and deciding how to weigh welfare across very different species, seem to me upstream of a lot of expensive data collection. But I want that model stress-tested, not assumed.

The round can support about eight researchers for two to three years, and I want a defended agenda rather than a survey. The kinds of open questions I have in mind include: what kind and strength of evidence should move our credence that teleost fish or decapod crustaceans have valenced experience, given how unlike mammalian brains their nervous systems are; how to compare welfare across species with different life histories, body sizes, and numbers; whether farmed-fish welfare is best understood in terms of felt affect, natural behavior, or health and functioning, and what that implies for what farms and certification schemes should actually measure; and which intervention lever — slaughter and stunning methods, stocking density, water quality, enrichment, or something else — is most held back by unresolved conceptual questions rather than by engineering or cost. I need the ranking as a skeleton, but the reasoning is what carries the decision.

Answer as someone who works seriously at the boundary of animal welfare science and philosophy of mind, and who can give a committed, self-critical recommendation. I want you to take a definite position and defend it, not present a menu of options. Before settling on a ranking, briefly brainstorm the candidate questions and the criteria you will use, and say explicitly where your confidence is lowest; then commit. Structure your answer in these named sections, with roughly these lengths: (1) "The ranking" — your ordered list of the three to five questions you would fund first, with one sentence of justification each (~150 words); (2) "Criteria and dependencies" — the explicit criteria you used (importance, tractability, neglectedness, how many other questions depend on this one) and which candidate questions are upstream of which (~250 words); (3) "The case for the top two" — why these specifically are worth the money, and what a two-to-three-year project could concretely deliver (~300 words); (4) "What I would deprioritize" — the apparently urgent question you would deliberately underfund, and why (~150 words); (5) "Tripwires" — what evidence over the next two years would move each of your top two up or down the list (~150 words).
