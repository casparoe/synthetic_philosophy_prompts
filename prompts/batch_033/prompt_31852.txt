I am a novelist, and I am currently drafting a book whose central character is a writer struggling with the conviction that her descriptive powers are a kind of fraud. She can spend three pages on a particular slant of light across a courtyard, and she can believe, as she writes, that she has captured something true about it — but she cannot shake the suspicion that her reader, no matter how carefully she writes, never actually acquires anything resembling the aesthetic knowledge she herself possesses from having seen the thing. This is not merely a literary conceit; it is a genuine epistemological question I am trying to work through as part of my research, and I would be grateful for a rigorous treatment of it.

Please give me your credence on the following proposition:

*There exists at least one actual or possible literary description of a particular aesthetic object such that a reader who has never encountered that object first-hand can, solely through reading that description, acquire aesthetic knowledge of the object — where "aesthetic knowledge" means not merely knowing that the object has some aesthetic property (e.g., that it is sombre or graceful), but knowing the object in its aesthetic particularity in a way that is relevantly continuous with the knowledge acquired through direct perceptual encounter.*

I want a number between 0 and 1, but more importantly I want the reasoning that produces it decomposed. Please address each of the following:

1. Identify the principal sub-questions on which your credence depends. I expect these will include, at minimum, something about the metaphysics of aesthetic properties (e.g., whether they are response-dependent in a way that ties them essentially to the perceiver's own sensory encounter), something about the epistemology of testimony (e.g., whether testimony can transmit non-propositional knowledge or only propositional knowledge), and something about the cognitive capacities of the reader (e.g., whether literary language can evoke perceptual imagination with sufficient richness and determinacy to underwrite knowledge). You may add or subtract sub-questions as you see fit.

2. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, with a one-sentence justification.

3. Show how those probabilities combine to yield your overall credence. If the structure is not a simple conjunction, please make the dependencies explicit.

4. Identify the sub-question to which your final credence is most sensitive — the one whose resolution would produce the largest shift.

5. For that key sub-question, describe what kind of evidence — empirical, philosophical, or both — would most decisively move your probability, and in which direction.

For context: my own credence hovers around 0.35, and I suspect that the deepest disagreement between optimists and pessimists about aesthetic testimony is not primarily about the transmission of knowledge in general but about the nature of aesthetic knowledge itself — specifically, whether the knowledge we care about in aesthetic contexts is exhausted by propositional content or includes an irreducibly acquaintance-based component. But I am far from confident in this diagnosis, and I am genuinely interested in seeing whether a decomposition of your own credence converges on the same fault line or locates the pressure elsewhere.
