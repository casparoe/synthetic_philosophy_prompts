I work as a software engineer on systems that use large language models to act on a user's behalf: drafting and sending messages, agreeing to terms, booking things, issuing refunds, apologizing to customers. I keep running into the question of whether any of that is really the system promising, agreeing, or apologizing, or whether the model is merely producing text that looks like those acts. I have no formal background in philosophy, and the most useful thing I have found for thinking about this is J. L. Austin's How to Do Things with Words — in particular his distinction between the locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary dimensions of an utterance, his account of the ways a performative can go wrong, and his idea that certain utterances are only happy if they secure uptake from the addressee. Austin died in 1960, long before large language models existed, so he never had to answer this question directly. I would like you to reconstruct, as carefully and faithfully as you can, what Austin would have said about the speech-act status of LLM-generated utterances, and to flag both the places where his framework extends cleanly and the places where it would have to be stretched or where he might have refused the question.

Specifically: would Austin say the model itself performs an illocutionary act, or that it performs only the locutionary act while the user or the deploying company supplies the illocutionary force, or that the whole situation is a derivative or etiolated use of language of the kind he set aside when he discussed actors on stage and words in poems? Given that his conditions for a happy performative include the right persons following the right procedure and, for acts like promising or apologizing, the speaker having the appropriate intentions or feelings, would an LLM's output count as a misfire, as an abuse, or as not a performative at all — or would Austin locate the intentions somewhere else, such as in the user who prompted the model or in the operator who deployed it? How much weight would he give to the fact that the recipient often treats the message as a promise and acts on it? And would he think the relevant speaker is the user, the company, the model, or some distributed combination, and does that question matter for whether the act is felicitous?

I am not asking for a survey of what later writers have said about speech acts and computers, and I do not need citations. I want a single, internally consistent reconstruction of Austin's likely position, built out of distinctions he actually drew, with an honest accounting of the points where a faithful application of his framework is genuinely indeterminate.
