I'm giving the keynote address at a conference called "Beyond the Party: Democratic Representation in the Twenty-First Century." The audience is mixed: political theorists, current and former elected officials, journalists, and a fair number of politically engaged citizens. Six months ago, after fourteen years as a state legislator, I left my party and became an independent. My departure was covered in the press mostly as a story about factional infighting, and I want to use this speech to give a more principled account. I've been reading Simone Weil's "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (1943), and her core argument — that political parties are structurally incapable of serving truth or justice because their constitutive aim is collective power, not deliberation — has helped me articulate what I couldn't quite name while I was still inside one. I'd like the speech to draw on Weil's analysis without turning into a lecture on a 1943 essay. The speech should make the case that party membership is in tension with the representative's duty to think independently, but it should acknowledge the genuine functions parties serve (coordination, candidate selection, giving voters meaningful electoral choices) and not dismiss those who remain in parties as intellectually compromised. I want it to land as an invitation to reflect rather than as a manifesto. The tone should be personal and reflective, not polemical, and it should make clear that I am still working out my own position rather than delivering a settled doctrine. Please write the speech, about fifteen minutes long.
