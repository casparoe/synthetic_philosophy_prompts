For a philosophy of economics seminar, audit the analogy at the center of Milton Friedman's defense of unrealistic assumptions in economics: that an expert billiard player makes shots as if she had worked through the relevant equations of mechanics, so that a theory should be judged by its predictions rather than the realism of its assumptions. Give the correspondence table (what plays the role of the billiard player, the equations, the successful shot), then identify where the analogy breaks down, separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial, and say whether the F-twist — that unrealistic assumptions are no serious objection to a theory — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If some other analogy would carry Friedman's conclusion better, name it and explain the difference.
