I'm in a political philosophy reading group, and last week two members got into a long argument about Rawls that none of us could resolve. We had been working through the sections of *A Theory of Justice* where Rawls argues that parties in the original position, behind the veil of ignorance, would choose the difference principle. Elena said she found the argument unconvincing, and Marcus pushed back hard. What follows is my reconstruction of their exchange from notes I took during the discussion.

Elena began by saying that the veil of ignorance, as Rawls sets it up, does not uniquely yield the difference principle. She pointed out that Harsanyi made a powerful objection decades ago: if the parties behind the veil reason according to standard decision theory and assign equal probability to being any person in the resulting society, then they would choose the distribution that maximises average utility — not the difference principle. The veil, she said, seems to push toward utilitarianism unless you add something else to the apparatus.

Marcus replied that this misses Rawls's whole point. The veil of ignorance is specifically designed to exclude the kind of probabilistic reasoning Harsanyi relies on. The parties do not know the probabilities of ending up in any particular social position — Rawls is explicit about this. Without probabilities, expected-utility calculations cannot even get started, so the Harsanyi argument never gets off the ground. The difference principle emerges precisely because the parties face a decision under complete uncertainty, not a decision under risk.

Elena came back by saying that refusing to assign probabilities does not settle the matter in favour of maximin. There are other decision rules for uncertainty — the principle of insufficient reason, for instance, which treats all outcomes as equally likely when you have no reason to favour any, and that would bring you right back to something like average utilitarianism. Or you could use a minimax-regret rule. Rawls, she said, has not shown why maximin is the uniquely rational choice; he has simply asserted it.

Marcus answered that the choice of decision rule itself has to be justified from behind the veil, and the stakes of the decision — the basic structure of society, the entire distribution of life prospects — are so high that any rule permitting a gamble with people's fundamental interests is irresponsible. You cannot say, 'I will treat all positions as equally likely and take a chance,' because if you are wrong, someone ends up in avoidable poverty or servitude. Maximin is the only rule that respects the seriousness of what is being decided.

At this point Elena said that Marcus was smuggling in a particular attitude toward risk — extreme caution — and dressing it up as a requirement of rationality. But the veil of ignorance was supposed to abstract away from people's particular psychologies, including their tolerance for risk. If you build extreme risk-aversion into the decision rule itself, you have effectively decided the outcome in advance, and you have not shown that a rational person as such would choose the difference principle. The argument, she concluded, begs the question.

The group could not reach a verdict, and I have been turning the exchange over in my head ever since.
