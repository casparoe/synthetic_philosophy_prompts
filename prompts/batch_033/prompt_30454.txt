My reading group has spent the last several weeks on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, and two of our members have reached a standoff. One of them—call this the supplementationist—holds that the standard subjective Bayesian apparatus, on which E confirms H just in case P(H|E) > P(H), cannot represent the confirmatory force of a piece of evidence that was already known before H was formulated, because P(E) is already 1 and conditioning on E changes nothing. On this view the problem is structural: the formalism needs something added, such as counterfactual or "would have expected" probabilities, a model of learning logical relationships among propositions, or a distinct notion of confirmation, if it is to make sense of the classic case in which a new theory explains an already-documented anomaly, such as the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion before general relativity. The other—call this the deflationist—holds that the problem is an artifact of a bad idealization: "known" was never really probability 1 in the relevant sense, or the old evidence should be conditionalized out of the background in a principled way, or confirmation is being misdescribed as an absolute property of a single theory rather than a comparative judgment against a relevant alternative. On this view, once the example is modeled correctly, the standard apparatus needs no revision.

We want the document these two members should have produced instead of arguing past each other: a joint statement in the style of an adversarial collaboration. It should open with a numbered section of agreed claims both can sign—about the formalism, about what the Mercury-type case requires an account to deliver, and about what would count as merely relabeling the problem rather than solving it. Then it should give a disagreements table in which each row contains: the disagreement stated in neutral wording; each side's formulation of its own view, phrased so that side would accept it; the crux or cruxes on which the row turns; and a concrete case, argument, or constraint that both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. Please flag explicitly which parts of their dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, and say why.

Finally, end with a short stability check: for each side, state the argument or result that would most likely make that side substantially revise its position, and where each side's own formulation is most likely to be question-begging or otherwise fragile. I am less interested in a verdict about who is right than in formulations precise enough that the remaining disagreement is genuinely load-bearing.
