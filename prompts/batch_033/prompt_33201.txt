I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Plato's political philosophy — fourteen students, one 75-minute session — and we are about to work through the opening books of the Laws, where the Athenian Stranger defends the drinking party against the charge that it is nothing more than a school of license. The claim I want the students to feel rather than merely be able to restate is that a symposium, for Plato, is best understood as a piece of moral technology: wine, taken under the supervision of a sober presiding official, loosens the soul's inhibitions enough to make a person's character visible, and the party becomes a controlled exercise in noticing one's own disorder and reasserting self-mastery — a test of character before it is a concession to appetite. I lectured through this material once and the distinction landed flat; they could repeat it back but did not see why it mattered. Design an in-class activity that makes them run into the idea through roles, rules, or a staged deliberation before it is named, without actual alcohol and without turning intoxication into a joke. The activity should reproduce the structure of the Platonic claim — controlled exposure, observation, and the reassertion of discipline — in a classroom-appropriate form.

Please include concrete roles or assignments, any materials, and a timeline for the 75 minutes, and then a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the moves of Laws I–II: the initial objection that drinking parties merely license appetite, the reply that the value of wine depends on the discipline under which it is taken, the function of the sober official, and the connection to courage and shame. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce — above all the pharmacological effect of wine and the social meaning of shame in a classical Greek symposium — and what I will need to supply in the debrief to bridge that gap.
