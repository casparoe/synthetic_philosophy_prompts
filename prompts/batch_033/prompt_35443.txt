I've been teaching the sea battle passage from De Interpretatione 9 for years and I thought I understood the moves, but a grad student in my seminar last week laid out a version of the argument that I'm having trouble finding a clean exit from. Here it is:

(1) For any proposition about the future, it's either true or false right now. (Bivalence holds for all propositions, future-tensed ones included.)

(2) If a proposition is true right now, then there is a fact right now that makes it true.

(3) If there is a fact right now that makes it true that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the sea battle tomorrow is already settled — it's not the kind of thing that could fail to happen given the way things currently are.

(4) So for any future event, either it's already settled that it will happen, or it's already settled that it won't. Which means deliberation is pointless: whatever you're going to do, you were always going to do.

(4) is absurd. But I can't figure out which premise to reject without paying a price I don't want to pay. If I reject (1), I'm stuck saying some propositions are neither true nor false, which means giving up classical logic for future-tensed statements — and I'd rather not. If I reject (2), I'm saying truth can float free of the world, which sounds like magic. If I reject (3), I need to explain how a fact can obtain right now that makes something true about tomorrow without thereby making that thing inevitable. The standard move is to say that the fact is just the fact that the event will happen, and that's not a "determining" kind of fact — but then I'm not sure what work "fact" is doing in (2) anymore, and I worry the whole truthmaker intuition collapses.

Tell me which premise to give up, and what the least bad option actually costs. I'd like to keep classical logic if I can, but I'm starting to suspect that's the price of the ticket. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of the question.
