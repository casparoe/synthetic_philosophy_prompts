I've been trying to pin down my views on the paradox of tragedy — why we deliberately expose ourselves to art that makes us feel awful — and I've realized I hold a set of commitments that I can't tell are consistent or not. I read the standard moves a while back (catharsis, the conversion theory, the make-believe/quasi-emotion view, the compensation story) and none of them felt like they were taking the phenomenon seriously; they all seemed to dissolve the puzzle by denying one of the things that makes it puzzling. So I sat down and tried to write out what I actually believe, staying close to the experience, and what came out is the list below. I'd like someone to audit it for joint tenability — which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest coherent revision would be.

Here are the commitments:

(a) The sadness, grief, and fear we feel in response to tragic fiction are the real thing, not pale copies. If I feel grief at the end of a novel, the phenomenal character of that grief is the same as grief about an actual loss, even though I know the events didn't happen. The quasi-emotion theory (that these are somehow differently-valenced states that merely resemble real emotions) strikes me as trying to legislate the phenomenology to save a prior commitment.

(b) We generally have reason to avoid experiences whose felt quality is, moment to moment, negative on net, and outside the aesthetic domain we reliably act on this reason. I don't knowingly put my hand on a hot stove, I avoid people who reliably make me feel terrible, and so on.

(c) For paradigm cases of tragedy — King Lear, Grave of the Fireflies, Dancer in the Dark — my experience considered as a hedonic stream is negative on net. If you sampled my affective state at random points during the work, the majority of samples would come back negative. The tension and distress are not, frame by frame, outweighed by some concurrent pleasure.

(d) I am not irrational for seeking out and valuing these works. My behavior here is not akrasia, not a failure of self-prediction, and not the result of some bias I should correct for.

(e) The value of these works is internal to the experience of engaging with them — it is something about the experience itself, not about downstream consequences. I don't think Lear is valuable because it leaves me feeling cleansed afterward (the catharsis story), or because it builds empathy I can deploy later, or because it signals something to my social group. The value is in the watching.

(f) The value of an experience can diverge from its hedonic quality. There are experiential states — perhaps "states" is too narrow; call them modes of engagement with the world — that are worth being in even though they feel bad.

The obvious clash is between (b), (c), and (d). I'm aware of it. What I want to know is whether (f) can actually resolve it, and what else has to give. In particular: if I accept (f), what is the principle that tells me when a bad-feeling experience is worth having and when it isn't? If the answer bottoms out at "it's worth having when it's art," that seems circular. If it bottoms out at "it's worth having when it's rich, complex, or insightful," then I seem committed to saying that feeling genuine grief at an actual funeral is also worth having for its richness, which is not a conclusion I want to be pushed into. So I need to know whether there's a stable principle in the vicinity that distinguishes the two cases without ad-hocery, or whether I need to give up one of (c), (d), or (f) — and if so, which.

I'd also like you to check for tensions I haven't noticed. One I'm unsure about is whether (a) and (f) pull against each other: if the sadness is genuine sadness and sadness inherently feels bad, can I coherently claim that an experience constituted partly by genuine sadness is valuable in a way that doesn't reduce to its hedonic payoff? This might be a pseudo-tension that dissolves once you distinguish the valence of a component from the value of the whole — a whole can have properties its parts lack — but I'd like that addressed explicitly rather than assumed away.

Please go through the commitments pairwise, rank the tensions by severity, and propose the minimal revision that yields consistency. For each tension you flag, tell me which commitment has to be weakened or dropped, what the revised version would say, and what the revision costs in terms of fidelity to the experience.
