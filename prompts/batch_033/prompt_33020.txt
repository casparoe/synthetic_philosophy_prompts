I've been persuaded by the conciliationist position in the epistemology of disagreement: learning that an epistemic peer disagrees with you gives you a defeasible reason to substantially revise your belief — you cannot simply dismiss the disagreement by insisting that you've assessed the first-order evidence correctly, since that would beg the question against your peer. But I want to know what this view is actually risking. What would count against it? I'd like you to map the space of possible defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward. In particular: (a) what would it take for the charge of self-defeat to stick — that is, for conciliationism to be rationally undermined by the fact that it is itself disputed among epistemologists? (b) Does conciliationism depend on the uniqueness thesis (that a given body of evidence permits only one rational doxastic attitude), and if so, would a successful defense of permissivism count as a decisive refutation? (c) Are there concrete cases — perhaps from moral or aesthetic disagreement, or from disagreements about very simple matters — where conciliationism gives results that even its defenders would find uncomfortable? (d) Has the view been modified over time with enough qualifications and domain restrictions that it no longer makes clear, risky predictions, and if so, is that a problem for a normative epistemological view? I'd also like to know which of these challenges a sophisticated conciliationist would concede as genuinely counting against the view, versus which they would dismiss as resting on misunderstandings.
