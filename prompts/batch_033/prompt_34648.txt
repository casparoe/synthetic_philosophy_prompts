I am a PhD student in political philosophy and serve as the graduate student representative on my department's graduate admissions committee. The committee is currently split over a proposal to restructure how we make admissions decisions, and I have been asked to draft a written opinion recommending a course of action.

Under the current system, the full faculty reads all application files and votes on the final slate of admitted students. The proposal would replace this with an arrangement in which the admissions committee — a small group of five or six faculty who do close readings of every file, conduct interviews, and build comparative knowledge of the applicant pool — makes binding decisions. The full faculty would retain only a consultative role: they could flag applicants they have particular knowledge of or interest in, but they would not vote on the final slate.

The argument for the proposal is what you might call epistocratic: the committee members are the only ones who have thoroughly engaged with the full set of files, and they have developed context about the applicant pool that the broader faculty cannot acquire from a quick scan of a few dozen folders. The quality of admissions decisions, on this view, depends on informed judgment, and the current system produces decisions based on thin and uneven information.

The argument against the proposal is democratic in character: every faculty member will teach, advise, and supervise these students for five to seven years, and each has a legitimate stake in who joins the department. Moreover, the willingness of faculty to invest in students they had no genuine say in selecting — and the perceived legitimacy of the admissions process among the faculty as a whole — may erode under a system that concentrates power in a small group.

The committee is divided roughly evenly, and both sides have asked that the opinion engage seriously with the strongest considerations on the other side rather than simply dismissing them. The opinion should recommend either adopting the proposal, rejecting it, or adopting a modified version (for instance, a two-tier system in which the committee makes provisional decisions subject to ratification by the full faculty, or a threshold mechanism by which a supermajority of the faculty can override a committee decision). Whatever the recommendation, it should specify the precise structure of the process and provide reasons that the losing side could recognize as fair. I am especially interested in how the opinion should handle the underlying tension between the epistemic case for concentrating decision-making in the hands of those with the most relevant knowledge and the democratic case for distributing it among all those with a stake in the outcome.
