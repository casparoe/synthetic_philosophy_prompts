Two friends of mine got into an argument about whether life is absurd and what follows from that. Mira defended a broadly Camus-style position: the universe is indifferent to our projects, our longing for meaning meets silence, and the honest response is to acknowledge the absurd and live intensely anyway — to rebel by embracing life without illusion. David pushed back with something closer to contemporary "meaning in life" accounts: meaning arises when we subjectively engage with things of objective value — relationships, creative work, understanding — and the fact that the universe as a whole is indifferent doesn't undermine the genuine meaning we find in these activities.

Their exchange went roughly as follows:

(1) Mira: "The problem isn't just that the universe is large and indifferent. It's that the question 'What's the point?' has no answer that doesn't eventually bottom out in something we simply decide to care about. You can say relationships matter, but if I ask why they matter, eventually you'll say 'they just do' or 'I just care about them.' That's exactly the silence Camus is talking about."

(2) David: "That's too demanding. No justification can be infinitely regressive — every chain of reasons ends somewhere. The fact that my reasons for valuing my daughter's well-being eventually run out doesn't mean that value is illusory. It means some things are properly basic. The absurdist sets an impossible standard for what would count as an answer."

(3) Mira: "I'm not setting an impossible standard — I'm pointing out that once you see the contingency of your basic commitments, the feeling that they're objectively important is hard to sustain. You can still live as if they matter, but you're doing so in full awareness that nothing outside your own caring anchors them. That's the absurd: the clash between the seriousness with which we live and the inability to justify that seriousness from outside."

(4) David: "But that clash only arises if you insist on an external justification. If meaning is constituted by the right kind of engagement with valuable things — not by some cosmic endorsement of that engagement — then the absence of cosmic endorsement doesn't create a clash. It's simply irrelevant. Your argument works only if you've already accepted that meaning requires something like cosmic backing, which is exactly what contemporary accounts deny."

(5) Mira: "Then I think your account changes the subject. When people ask whether life is meaningful, they're often asking whether it matters in a way that isn't just a fact about their own psychology. If your answer is 'it matters to you, and the objects of your engagement are valuable by human standards,' you've given a satisfying answer about well-being, maybe, but not about meaning in the sense that keeps people up at night."

(6) David: "I think that's the sense that keeps people up at night because they've been taught to want something incoherent. The philosophical work is to show that meaning, properly understood, is available — not to satisfy a demand for cosmic importance that nothing could meet."

They couldn't resolve it. Please adjudicate move by move: who has the better of each exchange, and where does the deeper disagreement lie? Is there a way to settle whether David is dissolving a pseudo-problem or Mira is rightly refusing a consolation prize?
