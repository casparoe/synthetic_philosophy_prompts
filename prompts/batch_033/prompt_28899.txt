I'm trying to get two people in my department to stop talking past each other, and I've finally talked them into doing a proper adversarial collaboration. One is a hardcore defender of the free energy principle — she thinks it's the closest thing cognitive science has to a unified theory, that any self-organizing system that persists has to look as though it's minimizing variational free energy, and that active inference gives real empirical traction on action, perception, and psychopathology. The other is a deflationist who thinks the principle itself is mathematically true but empirically empty — a modeling framework you can wrap around anything that persists, which is exactly why it can't fail — and that the actual empirical work is being done by predictive processing models downstream, which don't inherit any of the principle's supposed unifying power.

I'm drafting the joint statement they'd both sign after six months of good-faith arguement. It should have two parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: substantive enough not to be vacuous, precise enough that neither side feels caricatured. Include what they agree on about the math — variational free energy, Markov blankets, the formalism being nontrivial — but also the agreed limits, like what the principle by itself does and doesn't establish about the brain.

Second, a disagreements table. For each row: the disagreement stated in neutral language; the same disagreement restated seperately in the words each side would use for their own view, so each can say 'yes, that's what I think'; whether it's a verbal dispute or a real one; and, if it's real, the crux — the sub-question such that, if it were settled, at least one of them would change their mind.

Some specific fights have to show up somewhere: whether Markov blanket partitions carve a system at its joints or can be imposed arbitrarily; whether active inference makes novel predictions or fits anything post hoc; and whether predictive processing as an empirical theory stands or falls with the principle.

Then, for the two or three cruxes you judge are doing the real work, give me a concrete case, data pattern, or argument both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Be specific about what would count as evidence against the free energy principle. If the deflationist can't say what would falsify it for her, the document should say so — that's a crux in its own right. Same for the defender: if she can't specify a system the principle excludes, say so.

Don't give me a menu. I want you to make the calls: which disagreements are real, which are mostly verbal, which crux actually matters, and where you'd put your money if you had to. Give reasons, but take definite positions.

You're a philosopher of cognitive science who's been in this literature for years and has argued both sides of it. Before you write the document, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming what each side would actually sign, what they'd balk at, and where the temptation to write vacuous compromise lives. Then write the statement.
