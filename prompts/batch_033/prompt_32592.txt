Question: "Thomas Nagel argued that moral luck poses a serious problem for how we ordinarily think about moral responsibility. What are the main types of moral luck Nagel identified, and why does he think they create a problem for the idea that moral judgment should track only what is within a person's control?"

Answer: "Nagel identifies four kinds of moral luck. Resultant luck is luck in how things turn out — for example, two equally reckless drivers who run a red light, but one hits a pedestrian while the other passes through an empty intersection. We blame the one who caused harm far more harshly, even though the difference in outcome was entirely outside either driver's control. Circumstantial luck is luck in the situations we face — for instance, whether someone happened to grow up in a stable household or a chaotic one shapes their opportunities for moral development in ways they didn't choose. Constitutive luck is luck in our temperament, inclinations, and character traits, which are largely given to us rather than chosen. Finally, causal luck is the idea that every action is determined by antecedent causes stretching back before our birth, so in a deep sense nothing we do is ultimately within our control. Nagel's argument is that once we see how thoroughly luck infiltrates moral assessment, we have to admit that the control principle — the idea that people can be morally judged only for what is within their control — is simply false. He therefore proposes that we should revise our ordinary practices of praise and blame to factor luck in, for example by treating the two reckless drivers the same way regardless of whether anyone was hurt, because the outcome was just a matter of luck. This would bring our moral practices into line with what we know about the limits of human agency."
