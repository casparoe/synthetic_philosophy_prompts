Ok so my friend and I got into this absurdly long argument last night — we were at a bar and I think we annoyed the bartender — about the frame problem. Not just the original McCarthy and Hayes version where you need frame axioms so a robot knows its coffee cup moving doesn't change the ceiling color, but the deeper thing Dennett gets at in "Cognitive Wheels": how does any agent in a real world manage to ignore almost everything? I was arguing its a genuine epistemological crisis — you can never exhaustively justify your relevance judgments, because to check whether you should have considered X you'd need to already have a criterion for relevance, which is exactly what's at issue. My friend (he's a comp sci guy) kept saying no, its just about efficient knowledge representation and nonmonotonic logics and the philosophers are being dramatic. We went in circles for like two hours. At one point I literally drew a diagram on a napkin of the robot R1D1 and he said the diagram was itself a frame problem. We were insufferable.

But here's the thing — the argument got me thinking about poetic form. A sonnet has to decide, ruthlessly, what fits in fourteen lines and what gets left out. That's basically the frame problem in miniature: the form is a relevance filter, and whatever doesn't make the cut may as well not exist. And the volta is like the moment you realize your frame isn't working and you need a different one — except in the philosophical case you can't step outside the frame to check it, because any check already uses some frame.

So I want you to write a sonnet about the frame problem. The closing couplet should land on the vertigo of that: you act, and afterward you can't prove you didn't miss the one thing that would have changed everything.
