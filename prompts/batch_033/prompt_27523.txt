I am a PhD student in philosophy working on animal ethics, and I have been trying to get clear about how seriously to take the so-called scale argument concerning insects. The argument runs as follows: even if the probability that any given insect is phenomenally conscious is quite low, the sheer number of insects on Earth — on the order of ten quintillion at any one time — means that the expected number of sentient beings affected by human decisions could be enormous, potentially swamping the expected moral value of attending to human or livestock welfare.

I would like your credence on the following claim: the scale argument succeeds in establishing that insect welfare should be treated as a central consideration in moral decision-making, roughly on a par with or exceeding the importance of human and livestock welfare in at least some significant practical contexts.

Please decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions:

- The probability that insects of at least some orders (e.g., Hymenoptera, Diptera) possess phenomenal consciousness — that there is something it is like to be them.
- The probability that insect consciousness, if present, carries nontrivial moral weight — specifically, that the disvalue of an insect's suffering is not so vanishingly small relative to a human's that the scale argument's multiplier is neutralized even if the first sub-question is resolved in favor of sentience.
- The probability that we can identify, and meaningfully implement, welfare-improving interventions for insects at scale — that the argument moves from expected sentience to actionable moral demands rather than remaining a merely theoretical concern.
- The probability that, conditional on the three sub-questions above resolving favorably, the expected-value reasoning actually goes through — rather than being undermined by cluelessness about long-run indirect effects, by higher-order uncertainty about our own uncertainty estimates, or by the possibility that the sheer demandingness of the conclusion itself constitutes a reason to reject the argument's normative force.

For each sub-question, give a probability and a sentence or two of justification. Then explain how you combine them to reach your overall credence on the claim, and identify which sub-question your credence is most sensitive to — that is, which one, if you meaningfully revised its probability, would shift your overall credence the most. If you believe the decomposition should include additional sub-questions beyond those listed, please include and evaluate those as well.
