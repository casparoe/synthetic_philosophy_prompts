I'm hosting a philosophy-of-mind interview series, and my next guest defends the two-streams view of the split-brain: the claim that commissurotomy patients literally have two numerically distinct streams of phenomenal consciousness, not just two competing processing systems or a single stream that switches between hemispheres. I want to push her beyond the standard back-and-forth, and I'd like you to draft the questions.

The interview has four segments, and I need about eight to ten questions total, with follow-ups. Here's what I want to cover:

Segment 1: Press her on the behavioral unity of split-brain patients. They don't walk into walls, they don't report feeling split, and their everyday functioning is remarkably intact. The standard two-streams reply is that subcortical and brainstem structures preserve sensorimotor integration while the cortical split divides higher-level consciousness. But that reply seems to treat consciousness as epiphenomenal — if two streams can coexist in a body that acts as if it has one, what work does the claim "there are two streams" actually do? Give me a question on this that won't let her just cite the dissociation experiments from the 1960s and move on. Include a follow-up that anticipates her most likely move.

Segment 2: The individuation problem. What makes two streams of consciousness two rather than one? If the answer is causal or informational integration, then split-brain patients are a borderline case; but the two-streams view treats them as a binary case. Ask her to state the individuation criterion explicitly and then ask whether it doesn't also count some non-split brains as containing multiple streams (e.g., under sedation, in certain dissociative states, or even in ordinary cases where processing is modular). The follow-up should force her to say whether she accepts those implications or rejects the criterion.

Segment 3: The "who is the subject?" problem. If there are two streams, then when a split-brain patient says "I," which stream is speaking? The standard answer is that the left hemisphere's language system does the talking, and the right hemisphere's stream is mute or expresses itself nonverbally. But then the left-hemisphere stream is making claims about "my" experience that don't cover the right-hemisphere stream's experience — claims that are false if taken as reports about the whole organism. Ask her whether the speaking stream is systematically mistaken about its own ontology, and if so, what that means for using first-person reports as evidence for the view. The follow-up should connect this to the first segment: if the speaking stream's reports are unreliable, doesn't the absence of "I feel split" reports lose its evidential weight?

Segment 4: A question about what could count as evidence against her view. She's defended the two-streams interpretation of the commissurotomy data, but I want to know: what empirical finding, if it came in, would make her abandon the view? Don't let her say something vague like "if it turned out the hemispheres were fully integrated" — that's just the denial of the premise. Push for something specific: a particular kind of behavioral result, a neuroimaging finding, or a phenomenological report that would genuinely surprise her and shift her credence. The follow-up should ask whether this test would actually be feasible in practice, or whether the view is empirically insulated.

Finally, I want one closing question that steps back and asks whether the whole debate matters for anything outside philosophy. If a neurosurgeon said, "I just need to know the functional risks of the procedure, not how many subjects are on the table," what would she say?

If any of my framings rest on a confusion about the view or the literature, say so plainly instead of writing questions that reinforce the mistake. I'll fill in citations and names myself. Please write in American English.
