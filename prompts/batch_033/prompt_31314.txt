A close friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about middle knowledge, and by the end we had each restated our positions so often that I began to suspect the debate itself, not just the two of us, is stuck. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of it.

The background, as we both accept it, is this. Molinists hold that God possesses middle knowledge: knowledge, logically prior to his decision to create any particular world, of what every possible free creature would freely choose in any possible set of circumstances. This is supposed to let God exercise meticulous providence over a world of libertarian free creatures. He surveys the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, selects a world in which free creatures freely do what his purposes require, and actualizes it. The standard objection, which my friend pressed all evening, is that these counterfactuals of creaturely freedom appear to have nothing that makes them true. If Peter is genuinely free in a given set of circumstances, neither his nature nor the circumstances determine that he will deny Christ; and before God has decided to create Peter at all, there is no actual Peter, no actual choice, and no created world to ground the truth of the claim that if Peter were in those circumstances, he would deny Christ. So, the objection concludes, there is no fact of the matter there for God to know, or at least no truth that could guide a divine decision. Molinists reply, as I understand them, either that such counterfactuals are true but brute, or that they are grounded in the creature's individual essence or in the creature's possession of a would-property, or that truth does not always require a truthmaker and the demand for a ground is therefore misplaced. Each move just led my friend to ask what a brute truth could possibly be, or what a would-property is if not the very thing in question under a new name.

Here is my hypothesis about what is really going on. The two sides are not disagreeing about any exegetical, empirical, or even straightforwardly theological matter; they are disagreeing about the metaphysics of truth. One side is operating with the principle that every contingent truth needs something in the world to make it true, and the other side is willing to allow at least some truths to be primitive. If that is right, the Molinism debate is a local skirmish in a much older fight about truthmakers and grounding, and no amount of tightening the theology will move either side.

Is my diagnosis correct? Is the impasse really about truthmaking, or is it instead about whether libertarian freedom is compatible with there being facts about what a free creature would do, or about what God's knowledge requires, or about what "prior to the decree" could even mean given divine eternity, or about something else? Or is the question itself ill-posed? And on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a defensible semantics for these counterfactuals, a formal result about grounding, a sharper account of libertarian freedom, a reframing of the question, or something else? I want the structure of the disagreement laid bare, not a summary of Molinist and anti-Molinist positions.
