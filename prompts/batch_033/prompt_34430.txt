So I've been trying to figure out my friend Clara's emotional responses to art — specifically, when she finds sad or disturbing stuff cathartic versus when it just makes her miserable. She's weirdly consistent about it, and I've been keeping a mental list of her verdicts over the years. She loves Greek tragedies, the more devastating the better; she says she leaves the theater feeling wrung out but in a good way. She also loves bleak indie films where nothing works out for anyone. She's told me she finds sad music deeply satisfying, sometimes more than happy music. She's a huge fan of psychological horror — Hereditary, The Babadook, that kind of thing — but she walked out of a Saw sequel and says slasher films and torture porn are just boring, not even upsetting, just dull. On the flip side, she refuses to watch documentaries about real tragedies; she says they make her feel awful in a way that doesn't resolve into anything. She once read a long-form piece about a real child's murder and told me she genuinely wishes she hadn't, that it kept her up for two nights. She also hates those viral videos of soldiers coming home and surprising their kids — she says they make her cry but she resents the crying, like someone's pushing a button she didn't consent to have pushed. But she loved Schindler's List, which is based on real events, and described it as one of the most cathartic experiences of her life.

Here's the case I'm trying to predict: I just heard about a fictional film about a family dealing with a parent's terminal cancer diagnosis, but it's shot in a hyperrealistic, documentary-style way — handheld camera, no score, actors improvising, the whole thing designed to feel like you're watching someone's actual home videos. It's clearly labeled as fiction, but apparently it's close to unbearable in its realism. Would Clara find it cathartic (like the Greek tragedies) or merely distressing (like the real-child-murder piece)? 

I can think of at least two rules that fit all her past verdicts. One is that she simply can handle fictional suffering but not real suffering — she's fine with made-up tragedy but the real thing is too much. The other is that what matters isn't fictionality per se but aesthetic distance: she needs the suffering to be filtered through recognizable artistic conventions that remind her she's consuming art, not witnessing life. The hyperrealistic cancer film would seperate these two rules: it's fictional, but it has almost zero aesthetic distance. What would each rule predict about the film, which rule do you think is more likely to be right given her whole pattern, and what further case would most cleanly tell them apart? And if you think neither rule quite captures what's going on, what's a better account of Clara's psychology here?
