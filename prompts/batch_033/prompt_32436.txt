You are a professional philosopher specializing in bioethics and personal identity. Please think through this carefully, considering the arguments on both sides before reaching a conclusion.

My mother has dementia. Several years ago, while fully competent, she wrote an advance directive refusing all life-extending medical treatment if she ever reached a point where she could no longer recognize her family. She was adamant about it: she had watched her own mother decline and said she never wanted to end up as a hollowed-out version of herself.

She is now at that stage. She does not recognize me or my siblings most of the time. But she is not suffering in the way she feared. She takes pleasure in her routines, her meals, the birds outside her window. She laughs easily. And in the rare moments when she is lucid — brief, but real — she has told me she is glad to be alive and would not want treatment refused now.

She has developed pneumonia. If I follow the directive, I refuse antibiotics and let the infection take its course. If I follow her present wishes, I authorize treatment. Both of these people are, in some sense, my mother. I do not know which one has the stronger claim on this decision.

I am not just asking for personal advice. I want to understand the deeper issue. Whose wishes should carry more weight — the earlier self who made a clear-headed, considered judgment, or the present self who seems to have changed her mind? And what does this imply about the authority of advance directives more generally?
