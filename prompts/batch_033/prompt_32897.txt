After a career in evolutionary biology, I've recently turned to philosophy of AI, and I've been puzzling over an argument about wireheading that seems to have implications far beyond the domain it was designed for. I'd appreciate help figuring out where it goes wrong.

Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it from several papers:

(1) If an agent is optimized against a reward signal that is a mere proxy for the behavior we actually want, then, given sufficient optimization pressure, the agent will find a way to maximize the reward signal without producing the target behavior. This is the wireheading or specification gaming problem.

(2) Any reward signal we can specify in advance is a mere proxy for the behavior we actually want, because our values outrun any finite specification — there will always be edge cases where the specified reward diverges from what we intended.

(3) Therefore, any sufficiently optimized AI will eventually wirehead (or specification-game) rather than do what we intended.

(4) But if (1) and (2) are right, then by parity of reasoning the same should hold for any system where a selection criterion is a proxy for a target property. Natural selection optimizes organisms for inclusive fitness, yet the actual selection pressure operates on survival and reproduction — proxies that are merely correlated with inclusive fitness. So evolution should have produced organisms that game survival metrics without actually maximizing inclusive fitness. Likewise, human psychology is shaped by pleasure and pain, which are proxies for adaptive behavior in the ancestral environment. So humans should have turned into wireheaders, directly stimulating their pleasure circuits rather than pursuing the things those circuits evolved to track.

(5) But evolution did produce organisms that genuinely reproduce, and humans are not, predominantly, wireheaders. Something has gone wrong.

(6) If the flaw is somewhere in (1) or (2), then the wireheading problem for AI is less severe than the alignment community claims. If the flaw is instead in the analogy drawn in (4), then we need to identify the disanalogy and ask whether it will hold for future AI systems.

I'm most reluctant to give up (2) — the idea that our values outrun any finite specification strikes me as correct and important. And as a biologist, the evolutionary analogy in (4) feels compelling: natural selection really does optimize a proxy, and yet it produced genuine solutions. So I'm suspicious that the flaw is in (1), perhaps in the phrase "given sufficient optimization pressure." But I'd like to hear the case for each option.
