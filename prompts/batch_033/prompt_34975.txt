I've been auditing the meetings of the Meridian Foundation's fellowship committee for the last six months—research for a novel about the strange politics of arts funding, the kind of thing where a roomful of well-meaning people can't agree on what "merit" even means. The committee has three members: Lila, Marcus, and Naomi. They review applications for a year-long grant, and the foundation's charter says an applicant needs to satisfy two criteria: "artistic innovation" and "feasibility of completion." Both must be met for the grant to be awarded.

At each meeting, the three of them vote on the two criteria separately, and then the chair announces a final decision. I've been keeping a meticulous little notebook. Here's what I've observed across four past decisions:

- The translator working on a multilingual epic about the Silk Road: Lila voted yes on innovation and yes on feasibility; Marcus voted yes on both; Naomi voted no on both. The grant was awarded.

- The conceptual artist proposing a year-long performance piece about sleep: Lila voted no on both criteria; Marcus voted yes on both; Naomi voted no on both. The grant was denied.

- The novelist writing a fragmented account of a family's diaspora: Lila voted yes on both; Marcus voted no on both; Naomi voted yes on both. The grant was awarded.

- The poet working on a collection that uses only words of Anglo-Saxon origin: Lila voted yes on innovation and no on feasibility; Marcus voted no on innovation and yes on feasibility; Naomi voted no on both. The grant was denied.

So here's what's been nagging at me. There's actually two ways a committee like this can turn the criterion votes into a final decision. They could use what I've been calling the "criterion-first" method: take the majority on each criterion independently, and if both criteria get a majority yes, award the grant. Or they could use the "judge-first" method: each member first decides whether the applicant meets both criteria in their own head, and then the chair takes a majority on that overall thumbs-up-or-down. The foundation's charter is maddeningly silent on which method to use, and in most cases it doesn't matter. But in some configurations of votes, the two methods point in opposite directions.

Next week they're considering an applicant I know—a former workshop student of mine, actually, who's proposing a memoir structured as a field guide to urban weeds. Based on what I've gleaned of their tastes, I'm fairly confident Lila will vote yes on innovation and no on feasibility, Marcus will vote no on innovation and yes on feasibility, and Naomi will vote yes on both. So what happens? I'm trying to figure out whether the pattern of past decisions already reveals which aggregation method the committee has been using all along, or whether all four past cases are consistent with both methods. And if the past cases don't settle it, what's actually at stake—procedurally—when they vote on this next one? Which of the past cases, if any, comes closest to being a precedent for this situation, and what rule would the committee be implicitly adopting with whichever outcome they land on?
