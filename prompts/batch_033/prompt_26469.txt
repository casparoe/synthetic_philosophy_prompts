I serve on a hospital ethics committee, and over the past year I've kept notes on how one of our members — call her Dr. K — has voted on cases involving deception, confidentiality, and truth-telling. Her votes have been strikingly consistent with each other, but I can't tell whether she's applying a single general principle with nuance or whether she's a moral particularist who denies that such cases can be captured by any rule at all. Here are her votes:

1. A nurse lies to a violent and confused patient about the location of another patient he is threatening. Dr. K voted: permissible.
2. A surgeon tells a patient's family that a risky procedure "went smoothly" when there was a near-fatal complication that was resolved and left no lasting harm; the surgeon wanted to spare the family distress about a danger already past. Dr. K voted: impermissible.
3. A GP prescribes a placebo to a patient with a medically unexplained chronic pain condition, describing it as "a new treatment that helps some people." Dr. K voted: permissible.
4. A researcher omits from a consent form a remote but serious risk of a study drug, judging that mentioning it would cause disproportionate alarm and deter participation in a trial likely to benefit many. Dr. K voted: impermissible.
5. A junior doctor, asked by a dying patient whether they are going to die, says "we're doing everything we can" when the patient is terminal with days to live; the patient's family has begged the team not to reveal the prognosis, and the patient has not repeated the question. Dr. K voted: permissible.
6. A physician withholds from a competent adult patient a diagnosis of early-stage cancer, intending to disclose it once a treatment plan is in place, because the physician judges the patient too psychologically fragile to handle the news now. Dr. K voted: impermissible.

Next week we face a new case: a patient with moderate dementia repeatedly asks where her husband is; the husband died two years ago, and each time she is told the truth she experiences the grief as if for the first time, becoming inconsolable for hours. The nursing staff proposes telling her that her husband is "out right now" or "visiting his sister" to spare her this repeated trauma. The patient cannot retain the information either way and has no next of kin to consult.

Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion:

1. Your best prediction of Dr. K's vote on the dementia case, with the reasoning you think she would give.
2. The general principle that best fits her first six votes, stated as explicitly as the pattern allows.
3. An alternative rule or approach that fits her votes equally well, and the specific vote where its implications first diverge from the first principle's.
4. Whether her vote on the dementia case would favor one of these two accounts over the other, and why or why not.
5. A further hypothetical case — specify it concretely — whose verdict would cleanly separate the two accounts if the dementia case does not.
