Explain what pain asymbolia is, and evaluate whether its existence settles the debate between motivational internalism and externalism about pain. If it does not settle the debate, identify what further considerations would be needed.

---

**Student answer (Candidate 2741)**

Pain asymbolia is a rare neuropsychological condition, typically resulting from lesions to the insular cortex, in which patients continue to identify noxious stimuli as painful—they can tell you it hurts, and they can locate the pain and rate its intensity—but they no longer exhibit any aversion to it. They do not withdraw, wince, or complain; some even laugh during what should be agonising procedures. This is distinct from congenital insensitivity to pain, where the patient never registers the stimulus as painful at all, and also distinct from ordinary pain relief via opioids, though the underlying mechanism is thought to be similar: a selective suppression of the affective-motivational component of pain while sparing the sensory-discriminative component.

The philosophical significance of pain asymbolia is that it appears to provide a clean empirical refutation of motivational internalism about pain. Motivational internalism holds that the experience of pain necessarily includes a motivational element—that to feel pain just is, in part, to be motivated to stop or escape it. Pain asymbolia shows that the sensory and motivational components can come apart, so the motivational element cannot be essential to pain. The asymbolic patient is in pain but is not motivated to do anything about it; therefore internalism is false, and motivational externalism—the view that pain's link to motivation is contingent, mediated by a separate evaluative attitude or desire—is correct.

One might object that the asymbolic patient is not really in pain, but merely reports being in pain while undergoing a different experience. This objection fails, however, because the patient's sensory-discriminative capacities are intact: they can discriminate sharp from dull, hot from cold, and can rate intensity on a standard scale. Since the sensory component is all there is to pain, the patient is in pain. The only remaining question is why anyone ever thought the affective component was essential, and the answer is that in typical cases the two components co-occur so reliably that philosophers mistook correlation for necessity.

In sum, pain asymbolia settles the debate decisively in favour of externalism. The only further work needed is to characterise the nature of the external evaluative attitude that normally attaches to pain and is missing in asymbolia—perhaps a desire to be rid of the sensation, or a judgement that it is bad.
