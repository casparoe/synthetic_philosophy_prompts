Over the last few months I've been writing down my gut reactions to decision puzzles before reading what anyone else says about them, and they're starting to look inconsistent, so I want help figuring out what rule I'm actually following. In the classic Newcomb case — a predictor with an unbroken track record, one clear box with $1,000, and one opaque box that contains $1,000,000 iff the predictor thought I'd leave the clear box — I take only the opaque box. But if the boxes are glass and I can see the million already sitting there, I take both. If the predictor is only 55% accurate, I take both. And in the smoking-lesion setup, where a gene makes me crave cigarettes and also makes lung cancer likely, but smoking itself adds no extra cancer risk and I enjoy it, I smoke.

The rule I keep coming back to is something like: do the thing such that, if I learned I'd done it, the world would be better news. That rule tells me to one-box in the classic case and take both in the glass case, which matches my gut, but it also tells me not to smoke in the lesion case and to one-box even with the 55% predictor, and both of those feel wrong. Is there a single principle that gets all four, or close? If not, which of my verdicts should I drop and why? And what would the principle you land on say about a variant where the opaque box contains a one-time cure for a stranger's terminal illness and the clear box contains $1,000 for me? I genuinly don't know what I think about that one.
