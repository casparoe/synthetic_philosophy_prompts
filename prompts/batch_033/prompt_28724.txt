I have been reading an old English translation of Cicero's On Old Age, and there is a passage near the beginning that I keep returning to without feeling that I understand it. Cato has just finished saying that people who have no resources within themselves find every stage of life burdensome, while those who seek their goods from within find nothing that comes in the course of nature to be evil. He then says:

"I am wise in this respect, that I follow and obey Nature, the surest guide, as if she were a god, and it is utterly improbable that she has well arranged the other parts of life, and yet, like an unskilled poet, slighted the last act of the drama. There must, however, of necessity, be some end, and, as in the case of berries on the trees and the fruits of the earth, there must be that which in its season of full ripeness is, so to speak, ready to wither and fall,—which a wise man ought to bear patiently. For to rebel against Nature is but to repeat the war of the Giants with the Gods."

I take the general drift to be that old age is part of nature's design and that we should accept it rather than fight it. But when I try to pin down the argument, I lose the thread. It looks as if he is running together several different ideas: that a wise person accepts natural things, that nature would not have made a mess of the last part of life, and that everything alive has to end sometime. The middle idea seems to assume the very point at issue, namely that old age is a well-made part of life rather than a defect. I am also unsure what work the image of ripening fruit is doing: is it saying that old age is a kind of ripeness that makes death fitting, or only that decline is as natural as fruit falling and therefore to be endured? And does the instruction to follow nature amount to anything more than making peace with what cannot be changed? Could you walk me through what Cato is actually arguing here, where the assumptions are, and how much of the passage is argument as opposed to consolation? Please write your answer in American English.
