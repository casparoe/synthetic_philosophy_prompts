I've been trying to sort out what I think about supererogation — acts that are good but not required — by testing my gut on a bunch of cases. The trouble is my gut isn't consistent, and I can't find a principle that fits all of it. Here are my verdicts, roughly:

1. A child is drowning in a shallow pond. I'm in a suit that cost £200 and will be ruined if I wade in. I think I'm obligated to save the child. Not supererogatory, just required.

2. I get a letter from a reputable charity saying £200 would save a child's life overseas. I think giving the £200 would be supererogatory, not required. (I don't feel I've done something wrong if I bin the letter, though I'd feel good about myself if I donated.)

3. A stranger needs a bone marrow transplant. The registry contacts me as a match. The procedure is painful and involves a week of recovery. I think donating would be deeply admirable and clearly supererogatory — not something anyone can demand of me.

4. I see someone collapse on the pavement. Calling an ambulance costs me nothing but thirty seconds. I think I'm obligated to call. Not supererogatory.

5. My neighbour asks me to feed their cat while they're away for a weekend. I'm free and it's no trouble. I think agreeing is the decent thing, but I'm not sure it rises to obligation — and I definately wouldn't call it supererogatory. It's just... ordinary decency. But what's the difference between ordinary decency and obligation?

6. Someone spends their weekends training as a volunteer first responder, at real cost to their leisure and social life, so they can help in emergencies. I think this is supererogatory. Not required.

7. A parent works double shifts for years, sacrificing their health, to afford their child's medical treatment. My instinct is that this is obligatory — parental duty — but it also feels heroic in a way that doesn't sit neatly in either box. It almost feels like it's required and supererogatory at the same time, which can't be right.

8. Someone gives 90% of their income to effective charities and lives on the remainder, radically below what their peers spend. I think this is supererogatory — saintly, even. Definitely not required.

Here's what bothers me. Cases 1 and 2 look structurally similar: a £200 cost saves a life. But I call one obligatory and the other supererogatory. I could try to explain the difference by proximity, or salience, or the fact that in case 1 I'm the only person who can help — but then case 3 (bone marrow) also involves a unique match and I still call it supererogatory. So uniqueness isn't doing the work either. Meanwhile case 7 (the parent) feels like a counterexample to the whole obligatory/supererogatory distinction — it seems to be both.

What I want is a principle, or maybe a small set of candidate principles, that gets as many of these verdicts right as possible. I'm willing to overturn one or two if the principle is clean enough. But I want to know which ones I'd have to give up, and why. Also: what does your preferred principle say about the parent case (7)? Is there a way to make sense of its double aspect, or does the principle force me to pick one side?
