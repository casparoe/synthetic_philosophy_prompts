I'm taking a course on American pragmatism this semester and we've been reading Peirce and James back to back, and honestly I'm having trouble seeing why James is taken seriously. Peirce's view of truth seems really careful: truth is the opinion that would eventually be agreed upon by anyone who investigates long enough and well enough. It's like truth is the limit of inquiry, which sounds almost scientific. But then James comes along and says truth is what's useful to believe, what has cash value in experience, what works. And I just can't see how this doesn't collapse into "whatever makes me feel better is true." Like, if I'm grieving and I find it genuinely consoling to believe my dead relative is watching over me from heaven, or if believing I'm a misunderstood genius helps me get through my failures at work, then those beliefs are useful — they work for me, they have practical benefits. By James's lights, wouldn't they have to count as true? My proffesor keeps saying this is a misunderstanding of James but I've reread the Pragmatism lectures twice and I'm still not seeing the distinction. Is there actually a way James can block this, or did Peirce basically have it right and James just watered it down into something sloppy?
