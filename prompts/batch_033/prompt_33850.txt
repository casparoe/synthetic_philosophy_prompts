I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about the Fermi paradox — specifically, about how different kinds of evidence should update my credence that we are alone in the galaxy. Here are my verdicts on a series of discoveries, each considered in isolation (that is, conditional on not having made the other discoveries on the list):

1. Fossilized microbes found on Mars, dating to 3.5 billion years ago, biochemically similar to early Earth life but with a distinct chirality. My verdict: This makes the silence more puzzling, not less. If life arose twice in one solar system, the galaxy should be teeming, so the absence of signals is stranger than before.

2. A full-sky infrared survey reaches the sensitivity needed to detect Dyson spheres around any star within 10,000 light-years, and finds nothing. My verdict: This strongly updates me toward "we are alone." This is exactly the kind of observation that should have found something if there were anything to find.

3. Geochemical evidence firms up that abiogenesis on Earth occurred within 100 million years of the surface becoming habitable. My verdict: Pushes me toward the view that the Great Filter lies after abiogenesis — if life starts almost immediately, the bottleneck must be later.

4. Exoplanet surveys establish that all 50 of the nearest sunlike stars have Earth-sized planets in the habitable zone. My verdict: Only modestly updates me toward the silence being more puzzling. We already strongly suspected planets were common, so this isn't much of a surprise.

5. A narrowband radio signal at the hydrogen line is detected from a star 1,200 light-years away. It lasts 72 seconds, has no modulation, and despite years of follow-up never repeats. My verdict: I don't know what to make of this. My gut says it barely updates me at all, but I can't articulate a principle that justifies ignoring it while taking the Dyson sphere null result so seriously.

6. We find that the galaxy shows no signs of interstellar colonization — no von Neumann probes in our solar system, no stellar engineering, no anomalous infrared from waste heat. My verdict: This is the most damning observation. It strongly suggests that no civilization has ever reached the stage of launching self-replicating probes, which any expansionist civilization should have done by now.

7. But then: comparative biology reveals that complex, flexible intelligence — the kind that builds tools and solves novel problems — evolved convergently at least three times on Earth: in cephalopods, corvids, and primates. My verdict: This pulls me back toward "the galaxy ought to be full." If intelligence is convergent, then the filter isn't the evolution of intelligence, which means it's something else — and it's not obvious what.

I'm trying to find the principle (or a small set of principles) that governs these verdicts. I suspect it has something to do with the structure of the Great Filter argument — specifically, with how to distinguish observations that shift the "early filter versus late filter" balance from observations that shift the "filter versus no filter" balance. But I can't make it precise. The case that most bothers me is (5): the non-repeating signal. I want my principle to tell me how much to update on a one-off candidate technosignature that doesn't replicate, and why that differs (if it does) from a null result in a systematic survey. I also notice a tension between (1) and (7): both are "life or intelligence is easy" findings, but in (1) I said the silence got more puzzling, while in (7) I said it pulled me back toward expecting company. Are these consistent, or do I need to revise one?

Apply your principle to a few cases I haven't listed (for example: what if we find that the jump from prokaryotes to eukaryotes happened multiple times on Earth? What if we find a second independent abiogenesis on Earth, a "shadow biosphere"?). And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle forces me to abandon, if any.
