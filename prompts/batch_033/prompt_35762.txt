I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I'm teaching a unit on Ian Hacking's looping effects in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry. The class has about 15–20 students, and I have one 90-minute session. The core idea I want them to walk away with is that some classifications — what Hacking calls human kinds or interactive kinds — don't just describe the people they classify but change them, and the changed people in turn change the classification, producing a feedback loop that natural kinds don't generate.

Design a concrete, runnable in-class activity that makes them feel this looping dynamic directly rather than just hearing about it from me. The activity should use only ordinary classroom materials (cards, paper, whiteboard markers). The rules, roles, or incentives should be arranged so that participants get classified, find their behaviour shifting in response to the classification, and then see the classification itself shift in response to their changed behaviour — all before the phenomenon is named. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto Hacking's framework: the interactive kind, the looping effect, the idea of "making up people," and the contrast with indifferent kinds. Give me a timeline for the 90 minutes, a note on what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest assessment of which feature of Hacking's view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply separately. I want a single, committed design with clear rules — not a menu of options.
