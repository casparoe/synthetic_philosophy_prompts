I am a commissioning editor at a mid-sized publisher of serious popular science. Several times a month I have to decide, on the strength of a proposal and two or three sample chapters, whether a manuscript's central explanatory claim is one our list can stand behind or one that will embarrass us with working scientists. I have no formal philosophy training and about an hour per manuscript; I can escalate at most one manuscript in ten to an outside expert, and my assessment feeds an acquisitions meeting that makes the final call. I need a procedure I can run myself, and I need it to be reusable across manuscripts rather than a one-off judgement of the cases below.

Please write me that procedure, grounded in Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks and his notion of greedy reductionism. The procedure should sort a manuscript's central explanatory claim into four categories: legitimate crane, skyhook, greedy reductionism, and crane-under-construction (a promissory note). For our purposes: a crane is a bottom-up mechanism that does real explanatory work and is itself built from simpler, unmysterious processes; a skyhook is an explanatory posit that imports the very complexity, intelligence, or design it is supposed to explain; greedy reductionism is reduction that skips levels and explains away higher-level regularities instead of accounting for them.

The procedure should specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with 'decline' or 'escalate'; and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of judgement or values rather than fact. It should specifically help me distinguish a skyhook from a crane that simply has not been cashed out yet, and greedy reductionism from legitimate reduction that still respects higher-level patterns. Mark the steps at which I will be making a judgement call dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. A bestselling physicist's manuscript arguing that information is a fundamental feature of the universe that organises matter, and that this single principle explains life, mind, and society. I am under commercial pressure to sign it.
2. A manuscript explaining all of human culture — art, religion, law, kinship — as the direct expression of inclusive-fitness maximisation, with no account of the intermediate social, cognitive, and historical mechanisms.
3. A manuscript I personally dislike: a step-by-step evolutionary account of moral emotions and punishment, built from iterated interactions, reputation, and costly signalling, which the author uses to argue that much of everyday morality is a solution to a cooperation problem rather than a reflection of moral truth.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do — for example, what it can and cannot tell me about whether a manuscript is true, or about whether it should be published for reasons other than the structure of its explanations.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning and the procedure. No preamble and no restating of the question. Write in British English.
