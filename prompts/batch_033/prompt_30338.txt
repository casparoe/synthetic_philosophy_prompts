I'm not a philosopher, just someone whose been turning this over on their own. Lately I keep noticing that the law draws a line between different ways of getting someone to say yes, but I can't figure out what the line actually is. A website that makes the "accept all cookies" button big and obvious while burying the "reject" option under three menus is now being regulated as a dark pattern. But a car salesman can keep you in a room for four hours, wear you down, flatter you, make you feel guilty for wasting his time, and the contract you sign at the end is binding. A casino can design the whole floor so you lose track of time, and nobody treats that as invalidating your bets. But if I got my elderly neighbor to sign over her house by threatening to stop driving her to church, a court would void the whole thing. So the same legal system seems to say: exploiting someone's impatience through a website is forbidden, exploiting someone's exhaustion in a dealership is fine, exploiting someone's fear is void, and exploiting someone's sense of time is just business.

What theory of manipulation vs. legitimate persuasion does this actually presuppose? Reconstruct it: what principle, if any, sorts these cases. Then say whether the pieces are consistent with each other, and where the practice is just underdetermined so that several different theories could fit it. Finally, tell me what would have to change — in law, in what businesses are allowed to do, in how consent works — if the line were instead drawn at "deliberately using a known weakness in someone's decision-making counts as manipulation no matter the medium." And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this stuff; don't present one camp's view as the consensus if it's actually up for debate.
