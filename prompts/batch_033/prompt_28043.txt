I'm a PhD student in philosophy working in population axiology, and I have been struck by how stable the impasse over the repugnant conclusion has become. The main positions have not changed much in a long time. Totalists accept that for any population of people living excellent lives, there is some much larger population of lives barely worth living that is, on their view, better overall. Averagists avoid that conclusion, but at the price of saying that adding a life barely worth living to a population of flourishing lives makes the outcome worse, even when the added person benefits and no one else is affected. Critical-level and critical-range views avoid the conclusion by discounting or not counting welfare below some threshold, but the threshold can look arbitrary or gerrymandered to fit the result. Person-affecting views avoid the conclusion by denying that the mere addition of a happy person can make one outcome better than another, which forces them to give up the seemingly innocent thought that it can be good to create a happy life. Each camp already knows the standard objections, and each concedes that its own view has implications it finds uncomfortable. Formal results in the area also suggest that several independently plausible conditions on population axiology cannot all be satisfied at once, so the choice looks like a matter of which condition to abandon. And yet, as far as I can tell, almost nobody changes their mind.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about welfare or aggregation, but about which cases count as admissible data in the first place. In particular, I suspect the real fault line is whether our intuitive verdicts about astronomically large populations of lives barely worth living, populations nobody has experienced and that may exceed our capacity to imagine vividly, should be allowed to constrain a theory that also has to give plausible verdicts about ordinary, human-scale cases. Some participants treat those distant verdicts as central data; others treat them as noise or as artifacts of a cognitive system that was not built for such comparisons.

I want a diagnosis rather than another defense of one side. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is it substantive and normative, verbal, a difference in which cases are treated as data, a difference about the scope of axiology (whether a theory of the good should even be in the business of ranking outcomes containing entirely different people), or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new formal result, a conceptual distinction, a principled restriction on which intuitions count as evidence, a reframing of the question, or something else? If you think no progress should be expected, explain why, rather than just saying the issue is hard. Please answer in American English.
