Two friends of mine got into an argument at lunch about whether it is rational to be a cooperative person, and they ended up deep in game theory. Liam said that in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defecting strictly dominates cooperating: no matter what the other player does, you come out ahead by defecting. So anyone who cooperates in that situation is acting against their own interests, which is irrational. Maya replied that real life is not a one-shot game. We interact with the same people repeatedly, reputations spread, and in an iterated prisoner's dilemma, cooperating can be rational because it invites future cooperation — tit-for-tat and similar strategies show this. Liam came back with: even in an iterated game, if both players know how many rounds there will be, then on the final round there is no future to protect, so you should defect. But then, knowing that, on the second-to-last round the same logic applies, and backward induction unravels cooperation all the way back to round one. So the iterated game collapses into the one-shot logic after all. Maya said that only works when the number of rounds is known in advance, which almost never happens in real social life — usually the horizon is indefinite — and besides, actual people do not reason by backward induction; they cooperate, and it works out. Liam said that just means people are systematically irrational, not that cooperation is rational. They asked me to settle it and I could not. Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about what — which claims hold up, which do not, and whether either of them is missing something important?
