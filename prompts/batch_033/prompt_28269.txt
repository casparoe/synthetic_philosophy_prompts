I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how philosophical concepts get compressed into rhetorical shortcuts in public discourse. One phrase I keep encountering—in op-eds about AI alignment, in climate-policy debates where someone wants to dismiss gradualism, in a tech CEO's letter to shareholders about "making the most of constraints"—is "the best of all possible worlds." It's almost never used sincerely; it functions as a charge of Panglossian naïveté. But I suspect most people wielding it haven't thought through what Leibniz actually claimed, or what precise thesis they're attributing when they say someone is assuming this is the best of all possible worlds. Could you disambiguate the slogan for me? I want a menu of the distinct claims it could be expressing—from the trivial to the radical—with a verdict on each: which are obviously false, which are defensible, and which are so weak they're barely claims at all. And for each reading, tell me whether it's the one actually being invoked when the phrase is used as a casual dismissal in public debate.
