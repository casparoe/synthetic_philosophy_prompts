I'm a retired cognitive neuroscientist — did visual awareness work for about 35 years, EEG, fMRI, the usual — and I've gotten into philosophy lately, mostly philosophy of mind. I just read Dennett's stuff on heterophenomenology and it's the first thing in that literature that's actually made sense to me as someone who's spent a career collecting subjects' reports: you treat the reports as data about what it's like for the subject, you take them seriously as texts to be interpreted, but you don't treat them as infallible readouts of some Cartesian theater. That middle ground is exactly where I always wanted to be but didn't have the framework for.

Here's the thing. I'm now volunteering as a kind of informal methods advisor at a small consciousness lab — mostly grad students and a postdoc — and they keep asking me: okay, but what do we actually *do*? When a subject says something about their experience, when do we take it at face value, when do we discount it, and when do we treat a mismatch between report and behavior as the interesting thing rather than just noise? I want to give them something they can actually use day to day.

So: write me a decision procedure for handling first-person reports in a consciousness experiment, from the moment you're designing the report collection through to the analysis stage. It should be a sequence of questions to ask, in order, with guidance on what counts as a good enough answer to move on. It needs to handle the genuinely tricky cases: the subject who confidently reports seeing a stimulus they demonstrably could not have seen (timing doesn't work, or it was below threshold), the subject who reports no experience at all during a condition where their behavior clearly shows discrimination, and the report that sounds like a confabulation — internally coherent, detailed, but almost certainly fabricated. The procedure should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as method, and it should say explicitly what heterophenomenology *cannot* do for you — where you just have to make your best guess about what the subject experienced and live with the uncertainty.

Then run it on two cases so I can see it in action: (1) a blindsight subject who denies any visual experience in the blind field but performs well above chance on a forced-choice localization task, and (2) a subject in a backward-masking study who gives rich, metaphorical descriptions of stimuli shown below the objective threshold — descriptions that don't match the actual stimuli but have a strange internal logic to them. I want to see whether the procedure handles these differently, and I especially want to see a case where the procedure tells you something you'd rather not hear — where it forces you to discount a report you'd really like to beleive.
