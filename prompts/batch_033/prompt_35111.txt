I'm a junior taking a course called "Ethics of Artificial Intelligence" in the philosophy department, and we've been working through the alignment problem. One family of proposals that keeps surfacing is the idea of "low-impact AI" or "impact measures" — roughly, the thought that we could make AI systems safe by building them to minimize their side effects, to avoid changing the world too much relative to what would have happened anyway. The motivating intuition is compelling: if you tell an AI to fetch you a coffee, it shouldn't dismantle the solar system to do so, and a natural way to encode that constraint is to say the AI should leave everything else approximately as it was.

But the longer I sit with this proposal, the more I notice a stubborn conceptual knot at its center, and I'm wondering whether it has been identified and named in the philosophical literature — perhaps under a heading I haven't encountered yet. The knot is this: to say what counts as a "side effect" or an "impact" that should be minimized, you seem to need some prior specification of what matters. If you try to define impact in a value-neutral way — say, minimize changes to the number and configuration of atoms in the environment — you get absurdities: the AI might refuse to move anything at all, or it might allow a physically tiny change that is nonetheless catastrophic, like releasing a microgram of aerosolized neurotoxin, on the grounds that the perturbation is negligible by the metric. If you instead try to define impact in terms of what humans would care about, you are back to needing a full specification of human values, which is precisely the problem that low-impact approaches were supposed to circumvent. It looks as though you cannot define "side effects" without already knowing what the "main effects" are supposed to be, and you cannot know what the main effects are without knowing what you value. The proposal seems either to collapse into a demand for a complete value specification, or else to rest on an implicit and unprincipled distinction between what counts as "the task" and what counts as "everything else."

I am not asking whether this objection is fatal to impact-measure approaches — I know there are replies in the literature, such as defining impact relative to a baseline distribution of permissible trajectories or using an attainable-utility preservation criterion. What I am asking is whether this specific conceptual pattern — the difficulty of defining a notion of "side effects" or "low impact" in a way that is simultaneously value-neutral and action-guiding — has a name, or whether it connects to a broader philosophical problem that has been discussed outside the AI alignment context. It feels adjacent to something in the philosophy of action (the distinction between intended and merely foreseen effects?), or perhaps to the problem of specifying ceteris paribus conditions in the philosophy of science, or maybe it is an instance of the frame problem. But none of those quite captures the specific shape of the difficulty as I am experiencing it: the way the notion of "side effect" seems to go indeterminate the moment you try to detach it from a substantive picture of what is worth doing. If there is no established name, I would be interested in how you would characterize the phenomenon — what are its boundaries, what is it a special case of, and what would it take to dissolve the difficulty rather than merely solve a particular instance of it?
