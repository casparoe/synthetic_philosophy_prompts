I'm an undergraduate in an upper-division metaethics seminar, and we're working through non-cognitivism and the Frege–Geach problem this week. A handout my TA distributed summarizes the dialectic as follows:

"Expressivism is the view that moral judgments are not beliefs but expressions of non-cognitive attitudes, paradigmatically approval and disapproval; 'murder is wrong' is roughly equivalent to 'boo to murder.' The Frege–Geach problem is that moral sentences also occur in embedded contexts, as in 'If murder is wrong, then getting your little brother to murder is wrong,' where the speaker plainly is not expressing disapproval of murder, and so expressivism cannot explain what the sentence means there. The deeper difficulty is that valid inference, as in modus ponens, requires truth-apt premises; since expressivist moral judgments are neither true nor false, expressivism makes moral reasoning impossible. Expressivists have replied by saying that moral terms have both descriptive and prescriptive meaning, but this reply is generally regarded as abandoning the view, because once descriptive meaning is admitted moral judgments become beliefs again and the position collapses into a kind of subjectivism."

I have a vague sense that this runs together a few different positions and maybe overstates the force of the problem, but I don't trust my own reading enough to pin down where. Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful expressivist would accept, which are simplifications, and which are inaccurate or unfair? I'd particularly like to know whether the conclusion that expressivism "makes moral reasoning impossible" follows from anything true in the summary, and whether the descriptive-plus-prescriptive reply is really just an abandonment of expressivism.
