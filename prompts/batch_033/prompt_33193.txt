I'm taking a philosophy of mind course this semester, and we've been discussing split-brain cases and what they imply about the unity of consciousness. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two students who disagree about the upshot of the commissurotomy data. One should hold that split-brain patients genuinely host two distinct streams of phenomenal consciousness — that there is something it is like to be the left-hemisphere subject and something it is like to be the right-hemisphere subject, and these are not the same thing. The other should hold that the patient remains a single conscious subject, and that the apparent disunity (one hand buttoning a shirt while the other unbuttons it, the left hand selecting an object the right hand denies seeing) is better explained by disruptions to access, attention, or motor control without splitting the subject of experience itself. Each character should press the other on the hard cases: the speaking hemisphere confabulating explanations for actions initiated by the silent hemisphere, instances of inter-hemispheric conflict, and the question of what would even count as evidence that consciousness is genuinely divided rather than merely fragmented in its contents. Neither side should be a strawman; each should make concessions where appropriate and respond directly to the other's points rather than talking past them.
