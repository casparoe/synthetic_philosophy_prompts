I've been following the debates between longtermists and their critics about discount rates, and I keep noticing that the two sides often agree on the empirical issues — what different discount rates imply for policy, how sensitive cost-benefit analyses are to the choice of rate — while talking past each other on the normative core: whether pure time preference, the practice of discounting future welfare solely because it is farther in time, is ever rationally or morally defensible.

I'd like to see what an adversarial collaboration between a defender of pure time preference and an opponent of it would produce if both approached the question in good faith, treating it as a normative question about what we owe to future people rather than as a debate to be won.

Draft the joint statement they could both sign. Structure it as follows:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides accept — about what pure time preference is (as distinct from discounting due to uncertainty, opportunity cost, or extinction risk), about what standard economic practice does and doesn't show, and about which empirical facts would be relevant if either side were right.

2. A disagreements table. For each point of remaining dispute, give the precise formulation of each side's position, phrased so that side would endorse the wording. Include at minimum: whether pure time preference is rationally permissible; whether it is morally permissible; whether the distinction between agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons matters here; and whether the burden of proof lies with those who would discount or those who wouldn't.

3. A list of cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and for each crux, a concrete argument or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about what it shows stated in advance.

The deliverable is the document itself. Begin with the document, with no preface or commentary.
