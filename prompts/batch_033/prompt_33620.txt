I've been keeping a mental list of my colleague's verdicts on the Nozick-adjacent cases we've argued about over the years. She's an entitlement theorist, so they're mostly in that ballpark, but I think there's some daylight between the rules she could be running. Here's what I've got:

1. Wilt Chamberlain: the distribution that results from everyone freely paying to see him play is just, full stop, even though it trashes any patterned principle you could name.
2. Stolen car resold to an innocent buyer: the distribution is unjust, the original owner has a claim, and the buyer has to give it back — rectification isn't optional just because the current holder is blameless.
3. Homesteading part of an unowned forest, leaving plenty of equivalent land for anyone else who wants some: just acquisition.
4. Inheritance from parents whose wealth traces entirely to just acquisitions and just transfers: the heir's holding is just.
5. Factory dumping waste into a river, harming downstream farmers who never agreed to bear the risk: unjust, and the farmers are owed compensation — it's a rights violation, not just a negative externality to be priced.

Now we're chewing on a new one. A democratic government imposes a modest estate tax — say 2% on estates above a few million — and the revenue funds universal basic education. The existing distribution of wealth is, as a matter of plain historical fact, saturated with past injustices: stolen land, slavery, redlining, and so on. But the tax isn't keyed to any particular past wrong; it's a forward-looking policy aimed at equal opportunity.

If she wants to be consistent with her track record, what should she say? I can see at least two rules that fit her first five verdicts perfectly, and they pull opposite directions here. Which rule do you think she's actually operating with, what does it predict, and what's the further case — one we haven't discussed — that would cleanly seperate the two?
