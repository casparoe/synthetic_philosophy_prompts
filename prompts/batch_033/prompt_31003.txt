I teach a senior seminar in philosophy of mind, and we've just finished a unit on Dennett's intentional stance and the question of whether intentional explanations — the kind that cite beliefs and desires — are genuinely causal or merely convenient summaries of underlying neural processes. One of the central texts was Dennett's 1991 paper "Real Patterns," which introduces the careenium thought experiment. For those unfamiliar: imagine a giant, frictionless container filled with billions of tiny bouncing balls ("simmballs"), some of which are coated with a sticky substance so that when they collide they form aggregates. Over time, these aggregates develop their own macro-level dynamics — they drift, collide, and break apart in ways that are predictable at the aggregate level but computationally intractable if you tried to track every individual simmball. Dennett then asks: if we interpreted certain aggregate configurations as symbols, could those symbols be said to "push the substrate around"? That is, does the symbolic or pattern level have genuine causal powers, or does all the real causal work happen at the level of individual simmballs obeying microphysical laws?

I'm writing the final exam, and I want one essay question that forces students to go beyond summarizing the thought experiment. I want them to grapple with what the careenium actually establishes, where its limits are, and what hangs on the answer for broader debates about mental causation. The question should separate students who can only rehearse Dennett's line from those who can critically evaluate the argument and identify what the thought experiment does and doesn't show.

The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain, and it should flag the common misunderstandings that would pull an otherwise competent answer down to a C. Think carefully about what the crux of the question is — the point where genuine understanding diverges from memorization — and build the marking scheme around that crux.
