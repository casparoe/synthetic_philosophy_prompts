Question (from the exam):

"We distinguished numerical from qualitative identity, and we worked with the following version of the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII): necessarily, if x and y share all the same qualitative properties (intrinsic and relational), then x = y. (a) Explain the difference between numerical and qualitative identity, and say what PII would tell us about the relationship between the two if it were true. (b) Describe Black's two-sphere universe and explain what it is supposed to show about PII. (c) Does Black give a decisive counterexample to PII as stated above? Defend your answer, considering at least one objection to your view."

Student's answer:

"Numerical identity is the relation a thing bears to itself and to nothing else; qualitative identity is sharing all the same qualities. Two mass-produced coins can be qualitatively identical while still being two coins, so the two notions can come apart in ordinary cases. PII is the principle that, at the fundamental level, they cannot come apart: if two things were qualitatively indiscernible in every way, they would not be two things at all but one and the same thing. That makes PII an interesting constraint on how much variety a world needs in order to contain distinct objects.

Black's thought experiment asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of exactly the same size, shape, composition, and color, seperated by some fixed distance. Since nothing else exists, there is no observer and no background feature that could pick out one sphere as the left one or the right one. The two spheres share all their intrinsic properties, and they also share their relational properties: each is two miles from an iron sphere, and each is the same distance from the center of mass of the two-sphere system. Yet the description still says there are two spheres. So Black's universe is a possible world containing two distinct but qualitatively indiscernible objects, which directly violates PII as stated. This shows that PII is not a logical truth.

Someone might object that the spheres are not really indiscernible because each sphere is two miles from the other sphere. But that is a relational property both spheres possess: each has the property of being two miles from an iron sphere. The only properties that could distinguish them are impure properties like being identical to sphere A, and the version of PII we are using is restricted to qualitative properties, so those don't count. I think Black's example succeeds. Since PII fails in at least one possible world, it cannot be a necessary truth, and so the actual world must also contain distinct but qualitatively indiscernible things."
