I'm a product manager at a mid-size health-tech startup. We're building a wearable that tracks sleep stages, heart rate variability, blood oxygen, and a few other things — and we keep running into the same kind of decision: should we collect this additional metric? Should we share this data with a research partner? Should we let users grant access to a family member? The legal team gives us the compliance picture (GDPR, HIPAA, the usual), but that's a floor, and I've been around long enough to know that "we're allowed to" is not the same as "we should." I've been trying to get my head around the philosophy of privacy — the idea that privacy isn't just about hiding things or controlling information, that it's bound up with relationships, context, autonomy, the kind of person you get to be when you're not being watched — and I'm convinced that a purely checkbox-compliance approach misses what actually matters. But I'm not a philosopher, and my team needs something we can actually run when we're in a sprint planning meeting and someone says "hey, what if we added passive audio sampling to detect sleep apnea?"

What I want is a decision procedure — a checklist or a flowchart in words — that we can apply to any proposed data practice (collection, retention, sharing, derived analytics) to decide whether it's justified. It should be grounded in a coherent philosophical account of what privacy is actually for and why it matters, but it has to be usable by a product manager and an engineer in a room, not by someone who's read Nissenbaum. Tell me what questions to ask, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and — I'm insistent on this — where it says "a human has to make a judgment call here, and here's what the judgment is about." Then run it on two concrete proposals: (1) we want to add a passive mic that samples thirty seconds of ambient audio every hour during sleep to flag respiratory-distress patterns, and (2) we want to share de-identified long-term HRV and sleep trends with an academic group studying long COVID. 

And one more thing: when you lay out the procedure, tell me how confident you actually are in the philosophical claims it rests on, and single out the one step in the procedure where you're least sure — the place where someone who starts from a different theory of what privacy is for would construct the checklist differently, and what that alternative would look like.
