I'm giving a talk at a decision theory workshop next month defending the claim that the Pasadena game doesn't refute expected utility theory — it just shows the theory was never meant to handle conditionally convergent expectations in the first place. My line is: restrict EU to gambles with absolutely summable payoffs, and for the rest, fall back on statewise dominance plus a couple of other structural principles (like, don't pay to swap labels on states). I am NOT going to argue for a summation convention, weak expectations, or any other way of assigning a definite value to the Pasadena game — I think that whole project is a mistake.

The audience: about twenty people, mostly formal epistemologists and decision theorists. Two of them (I know who they are) are committed to the idea that rationality requires complete preferences over all gambles, and they think countable additivity plus some kind of summation convention can save that. There's also a philosopher of probability who thinks the real lesson is that we should reject the use of infinite state spaces in decision theory altogether. And there's an economist who thinks this is all angels on pinheads because no real gamble has an infinite payoff structure.
