I'm prepping for a panel discussion on AI ethics that my local rationalist meetup is hosting, and we've got a philosopher coming in who defends the existence of responsibility gaps for autonomous systems. You know the argument — roughly, when a learning system does something harmful post-deployment, the designers couldn't have predicted it from the training setup, the users deployed it in good faith, and the system itself isn't a candidate for moral responsibility, so no one's left holding the bag. He's been writing about this for years and I want to push past the introductory stuff and get into the actual cruxes.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions with follow-ups. A few specific things I want to hit:

- Whether the gap is supposed to be metaphysical or "merely" epistemic. If we had a god's-eye view of the training data, the optimization landscape, and the exact sequence of weight updates, would the gap close? And if he concedes it's epistemic, does that actually matter for responsibility ascription — like, is there a principled difference between "no one can be held responsible given what we know" and "no one is responsible"?

- The analogy to the "many hands" problem in organizational contexts. Defenders of the gap often say autonomous systems are different because with a corporation you can at least in principle trace decisions back to identifiable people, whereas with a trained model the causal chain is smeared across the training distribution and the optimizer in a way that dilutes agency. I want a question that pushes on whether that dilution is really different in kind or just in degree — and if it's just degree, why the existing legal tools for diffused responsibility (like corporate liability) can't be adapted.

- What the view actually implies about deployment decisions. If there's genuinely no one to blame when things go wrong, does that mean we should ban autonomous systems from high-stakes domains outright, or is there a way to patch the gap with something like mandatory insurance pools or no-fault compensation funds? And if he's ok with no-fault compensation, does that amount to admitting we can handle the practical problem without solving the philosophical one — in which case, what work is the philosophical gap doing?

- The standard reply that goes: designers should just be held to a negligence standard, and deploying a system you know might learn unexpected behaviors is itself negligent. I want a question that makes him engage with this directly, and then a follow-up for when he says that stretches negligence beyond recognition. Press him on whether negligence law already handles radical unpredictability — like in medical malpractice where a drug has side effects no one could have forseen — without collapsing, and what's different here.

- Whether the problem changes if the system is corrigible or has some kind of off-switch that's robust to the system's own optimization. If the user can intervene but doesn't, does that shift responsibility back onto the user? And does the literature on automation bias and the vigilance decrement complicate that — like, is it realistic to expect a human supervisor to stay meaningfully "in the loop" when the system is right 99.9% of the time?

- Whether the responsibility gap is a feature rather than a bug in some cases. Like, sometimes we deliberately want to offload decisions to a system precisely so no individual has to carry the moral weight (think of the classic "who should the trolley hit" scenario). Does his framework have room for distinguishing between cases where the gap is a problem and cases where it's actually the point?

- And a final question about what would change his mind. What's an empirical or technological development that would make him say "ok, responsibility was properly assignable here, the gap is closed"? What features would a system have to have, or what would the deployment context have to look like?

Make the follow-ups actually anticipate the move he's most likely to make and press the weakest point — none of this "could you expand on that" filler. I want the kind of questions where he has to pause and think.
