I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, working on higher-order theories of consciousness. My advisor has given the following verdicts across seminars and conversations:

- Adult humans are conscious.
- Dogs are conscious.
- Human newborns are conscious.
- Blindsight patients are not conscious of stimuli in the blind field.
- Current large language models are not conscious.
- A person in deep, dreamless sleep is not conscious.
- A person with advanced dementia who can no longer form thoughts about their own mental states is not conscious.
- A split-brain patient whose right hemisphere is shown a stimulus is conscious of it, even though the speaking left hemisphere denies seeing anything.

She hasn't yet addressed the following case. A person is in a flow state — fully absorbed in a skilled activity, highly responsive to the environment, but later unable to report many details of the experience. Neuroimaging suggests that during the flow state, activity in prefrontal regions associated with higher-order thought and self-monitoring is reduced relative to normal waking consciousness, while sensory and motor regions remain highly active. Is this person conscious throughout?

I'm less interested in the answer itself than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her verdicts equally well, and which of those rules would give different answers here. Please separate what is settled among higher-order theorists from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
