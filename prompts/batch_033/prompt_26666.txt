Two colleagues in my department got into an argument over lunch about Maxwell's demon, and I have been turning it over ever since. I would like you to go through their exchange and adjudicate it — not just who is right overall, but who is right about which specific claims and why.

Here is roughly how it went.

Chen said that the demon thought experiment shows that information is physical. The demon cannot actually violate the second law because acquiring information about molecular positions and velocities necessarily costs entropy — measurement itself is dissipative. He cited Szilard's 1929 paper and said this was the standard resolution for decades.

Dana pushed back. She said that picture had been superseded. Bennett showed in the 1980s that measurement can in principle be done reversibly — you can imagine a demon that determines a molecule's position without any thermodynamic cost. The real resolution, she said, is Landauer's principle: it is the erasure of information, not its acquisition, that carries an unavoidable entropy cost. The demon can measure reversibly but eventually fills its memory, and when it erases that memory to make room, the entropy increase from erasure exactly compensates for the apparent decrease from sorting. So the second law is saved, but at the erasure step, not the measurement step.

Chen replied that this just pushes the problem back. If the demon can measure reversibly for some finite time, does that not mean the second law can be temporarily violated during that window before erasure kicks in? He said that even a temporary violation ought to worry someone who takes the second law seriously.

Dana answered that the second law is a statistical claim about ensembles over sufficient timescales, not a prohibition on every momentary fluctuation. Over the full cycle — measurement, sorting, and eventual erasure — the net entropy change is non-negative, and that is all the second law requires. There is no violation, not even a temporary one, of the properly understood law.

Chen then shifted ground. He said that even if Dana was right about the mechanism, the slogan "information is physical" still captured something true and important: the demon paradox reveals a deep connection between thermodynamics and information that was not appreciated before. Whether the cost comes at measurement or at erasure, the fact that information and entropy are linked is the real lesson.

Dana disagreed. She said "information is physical" is too vague to be useful — it does not tell you which physical operations on information are reversible and which are not. The precise lesson, she said, is that logical irreversibility implies thermodynamic irreversibility. That is a much more specific and powerful claim, and it is what makes Landauer's principle interesting. Saying "information is physical" just sounds profound without actually constraining anything.

Chen said that seemed like a rhetorical point, not a physical one — they agreed on the physics and were just arguing about how to summarize it.

Dana said no, the summary matters, because the older "measurement is dissipative" picture actively misled people about what was possible in principle, and getting the summary right is part of getting the physics right.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each turn, say who has the better of it and why. Then step back and say what the demon thought experiment actually teaches us about the relationship between information and thermodynamics, and whether either of them has missed something important that would reframe the whole dispute.
