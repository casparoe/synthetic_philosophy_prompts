im working on a piece about why its so hard to draw the line between healthy skepticism and conspiracy thinking, and ive been reading some of the philosophy on this. Heres the tension I keep coming back to: some philosophers (the particularists, I guess?) basically say theres no principled epistemic difference between a conspiracy theory and, say, investigative journalism — both involve positing hidden causes, questioning official accounts, connecting dots. The label "conspiracy theory" is just a pejorative we slap on theories we dont like. Meanwhile other philosophers say no, conspiracy theories have structural features — being self-sealing, relying on what they call crippled epistemology, that kind of thing — that make them genuinely distinctive as a bad epistemic kind. What are the most important implications of this debate for how ordinary people should actually navigate a world where some official accounts really are lies and some alternative accounts really are paranoid fantasies? If the particularists are right, does that mean we should be way more epistemically humble about dismissing fringe claims? And if the generalists are right, can we actually operationalize their criteria in a way that doesnt just reduce to "trust the mainstream"? I'm less interested in who wins the abstract debate and more in what hangs on it for actual epistemic practice.
