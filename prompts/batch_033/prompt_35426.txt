I'm a journalist preparing a feature on philosophical approaches to vagueness for a magazine that covers science and ideas. I'm trying to get clear on the substantive differences between epistemicism and supervaluationism as rival accounts of vague language. Both theories, as I understand them, aim to resolve the sorites paradox without simply discarding classical logic, and both reject the idea that borderline statements are straightforwardly neither true nor false in a way that would require us to revise our basic logical framework. Yet they locate the source of vagueness in fundamentally different places—one in our knowledge, the other in our language. Could you walk me through where these two views come apart, both in their underlying metaphysical commitments and in what they predict about particular borderline cases? I'm especially interested in how each handles higher-order vagueness—the worry that notions like "admissible precisification" or "determinate truth" might themselves be vague—and in whether one theory does a better job of explaining the phenomenology of vagueness: why we feel genuinely torn about borderline cases rather than simply aware that we lack precise information.
