I've been working on a piece about modal metaphysics — you know, possible worlds, what it means to say something "could have been otherwise" — and I've gotten myself genuinely stuck on the Lewis vs. Kripke divide over counterpart theory. For readers who haven't spent their week down this particular rabbit hole: when we say "I could have been a journalist in Paris instead of New York," are we pointing to a possible world where I myself — numerically identical me — am in Paris, or are we pointing to a world containing someone very similar to me, a "counterpart," who's doing the Paris thing? Kripke and most philosophers go with transworld identity (it's me in that world); David Lewis says no, individuals are world-bound and we're always talking about counterparts, and the similarity relation that picks them out is vague and context-dependent.

Here's what I want: give me your actual credence that Lewis's counterpart theory is the right account of de re modal predication. Not "it depends on your metaphysics of modality" — I want a number and the decomposition. How much of your credence rides on whether possible worlds are concrete (Lewis's modal realism) versus abstract or ersatz? How much on whether our ordinary modal talk really tracks identity or similarity? How much on whether the problem of accidental intrinsics — the puzzle about how I can be five-foot-ten in this world but five-foot-eleven in another without contradiction — actually forces us to counterparts rather than some other fix? And what sub-question would move your number the most if you got new evidence or arguments on it? I'm less interested in the final number than in seeing where the load-bearing joints in the reasoning actually are — my editor keeps telling me I need to stop reporting on what philosophers beleive and start reporting on why they disagree, and this feels like a way in.
