I was talking with my roommate last night — he's a physics grad student — and he basically laughed when I brought up causation by omission. He said, "An omission isn't a thing, it's the absence of a thing. You can't point to it in spacetime. So how could it possibly cause anything?" And honestly, I don't have a good answer. I tried saying something about counterfactuals — like, if I had watered the plants, they wouldn't have died — but he shot back that counterfactuals are just a way we talk, not a metaphysics of causation. He said if I really believe omissions cause things, I might as well say the entire rest of the universe caused the plants to die, since every person who didn't water them is equally guilty of an omission, and that's clearly absurd.

Now I'm spiraling. If omissions aren't real events, and causation has to involve real events, then all this talk about "causing by failing to act" is just confused moralizing dressed up as metaphysics, right? It feels like we notice the omissions that matter to us for practical or moral reasons and then retroactively pretend they're causes. But then why do so many smart philosophers take causation by omission seriously — is there actually a way to make sense of it, or is my roommate right and the whole thing is a category mistake?
