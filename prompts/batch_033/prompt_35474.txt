I teach an upper-level philosophy of physics seminar, and this term we spent several weeks on the Everett interpretation and the problem of probability within it. My students have worked through the formal picture of branching, the failure of any simple branch-counting rule, and the main families of response: decision-theoretic derivations in the style of Deutsch and Wallace, self-locating-uncertainty accounts along the lines of Sebens and Carroll, Zurek's envariance-based arguments, and the skeptical objections that these derivations are circular or presuppose the very probabilities they claim to derive. In past years the exam answers on this topic have tended either to recite the taxonomy of positions or to drift into general remarks about determinism, so this year I want a question that cannot be answered that way.

For the final, to be written in 75 minutes, I would like one essay question that forces students to do two things at once: state precisely why the Born rule poses a problem in a theory whose dynamics are deterministic and whose branches are all equally real, and evaluate whether any proposed solution manages to ground rational constraints on credence without already treating branch weights as probabilities. Please write the question, a model answer of around 800 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks that distinguishes a first-class answer from a merely competent one by naming the specific moves each must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — for example, conflating subjective uncertainty with objective stochasticity, or asserting that branch weights just are probabilities.
