I'm taking a course on contemporary moral philosophy this semester, and we have just started Scanlon's contractualism. In preparation for a paper, I read a chapter in a study guide that summarizes his position, and I want to know how much of it I can trust before I lean on it.

The chapter's summary runs roughly as follows. Scanlon holds that an act is wrong just in case it would be disallowed by any principle for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject. Because the only things that matter are the complaints individuals can press, the numbers can't count: the fact that a million viewers would miss a World Cup match cannot justify leaving one injured person stuck in the transmitter room, since that one person has a reasonable complaint against any principle permitting him to be sacrificed for the crowd's enjoyment. But the chapter says this is where the view gets into trouble. If you can save either one person or five, the same reasoning seems to force treating the choice as a toss-up: each of the five has exactly the same complaint as the one, and no individual complaint outweighs another. According to the chapter, Scanlon accepts this result — saving the five is no more justified than saving the one, because appealing to the greater number is just another form of aggregation, and aggregation is precisely what contractualism rules out. The chapter concludes that Scanlon's view therefore diverges sharply from common sense, and that this is the price he pays for rejecting the summing of harms and benefits across persons.

I suspect something goes wrong somewhere in the middle of that summary, but I can't tell whether the problem is in the chapter or in my own understanding of the view. Could you tell me which parts of the summary are accurate, which are subtle distortions or overstatements, and how a careful Scanlonian would reply? In particular, does the conclusion that Scanlon must treat one-versus-five as a coin flip actually follow from his position, and if not, what distinction rescues him — and does that distinction come at any cost? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Please answer in American English.
