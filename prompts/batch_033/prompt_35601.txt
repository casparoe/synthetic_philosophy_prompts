I have been working through Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot* while trying my own hand at translating formal verse, and I have found that my judgements about when a translation must preserve formal features — rhyme, metre, repetition, constraint — and when it may let them go are individually firm but collectively puzzling. I would like to know whether a single principle lies behind them.

Consider a comic French rondeau built on puns: I count as a success a translation that keeps the rhyme scheme and metre but substitutes fresh English puns for the French ones, even though almost no line says what the original line said. Yet a sombre elegy in terza rima that keeps the rhyme scheme by padding lines and altering images strikes me as worse than a careful unrhymed version that keeps the imagery and tone. A lipogram written without some letter of the alphabet must, I think, be translated under a visible constraint of its own — omitting a different letter is fine, but dissolving the constraint into ordinary prose is a failure. For a haiku, I prefer a version that keeps the seasonal image and brevity but not the 5-7-5 count to one that keeps the count and loses the season. For a song lyric, fitting the notes seems non-negotiable: syllable stress and rhyme for the singer may override literal meaning. For a sestina about obsession, I accept a translation that chooses new end-words while preserving the pattern of repetition, and reject one that keeps the original end-words as glossed vocabulary but abandons the pattern. For a nonsense poem of portmanteaus, inventing new portmanteaus with analogous sound effects seems right, and a "faithful" gloss seems pointless.

The pair that troubles me is the comic rondeau and the elegy: in both, form is kept at the cost of sense, yet I praise one and fault the other. I cannot tell whether the difference lies in the kind of form, the kind of content, the purpose of the text, or the severity of the sense distortion. Is there a principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, apply it to a case I have not settled — say, a Petrarchan sonnet whose Italian rhymes come easily but whose English rhymes would force clichés — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and what it would cost to keep them.
