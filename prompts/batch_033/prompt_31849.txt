I'm an analyst at a large e-commerce company, and I've been studying how our executive team has handled metric problems over the past five years. I've compiled their track record on seven occasions where a team reported that a performance metric was being gamed:

1. **Warehouse**: workers were meeting their "items picked per hour" target by skipping quality checks. The executive team replaced the metric with a composite of speed and accuracy. (*Changed the metric.*)

2. **Customer service**: agents were hitting their "tickets closed per day" target by closing tickets prematurely, forcing customers to open new ones. The executive team kept the metric but added a "reopen rate" counter-metric and a monthly audit of a random sample of closed tickets. (*Kept the metric, added safeguards.*)

3. **Sales**: the team was hitting quarterly revenue targets by discounting heavily in the final weeks, which eroded margins. The executive team kept the revenue target but added a margin floor below which deals wouldn't count toward the target. (*Kept the metric, added constraints.*)

4. **Engineering**: the "story points completed per sprint" metric was leading teams to inflate their estimates. The executive team removed the metric entirely and instructed engineering managers to evaluate team output qualitatively. (*Removed the metric.*)

5. **Marketing**: the "cost per lead" metric was being gamed by running campaigns that generated large volumes of low-quality leads. The executive team replaced it with "cost per qualified lead," where qualification required that a lead had completed a sales call. (*Changed the metric.*)

6. **Content**: the "articles published per month" metric was driving a decline in quality. The executive team kept the metric but added a mandatory editorial review step before publication. (*Kept the metric, added safeguards.*)

7. **Recruiting**: the "time to fill" metric was leading recruiters to rush candidates through the pipeline. The executive team kept the metric as-is, arguing that the problem was in execution, not measurement, and that managers should coach their recruiters to resist the pressure. (*Kept the metric, no changes.*)

Now we're facing a new case. The product team's "features shipped per quarter" metric has been in place for three years. Over the past year, the team has been shipping smaller and smaller features — many are minor UI tweaks that users barely notice. The team's velocity number looks excellent, but the product isn't meaningfully improving. The head of product wants to keep the metric but add a requirement that each feature receive a "user impact" score before it counts toward the quarterly target. The head of engineering argues that any shipping metric will eventually be gamed and that we should abandon quantitative targets for shipping entirely and evaluate the team's output qualitatively, as we did with engineering story points.

Based on our executive team's track record, what would they most likely decide? Which precedent is the closest analog to this situation? And if their precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle would they be adopting with each choice?
