I am a philosopher of action and moral responsibility, and I want to test a suspicion about the debate over Frankfurt cases and the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. My suspicion is that the causal and modal structure of the debate translates almost exactly into the vocabulary of control engineering, but that everything normative — above all, the concept of moral responsibility itself — is left behind as residue, and that the exercise may reveal that the philosophical debate has been running together a descriptive-modal question and a normative question that control engineering keeps distinct.

Please assume I can follow control-theoretic vocabulary at the level of a well-informed outsider rather than a practitioner: I know what state variables, outputs, sensors, actuators, controllers, setpoints, feedback and feedforward control, disturbances, observability, controllability, reachable sets, overrides, dead man's switches, watchdog timers, and supervisory control are, and I can read a simple block diagram. Do not assume I know recent technical results or specialized notation.

I would like you to do two things.

First, restate the following elements of the philosophical debate in that vocabulary, one at a time, as faithfully as you can, without smuggling in control-theoretic assumptions and presenting them as part of the original view:

1. The Principle of Alternative Possibilities: an agent is morally responsible for what she did only if she could have done otherwise.
2. A standard Frankfurt-style case, in which an intervener stands ready to make an agent decide and act in a certain way if the agent shows some prior sign that she is about to do otherwise; the agent goes ahead and decides on her own; the intervener never acts; and the philosopher draws the conclusion that the agent is still morally responsible even though the intervener's setup made it impossible for her to do otherwise.
3. The distinction between the actual sequence — what actually happened — and the alternative or counterfactual sequences that the agent's own capacities would have made available.
4. The flicker-of-freedom reply, which concedes that the agent could not have done otherwise with respect to the terminal action but insists that she retained some alternative possibility — for example, she could have done otherwise with respect to the prior sign or initiating event that would have triggered the intervener.
5. The standard rejoinder that such a flicker is too slight, too involuntary, or too remote from the action to be the sort of alternative possibility that could ground moral responsibility.
6. The distinction among leeway incompatibilism, sourcehood incompatibilism, and semi-compatibilism: roughly, the views that responsibility requires alternative possibilities; that responsibility requires the agent to be the ultimate source of her action in a way incompatible with determinism; and that responsibility does not require alternative possibilities even if free will does.

Second, give me a residue report. For each of the six items, say whether it translates cleanly, only with distortion, or not at all, and mark the places where you had to fudge. In particular:

- Does being morally responsible have any counterpart in control engineering, or does the translation quietly replace a normative property with a descriptive or modal one?
- When the engineering vocabulary distinguishes the agent's own dynamics from the composed system that includes the supervising intervener, which of the two is the right object of the claim that she could not have done otherwise? Does the philosophical debate have a stable answer here, or does the translation expose an ambiguity the debate has been carrying?
- Does the question whether the sign the intervener monitors is itself a voluntary act or a mere event correspond to a real engineering distinction — for example, between an observable output under the agent's control and an internal state variable that is not — and does that mapping help or distort?
- Is there anything in the philosophical debate that has no counterpart in the control-theoretic vocabulary at all? If so, is that because the vocabulary is missing something, or because the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked?

Finally, give me your overall assessment of my suspicion. Is control engineering able to express the structure of Frankfurt cases exactly while leaving the normative content as residue? And does the exercise show that the philosophical debate has been running on two separable questions — a descriptive-modal question about what the system could have done, and a normative question about what the agent is answerable for — that its own vocabulary keeps fused?
