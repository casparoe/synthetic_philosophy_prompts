I spent thirty years in a wet lab before retiring, so I'm new to this stuff — forgive me if I'm mangling the terminology. I've been reading about self-knowledge and introspection, and I've built a little argument that seems to prove we know nothing about our own minds. That's obviously nuts — I know I have a headache right now — but I can't locate the mistake. I'd be grateful if you'd diagnose it, working through it slowly. You're an expert philosopher of mind; please think step by step and don't pull any punches.

Here's the argument.

(1) Introspection is a process. It takes time, involves mechanisms, and can go wrong. (If it weren't a process — if it were direct, unmediated access to our own mental states — there'd be nothing for psychologists to study, and all the careful experimental work on introspective failure would be impossible.)

(2) For any process that can go wrong, we can ask: under what conditions is its output reliable? And to answer that, we need a theory of the process — what it's sensitive to, what noise it picks up, what its boundary conditions are.

(3) To build a theory of introspection, we need data: cases where introspection succeeds and cases where it fails. We get this data by comparing introspective reports against something else — behavior, physiology, the reports of others, theoretical coherence. No way around it — you can't calibrate a instrument using only the instrument itself. My old lab would've thrown me out for trying.

(4) But here's the bind: in every case where we catch introspection failing, we're relying on some other source of evidence — third-person data — to overrule the introspective report. And in every case where we confirm that introspection succeeded, we're doing the exact same thing. So our theory of when introspection is reliable rests entirely on non-introspective evidence. There's no point in the whole enterprise where introspection gets to certify itself.

(5) If our theory of when introspection is reliable rests entirely on non-introspective evidence, then introspection never provides independent justification for any particular introspective judgment. The justification always bottoms out in third-person data. Which seems backwards — shouldn't introspection be the thing that gives us our most direct grip on our own minds?

(6) But if justification for introspective judgments always depends on third-person data, then I don't have first-person authority about my own mind. In fact, I have less authority than an outside observer with all the data — their in at least as good a position as I am to say whether I'm in pain.

(7) That can't be right. When I stub my toe, I don't need to run an experiment to know I'm in pain. So something between (1) and (6) has to give.

I'm most reluctant to abandon (1) — the idea that introspection is a process seems like basic scientific realism about the mind, and giving it up feels like throwing out psychology to save philosophy. The move from (6) to (7) strikes me as solid, though maybe "first-person authority" is doing more work than I realize. I suspect the trouble is somewhere around (4) or (5), maybe in the phrase "rests entirely on" — but I can't see how to weaken it without letting the skeptical conclusion back in through a side door. Walk me through where the argument breaks, and tell me honestly what it costs to stop it. Please write your answer in American English.
