(1) slurs and their neutral counterparts have the same truth-conditional content. "john is a [slur]" is true iff john is a member of the relevant group, full stop.

(2) the offensiveness of slurs is best analyzed as a conventional implicature in the potts-style framework — it's not at-issue content, it's not a presupposition, it's a CI.

(3) relatedly, I think conventional implicatures are speaker-oriented — they commit the speaker, not the common ground — and this is the right way to think about how slur offensiveness works.

(4) I think slurs can be used non-offensively in quotation contexts and in metalinguistic discussion. like, reporting what someone said, or discussing the word itself as a linguist.

(5) but I also think the offensiveness of a slur *cannot* be cancelled by speaker intentions. you can't say "john is a [slur], but I don't mean anything derogatory by it" and have the utterance come out fine. it just doesn't work.

(6) I think in-group reclamation is a real phenomenon — the same lexical item can be offensive when used by an out-group member and non-offensive (or at least not-offensive-in-the-same-way) when used by an in-group member.
