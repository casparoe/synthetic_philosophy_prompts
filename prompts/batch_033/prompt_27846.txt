I'm a PhD student in political theory, so I read a fair bit of moral philosophy but I'm not really in that field. Lately I've been noticing a pattern in how people (me included) try to decide what to do when they aren't sure which moral view is right. The usual move is to treat it like any other uncertainty: list the candidate views, weight each by how plausible you think it is, and pick the action with the best expected score. But the output seems weirdly senstive to how you list the views. If I'm torn between two broad famillies, and one of them I've only ever seen stated as a single tight position while the other I've seen in a dozen slightly different formulations, then just listing 'the theories' and weighting them equally gives the second family twelve times the pull, even though my actual credence is basically fifty-fifty between the families. I saw the same thing in a seminar once: someone treated consequentialism as one view but deontology as a loose bag of contractarian, Kantian, and Rossian views, then concluded that the class was mostly deontologists — but the conclusion was really an artifact of the sorting, not of anyone's credences. Is there a name for this, the way the result depends on how finely you individuate the theories you're hedging across? I know decision theory has things to say about partition-dependence and how you carve up states, but this seems different, because the 'states' here are normative views and there's no obvious principled way to count them. And is the standard response just 'don't double-count near-duplicate theories', or is that a band-aid over a deeper problem about whether any weighting across moral views is well-defined in the first place? I'm not really after a label — I want to know what the phenomenon is, how it relates to the nearest concepts, and where it breaks down.
