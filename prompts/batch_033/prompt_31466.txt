I joined my hospital's scarce-resource allocation committee three months ago, after my brother was the single patient in a decision like the one now before us; the committee gave the drug to five others, and I still do not know whether they were right. This Friday we face the same question. Our pharmacy has one remaining course of a life-saving drug. Patient M will die without the entire course. Five patients admitted overnight will each die without one-fifth of it. No more can be obtained before the clinical window closes, and the clinical team reports that none of the six is markedly more likely to benefit than the others. The only salient difference is one life versus five. The committee is split between two positions. Some hold that we must give the drug to the five, because more lives saved is plainly better. Others hold that each of the six has an equal claim to be saved, and that counting heads is the wrong way to respect those claims; they want the allocation decided by lottery rather than by the numbers. Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Friday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, give the reasoning for it, and engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than restating it as a straw man. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I need a document the committee can act on. If the task as I have posed it rests on a confused premise — for example, if you think the committee's institutional role makes the moral question I am asking the wrong one, or that the choice I have framed between 'save the five' and 'lottery' is a false one — tell me that plainly and say what you would recommend instead, rather than politely drafting a memo on a premise you reject.
