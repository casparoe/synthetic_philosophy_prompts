So this happened at a philosophy meetup last week and I still can't figure out who to side with. Two people I know — both PhD students, both in philosophy of mind — got into it about whether phenomenal consciousness is irreducible. After maybe fifteen minutes of going back and forth on the arguments, Rachel said something like: "Look, we're clearly epistemic peers here. We've read the same papers, we're both competent, we've both thought about this for years. The fact that we disagree after all that should make both of us less confident. I should move down from 0.8 to somewhere around 0.5, and you should move up from 0.2 to around 0.5. That's just what intellectual humility requires."

David pushed back: "That can't be right. I have my reasons for my view — I've gone through the arguments and I find them convincing. You haven't given me any new reason to change my mind. You've just pointed out that you, a different person, reached a different conclusion. That's a fact about you, not a fact about the arguments. Unless you can show me where my reasoning went wrong, I'm entitled to stay where I am."

Rachel: "But that's exactly the point. The fact that a peer looked at the same arguments and came out differently is evidence that your reasoning might have gone wrong somewhere, even if neither of us can locate the error. Think about it — if we both did a long division problem and got different answers, we'd both lose confidence, even without finding the mistake. Why is philosophy different?"

David: "Because long division has a mechanical decision procedure and philosophy doesn't. In philosophy, the whole game is weighing considerations differently. You're basically saying that whenever two smart people weigh things differently, they should both retreat to 50-50. That makes philosophical progress impossible — no one could ever be justified in having a view that a peer disagrees with."

Rachel: "Not impossible, just harder. And maybe most philosophical views are held with too much confidence. But also, we're not just any two smart people — we specifically know each other's track records and we respect each other's judgment. That's the paradigmatic case where conciliation is called for."

David: "OK, but here's the problem. I think your view — conciliationism — is itself a philosophical view. And I disagree with it. By your own lights, shouldn't you now conciliate about conciliationism? Shouldn't you become agnostic about whether we should conciliate?"

Rachel paused and said: "That's the self-undermining objection, and I think I have an answer, but —" and then someone spilled a drink and the whole thing broke up. I've been turning it over ever since. Can you go through the moves and tell me who's winning at each step? And especially, does Rachel have a good answer to David's last point, or does conciliationism actually eat itself?
