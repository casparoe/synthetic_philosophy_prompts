So I've been reporting on algorithmic fairness for a piece and I've ended up in a bit of a logical cul-de-sac. Maybe you can help me find the exit.

Here's the chain of reasoning I keep coming back to:

(1) If an algorithm is fair, it should treat different groups — say, men and women applying for loans — the same way when it comes to its mistakes. That means three things: the false positive rate should be the same across groups (it shouldn't reject qualified women at a higher rate than qualified men), the false negative rate should be the same (it shouldn't approve unqualified men more often than unqualified women), and the algorithm should be calibrated across groups (when it says someone has a 70% chance of repaying, that should actually be true for 70% of the people it says that about, regardless of group).

(2) There's a formal result — Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan proved it, and Chouldechova got there independantly around the same time — showing that you can't satisfy all three of those criteria at once unless either the base rates are identical across groups (which they almost never are in the real world) or your algorithm is literally perfect (which it never is).

(3) So for any real-world algorithm making decisions about real people, at least one of those fairness criteria has to be violated.

(4) But if you're violating a basic fairness criterion, your algorithm isn't fair.

(5) Therefore, no real-world algorithm can be fair.

(6) Which means all these fairness certifications and bias audits that companies are rolling out are, at bottom, selling something impossible.

I know the conclusion is too strong — it feels like one of those arguments that proves we can't have a perfectly round square and then declares geometry bankrupt. But I can't pinpoint where the slide happens. Is the problem in (1), that I'm assuming fairness is just about error-rate parity? Is it in (4), that violating one criterion doesn't mean you're unfair full stop? Or is the impossibility result itself being misapplied somehow? And if I have to give one of these up, what's the least bad one to lose?

I'd love the answer in British English if you don't mind — house style.
