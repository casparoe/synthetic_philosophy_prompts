I'm in a philosophy of mind course, and we have just finished the unit on the extended mind thesis. Before I go further in the literature, I want to see whether my own reactions to cases are even consistent, and whether there is a principle behind them. Here they are. An Alzheimer's patient who writes new information in a notebook he carries everywhere and consults without deliberation: I think he knows what is in it, in much the same way someone with ordinary biological memory does. A colleague who keeps addresses in a pocket address book he has to search for, and sometimes leaves at home: I do not think the book is his memory. Someone who cannot navigate anywhere without turn-by-turn directions on her phone: I am drawn to saying the phone is part of her navigational competence. But when the phone dies mid-trip, I do not say she forgot the way; I say she lost a tool. A deaf person whose cochlear implant turns sound into signals her auditory nerve can use: I think the implant is part of her hearing. A chess player who can only calculate by physically moving pieces on a board: the board looks like part of the thinking. But a novelist who dictates drafts to a recorder and says her ideas are on tape: I do not want to call the tape part of her mind. A monk who has memorized a liturgy knows it; a monk who relies on a spinning prayer wheel to do the reciting does not know it. Is there a single principle, or a small set of principles, that gets all or nearly all of these verdicts? What does the principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on, of someone who keeps a diary she rereads every morning and says it holds the person she is? Please state the principle precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, apply it to a further case I have not listed, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Write the answer in continuous prose, as in an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
