So I cover AI and the behavioral sciences for a living, and i've been hearing the same pitch from every direction lately: our system watches what people do and recovers what they actually want. Sometimes it's a robot learning from demonstrations, sometimes it's a model fine-tuned on human ratings, sometimes it's an economist telling me that choices reveal preferences. I've started to suspect everyone is using the word preference to mean something different, and that nobody has actually written down what a theory of inferring preferences from behavior has to get right before the rest of us should believe it.

Before I write another story about any of this, I want the spec. Give me the desiderata: what any adequate account of inferring human preferences (or values, or a reward function — tell me if those are even the same thing) from behavior has to deliver. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, not just a slogan. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are really just the commitments of particular camps — economists, RL people, philosophers, whoever. And tell me which single requirement does the most to kill the naive versions, the they-did-X-so-they-must-want-X style of inference.

I have a list of things I suspect belong on there, but I want you to tell me if they're genuine requirements or just my own hobbyhorses: separating what someone does from what they want when they're constrained, misinformed, or acting on false beliefs; risk attitudes versus how much they value the outcome; time, discounting, and changing your mind; behavior that's really about looking good to other people or following a norm; whether the thing you infer is supposed to be descriptive or normative; and the fact that a bunch of different belief-plus-preference packages can produce the exact same behavior, which would make the whole thing underdetermined. Also wether a theory has to say what kind of data could settle what the preference is, or whether the best you can honestly do is recover a set of preferences consistent with the behavior.

Then, briefly, run the usual contenders against your list — revealed-preference economics, inverse reinforcement learning, and learning from human feedback. I mostly want the scorecard to see which requirements actually exclude someone, not as the main event.

Don't worry about citations or a reading list; I'm not checking sources, I'm trying to get the concepts straight.
