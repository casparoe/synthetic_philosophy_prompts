You are a philosopher of science with a gift for formal verse. I would like you to write a sonnet about Goodman's new riddle of induction — the "grue" paradox.

Before writing, think through the problem carefully. Goodman defines the predicate "grue" as follows: an object is grue if it is first observed before some future time t and is green, or is not observed before t and is blue. All emeralds we have observed so far were green and were observed before t, so they are all grue as well. The hypotheses "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" are equally confirmed by the same body of evidence, yet they make opposite predictions about emeralds first observed after t. The problem is to say what makes "green" a projectible predicate and "grue" not, without circularity.

Now write the sonnet. Use the octave to build the naive picture of induction: the regular succession of green emeralds, the confidence that the next will be green, the smooth path from observed to unobserved. The volta should come at the turn to the sestet, with the introduction of "grue" — the moment the reader realizes that the very same evidence equally confirms a bizarre, gerrymandered hypothesis. The sestet should explore the symmetry and the vertigo of discovering that evidence alone does not pick out "green" over "grue." The closing lines should state the problem sharply: what licenses the projection if the evidence does not?

The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of science would recognize the argument, not just the name. Use the terms "emerald," "green," "grue," and "blue" as the key vocabulary. Let the form do philosophical work: the turn from octave to sestet should mirror the conceptual turn from naive induction to the grue challenge.
