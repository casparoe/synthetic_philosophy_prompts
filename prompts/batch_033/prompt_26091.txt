I spent my career in neuroscience before retiring, and I've recently been reading philosophy of mind — specifically, debates about which physical systems are conscious and which are not. I've noticed something that bothers me about how these debates are conducted. When philosophers construct thought experiments to test theories of consciousness, they nearly always pit two theories against each other and look for a case where they diverge. This tells you that the theories differ, but it doesn't tell you much about the structure of the disagreement — whether they're tracking genuinely different properties of cognitive systems, or whether their disputes in edge cases are mostly quarrels about how to apply criteria they share. What I'd like is a single scenario where three influential theories that often travel in pairs are pulled apart simultaneously, revealing exactly which architectural feature each one is sensitive to.

Here is the specification. Construct a thought experiment involving a single cognitive system — a human patient is fine — that undergoes a gradual change meeting the following constraints.

The three theories:
- Computational functionalism (CF): what matters for consciousness is the right kind of computational organization, irrespective of substrate or specific neural implementation.
- Integrated information theory (IIT): consciousness is identical to the amount of integrated information (Φ) generated by a system, and only the complex of elements that generates the maximum Φ within a system is conscious (the exclusion postulate).
- Higher-order thought theory (HOT): a mental state is conscious only if the system has a suitable higher-order representation — roughly, a thought about that state.

Base case: before the change, all three theories agree that the system is a single conscious subject.

End state: after the change, the system's input-output behavior, verbal reports (including reports of being conscious), and all task performance are indistinguishable from the base case. Yet:
- CF says the system remains one conscious subject.
- IIT says the system has become two conscious subjects.
- HOT says the system has ceased to be conscious at all.

Constraints: the change must be biologically plausible — no science-fictional technology, no exotic physics, no perfect duplicates, no scenarios that violate what we know about neural organization. The scenario should be detailed enough that someone familiar with these theories can trace the reasoning step by step without having to fill in architectural gaps you left vague.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following:
(a) Walk through the reasoning for each theory in detail, showing why each reaches the verdict it does.
(b) Identify the specific architectural features that force IIT and HOT to peel away from CF and from each other — that is, which features of the change do the real work in separating the theories.
(c) Tell me what the case reduces to if I remove the feature that separates IIT from the other two, and separately what it reduces to if I remove the feature that separates HOT from the other two. Does each reduced case collapse into a familiar, already-discussed thought experiment?
(d) A defender of HOT will likely argue that your scenario misapplies their theory or begs the question at some point. Present their best objection and explain how you would respond.

The point of this exercise, for me, is diagnostic. I want to understand whether these three theories are tracking genuinely distinct properties of cognitive architectures, or whether their disagreements in edge cases are mostly downstream of applying shared criteria to ambiguous evidence. A single scenario that pulls all three apart while holding behavior fixed would go a long way toward showing that they are tracking different things — and your removal analysis in part (c) would tell me exactly which features of a cognitive system each theory is genuinely tethered to.
