I'm a retired physicist, been reading philosophy of mind on my own for the last couple years. There's this pattern I keep running into with the predictive processing / free energy people and I can't tell if it has a name, or if I'm just missing something obvious.

Here's what happens. I'll be reading a paper or a blog post that says the brain minimizes free energy, or minimizes prediction error, or maximizes Bayesian model evidence — all roughly equivalent. And I'll think of what looks like a counterexample. Say, someone deliberately seeking out surprises, or doing something self-destructive, or just sitting there letting their mind wander without any clear goal. And when I bring it up, the response is always: ah, but that behavior is minimizing free energy too, just over a different timescale, or under a different set of priors, or with a different model of the agent's expected states. The novelty-seeker is minimizing prediction error about their own rate of learning. The self-destructive person has priors that make that behavior unsurprising. The mind-wandering is just the brain sampling from its generative model to reduce uncertainty about the state space.

And I realize — I can't think of any behavior at all that couldn't be redescribed this way. The theory's vocabulary (priors, precision-weighting, expected free energy, active inference, Markov blankets) seems flexible enough to absorb absolutely anything. It's not that the defenders are adding ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses, exactly — they're not saying "the theory works except in these cases" or "we need a special clause for this." They're just showing that the theory, as stated, already covers the case, because you can always pick the right priors or the right level of description or the right partition of the system to make it fit.

This feels different from ordinary unfalsifiability. With an unfalsifiable theory like Freudian psychoanalysis, the move is usually to posit a hidden mechanism (repression, reaction formation) that explains away the counterexample. Here the defenders don't seem to be adding anything — they're just pointing out that the existing conceptual machinery is so abstract that it was never really at risk. The theory is spongy. It expands to fit whatever you throw at it.

So I have two questions. First, is there an established name in philosophy of science for this specific pattern — a theory whose conceptual vocabulary is so flexible and so abstract that any ostensible counterexample can be smoothly redescribed as an instance of the theory, without adding new assumptions? (I know about "unfalsifiable" and "not even wrong" and "immunizing stratagem" but those all feel like they're gesturing at something slightly different.) Second, if there isn't a name, what would you call it, and can you define it precisely enough that I can tell this pattern apart from nearby things like triviality, tautology, ad-hoc-ness, and underdetermination?

If you can, give me the answer as a JSON object with these fields:
- "concept_name": the established name if one exists, or the name you coin
- "established": boolean — whether this is a known named concept in the literature
- "definition": a precise two- or three-sentence definition of the pattern
- "nearest_concepts": an array of objects, each with "name" and "how_it_differs" — the closest existing concepts and exactly how the described pattern is different from each of them
- "boundary_cases": an array of short examples that someone might mistake for this pattern but aren't quite it, each with a sentence on why it's different
- "verdict": your honest take on whether the free energy principle actually exhibits this pattern or whether I'm being unfair
