I'd like a poem that renders Hegel's lord–bondsman dialectic — the movement usually called the master–slave dialectic in the Phenomenology of Spirit — with enough precision that someone who teaches the text could map the stanzas onto the argument, and with enough craft that a reader who has never read Hegel gets a real poem rather than a summary in line breaks.

Please write it as a pantoum of five quatrains, and use the form's mechanics as the argument itself. In a pantoum, each stanza's second and fourth lines become the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza folds the opening lines back in, so the poem returns to where it began. That return is the philosophical point and should not be merely formal: the dialectic ends with the same two self-consciousnesses it began with, but their relation has been worked through and reversed, and the closing return should make the same lines sound different — not a tidy reconciliation, but the difference that the struggle and the labor have made. Meter and rhyme are yours to choose; the repetition scheme is the constraint that matters.

The poem should move through the actual sequence: two self-consciousnesses each demanding recognition from the other; the life-and-death struggle; the asymmetry that results when one yields out of fear of death and becomes the bondsman while the other becomes lord; the lord's discovery that recognition from a consciousness he refuses to count as an equal is hollow; and the bondsman's discovery, through fear and through formative labor on the thing, that his own independence shows up in the object he shapes. The reversal must happen in the repetitions: a line that first expresses the lord's mastery should read on its return as a confession of dependence, and a line that first expresses the bondsman's servitude should read on its return as the shape of his freedom. Write in the third person, keep both positions in view, and do not end on a slogan about equality — Hegel's section ends with the bondsman's labor and the beginning of a freedom the lord cannot reach, so leave it there.
