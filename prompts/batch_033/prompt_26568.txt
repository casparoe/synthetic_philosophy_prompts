A colleague presented the following argument at a workshop, and I've been turning it over ever since. It seems to prove that the most celebrated results on the epistemology of disagreement and markets are either empirically irrelevant or normatively devastating, and I can't find the exit.

(1) Aumann's agreement theorem shows that if two agents are rational Bayesians, share the same prior probabilities, and have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities for some proposition, then their posteriors must be identical: they cannot agree to disagree.

(2) The Milgrom-Stokey no-trade theorem extends this insight to markets: under common priors and common knowledge of individual rationality, there can be no purely speculative trade — trade based solely on differing information or beliefs, rather than on differing endowments, hedging needs, or risk preferences. Any offer to trade would reveal the offeror's private information, causing the offeree to revise her beliefs and reject the trade.

(3) These are not presented as mere formal curiosities. They are widely taken to illuminate something deep about the structure of rational belief and the informational efficiency of markets.

(4) Now look at the world. We see persistent, deep disagreement everywhere: macroeconomists disagree about the effects of fiscal stimulus; jurors hear the same testimony and reach opposite verdicts; intelligence analysts examine the same raw reports and disagree about the likelihood of a threat. We also see enormous volumes of speculative trade: currency markets, equity markets, prediction markets — where participants trade precisely because they disagree about the future value of an asset.

(5) It seems to follow that in all these cases, at least one of the theorem's conditions must fail. Either the parties are not fully rational, or they do not share common priors, or their beliefs are not common knowledge (in the iterated "I know that she knows that I know" sense).

(6) But each of these options is troubling. If rationality is failing, then the theorems are normatively idle — they describe agents we never encounter, and they give us no guidance about how to conduct ourselves when we disagree with actual people. If the problem is heterogeneous priors, then rational disagreement is possible but only because people start from different arbitrary starting points, and the theorems tell us nothing about whose prior is better or how to bring them into alignment. If the problem is a failure of common knowledge, then the theorems are extraordinarily fragile — they apply only when the infinite regress of mutual knowledge is satisfied, which it almost never is in practice, and even a tiny deviation from common knowledge collapses the result.

(7) So the theorems seem to prove too much: either they show that almost all real-world disagreement and speculative trade is irrational, or they reveal that the conditions for their own applicability are so demanding that the theorems are epistemically idle. On either horn, they appear to undermine the very picture of rational discourse they were meant to illuminate.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'm especially interested in whether the fragility diagnosis in (6) is the right way out, and if so, whether anything positive can still be salvaged from the theorems — perhaps as normative ideals, or as revealing something about the limiting structure of rational belief even if the limit is never reached. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these topics, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
