In a seminar on evolutionary epistemology, we have been working through the role that Popper's analogy between the growth of scientific knowledge and Darwinian natural selection plays in his overall philosophy. The analogy, as Popper develops it, runs roughly as follows: our theories are like biological organisms, our conjectures are like mutations, and the process of critical testing and falsification is like environmental selection pressure. Just as blind variation and selective retention drive biological evolution, so too does the blind generation of conjectures followed by rigorous elimination of error drive the growth of knowledge. Popper leans on this analogy to underwrite several important claims: that the growth of knowledge is not a matter of accumulating justified true beliefs by induction, that there is no need for a conscious "author" directing the process, and that epistemology can be naturalized in a way that respects the objectivity of scientific knowledge without appealing to a knowing subject.

I want to know how far this analogy actually holds. Please give me a systematic audit. First, provide a correspondence table: what plays the role of the organism, the mutation, the environment, the selective pressure, and the resulting adaptation? Then identify the points where the mapping breaks down—ideally separating the disanalogies that matter for Popper's intended conclusions from the ones that are merely superficial. Pay particular attention to the following: in biological evolution, mutations are genuinely random with respect to fitness, whereas scientific conjectures are guided by problem situations, background knowledge, and methodological constraints. Does this difference undermine the claim that knowledge growth is a "blind" process, or can Popper accommodate it? Also, Popper notoriously wavered on whether natural selection itself is a testable scientific theory or merely a "metaphysical research programme." Does that ambivalence about the status of the source domain affect what the analogy can license about the target domain? Finally, tell me whether the three conclusions listed above—the rejection of induction, the dispensability of a knowing subject, and the naturalization of epistemology—survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy (or a modification of this one) would do the work more cleanly.
