Before reading further in the secondary literature on Kant's Formula of Universal Law, I want to fix the standard by which an interpretation of the universalizability test should be judged. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate interpretation of the Formula of Universal Law (or the Formula of the Law of Nature) must deliver.

The list should include, for each requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any interpretation that claims to be Kant's own view (as opposed to a revisionary Kantian proposal), and which are preferences of particular interpretive camps. If any pair of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — given the structure of the test, the texts, or the cases — flag that and explain the tension.

Among the things I expect the list to address: Kant's own four examples from the Groundwork (perfect and imperfect duties to self and to others), the distinction between contradiction in conception and contradiction in will, the maxim-formulation problem (how the level of generality at which an agent's maxim is described is to be fixed), the problem of false positives and false negatives, and the question of why a contradiction uncovered by the test should carry normative force for a rational agent.

Optionally, briefly indicate how the main interpretive approaches — the logical-contradiction reading, the practical-contradiction reading, and the teleological reading — fare against the list. But the weight of the response should be on the criteria themselves and their justification, not on the scorecard.
