I am a high school senior and serve as the academic chair of my school's philosophy society. This spring the society is hosting an interschool competition in formal epistemology, and I have been asked to draft the qualifying essay paper on Fitch's paradox of knowability. The competitors are advanced high-school students who have already worked through the basics of modal logic (box and diamond, possible-worlds talk) and epistemic logic (the K operator, the factivity of knowledge, and the distribution of knowledge over conjunction), but the paper is meant to reward diagnosis rather than recall of a standard proof.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works in formal epistemology and has experience setting university-level examination papers. Before drafting anything, think through the two or three strongest candidate diagnoses of the paradox, and use those to shape the marking scheme. Then write me three things: (1) the exam question, for a 45-minute supervised essay; (2) a model answer of roughly 600–700 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 marks.

For background, the standard Fitch derivation runs as follows. Start with the unrestricted knowability principle: for every true proposition p, it is possible for someone to know p. Now suppose, for reductio, that some proposition p is true but unknown. By the principle, it is possible to know the conjunction p-and-p-is-unknown. But consider any world in which that conjunction is known: knowledge distributes over conjunction, so in that world someone knows p and someone knows that p is unknown. Factivity turns the second item into the truth that p is unknown, contradicting the first. So the conjunction cannot be known in any world, contradicting what the principle required. The reductio assumption is therefore impossible: no truth is unknown. The unrestricted principle thus seems to collapse into the absurd claim that every truth is actually known.

The exam question should state this derivation in compressed form, so that competitors are not being tested on reproducing it from memory, and it should force them to say what the paradox actually shows. I want a question that cannot be answered by a student who has merely memorized that Fitch refutes anti-realism. Competitors should have to do at least the following: identify the logical principles the derivation depends on; distinguish the unrestricted knowability principle from the underlying anti-realist or verificationist motivation; evaluate at least one principled repair — for example, restricting the principle to propositions that can be known without self-defeating consequences, or reformulating "knowable" so that the paradox no longer follows — and assess whether that repair is ad hoc; and finally take a side on whether the paradox refutes anti-realism outright or only refutes a too-simple formalization of it.

The model answer should be committed: it should defend one diagnosis of the paradox as the strongest and give its reasons, not lay out a neutral menu of options. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain, should allocate all 20 marks to named intellectual moves rather than general impressions, and should add a one-line note for each main band saying what candidates at that level typically get wrong and which misunderstanding each error reflects.
