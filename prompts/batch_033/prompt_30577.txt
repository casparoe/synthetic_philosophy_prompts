Over the years, a colleague of mine has recorded her considered verdicts on a series of cases concerning moral responsibility, and I should like you to reconstruct the principle she is implicitly applying. Her verdicts are these. A manager who falsifies an expense claim to pay for a holiday: responsible. A bank clerk who hands over the contents of the till because an armed man is holding her child: not responsible. Jones, who decides on his own to embezzle from his employer whilst, unbeknownst to him, a neuroscientist's device would have made him decide to embezzle had he shown any sign of deciding otherwise: responsible. A kleptomaniac who steals from an impulse she disavows, has sought treatment for, and is ashamed of: not responsible. A man with an undiagnosed brain tumour who suddenly attacks a nurse: not responsible. She has now been asked about a further case. A lifelong pacifist refuses to report for military service, knowing she will be imprisoned, and says that, given the person she is, she could not have done otherwise; suppose it is stipulated that her incapacity is deliberative rather than merely physical, so that however strong the reasons for reporting for service, her deliberations would still have issued in a refusal. Predict her verdict on this new case. In your essay, identify the principle that best explains her recorded verdicts, set out the other principles that fit them equally well, explain which of those principles the new case discriminates between, defend your prediction, and describe a further case that would settle which principle she is running.
