Next month I am giving a colloquium paper in a philosophy department whose metaphysics group has spent the term arguing about personal identity. The paper is about Dennett's brain-transplant scenario: a person's brain is removed, kept alive in a vat, and linked to his still-active body by a radio link, so that the body goes off and acts while the brain sits in the lab. My thesis is that this scenario does not establish that the person is located where the brain is. I will argue that it shows instead that the ordinary question where I am silently bundles together several distinct questions—where my living human organism is, where the biological seat of my consciousness is, where my point of view is, and where I am as a social and legal agent. The scenario makes these answers come apart, but I will claim there is no further fact of the matter that picks one of them out as the question about me. The upshot is that the scenario cannot deliver the verdict it is usually taken to deliver in debates about personal identity: it dramatizes an ambiguity in our question rather than revealing where the person goes.

The audience is mostly professional philosophers, and three members matter most. One is an animalist: he holds that each of us is identical with a living human organism, and that facts about memory and consciousness are at best fallible evidence about which organism is which. Two are neo-Lockeans: they hold that what matters to our persistence is overlapping chains of psychological continuity, and they regard Dennett's scenario as evidence that the person goes with the brain. There will also be several philosophers of cognitive science who are generally suspicious of intuition pumps and will press me on what a point of view is supposed to be, and two metaphysicians who will press on the difference between semantic indecision and genuine ontic indeterminacy. The animalist and the neo-Lockeans know each other's positions well and will aim at least part of their questioning through my paper at each other.
