I have been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty and find myself stuck on the status of the so-called hinge propositions. As I understand the view, there is a class of propositions—candidate examples include the claim that the earth has existed for many years before my birth, that I have hands, that physical objects do not vanish when no one perceives them—which are never themselves the subject of inquiry. They are not things we know, because knowing something requires being able to give grounds for it, and for these propositions no grounds are ever produced; but they are also not things we can sensibly doubt, because they set the frame within which doubting and grounding make sense at all. They are, in the metaphor, the hinges on which the door of inquiry turns.

This is where I lose the thread. If a hinge proposition is neither known nor doubted, if it is held without grounds and is in some sense exempt from questioning, then why isn't it simply a prejudice that we happen to be psychologically unable to give up? The skeptic has always charged that our ordinary beliefs rest on unexamined assumptions; this looks as though Wittgenstein is conceding the charge and then declining to be troubled by it. What is the mistake in saying that a hinge is just an ungrounded assumption, and that a system of beliefs built on such assumptions is irrational by the very standards it claims to honor?
