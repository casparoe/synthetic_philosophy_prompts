I run a small pharmacovigilence group at a regional health authority, and every few weeks we have to decide whether to issue a safety warning about a drug. The hard cases are the ones where the hypothesis is new but the evidence is old: someone proposes that a drug we've used for twenty years causes some rare problem, and the supporting data is stuff we've already had sitting in our database for years — old trial results, spontaneous reports, registry entries. I keep going in circles on a basic question: if we already knew the data before anyone thought of the hypothesis, does the fact that the hypothesis fits that data count as evidence for it? My Bayesian textbook says old evidence can't confirm anything (if P(E)=1 then P(H|E)=P(H)), but that seems wrong — people treated Mercury's perihelion as confirming general relativity even though the anomaly was old news. I want a reusable decision procedure my group can run in a two-hour meeting to decide how much an already-known dataset should move our credence in a new safety hypothesis. It should be a checklist or flowchart in words, grounded in what formal epistemology actually knows about old evidence but usable by clinicians and statisticians who don't care about the theory, and it has to leave a paper trail the medical director will sign off on: what to ask in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where we stop and say this doesn't confirm at all, and where the decision has to be handed to a person because it's a judgment call. Cover the distinctions that actually matter here — whether the hypothesis was reverse-engineered from the same data, whether we can reconstruct a state of mind in which we didn't know the data yet, whether the hypothesis also predicts something new we can go check, and whether the old data was collected for a purpose unrelated to the hypothesis. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a hypothesis about birth defects that was thought up after staring at the registry and fits nothing else; (2) a hypothesis formulated before the proposer saw the old data, which then turns out to fit it; (3) a hypothesis that fits the old data but makes one checkable new prediction that would be very unlikely otherwise. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us something we won't like, and be honest about which steps are dressed-up judgment calls and where the procedure is likely to misfire.
