I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I lead a team that builds conversational language models. We recently had one of our systems pass a Turing-style evaluation: across several hundred short text conversations, a panel of human judges identified the human interlocutor no more often than chance, and follow-up questioning surfaced no reliable tells. The team is now split, and the split is beginning to affect design decisions.

One camp holds that, operationally, there is nothing more to understanding or having a mind than sustained behavioral competence of exactly this kind: if a system converses, answers novel questions, tracks context, notices when it has been misunderstood, and generalizes across sufficiently varied situations, then it understands in the only sense that can matter to us as engineers. The other camp holds that the evaluation shows only that the system can reproduce the outward shape of a mind: behavioral tests are evidence at best, and could in principle be passed by a system with no understanding and no conscious experience, so passing the test cannot settle whether the system actually has a mind.

I want to replace the arguing-past-each-other with a document both camps could actually sign. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two positions — call them behavioral operationalism and behavioral insufficiency unless you have better labels.

The document should include: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides accept, including claims about what the evaluation did and did not show, what behavioral evidence can and cannot establish, and what further observations would be relevant; (2) a disagreements table with the precise formulation of each remaining disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete experiment, case, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Where part of the dispute looks merely verbal, say which part and why.

Keep the whole document under 900 words. Do not include citations or reading lists; this is for an engineering team, not a seminar.
