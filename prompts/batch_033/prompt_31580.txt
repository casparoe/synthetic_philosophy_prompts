"A rigid designator picks out the same object in every possible world where it picks out anything at all. Proper names are Kripke's main example: 'Aristotle' refers to Aristotle in every world where it refers, whereas a definite description like 'the teacher of Alexander' could have referred to someone else. Since 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are both rigid, and they actually refer to the same thing, Venus, the identity statement 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is true in every possible world. But we had to do empirical astronomy to learn that Hesperus is Phosphorus, so the proposition is a posteriori. So one and the same proposition is necessary and a posteriori. Kripke also takes this to show that names have no descriptive content and simply refer directly to their objects. For the same reason, the proposition that water is H2O is necessary, because 'water' and 'H2O' are both rigid designators, even though it took chemistry to discover it. So Kripke refutes the traditional view that all necessary truths are a priori and all a posteriori truths are contingent."
