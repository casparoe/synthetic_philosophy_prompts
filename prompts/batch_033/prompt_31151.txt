I am a retired geologist who now sits on the governing council of a learned scientific society, and I have recently been reading Burke and the literature on Chesterton's fence. Several times a year the council votes on proposals to alter or abolish long-standing practices — a named prize, a fellowship requirement, a publication ritual, a rule of order — decisions that affect the society's reputation, its finances, and the trust of its members. I would like a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the Burkean idea that an established institution should not be torn down until one understands what it is doing and what would be lost, and that the burden of justification falls on the reformer. Please write the procedure the council could actually run on any such proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation against change or a deferral, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The council meets for two hours, its members have no philosophical training, and the procedure must be usable by someone who has not read Burke. Then run the procedure on three proposals: renaming a medal named after a founding member now known to have held eugenic views; abolishing the rule that fellows must have published in the society's own journal; and ending the printed bound proceedings volume that almost no one reads. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the council will not like, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as a checklist and where the procedure will misfire. Keep the response under 1,200 words.
