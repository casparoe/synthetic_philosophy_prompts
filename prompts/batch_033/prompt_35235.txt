I keep noticing the same move in arguments and I'm wondering if it has a name in the philosophy of science or formal epistemology literature.

Here's the pattern. Person A says "my theory is simpler, so by Occam's razor we should prefer it." Person B says "no, my theory is actually simpler." And then they both proceed to re-describe their theories to make them look more parsimonious. What's striking is that this seems basically always possible: you can break your opponent's "one assumption" into three by pointing out it's not really atomic, or you can bundle your own three assumptions into one by giving them a shared label. Suddenly dualism is simpler than physicalism because consciousness is just one extra thing, while physicalism smuggles in bridge laws and emergence and all this hidden machinery. Or physicalism is simpler because it posits only one kind of stuff, while dualism needs two kinds plus an interaction problem. The multiverse is simpler because it replaces a fine-tuned constant with a single mechanism that generates everything. No, the multiverse is wildly complex because it posits 10^500 universes. I've seen the same thing in arguments about God (is a designer an extra entity, or does it simplify the explanation of everything else?), about nominalism vs. realism about universals, and honestly in half the debates on r/philosophy. 

I'm not a simplicity skeptic — I think Occam's razor is tracking something real. And I know there's technical work on formalizing simplicity (Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, MML, etc.). But I'm specifically interested in the phenomenon where the unit of counting — what counts as "one" assumption — is up for grabs, and people can sincerely disagree about which theory is simpler because they individuate assumptions differently. It feels like there's a gap between the formal machinery and the way simplicity actually gets invoked in philsophical arguments, where nobody is writing down their theories in a formal language with a fixed primitive vocabulary.

Does this problem have an established name? Is it just "the problem of language-dependence of simplicity" or is there a more specific term? And if there's no standard name, what's the closest concept in the literature and how should I be thinking about what exactly the problem is and isn't? I'm not just after a label — I want to know how the problem is carved up, what the standard responses are, and whether anyone thinks they've solved it.

Also, if the way I'm setting this up is confused — if e.g. the formal approaches actually do solve this and I'm just missing how — tell me that instead of humoring the question.
