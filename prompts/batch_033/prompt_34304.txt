I'm running a philosophy reading group — mostly grad students but from all over the department, ethicists, historians, a couple people in aesthetics — and next week we're tackling money pumps as an argument for diachronic rationality requirements. I've got about seventy-five minutes, and I really want them to see why the money pump is supposed to be doing normative work, not just be a clever gotcha. The last time I saw someone present this material, the whole thing derailed in the first five minutes: someone said "but if I know I'm being pumped, I'll just refuse the next trade," and the presenter had to backpedal and explain that the agent's preferences are stable and known, but by then everyone was already arguing about whether real people fall for pumps, and the actual philosophical point — that cyclic preferences make you vulnerable to exploitation in a way that seems to indict you as irrational — got completely buried.

So I need a teaching plan. What absolutely has to be established before I even introduce the pump scenario? I'm thinking I need to make the distinction between synchronic and diachronic requirements clear, and also the idea that the pump is a *representation* of a certain kind of irrationality, not a psychological prediction — but I'm not sure about the exact order, and I'm worried I'll front-load too much abstract machinery and lose everyone before we get to the example. What example should carry the whole thing? Is the classic coffee/tea/juice cycle the best, or should I use something more concrete that doesn't feel like a made-up philosophy problem? I want one example I can keep returning to, and I want it to make the structure viscerally clear.

I also need a map of misconceptions: which ones will come up in which order, and where exactly I should head each one off. I'm especialy worried about: (a) "I'd just notice the cycle and stop," (b) "this is just about being stupid or having bad memory," (c) "the money pump shows cyclic preferences are pragmatically bad, not irrational," and (d) conflating this with Dutch book arguments. Are there others I'm not seeing? And what should I openly refuse to discuss at this level? I'm guessing the formal epistemology of whether money pumps actually ground rationality requirements — the "why be rational?" question — is too much, and maybe the relationship to representation theorems, but I want to know where to draw the line and what to say when I'm drawing it.

And finally: what's one question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they understood the normative structure of the argument, rather than just finding the puzzle clever or amusing? Something that tests whether they see why the pump is supposed to *matter* for what we think rationality requires of agents over time, not just why it's a fun trap.
