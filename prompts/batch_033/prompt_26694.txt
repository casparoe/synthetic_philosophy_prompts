I've recently moved from academic philosophy into a risk analysis role at a financial institution, and I'm struck by how much of my day consists of making assertions under uncertainty to people who will act on them — sometimes with enormous sums at stake. The norms of assertion debate, which I used to treat as a somewhat intramural epistemological exercise, has become urgently practical for me. I want to think through it by analyzing a case where assertion under uncertainty went catastrophically wrong: the credit rating agencies in the lead-up to the 2008 financial crisis.

The episode, in outline: Moody's, S&P, and Fitch assigned AAA ratings to vast quantities of mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations, many of them built from subprime mortgages. These ratings were not merely opinions offered for reflection; they functioned as assertions about creditworthiness that institutional investors — pension funds, insurance companies, money market funds — relied on as a basis for action, often because regulations required them to. The assertions turned out to be false at a staggering scale, and they played a causal role in the near-collapse of the global financial system.

I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of the four main competing norms of assertion: the knowledge norm (assert p only if you know p), the truth norm (assert p only if p is true), the belief norm (assert p only if you believe p), and the reasonable belief norm (assert p only if you reasonably believe p). For each norm, work through whether the agencies violated it, and what exactly that verdict condemns or excuses. Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode reveal about the strengths and weaknesses of each norm? I'm particularly interested in two pressure points. First, the reasonable belief norm seems to let the agencies off lightly — their models were industry-standard, the historical data they relied on was limited but not obviously cherry-picked, and the possibility of a nationwide housing decline was widely dismissed. If that norm excuses them, does that count against the norm, or does it suggest we're mislocating the wrong? Second, the knowledge norm seems impossibly demanding for assertions made under genuine uncertainty and institutional pressure; if it's the right norm, then much of what financial professionals, doctors, and policymakers say day to day is impermissible. Is that a reductio of the knowledge norm, or a bullet we should bite?

If you think none of the four norms fully captures what went wrong in this case, say what's missing — whether it's a norm about the process by which one arrives at an assertion, a norm about the duties one has to inquire further before speaking, or something else entirely. I'm not looking for a historical narrative; I know the basic facts. I want the philosophical analysis, and I want it to be honest about where each norm strains against the case rather than forcing the case to fit the norm.
