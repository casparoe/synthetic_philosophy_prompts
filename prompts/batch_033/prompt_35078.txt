I just spent an hour arguing with a friend about the surprise exam paradox, and I left the conversation convinced that most people — including plenty of philosophy students — can rehearse the backward induction without ever pinpointing what makes the paradox genuinely hard to resolve. I teach an upper-level epistemology course, and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on the surprise exam paradox that can't be answered by summarizing the scenario and the student's reasoning. It should force students to isolate a specific epistemic step that fails, and to defend why that step — rather than some other — is where the argument breaks down.

Please provide the following three sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths given:

**1. The exam question** (roughly 100 words). One paragraph. The question should present the paradox in brief and then ask the student to identify and defend the single weakest epistemic step in the would-be examinee's reasoning. It should explicitly instruct them not to simply rehearse the backward induction, but to engage with why the paradox matters for our understanding of knowledge, justified belief, or self-referential announcements. The question should be answerable in about 45 minutes of exam time.

**2. A model answer** (roughly 500 words). This should be the kind of answer that earns a high A. It should name a specific step — e.g., the move from "if the exam hasn't come by Thursday night, we will know it is Friday" to "therefore it cannot be Friday," or the assumption that the students' knowledge survives the elimination of later days — and explain exactly what epistemic principle that step relies on and why the principle is questionable. It should connect the diagnosis to at least one broader issue in epistemology (the KK principle, the stability of knowledge under deductive closure, the Moorean character of the announcement, the distinction between knowing an event will occur and knowing one will be surprised by it, or something of comparable depth). It should also briefly acknowledge at least one alternative diagnosis and explain why the chosen one is more fundamental or more illuminating.

**3. A marking scheme** (roughly 300 words). Total: 20 marks. Break the marks across four criteria. For each criterion, state the number of marks it is worth; describe what an A-range answer does that a B-range answer does not (name the specific moves, distinctions, or arguments); and note the single most common mistake students make on that criterion. The four criteria should be, in your chosen order: (i) accuracy and precision in presenting the paradox and the candidate's chosen weak step; (ii) depth of epistemic analysis — how well the answer connects the weak step to a substantive issue in epistemology; (iii) handling of an alternative diagnosis — acknowledging a different way to locate the flaw and explaining why the chosen diagnosis is preferable; (iv) clarity, structure, and argumentative coherence. The mark distribution should reflect that (ii) is the most heavily weighted.

The question's difficulty should come from the crux: I want to separate students who merely understand that the paradox is "about knowledge" from those who can say exactly which commitment about knowledge generates the trouble, and why it is that commitment rather than another.
