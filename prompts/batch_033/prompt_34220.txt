I've been sitting in on this reading group on population ethics and I keep going back and forth on the repugnant conclusion. I figured I'd write up a dialogue between two of the regulars who've been arguing about it for weeks — it's kind of become a running thing in the department. They were at it again last Tuesday at the grad student pub, and I tried to capture the gist of the exchange. Could you tell me who you think has the stronger case, and where you think the other one goes wrong?

NATE: Look, I'm not saying the repugnant conclusion is intuitively appealing. I'm saying all the alternatives are worse. Every way of avoiding it forces you into something even more absurd.

SOPHIE: That's the standard move, right? "My view has a counterintuitive implication, but at least it's not as bad as yours." But I think the repugnant conclusion is just the tip of the iceberg. Once you accept that mere addition of lives barely worth living can make the world better, you've basically committed to the idea that we have a moral obligation to maximize total welfare by any means — turning the universe into a maximally efficient happiness factory. That's not just counterintuitive, it's fanatical.

NATE: That's a different argument though. You're making a slippery slope claim about what I'd be committed to in practice, but the repugnant conclusion is a claim about axiology — about what makes one state of the world better than another — not about what actions we should take. My practical obligations are mediated by feasibility constraints, side constraints, uncertainty. I don't have to advocate for the happiness factory.

SOPHIE: Fair, but the axiological claim is still doing work. If you genuinely believe Z is better than A, then in a situation where you have to choose between them — say, some policy choice that affects population size and average welfare — you're going to tilt toward Z. And that seems like a mistake to me.

NATE: Okay, but let's trace through the actual steps of the mere addition paradox. You agree that adding a group of happy people to an already-happy population doesn't make the world worse, right? That's just the Pareto principle applied across possible populations.

SOPHIE: I actually don't agree. I think adding happy people is neutral, not good. It's not worse, but it's not better either. There's an asymmetry: we have strong reasons to prevent miserable lives from coming into existence, but we don't have symmetric reasons to create happy lives. The absence of a happy life isn't a loss — there's no one to whom the loss accrues.

NATE: But that asymmetry is itself really hard to motivate. Why should the zero point be special? You're saying that moving someone from nonexistence to +10 welfare is neutral, but moving them from nonexistence to -10 welfare is bad. But the difference between +10 and -10 is just 20 units of welfare either way. If welfare matters, it should matter symmetrically.

SOPHIE: It's not about the zero point being special in value space. It's about the metaphysics of persons. A nonexistent person doesn't have a welfare level. You can't benefit or harm someone who doesn't exist. So when you create a happy person, you're not making anyone better off — you're just bringing someone into existence who will then have a good life. That's fundamentally different from making an existing person worse off.

NATE: That's the person-affecting restriction, but it has its own problems. The non-identity problem, for one. And it implies that a world with a trillion happy people is no better than a world with just one happy person, ceteris paribus. That seems like a much more radical bullet to bite than the repugnant conclusion.

SOPHIE: I don't think it's more radical. The repugnant conclusion asks us to prefer a world of billions of lives barely worth living — lives where every moment is a mild struggle, just barely above neutral — over a world of, say, a million people genuinely flourishing. That's not just counterintuitive. It's hard to even hold in your head as a genuine preference.

NATE: But you're smuggling a lot into "barely worth living." Parfit's formulation doesn't say "just barely above neutral." It says lives worth living. And I think our intuitions about what "barely worth living" looks like are distorted. A life that's net positive overall — even if modest — is still a life where the good moments outweigh the bad. Multiply that by a sufficiently large number, and yes, I think the aggregate matters more than the average.

SOPHIE: So you're biting the bullet. But then let me push on this: if you're willing to trade off average welfare for total welfare when it comes to happy lives, are you equally willing to do it for suffering lives? Would you accept the reverse repugnant conclusion — that a world with a huge population of people suffering horribly is worse than a world with a smaller population suffering even more intensely per person? Because that seems to follow from the same logic.

NATE: I think it does follow, and I actually think that's fine. That's just the mirror image — if total welfare is what matters, it matters for suffering too.

SOPHIE: Right, but then consider this. Under total utilitarianism, for any world with some suffering, you can improve it by adding enough happy people to outweigh the suffering — even if you're not reducing the suffering at all. That means you could justify creating a vast number of moderately happy people to "offset" a genocide, without actually helping any of the victims. That seems like a reductio to me.

NATE: That's a powerful objection, but I think it conflates the axiological claim with the deontic one again. The fact that adding happy people makes the world better in aggregate doesn't mean it's the right response to genocide. Justice, rights, retribution — these all constrain what we should do. Total utilitarianism is a theory of the good, not a complete decision procedure.

SOPHIE: I'm just not sure you can keep those cleanly separated. The axiology is going to leak into your practical reasoning eventually. But let me try a different tack. Do you think there's any upper bound on the number of lives barely worth living that would outweigh a single flourishing life? If I said one flourishing life is worth more than a googolplex of barely-worth-living lives, would you disagree?

NATE: I'd disagree. If each barely-worth-living life has positive value ε, then for any finite value of a flourishing life, there's some number n such that nε exceeds it. That's just arithmetic.

SOPHIE: But that's exactly what makes the conclusion repugnant! The fact that you're willing to say a googolplex of drab, mediocre lives outweighs a single life of profound joy and achievement — that's the whole problem. You're treating lived experience like it's a fungible commodity.

NATE: I think it's weirder to say that there's some magic threshold where adding more happy lives stops mattering. At what population size does the value of an additional happy life become zero? That seems arbitrary.

SOPHIE: It's not about a threshold. It's about the fact that welfare isn't the kind of thing that can be summed across persons in the first place. The separateness of persons means that my welfare and your welfare aren't commensurable in the way total utilitarianism requires — Rawls made this point, we can't just add them up and call it a day.

NATE: That's a deep objection, but it's also a much bigger claim than just rejecting the repugnant conclusion. You're basically rejecting consequentialism wholesale. And the separateness of persons cuts both ways — if you can't aggregate welfare across persons, how do you make any tradeoffs at all? How do you decide between a policy that helps one person a lot and a policy that helps a million people a little?

SOPHIE: I'm not saying we can't make tradeoffs. I'm saying the tradeoffs can't be reduced to simple summing in all cases. But look, I think we've been going back and forth on this for like an hour and the beer's getting warm. Want to pick this up next week?
