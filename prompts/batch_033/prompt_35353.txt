I'm in a seminar on Madhyamaka and I've been trying to reconstruct Nāgārjuna's argument against arising, and the more I look at it the more it seems to prove too much. Here's the version I have in my notes:

(1) If something arises, it has to arise from itself, from something other than itself, from both, or from neither.
(2) It can't arise from itself, because then it would already exist, and producing an already-existing thing is pointless.
(3) It can't arise from another, because if cause and effect are genuinely distinct then there's no real connection between them, and anything could produce anything.
(4) It can't arise from both, since that's just the two failed horns stuck together.
(5) It can't arise from neither, because causelessly arising things would pop up at random or never.
(6) So nothing actually arises.

But that can't be the point, because the whole tradition is built on dependent origination, and dependent origination is supposed to be compatable with emptiness, not refuted by it. I have a feeling the four options in (1) are doing more work than they look like, but I can't make the objection stick. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd really like to keep the conclusion that arising is empty, but I don't want to be stuck saying that no one ever stubs their toe.
