I run an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology and social choice: fourteen students who have had a semester of probability, one eighty-minute meeting. I want a runnable in-class game, not a lecture, that makes them feel the Condorcet jury theorem and, more importantly, the exact conditions under which its conclusion breaks. By the end I want them to hold three things at once: a felt sense of why majority rule can outperform any individual voter when voters are mildly better than chance and their errors are conditionally independent given the state; a felt sense of how fragile that advantage is once errors share a common cause or the group is really just pooling one public signal; and a clear grip on the difference between "the majority beats any single voter" and "the majority is likely to be right in this particular case" — including the case where a single known expert with much higher competence would beat the majority.

The folk version of this theorem gets invoked constantly in online discussion of prediction markets and crowdsourced judgment, usually with the independence condition silently dropped; I want my students to have felt that condition fail before anyone names it. So the game should be arranged so that the failure modes arise from the mechanics and incentives, not from the facilitator's commentary. Please give me: full rules; materials (cards, tokens, a hidden-fact generator, or similar); a point-based payoff scheme that uses no real money but gives participants a genuine reason to care; a timeline for the eighty minutes; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto the theorem's assumptions — individual competence above one half, conditional independence, sincere voting, equal competence — and onto the ways the game violates them. Include a round in which adding voters with correlated errors makes the group worse rather than better, and a round in which participants may deliberate before voting, so the debrief can distinguish genuine information pooling from correlated error and cascades. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which features of the theorem the game cannot reproduce — asymptotic behavior with large juries, genuinely independent judgments in a room where participants can see one another, and competences that are known rather than merely estimated are the obvious candidates — and what the facilitator will have to supply in words.
