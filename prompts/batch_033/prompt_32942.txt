I've been reading the Zhuangzi butterfly dream passage for years now and I keep running into the same impasse, both in the secondary literature and in conversations with people who work on Chinese philosophy. The passage is short — Zhuang Zhou dreams he's a butterfly, fluttering around happy, not knowing he's Zhuang Zhou. He wakes up, and now he doesn't know whether he's Zhuang Zhou who dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming he's Zhuang Zhou. And then there's the line about "transformation."

The debate that seems to have completely stalled is whether this is a skeptical argument — like, an argument that we can't know we're not dreaming, that challenges our justification for ordinary beliefs about the world — or whether it's doing something else entirely, something about the fluidity of boundaries between self and world, or about accepting transformation rather than seeking certainty. The Western-philosophy readers keep wanting to slot it alongside Descartes' dream argument; the sinologists keep saying that's a misreading, that Zhuangzi isn't in the business of epistemology in that sense, that the point is therapeutic or perspectival. But then the skeptics-about-the-skeptical-reading also can't agree on what the positive alternative actually is, and the whole thing has been going in circles for decades.

Here's my hypothesis: the disagreement isn't really about what the text says — everyone can read the same Chinese. It's a disagreement about what counts as "doing epistemology" and whether you can have skeptical worries without having a worked-out theory of knowledge. The people who read it as skepticism think that if you're raising a doubt about whether you know you're awake, you're doing epistemology regardless of what else you're doing; the other side thinks that's imposing a category from a tradition Zhuangzi wasn't part of. If I'm right, then the debate is a verbal dispute about the word "skepticism" or "epistemology," and the real work is just to describe what the passage is doing without fighting about the label.

But I'm not sure I'm right. It could be that there's a deeper disagreement about how to individuate philosophical concepts across traditions — whether "skepticism" names a particular argument form that has to be embedded in a certain kind of project, or whether it names a more general move that can show up in different projects. Or it could be that the two sides are answering different questions: one is asking "what does this passage argue?" and the other is asking "what is this passage for, in the context of the whole Zhuangzi?"

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, with reasons drawn from what each side says. And then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction that would unstick it, a kind of evidence that would matter, or whether we should just accept that it's not going to move and that's fine. I want a committed take, not a "on the one hand, on the other hand."
