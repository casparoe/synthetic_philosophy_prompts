Okay, I'm a PhD student working on moral responsibility and I've been going in circles on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. The conclusion is that nobody is ever truly morally responsible for anything they do, which can't be right — or at least I really don't want it to be right, since it would make resentment, gratitude, praise, blame, and punishment all rest on a mistake. But I can't find the step I'm willing to deny. I'd most like to keep the claim that ordinary people are sometimes morally responsible for what they do, but I'm not sure I'm entitled to it. Here's the arguement as I understand it:

(1) What you do, in any situation, is a function of the way you are — your beliefs, desires, values, and character.
(2) To be truly morally responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in the respects that matter for the action.
(3) To be truly responsible for the way you are, you would have to have intentionally brought it about that you are that way.
(4) But you can't intentionally bring it about that you are that way unless there was already some prior nature N in virtue of which you did the bringing about.
(5) And then, to be truly responsible for N, you'd need to have intentionally brought N about, which requires a still earlier nature, and so on without end.
(6) So you are not truly responsible for the way you are.
(7) So, by (2), you are not truly responsible for anything you do.

What bothers me is that the argument doesn't seem to depend on determinism at all — it's supposed to go through even if your choices are undetermined, because the regress is about the source of the self that chooses, not about the causal history of the particular action. So I can't get out just by insisting on free will in the usual sense. Which premise goes, and what exactly does it cost to reject it? If the argument is valid, I want to know which premise is the least costly to give up and what the price is. If it's not valid, I want to know which step doesn't follow and why it looked like it did.
