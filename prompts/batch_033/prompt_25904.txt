So I went down this rabbit hole about how you're supposed to reason when you don't know which observer you are in a big universe — like, are you a random person among all who exist, or among all who *could* exist? — and my friend who's read a bunch of philosophy blogs explained it to me like this:

"There's basically two ways to go. The self-sampling assumption (SSA) says you should reason as if you're randomly picked from all the observers that actually exist in the world. This is what gives you the Doomsday Argument: since most humans who will ever live are probably near the end of the timeline, you should expect we're near the end too, so doom soon. The self-indication assumption (SIA) flips it: you should reason as if you're randomly picked from all *possible* observers, but weighted by how many each hypothesis says exist. So if hypothesis H1 says the universe contains a trillion observers and H2 says a billion, SIA says finding yourself alive is way more likely under H1 — it's a trillion-to-a-billion update in favor of the bigger universe, just from the fact that you exist at all. The killer objection to SIA is the Presumptuous Philosopher: imagine some guy says 'I've got a theory T that says there are a trillion trillion observers, and I'm only slightly less confident in it than your theory with a billion observers. SIA says my tiny bit of credence gets multiplied by that enormous number of extra observers, so T wins overwhelmingly.' That's obviously insane, so SIA is dead. Everyone serious goes with SSA and just lives with the Doomsday conclusion."
