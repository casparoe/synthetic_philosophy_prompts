A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether legal systems ultimately rest on force rather than justice, and I have not been able to stop turning over something he kept saying. He appealed to the seventeenth-century thinker Blaise Pascal, whose position, as he summarized it, was roughly this: justice that cannot be enforced accomplishes nothing, force untempered by justice is mere tyranny, and since human beings are unable to supply power to the just, they have instead supplied the appearance of justice to the powerful. I resisted this as cynical, but afterward I began to notice that the legal institutions around us seem to do three things at once. A court will punish a creditor who uses force on his own initiative to collect a debt, while the court's own officers may seize property to collect the very same debt; the court treats a rule as law largely because it was enacted by whoever happened to hold power, whatever the moral quality of the rule; and yet the institution presents itself as administering justice rather than merely managing force. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of the relation between justice and force that this practice presupposes. Does the practice strictly presuppose something like the Pascalian view, or is it compatible with several different theories? Are the three elements I noticed consistent with one another, or does the official self-presentation as justice quietly deny what the other two elements concede? And what would have to change in the institutions, and in the way we speak about them, if the implicit theory were replaced by the view that legal authority is, at bottom, organized force?
