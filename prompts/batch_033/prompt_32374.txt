You are an expert philosopher of mind, specialising in consciousness and the metaphysics of qualia. Think through your response carefully before writing.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters, Maya and Sam, who are grad students in philosophy grabbing coffee. Maya defends epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness — the view that qualia are real but causally inert, produced by physical processes without feeding back into them. Sam thinks epiphenomenalism faces a fatal problem: the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Roughly, if qualia dont cause anything, then our judgments *about* qualia (including Maya's judgment that epiphenomenalism is true) aren't caused by qualia themselves. So how can Maya claim to know about qualia at all? Isnt her position self-undermining?

The dialogue should have real back-and-forth. Maya should have substantive replies — don't make her a strawman. For instance, she might distinguish between the *content* of a judgment and its *etiology*, or appeal to a covariation account of knowledge, or argue that Sam's objection tacitly assumes a causal theory of knowledge that begs the question. Sam should push back on those moves. Neither should "win" outright, but the reader should come away understanding why this is a genuinely hard problem.

At the end, add a brief note on where the dialogue takes interpretive liberties with the philosophical literature — e.g., if a move Maya makes is attributed to a specific philosopher but you've simplified it, or if there's a well-known reply you left out. And if you think the whole setup of the dialogue rests on a confusion (like the paradox being based on a misunderstanding of what epiphenomenalism claims), say so plainly instead of just playing along.
