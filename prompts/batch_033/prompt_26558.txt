I've been trying to work out my views on the moral difference between lying and misleading, and I'd appreciate a consistency check before I commit to anything in writing. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Lying is pro tanto morally worse than misleading, all else equal. The distinction is real and tracks something morally important.

(2) The best explanation of (1) is that lying abuses the practice of assertion. When you assert P, you invite your hearer to take your word for it—you offer a distinctive kind of assurance. Lying accepts that invitation on the hearer's behalf and then betrays it. Misleading, by contrast, works by exploiting the hearer's own inferences: the speaker says something literally true and counts on the hearer to draw a false conclusion. The hearer's reasoning does the work, so the speaker's responsibility is diminished.

(3) The size of the moral gap between lying and misleading depends on context. In low-trust contexts—adversarial negotiations, political speeches, games of bluffing—the gap is large, because hearers are not entitled to rely on speakers' assertions in the first place. In high-trust contexts—close friendships, marriages, doctor-patient relationships—the gap narrows, because misleading in those contexts also betrays a reliance interest.

(4) In certain high-stakes fiduciary contexts—a witness under oath, a doctor obtaining informed consent, an attorney disclosing a conflict of interest—the moral gap between lying and misleading collapses entirely. Both are equally wrong.

(5) An utterance counts as a lie only if the speaker intends to deceive the hearer about the content of the assertion. A false statement made in honest error is not a lie. A literally true statement made with the hope that the hearer will draw a false inference is misleading, not lying.

(6) The moral wrongness of lying is not reducible to the wrongness of violating conversational norms, even though conversational norms often track the same distinction. There is a basic moral principle against lying that is not merely a matter of etiquette or pragmatics.

I'm not sure these all hang together. In particular, (2) and (4) seem to pull in opposite directions: if what makes lying specially wrong is the abuse of assertion, and misleading doesn't involve assertion in the same way, then why does the gap between them disappear in fiduciary contexts? Is the idea that misleading in those contexts constitutes a form of assertion-abuse after all, and if so, does that stretch (2) past its natural limits? And does (3) actually follow from (2), or does it require a separate principle about trust and reliance that (2) doesn't provide? I'm not angling to keep any particular one of these—I want to know which commitments are in tension, which pairs only look like they are, how severe the genuine tensions are, and what the cheapest revision would be.
