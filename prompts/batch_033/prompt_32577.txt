I have been working through the Frege–Geach problem and have arrived at an argument that seems to me to prove too much — not just that expressivism faces a technical difficulty, but that a whole family of use-based semantic theories is ruled out in one stroke. I cannot tell whether I have made a subtle mistake, or whether the argument is sound and the landscape is simply bleaker than I thought. I would like a diagnosis.

Here is the argument:

(1) According to expressivism, the meaning of an atomic moral sentence — for example, "lying is wrong" — is constituted by the non-cognitive attitude it conventionally expresses (say, disapproval of lying).

(2) Natural languages are compositional: the meaning of a complex expression is a function of the meanings of its constituents and their mode of syntactic combination.

(3) From (1) and (2), the meaning of a complex moral sentence, such as the conditional "If lying is wrong, then getting one's brother to lie is wrong," must be a function of the attitudes expressed by its constituent moral clauses together with the semantics of the conditional connective.

(4) But a moral clause embedded in a conditional does not express the attitude it expresses when it occurs unembedded. A speaker who sincerely utters the conditional above need not disapprove of lying — she may be reasoning hypothetically from a premise she rejects. The embedded occurrence of "lying is wrong" is not the vehicle of any attitude of disapproval.

(5) Therefore, the embedded occurrence of "lying is wrong" does not have the meaning attributed to it by (1). Its meaning, in that context, cannot be the attitude it fails to express.

(6) Consequently, the conditional's meaning is not determined by the meanings of its parts in the way compositionality requires. Expressivism is incompatible with the compositionality of moral language.

(7) The argument seems to generalise. Consider any semantic theory that ties the meaning of a sentence constitutively to the speech act it is used to perform — for instance, views on which the meaning of an indicative sentence is the assertion it standardly makes, or views on which meaning is exhausted by the inferential role a sentence plays when asserted. Each such theory faces the same problem when the sentence appears embedded: the speech act is not performed, so the posited meaning-bearer is absent, yet the sentence makes the same compositional contribution. If the argument works against expressivism, it works against any force-based or use-based semantics.

(8) But that conclusion is too strong. Inferentialism, dynamic semantics, and various forms of pragmatism are live research programmes. The embedding problem is standardly treated as a special challenge for expressivism, not as a refutation of every theory that ties meaning to use. So the argument must go wrong somewhere.

Where does it go wrong? Which premise should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost the expressivist? And does the argument genuinely generalise in the way step (7) suggests, or does the generalisation rest on a confusion that is absent from the narrower argument against expressivism?
