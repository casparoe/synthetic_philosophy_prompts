Consider a legal regime of the kind found in several jurisdictions that permit medical aid in dying. A competent adult with a terminal illness and a prognosis of no more than six months may request a prescription for a lethal dose of medication. A physician may examine the patient, confirm the eligibility criteria, and write the prescription; but the patient must then self-administer the drug, and the physician is legally prohibited from administering it, even if the patient is present and reiterates the request. This is not merely a technicality: if the patient cannot swallow or lacks the use of their hands, the prohibition still stands, so the very condition that qualifies a person for assisted dying can also prevent them from receiving it in the only form the law allows. At the same time, the same legal system recognizes a competent adult's right to refuse any life-sustaining treatment at any time and for any reason, and to have that refusal honored even if death results, with no requirement of terminal illness or a short prognosis.

This pattern is philosophically puzzling. What view of the wrongness of killing, of the moral significance of the patient's own act, or of the physician's role does this combination of rules presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. In particular: does the self-administration rule presuppose that there is a morally weighty difference between a physician's administering a lethal drug and a patient's administering it to themselves, when both acts are deliberate, informed, and jointly intended to end the patient's life? Or does it instead presuppose something about autonomy — for example, that the final act must remain the patient's own — or something about the medical profession's self-understanding, such as a bright line against the physician's being the proximate cause of death? Assess whether the presupposed view is coherent with the same regime's treatment of treatment refusal and with its usual stated rationale of relieving suffering. Distinguish carefully what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories could license it. Finally, explain what would have to change in the law, or in the reasoning offered to defend it, if the presupposed view were replaced by one according to which what matters is only the relief of the patient's suffering, or only the patient's autonomous request, rather than the identity of the person who performs the final act.
