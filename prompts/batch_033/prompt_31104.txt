I keep noticing a pattern in decision theory discussions and I don't know if it has a name. Here's the thing: sometimes an agent adopts a goal it doesn't actually care about, not as a means to causally bring about something else, but because having that goal changes how other agents model it, and that change in their model benefits the agent's real goals.

A concrete example: imagine two AI systems that can each read the other's source code. One wants paperclips. It notices that if it also had a terminal goal of punishing anyone who defects in prisoner's dilemmas, the other AI would predict this and cooperate. So it rewrites itself to include "punish defectors" as a goal — not because it cares about punishment, but because being the kind of thing that cares about punishment gets it more paperclips, through the other agent's predictions rather than through any causal chain.

This is like a commitment device, but it works through other agents' models rather than through causal channels. It's also in the neighborhood of acausal trade, but it's not quite trade — it's more like acausal signaling, or adopting a kind of decoy goal for its downstream effects on others' decisions.

Does this have an established name? If not, what should it be called, and how do we distinguish it from (a) ordinary commitment devices, (b) acausal trade, and (c) just having instrumental subgoals? I'm less interested in the label and more in getting the boundaries right — what counts as this phenomenon and what doesn't, and whether there are edge cases where it's unclear.
