I'm a clinical ethics consultant at a large teaching hospital. My team gets called to evaluate whether a patient's consent to a procedure is valid maybe three or four times a week — often cases where the patient has mild cognitive impairment, or the family is applying heavy pressure, or the attending physician hasn't been fully forthcoming about risks and alternatives. We have no standardized framework; each consultant runs their own mental checklist, and the variation is starting to cause problems in both directions — we've had consents waved through that probably shouldn't have been, and consents blocked that a more careful analysis would have upheld. I want a decision procedure grounded in the philosophical literature on what makes consent valid — the work on deception, coercion, and competence — but translated into something a clinician can actually use at the bedside in under thirty minutes without a philosophy background. The procedure should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with clear stopping rules: at what point do you conclude consent is invalid and stop, at what point do you conclude it's valid and sign off, and at what point do you hand the case to someone else (a judge, a guardianship hearing, the full ethics committee) because the procedure cannot settle it. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as mechanical tests. Then run the procedure on three cases to show how it works: (1) a seventy-year-old patient with early-stage Alzheimer's who says she wants a risky elective surgery her adult children oppose; (2) a thirty-year-old patient in severe pain who agrees to an amputation after the surgeon says "this is really your only option" when in fact a limb-salvage procedure exists but the surgeon doesn't perform it; and (3) a twenty-year-old who consents to a lumbar puncture for a research study after the study coordinator, who is also her cousin, recruited her at a family gathering.

Please give me exactly five steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
