Analyse the eradication of smallpox, and the subsequent unresolved debate over whether to destroy the last declared laboratory stocks of the variola virus, as a case study in negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics. I am not asking for the history; take the standard facts as given: the last naturally occurring case occurred in 1977, eradication was certified in 1980, and the two declared stocks remain in high-security laboratories in the United States and Russia. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, in British English, with no introduction or conclusion. The five points should together do the following. (1) Identify who the relevant agents were and what a consistent negative utilitarian would say they faced and should have done, at both stages: the eradication campaign and the decision about the remaining stocks. (2) Say which features of the episode the theory explains naturally, and why. (3) Say which features it misdescribes, is silent about, or can accommodate only with strain; attend at least to the difference between preventing deaths and preventing suffering, the moral status of a non-sentient pathogen, and obligations to future generations under risk. (4) Say what the episode in turn shows about the theory: whether it emerges as a useful guide to this kind of decision, a distortion of it, or a case it never anticipated. (5) State which single argument or consideration would most likely change your overall verdict, and where your reasoning in the earlier points is least secure.
