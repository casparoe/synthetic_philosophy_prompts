A colleague in constitutional theory offered an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I have not been able to locate the step that fails.

(1) Ulysses is rational to have himself bound to the mast. He knows that his future self, once within hearing of the Sirens, will be in the grip of a preference he now disavows, and he acts now to prevent that future self from acting on it.

(2) What justifies this self-binding is that the earlier self may treat the later self's preference as non-authoritative: the preference is a foreseen and temporary distortion, and the earlier self acts on behalf of the person's considered interests.

(3) A constitutional provision is a collective precommitment of the same form. At a founding moment the polity treats certain future preferences as foreseeably distorted — by panic, faction, or short-sightedness — and binds itself so that those preferences cannot overturn rights and procedures that serve the polity's enduring interests.

(4) If the reasoning in (2) justifies one self binding a later self, then by parity it justifies one generation binding later generations through entrenched constitutional provisions.

(5) But that parity justifies far too much. It would equally justify a founding generation entrenching provisions that later generations would almost unanimously reject: a permanent exclusion of some group from office, a rule that all future amendments be ratified by a body no longer answerable to anyone, or an eternity clause protecting the founders' own interests.

(6) No founding generation has that open-ended authority over the future.

(7) So the parity argument must fail, and with it the standard justification of constitutional precommitment. We are left either with no principled basis for constitutionalism or with a disanalogy between the personal and the political cases that no one has stated precisely.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (6) and to locate the failure in the parity move at (4), but I cannot state the disanalogy without either erasing the difference between a person over time and a succession of persons, or making constitutionalism look like a fiction. Where does the argument go wrong? If it does not go wrong at (4), which premise should be given up, and what does giving it up cost? If it does go wrong at (4), state the disanalogy precisely and say what, if anything, remains of the justification of constitutional precommitment.
