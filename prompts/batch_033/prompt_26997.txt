I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend about whether religious citizens in a liberal democracy should refrain from relying on religious reasons when advocating for coercive laws. We went through the familiar moves — Rawls's proviso, the charge that excluding religious reasons is itself illiberal, the reply that religious reasons can enter public debate so long as they're eventually translatable into accessible terms — and by two in the morning we had managed to clarify almost nothing. What bothers me isn't that we disagreed; it's that I came away unsure which of our disagreements actually matter.

I'd like you to identify and rank the open questions in the public reason literature on religious arguments in politics, where the ranking is by how much settling the question would change what we should expect of citizens and legislators in practice. For each question: explain why it's upstream of other disputes, what turns on it for the conduct of actual political actors, and whether resolving it is primarily a philosophical task or whether it depends on empirical claims — about what kinds of justification actually sustain reciprocity among citizens, about what happens to democratic stability or minority inclusion when religious reasons are or aren't offered, and so on. I also want to know which questions the literature spends disproportionate energy on: questions that are downstream of something else, or that wouldn't change anyone's practical obligations much no matter how they're resolved.

Assume I'm not looking to contribute to the academic literature myself. I'm trying to figure out, as someone who votes, argues, and occasionally organizes, which parts of this debate I should actually care about — and which I can safely ignore while still taking the underlying normative concerns seriously.
