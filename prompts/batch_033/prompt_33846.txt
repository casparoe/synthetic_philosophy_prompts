"Explain how the accuracy-first program argues for probabilism. Then discuss whether the same strategy can be extended to justify conditionalization. Are the two arguments structurally on all fours?"

And here's the student's answer:

"The accuracy-first approach, developed mainly by James Joyce in his 1998 paper 'A Nonpragmatic Vindication of Probabilism,' treats credences as aiming at accuracy the way beliefs aim at truth. The key move is to measure the inaccuracy of a credence function at a world using a strictly proper scoring rule like the Brier score. A scoring rule is strictly proper if the expected score of reporting your true credences is at least as good as reporting any other credences — you can't expect to do better by being dishonest about what you believe.

Joyce's argument for probabilism is a dominance argument. It shows that if your credences violate the probability axioms, there exists some probabilistically coherent credence function that is strictly more accurate than yours in every possible world. Since accuracy is your only epistemic goal, you shouldn't adopt a dominated credence function. This is structurally similar to a Dutch book argument except it's purely epistemic — no betting, no pragmatic considerations.

Now, the argument for conditionalization works in essentially the same way. If you plan to update by some rule other than conditionalization, then there's an alternative updating plan that accuracy-dominates yours: it's more accurate no matter what evidence you recieve. So conditionalization is the unique update rule that avoids accuracy dominance. Both probabilism and conditionalization fall out of the same basic idea — don't adopt a dominated state — just applied to update plans rather than static credences.

One important limitation is that the accuracy-first framework presupposes the inaccuracy measure is strictly proper. If you use an improper scoring rule, the dominance results don't go through. But since strict propriety is independently motivated — it enforces a kind of immodesty where each credence function considers itself optimal — this isn't really a problem. The framework stands on solid ground."
