I hold the view that the three stances framework — the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, as developed by Daniel Dennett — is not merely a set of pragmatic heuristics we resort to because of our cognitive limitations. Rather, these stances track real patterns in the world, and the patterns picked out by the intentional stance in particular are ineliminable in a strong sense: no description couched purely at the physical or design level could match the predictive and explanatory power of the intentional stance for a wide class of systems (roughly, those complex enough that treating them as agents with beliefs and desires yields reliable, projectible predictions). The intentional stance, on this view, latches onto something genuinely real — real patterns of intentionality — and these patterns would remain real and useful even for an ideal predictor with unlimited computational resources, because the patterns themselves are part of the furniture of the world and not merely artifacts of our bounded perspective.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for a summary of existing criticisms from the literature; I want a map of the view's exposure. What evidence, arguments, counterfactual developments, or empirical results would a defender of the view have to recognize as counting against it? I would like the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be: which would be fatal, which would be merely awkward, and which would force a retreat to a weaker version of the position (e.g., conceding that the intentional stance is in principle eliminable even if not in practice, or that the patterns it tracks are real but wholly reducible to design-stance or physical-stance patterns after all).

In particular, please address the following:

- What would it actually look like for a physical-level or design-level account to outperform the intentional stance in its home territory — say, predicting human behavior in ecologically valid, real-world contexts? Is this outcome in principle possible, and if it were to occur, would it refute the view outright or merely shrink the domain of systems for which the intentional stance is ineliminable?
- Does the view make any predictions that differ from the instrumentalist alternative (according to which the intentional stance is useful but does not track anything mind-independent)? If the two views make all the same predictions, is that a problem for the realist version, and if so, what kind of problem is it?
- Are there actual cases — from neuroscience, artificial intelligence, or elsewhere — that already put pressure on the view, even if they do not refute it? For instance, cases where the intentional stance systematically misleads, or where a lower-level account delivers predictions the intentional stance cannot?
- Which of the potential defeaters would a committed defender of the view concede as genuine (even if they think the view ultimately survives them), and which would they dismiss as changing the subject or resting on a misunderstanding of what the view claims?
- Does the view have a clear story about what would make the intentional stance break down for a system we currently apply it to successfully, and what that breakdown would imply?

Finally, I would like an honest assessment of whether the view, as I have stated it, has been or can be qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a genuine problem for a view of this kind, or whether the demand for straightforward empirical falsifiability is misplaced when the claim is about the ineliminability of a pattern relative to all possible descriptions at other levels.
