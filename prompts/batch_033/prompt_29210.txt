Wear your philosopher-of-action and decision-theory hat for this one, and think carefully before you answer — maybe start by brainstorming the sub-questions you'd need to settle, then commit.

Take Kavka's toxin puzzle: a billionaire will pay you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinly intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin makes you sick for a day but does no lasting damage, and you don't actually have to drink it — the money is for the intention alone, at midnight. You know all this, and you're rational.

What's your actual credence that a rational agent in that setup can collect the million dollars? I don't want "it depends on your theory of intention" — I want a number and the derivation. Decompose it into the sub-questions you think carry the weight: e.g., how much rides on whether intentions are under direct voluntary control, how much on whether you can intend to do something you currently expect you won't do, how much on whether there's a workable loophole (self-binding, hiring someone to force you, intending-to-drink-but-expecting-to-change-your-mind), and how much on whether the puzzle's setup is even coherent. Give a rough probability for each sub-question, with one line of justification each, and show how they combine into your final credence. Then tell me wich sub-question your number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it the most.
