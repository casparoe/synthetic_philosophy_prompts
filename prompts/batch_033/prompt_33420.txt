Every time our seminar on minds and machines gets to the Ant Fugue — the dialogue with Aunt Hillary the ant colony — somebody ends the argument with "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts," and everyone nods like that settles it. The question it's usually being used to settle is whether the colony itself can think, or have a self, over and above all the individual ants. But I've started noticing the same sentence doing different work depending on who says it: one person used it to mean the colony has a mind that can't be reduced to the ants, another used it to mean just that colonies do things no single ant does, like move a pile of dirt or regulate the nest temperature, and a third used it to say the whole mind question is a category mistake.

I have to write a response paper on this and I dont want to just wave the slogan around myself. Could you lay out the distinct claims that one-liner might actually be making, from the boring to the wild, and for each say whether it's true, whether it's trivial, and whether it actually gets you to "an ant colony has a mind" — or at least to "that isn't nonsense"?
