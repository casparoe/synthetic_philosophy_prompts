I'm a philosophy professor now, but before grad school I spent six years as an ML engineer, so I'm genuinly fluent in that vocabulary — features, labels, hypothesis classes, generalization, overfitting, distribution shift, all of it. I want you to do a translation exercise for me, and I want it done honestly.

Take the debate between moral generalists and moral particularists. By generalism I mean the view that there are at least some true, informative moral principles that cover more than one case: principles that say a given feature counts morally in favor across contexts, even if it can be outweighed. By particularism I mean the stronger view that moral judgment isn't a matter of applying principles to cases at all. A consideration that is a reason for an act here can be a reason against a similiar-looking act there, and no finite set of true principles codifies rightness. The particularist thinks the generalist's project is like trying to write down rules where the contribution of each feature depends on the whole configuration, and the interactions never bottom out.

Restate this debate in the vocabulary of machine learning, as faithfully as you can. Don't start from the assumption that there must be some function from features to rightness and then declare the generalist the winner by default; if the ML vocabulary can't state the particularist's claim, I want that on the table as residue, not fudged. I'm especially interested in how you'd cash out these pieces: the holism of reasons (feature interactions that flip sign depending on context?), the idea of a codifiable moral theory (the hypothesis class being learnable?), the particularist's claim that judgment is a skill trained on cases rather than rule-following (case-based reasoning? in-context learning?), and the generalist's reply that without principles we can't explain why a new case is like an old one (generalization error?).

Then give me the residue report. Which parts map cleanly, which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no counterpart in the ML vocabulary at all. And tell me whether the residue points to a limitation of the ML framework — for example, it's built around learning functions and can't even express the thought that there's no function there to learn — or whether the philosophical debate was leaning on distinctions that don't survive contact with a sharper vocabulary. Be honest about where the translation does violence to one side or the other.
