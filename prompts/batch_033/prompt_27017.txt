You are a philosopher of science who works on the demarcation problem and has a working knowledge of U.S. evidence law. Consider the Daubert standard under which federal judges act as gatekeepers for expert testimony: before admitting proffered scientific or technical evidence, they are instructed to weigh such factors as whether the theory or technique can be and has been tested, whether it has been subjected to peer review and publication, whether its known or potential error rates are established and controlled, and whether it is generally accepted in the relevant scientific community. Reconstruct the implicit theory of demarcation — the account of what separates reliable science from non-science or unreliable expertise — that this screening practice presupposes, or argue that no single such theory is presupposed. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; say whether its factors presuppose one coherent epistemology or quietly combine rivals, for example a Popperian testability criterion alongside inductivist appeals to peer consensus and error rates; and then spell out what would have to change in how judges screen expert evidence if that implicit theory were replaced by a Bayesian confirmation account, or by the view that there is no principled line between science and non-science. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the strongest reading of each factor before settling on a reconstruction.
