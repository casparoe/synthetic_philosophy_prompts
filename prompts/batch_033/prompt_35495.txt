I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar on concepts and categories — about 15 students, 80-minute sessions. Before we read Hofstadter and Mitchell on Copycat and fluid concepts, I want to run an activity that makes students feel conceptual slippage from the inside: the way the boundaries of a concept can shift fluidly depending on context, without the person noticing it happened. I do not want to front-load the theory or even mention the term slippage; I want them to experience it directly and only then name what they experienced.

Design an activity built around letter-string analogy problems of the kind Copycat works on — for instance, abc → abd, so ijk → ? — but extend it so that the group encounters genuinely ambiguous cases where multiple answers are defensible and which answer feels right depends on how you conceptualized the relationship in the first place. The rules or structure should be arranged so that students surface the moments where their concept of the same rule or the same relationship shifts without their awareness, and then must articulate to one another what just happened and why.

Include: rules, materials, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the theoretical notions of slippage, the terraced scan, and the temperature parameter. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong — which conceptual leaps students will resist, where the activity will stall — and be honest about which features of the running Copycat architecture (the parallel terraced scan, nondeterminism) the classroom activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator will need to supply by explanation afterwards. No lecture up front.
