A friend in my logic group has been insisting this shows knowledge is closed under known entailment only if we're willing to accept something insane, and I can't find the bad step. Here's the argument:

(1) If you know P, and you know that P entails Q, then you know Q. You might not have written Q down, but you can't fail to know it — the inference is right there, you just have to take the last step.

(2) Take a bright student who's finished a first course in classical propositional logic. They know the axioms of the system, and they know the general fact that every theorem of the system is entailed by the axioms — that's basically the soundness direction they were taught.

(3) So for any particular theorem T, the student knows that the axioms entail T.

(4) By (1), then, the student knows T.

(5) So the student knows every theorem of the system.

(6) But among the theorems is some tautology with, say, a trillion symbols — one nobody has ever actually written down or even considered.

(7) So the student knows a trillion-symbol tautology nobody has ever considered. That can't be right.

(8) So (1) has to go, and standard epistemic logic, which builds in exactly that closure principle, is false.

I'd rather keep (1), since it seems like the least I can ask of a notion of knowledge, but I also dont want to say the student is somehow blocked from knowing general facts about the system they've just learned. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
