I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar called "The Ethics of Belief," and we've spent the last two weeks on William James's "The Will to Believe" and W.K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief." I'm finding the debate genuinely difficult to settle. Both sides seem right to me in different moods. On a Tuesday I'm a Cliffordian: how could it possibly be permissible to believe something when the evidence doesn't support it? Belief isn't a private act — it shapes how we act, and our actions affect others, sometimes gravely. When the shipowner in Clifford's essay suppresses his doubts and sends an unsound vessel to sea, the wrongness seems to lie in the believing itself, not just in the sinking. But by Thursday I'm a Jamesian: there are choices in life where you can't wait for the evidence to come in, and suspending judgment is itself a choice with practical consequences. If you refuse to believe in the possibility of a friendship or a career path until you have conclusive evidence that it will work out, you've already foreclosed it. The religious hypothesis is the most dramatic case, but James's point about "forced, living, and momentous" options seems to apply much more broadly.

I've been arguing about this with my flatmate, and we keep talking past each other. I'll make a point about the shipowner, and he'll counter with the friendship case, and then I'll say that friendship isn't really a belief, and then we're stuck. What I think I really need is to hear the two positions worked out in genuine conversation — not just a summary of each view, but a real back-and-forth where each side has to respond to the other's best points, where concessions are made, and where the shape of the disagreement becomes clearer.
