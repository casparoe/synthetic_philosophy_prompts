I run a small community mediation practice, and a lot of my cases arent really about interests — they're about values. A congregation says it has a religious duty to shelter homeless people; the neighborhood association says it has a duty to keep the block safe and quiet for the kids. A worker co-op says equal pay is a non-negotiable principle; the investor says oversight and performance pay are just what responsible stewardship requires. I've started to notice that some settlements work because each side cares about different things to different degrees, so they can trade: each gives up something it values a little to get something it values a lot. Like gains from trade, but with moral commitments instead of goods. But I also see settlements that feel like somebody got bought off, or like both sides are happy because they dumped the cost on someone who isn't in the room.

I want a reusable procedure I can run on a proposed settlement before I put it in front of the parties. I'm not a philosopher and neither are they, so it has to be plain language. I've usually got about an hour per case, the parties have to sign off at the end, and my real worry is that I'll bless a deal that's actually one side capitulating, or a third party paying for everyone's good feelings.

Please write the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to stop and say "this is a values call a person has to make, not something the checklist can settle." It should also tell the difference between a real difference in what the parties care about and one side just having less power or fewer options. And be honest about what the procedure cant catch even when it's run perfectly.

Then run it on these three proposed settlements:

1. A congregation wants to open a 30-bed homeless shelter in its building. The neighborhood association opposes it, saying they value the quiet and safety of the block and have a duty to protect their kids. The proposed compromise is a 15-bed shelter with a 9pm check-in, a hired security person, and the congregation pays for a neighborhood liaison. The congregation says 15 beds is far less than their duty requires, but they'd take it to avoid a years-long zoning fight.

2. A worker co-op that pays every member the same hourly rate is taking an investment from someone who wants a voting board seat and performance bonuses for a new technical hire. The compromise: the investor gets a non-voting board seat, the new hire gets market pay, and the co-op pools the difference and redistributes it equally each quarter. The co-op says equal pay is a core value, but this keeps the business alive; the investor says oversight is a core responsibility, but a non-voting seat is enough for now.

3. A union and management both want to avoid layoffs at a small manufacturing plant. They propose covering the shortfall by cutting retiree health benefits. Both sides at the table like the deal: current workers keep their jobs and management keeps production going. The retirees aren't represented in the mediation.

I especially want to know what the procedure says about the third one, because I suspect my checklist would wave it through and I need to know if that's wrong.
