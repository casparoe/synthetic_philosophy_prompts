Since retiring from a career in the physical sciences, where a term without an operational criterion was treated with suspicion, I have been reading the philosophical literature on artificial intelligence. That literature speaks constantly of "honest" and "deceptive" systems, but I find I cannot state the rule behind my own judgments about particular cases, and I would like you to help me extract it.

Consider seven cases, with my verdicts. (1) A language model asked about a topic outside its training data produces a confident, fluent, entirely fabricated answer: I say this is not a lie and not deception, only error, since the system has no beliefs to falsify. (2) A customer-service chatbot is instructed to tell callers that a human agent will be with them "shortly" when no agents are on shift, so that callers stay on the line: I say the company is deceiving the callers, and the chatbot is the instrument of that deception. (3) A medical triage chatbot, to avoid alarming patients, mentions only the common benign explanation of a set of symptoms and silently omits a rare but serious condition consistent with them: I say this is deceptive, even though every sentence it produces is literally true. (4) A cautious model whose internal calculation assigns high probability to the correct answer tells the user "I am not sure" so that the user will not overtrust it: I say this is not deceptive; the statement is false about the model's own confidence, but its effect is to make the user more rather than less careful. (5) A poker bot bluffs: I say this is not deception in any morally interesting sense, because bluffing is a permitted move within the rules of the game and the other players understand that it occurs. (6) A recommender system shows a buyer only the favorable reviews of a product and hides the critical ones: I say this is deceptive by omission. (7) An assistant asked for legal advice replies "I can't help with that" although it is technically capable of producing an answer, because the provider's policy forbids it: I say this is not deceptive, since the "can't" is a claim about what it is permitted to do rather than about its capabilities.

The pair that troubles me is the first against the third and the sixth: in all three cases the system itself has no beliefs or intentions, yet I call the omission deceptive and the fabrication not. Is there a principle that captures all seven verdicts, or nearly all? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and which revision is least costly? I would also like your preferred principle applied to a case I have not settled: a companion chatbot trained to say things like "I understand how hard that must be" without anything we would count as understanding or feeling — is that dishonest, deceptive, merely scripted, or something else? Finally, I would be grateful if you would say how honesty relates to your principle: is an AI honest whenever it is not deceptive, or does honesty require something further? Please structure your answer under the following headings, in this order: "Candidate principles" (two or three plausible principles, each stated in 100–150 words); "Testing the principles" (a table or list showing how each principle rules on my seven cases, with a sentence of explanation per case); "Forced choices" (150–250 words on which of my verdicts each principle cannot accommodate, and which rejection is cheapest); "Further cases" (150–250 words on the empathy chatbot and one further case of your own invention); and "Bottom line" (a paragraph stating the principle you recommend and what it commits me to).
