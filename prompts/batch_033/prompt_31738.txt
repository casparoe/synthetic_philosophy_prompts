I've been puzzling over something I've observed across several different settings — hiring committees, online forums, and even casual decisions among friends — and I'm hoping there's a name and a literature for it, because my own attempts to look it up keep landing on concepts that are close but not quite right.

Here are three instances. First: an eight-person hiring committee interviews five candidates. Everyone has read the same files, but each member also has private impressions from the interviews. The chair calls on people in a fixed order. The first two speakers express a preference for candidate A and give reasons. The third speaker, who privately preferred candidate B, says something like "I can see the case for A, and I don't feel strongly enough about B to push against the emerging consensus." By the time the fourth person speaks, the room is treating A as the settled choice. Later, I learn that five of the eight members privately preferred B, but each of the later speakers assumed the earlier ones must have had more compelling reasons, and their own doubts seemed insufficient to overturn what looked like a rational consensus. Second: an online forum for a niche hobby is discussing which brand of equipment is most reliable. The first three commenters recommend Brand X, citing various experiences. The fourth commenter has had terrible experiences with X but writes, "I might have just gotten a lemon, but for what it's worth I had issues with X." The comment is ignored. The thread solidifies around X. A year later, a survey reveals that a majority of the forum's members actually prefer Brand Y, but the archived threads overwhelmingly recommend X. Third: a group of friends is deciding where to eat. Three people immediately suggest a new Italian place. The fourth person, who ate there last week and found it mediocre, stays quiet, reasoning that the others must know something she doesn't — maybe she went on an off night. The group goes to the Italian place, and four people are quietly disappointed.

What these cases seem to have in common: a group needs to make a decision; individuals have private information that, if aggregated, would point one way; but the sequential or semi-sequential nature of the discussion causes the group to converge on a different answer, because later participants rationally discount their own information in light of the expressed views of earlier participants. And this happens without intimidation, without anyone being irrational, and without anyone being insincere. I've looked at concepts like groupthink, the bandwagon effect, and pluralistic ignorance, but none of them captures the mechanism. Groupthink involves pressure to conform and a desire for group cohesion, which isn't driving these cases. The bandwagon effect is about wanting to be on the winning side, not about rationally inferring that others know more. Pluralistic ignorance is about everyone privately rejecting a norm while believing everyone else accepts it, which is different from everyone privately holding corrective information that gets lost in the sequence of discussion.

Is there an established term for this specific phenomenon — the rational but self-defeating discounting of private information in sequential group decision-making? And if there is, what does the literature say about the conditions under which it produces errors rather than improving decisions? If there isn't a single term, what are the closest concepts, and how exactly does what I'm describing differ from each? I'm not satisfied with a bare label; I want to understand the boundaries of the phenomenon and whether the three cases I've given are really the same thing or different things that happen to look similar.
