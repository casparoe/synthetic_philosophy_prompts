I cover neuroscience and the science of mind for a general-interest magazine, and after a year of reporting on whole-brain emulation I have accumulated a set of gut verdicts about which procedures would preserve a person and which would merely produce a copy. I would like to know whether there is a principled account behind those verdicts, or whether I am simply inconsistent. Here are the cases as I find myself judging them.

1. Gradual in-place replacement. Over roughly a decade, a patient's biological neurons are replaced one at a time by silicon neurons that are functionally equivalent to the ones they replace, each new neuron wired so that the surrounding network cannot detect a functional difference. The patient is awake for much of the process and reports an unbroken sense of being the same person. Verdict: the patient survives, and the silicon-brained person at the end is the same person as the original.

2. Destructive scan-and-run. A person's brain is scanned at a resolution sufficient to reconstruct its connectivity, a process that destroys the tissue, and the resulting map is used to run a simulation of that person. Verdict: the person dies; the simulation is a new person with the same memories.

3. Non-destructive copy. The same kind of scan is performed without destroying the brain. The original leaves the scanner, and a simulation of them is started on a computer. Verdict: the original is the person; the simulation is a distinct person, a kind of twin rather than a continuation.

4. Whole-brain replacement under anesthesia. A patient is anesthetized, and the entire brain is replaced in a single operation by a silicon brain that implements the same functions. On waking, the patient has the same memories, skills, and personality. Verdict: I am torn. My first reaction is that this is death followed by a copy, but I cannot identify anything that happened in the operation that differs in kind from case 1, only in speed and in the absence of consciousness during the transition.

5. Piecemeal transfer to a computer. A patient's brain is connected to a computer, and over many months subsystems are transferred one at a time: a cortical module is simulated on the machine, its biological counterpart is then disabled, and the rest of the brain begins interacting with the simulated module. The patient remains awake and reports no interruption. At the end the biological brain is inert and the entire mind runs on the computer. Verdict: the patient survives, and the final simulation is the same person.

6. Restoration from a backup after death. A person dies suddenly, and an hour later a backup made the previous week is run as a simulation. Verdict: this is not the person; it is a copy with a week of missing memories.

7. Branching during piecemeal transfer. In a variant of case 5, each biological module is not destroyed when it is simulated. Instead it is kept alive and its activity is fed back into the system in parallel with the simulated module, so that for a while the person has, in effect, two copies of each transferred subsystem running at once. Eventually the biological modules are disconnected, and the vat-supported biological brain wakes up as a second claimant to the person's identity. Verdict: I have no stable intuition. It seems wrong to say both are the original, but I cannot see a non-arbitrary reason to pick one.

Is there a single principle of personal identity — or of what matters in survival — that yields most of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, say what it predicts for cases I have not listed, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. I especially want to know whether the tension between case 2 and case 5 forces a choice: if the only differences are the pace of the process and whether the system is kept running throughout, must I give up one of those two verdicts, or accept a threshold view with all the arbitrariness that invites? Please also apply the principle to three further cases: a person whose brain is replaced while awake but very quickly, a tenth of the brain at a time over the course of an hour; a person whose brain is replaced gradually but under continuous anesthesia, waking only after the process is complete; and two simultaneous, non-destructive copies made from one original at a single moment.

Because I intend to use this in a reported piece, I need to know how much of this is settled. Please say how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the specific point in your answer you are least sure of.
