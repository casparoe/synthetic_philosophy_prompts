There is a long-running dispute about whether the Kelly criterion — maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth across repeated bets — is the uniquely rational policy for an agent facing a sequence of favorable gambles, or whether it is simply one possible risk preference among many, derivable from a logarithmic utility function but not rationally mandatory for anyone who does not happen to have that utility function. On one side, the claim is that Kelly betting is distinguished by its asymptotic properties: it dominates other strategies in the long run, it maximizes the growth rate of capital, and it avoids the near-certain ruin that more aggressive betting brings. On the other side, the claim is that long-run arguments only have force if you already care about log wealth — that rationality cannot be read off the environment without specifying what the agent values, and that maximizing expected utility with any reasonable utility function is consistent with rationality. The dispute has absorbed contributions from finance, probability theory, ergodicity economics, and decision theory, and yet it does not seem to have moved in decades: the same arguments are rehearsed, and neither side concedes.

Are the two sides in fact answering different questions (one about optimal growth, one about preference consistency) and mistaking them for the same question? What would count as progress — a formal theorem, a conceptual distinction, a new kind of decision problem — and is there reason to think it could settle the matter?
