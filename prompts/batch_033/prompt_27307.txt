I've been thinking about personal identity lately, specifically what makes someone the same person over time, and I keep going back and forth. I'd like to lay out my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and see if there's a principle that actually fits them — or if I need to give some of them up.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. **Standard teletransporter.** I step into a scanner on Earth. It records the exact state of every cell in my body, destroys me in the process, transmits the data to Mars, and builds an exact replica out of new matter. The person who wakes up on Mars has all my memories, personality, quirks, everything. I think that person *is* me. I survive.

2. **Branch-line case.** Same as above, except the scanner malfunctions: it records me and transmits the data, but doesn't destroy the original. The original me on Earth learns that my heart was fatally damaged in the scan and I'll die in three days. The replica on Mars is fine. I think the Mars person is me, and the dying original is also me — there are now two of me, one of whom is about to die. I still count this as surviving.

3. **Fission.** Same scan-and-replicate process, but this time two replicas are made, one on Mars and one on Venus. Both are psychologically continuous with the original, and the original is destroyed. Neither replica ever meets the other. I don't think either one is me. I think I died. This feels different from case 1, even though in case 1 there's one replica and here there are two.

4. **Gradual replacement.** Over the course of ten years, surgeons replace each of my neurons one by one with silicon chips that perform the same function. At the end, my brain is entirely silicon but my psychology is continuous — no gaps, no sudden jumps. I think I survive this. It's still me.

5. **Uploading (destructive).** My brain is destructively scanned and a perfect software emulation of it runs on a computer. The emulation has all my memories and traits. I think this is me, I survive — basically the same intuition as the teletransporter.

6. **Uploading (nondestructive).** Same as above, but the scan doesn't destroy my biological brain. The original biological me wakes up from the scan and continues living. The software me also wakes up in the computer. I think the software me is me, and the biological me is also me — two of me, like the branch-line case. But then I notice: this is structurally just like the fission case (one original, two continuers), except one continuer is biological and the other is digital. And yet my verdict flipped — here I say I survive, there I said I die. That bothers me.

7. **Total amnesia plus radical personality change.** I get in a car accident, lose all episodic memories, and my personality changes so drastically that my friends say I'm a different person. But there's no break in consciousness — I was in a coma, not dead. I think I survive, but something deeply important is lost. This isn't the same kind of thing as the teletransporter cases; it feels like a different axis.

8. **The teletransporter with a delay.** The scanner destroys me, but the replica isn't built until five years later. During those five years, no one exists who is psychologically continuous with me. I'm not sure what I think here. It feels like I died and then someone exactly like me was created later, which doesn't feel like survival. But if the delay were five seconds I'd say it's fine. I don't know where the cutoff is or why there should be one.

So here's what I'm asking: is there a single principle about what matters in survival — or about personal identity — that gets most of these right? In particular, can any principle handle the fact that I treat the fission case (case 3) as death but the branch-line and nondestructive upload cases (2 and 6) as survival, when they seem to have the same structure? And what would that principle say about case 8, and about a case I haven't thought of — say, a teletransporter that makes a replica but the replica is only 80% psychologically similar to the original? If no clean principle fits, which of my verdicts is the one I should probably abandon?
