The question was:

"Explain the pessimistic meta-induction argument against scientific realism. Then explain how structural realism attempts to respond to the argument, and evaluate whether this response succeeds."

Here's the student's answer:

"The pessimistic meta-induction (PMI) is an argument against scientific realism first clearly formulated by Larry Laudan in his 1981 paper 'A Confutation of Convergent Realism.' The argument goes as follows: when we look at the history of science, we find many theories that were empirically successful by the standards of their time—they made accurate predictions, explained phenomena, and were widely accepted—but which we now believe to be radically false. Examples include Ptolemaic astronomy, phlogiston theory, the caloric theory of heat, and the ether theory of light. From these cases, Laudan draws an inductive conclusion: there is a strong historical pattern of successful theories turning out to be false, so by induction, our current successful theories are also likely to turn out to be false. This undermines the 'no miracles' argument for realism (associated with Putnam and Boyd), which claims that the empirical success of science would be a miracle unless our theories are at least approximately true.

Structural realism, proposed by John Worrall, is one of the most influential realist responses to the PMI. Worrall argues that the PMI only threatens realism about the nature of unobservable entities—what he calls the 'content' of theories—but not realism about their mathematical or structural features. He uses the transition from Fresnel's ether theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory as his key example. Fresnel's equations describing reflection, refraction, and polarization were carried over essentially unchanged into Maxwell's theory, even though Maxwell rejected the ether as the carrier of light waves. Worrall concludes that what is preserved across theory change is structure, not content, and therefore the realist should only commit to the structural claims of theories.

In my view, structural realism successfully defuses the PMI. Since the mathematical structure of our theories is what gets preserved across revolutionary theory change, we can be confident that the structural claims of our current best theories will be retained in future science. The PMI only shows that we should not be realists about the specific entities postulated by theories, which is a concession realists can live with. The fact that Fresnel's equations survive in Maxwell's theory gives us inductive grounds for optimism about the structural content of our current theories, rather than the pessimism Laudan advocates. The structural realist thus keeps the key realist intuition—that science tracks truth—while avoiding the historical counterexamples Laudan raises."
