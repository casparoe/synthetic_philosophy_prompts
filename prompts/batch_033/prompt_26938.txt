I am a PhD student in formal epistemology working on self-locating belief and anthropic reasoning. I would like you to write a rigorous philosophical dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors, one committed to the self-sampling assumption and the other committed to the self-indication assumption, as they work through the presumptuous philosopher scenario and several connected problems. The scenario should be this: scientists have narrowed the fundamental theory down to two hypotheses that fit all non-anthropic evidence equally well, but one hypothesis predicts an enormously larger total population of observers than the other. The defender of the self-indication assumption should maintain that one's own existence gives strong evidence for the hypothesis with many observers. The defender of the self-sampling assumption should resist that conclusion and argue that the inference is illicitly sensitive to an arbitrary choice of reference class. The dialogue should include real back-and-forth: the self-indication defender should press the self-sampling defender to state a principled reference class and to explain why the resulting probabilities are not objectionably arbitrary; the self-sampling defender should press the self-indication defender to explain why one's own existence is evidence for the number of other observers, and whether the same reasoning would license overconfident inferences in everyday cases. The two should also connect the dispute to the doomsday argument and to the Sleeping Beauty problem, and each should respond to the other's actual points. At two or three junctures, one character should explicitly concede a local point or refine their position before offering a rejoinder. The dialogue should avoid strawmanning and should reflect the technical substance of the current debate rather than summarizing the literature.
