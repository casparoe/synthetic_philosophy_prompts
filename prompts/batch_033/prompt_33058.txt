I've been goin back and forth on this for weeks and my philosophy of probability professor just keeps grinning at me when I bring it up, so I'm hoping someone can actually walk through the diagnosis.

Here's an argument that seems airtight but lands somewhere completely insane.

(1) Standard Bayesian reasoning says: when two theories fit all the data equally well, prefer the one that makes your actual observations more probable.

(2) Consider two cosmological theories. Theory A says the universe contains roughly a trillion observers. Theory B says it contains roughly a trillion trillion observers. In every other respect—the laws of physics, the distribution of galaxies, everything we could ever measure—they are identical. The extra observers in Theory B are in regions beyond our causal horizon, or in other branches of the wavefunction, or whatever mechanism you like; the point is we can never detect them.

(3) One of my observations is that I exist. Under the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), the probability that I in particular would find myself existing is proportional to the total number of observers the theory posits. So Theory B makes my existence more likely by a factor of about a trillion.

(4) Therefore, by (1), I should be overwhelmingly confident in Theory B—a theory that differs from Theory A only by claiming there are vastly more people I can never see, never interact with, and never get evidence about.

(5) But that's obviously nuts. A theory doesn't get confirmed just by multiplying the number of unobservable minds. If this reasoning is right, then for any theory you could cook up a variant that posits a trillion times more observers and it would automatically be favored, which makes a mockery of empirical science.

(6) So something in (1)–(4) has to give. The natural move is to reject SIA in favor of the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA): you should reason as if you are randomly selected from the set of all observers that exist. That kills the presumptuous philosopher—under SSA, Theory A and Theory B are equally likely because in either case you're just one observer among many.

(7) But here's the problem. If you go SSA, you seem to walk straight into the Doomsday Argument. Roughly: if humanity lasts a long time with a large future population, then most humans who will ever live are in the future, and it would be wildly unlikely that I happen to be among the first few percent. So SSA says I should expect humanity to end relatively soon. Which is also insane.

(8) So we seem trapped: SIA gives us the presumptuous philosopher, SSA gives us doomsday. Both start from plausible principles and land in absurdity.

I want to know: which step in the chain (1)–(4) is actually the wrong one, and what does rejecting it cost? Or is the real problem somewhere else—maybe in the way I'm framing the choice between SIA and SSA as exhaustive, or in the setup of (2) where the two theories are treated as live options? If you think the argument is valid and the move is to bite a bullet, tell me which bullet you'd bite and why it's less bad than the other one.
