I'm a software engineer on an ML team that builds screening models for hiring — resume scoring, initial candidate ranking, that kind of thing. I don't have any formal background in philosophy or ethics, but I've ended up as the person on the team who reads the fairness literature and tries to translate it into actual model constraints, which means I spend a lot of time confused.

Here's the thing I keep running into, and I'm hoping there's an established concept for it. We want our model to be fair across demographic groups. But "fair" seems to decompose into several different properties, and they conflict in a way that feels less like a normal engineering tradeoff and more like a mathematical impossibility. Specifically: we can ask that the false positive rate be equal across groups — the model shouldn't recommend unqualified candidates from one group more often than from another. We can ask that the false negative rate be equal — the model shouldn't miss qualified candidates from one group more often. And we can ask that the model's positive predictions be equally well-calibrated across groups — when the model assigns a score of, say, 0.8 to candidates from two different groups, the actual probability that those candidates are good hires should be the same regardless of group membership. The trouble is that as soon as the base rate of qualified candidates differs between groups — which it does in our data, for reasons that have everything to do with structural inequality in the pipeline — we cannot satisfy all three at once. If we equalize false positive rates, calibration breaks. If we force calibration, the error rates diverge. It feels like a conservation law: you get to pick two.

I've seen terms like "demographic parity" and "equalized odds" and "predictive parity" floating around, and I gather they name the individual criteria. But what I'm looking for is the name, if there is one, for the result that says you cannot simultaneously satisfy this whole cluster of criteria when base rates differ. Is this a named theorem or impossibility result? How exactly is it stated, and what are its assumptions — does it require something strong like the base rates actually differing, or does it hold under even weaker conditions? And are there genuine escape hatches, or do all the proposed responses just amount to picking one criterion, relabeling it "fairness," and living with the violation of the others? If there's no established name, I'd want a term that captures why this is a harder problem than an ordinary tradeoff — something about the criteria being individually reasonable but jointly unsatisfiable.
