For a graduate seminar on the cognitive science of religion, write a decision tree that takes a reader from a single opening question to a named position in the naturalistic study of religion. Build the tree from the logical space of explanatory commitments rather than from a list of names, and phrase every branch node as a concrete case or claim that a reader can answer without already knowing the labels. The point is to sort readers by the explanatory commitments they actually accept, not by which label they prefer. Use cases such as these: a minimally counterintuitive story about a watchful ancestor spirit that spreads through a population; a community that demands costly, time-consuming initiation rites from its members; and the historical emergence of moralizing high gods in large-scale societies. The opening split should ask whether the cross-cultural recurrence of religious beliefs and practices requires a functional explanation at all, or whether ordinary cognitive machinery plus cultural transmission is sufficient. The leaves must include at least: the by-product account; the costly signaling account; the supernatural-punishment (Big Gods) hypothesis; a cultural group-selection account; a Durkheimian ritual-solidarity account; and the fractionationist position that 'religion' is not a natural kind. Aim for six to nine leaves and no more than seven branch questions. For each leaf, add one sentence on the commitment its defenders find hardest to discharge. Then identify any combination of branch answers that no live position occupies, and say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored; in particular, address the combination of a by-product explanation with the claim that 'religion' is nevertheless a natural kind. Finally, flag any leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate — the by-product and fractionationist positions are likely candidates, since many by-product theorists deny that 'religion' is a natural kind — and state what further case or question would separate them.
