I run a monthly film discussion group whose members are thoughtful and engaged but mostly without any formal background in philosophy or linguistics. Next month we are watching Denis Villeneuve's film Arrival, and I have been reading on my own about the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis: the idea that the language a person speaks shapes, and in its stronger forms determines, the way they think and perceive the world. I would like you to write a reading of the film through that lens, aimed at people who know the film well but have not read the academic literature.

The film turns on a linguist, Louise Banks, learning an alien written language that does not lay time out as a sequence; as she learns it, she begins to experience her own life out of chronological order, including events that have not yet happened. Please take both the film and the actual debate seriously. Does the film commit itself to strong linguistic determinism, or only to a weaker claim that a language can make certain thoughts and perceptions easier or more natural? Where does it dramatize something that genuine research into how language encodes time and space might support, and where does it go beyond what any plausible version of the hypothesis says? I especially want help separating two things the film seems to run together: a claim about what a language can do to a speaker's mind, and a claim about time itself, namely that the future is already fixed and becomes visible once the language removes a habit of thinking sequentially. Are those two claims distinct, and which of them does the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis actually license?

I would also like concrete help for the discussion: which scenes should I point the group to, and where should I tell them the film and the hypothesis part ways? Please do not reduce the film to a mere illustration of the hypothesis, and where the film resists a tidy Sapir–Whorf reading, say so rather than forcing it. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about language and thought that an essay on linguistic relativity alone could not.
