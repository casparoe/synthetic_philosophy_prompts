I'm giving a talk at EAG next month defending the claim that individual consumer choices — going vegetarian, avoiding flights, divesting from fossil fuels — are morally significant even when the expected marginal impact of any one person's choice is vanishingly small. The core argument is that expected utility reasoning says a tiny probability of making a large difference (e.g., being the threshold consumer whose demand pushes a firm or market over a tipping point) can yield a non-trivial expected value, and the standard inefficacy objection only works if you're already committed to a purely actual-consequence view of moral responsibility rather than an expected-consequence one. I also have a secondary argument that individual actions have expressive and norm-shaping effects that the objection ignores.

The audience is EAG attendees: maybe 60% are people who already basically accept the expected-utility reply and are mostly there for the norm-shaping stuff, but the other 40% are either new to EA or from adjacent movements (climate activism, animal rights, effective altruism skeptics) who think the whole 'expected utility defense of individual action' is a cope. Among that 40%, there's a subset who are actually pretty sophisticated about it — they've read the back-and-forth blog posts and maybe a paper or two, and they think the expected utility reply is question-begging because it treats the probability distribution over outcomes as given, when the real issue is whether your action is the kind of thing that can be a cause of the outcome at all. Some of them also worry that the threshold argument, if taken seriously, implies you should do all sorts of weird things — like, if you're the millionth person to sign a petition that triggers a policy change, should you get credit for the whole policy? That seems wrong but they can't quite articulate why.
