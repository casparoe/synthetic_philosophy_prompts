I spent most of my career doing error analysis in geophysics, so when I retired and started reading moral philosophy, the stuff on moral uncertainty felt like home turf. The position I've drifted into is this: when I don't know which moral theory is correct, I should treat the theories like hypotheses, give each a credence, score each available action on each theory's account of how choiceworthy it is, rescale those scores to some common currency (normalizing each theory's range or variance, say), and pick the action with the highest credence-weighted expected choiceworthyness. I get that there are rivals — acting on your most probable theory, or giving some theories a veto, or saying moral uncertainty just doesn't require anything — but the expected-choiceworthiness picture keeps looking to me like the only one that take both the uncertainty and the moral content seriously.

What I'd like is a map of what would count against this view. Not the standard one-line objections, but the defeaters a defender of the view would actually have to take seriously: which possible cases, intuitions, or formal results would push against it, and which would a committed proponent concede as real costs rather than dismiss as question-begging. I'm especially worried about two spots — the intertheoretic comparability step, and what happens when a low-credence theory has unbounded values, where I keep seeing the fanaticism worry gestured at but never actually laid out. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the view can be qualified enough to avoid them and, if it can, whether that means it no longer risks anything.
