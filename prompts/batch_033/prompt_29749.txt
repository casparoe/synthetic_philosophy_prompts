I am taking a seminar on Indian Buddhist philosophy, and we have been working through the Madhyamaka treatment of the catuṣkoṭi, the four alternatives according to which a statement might be true, false, both true and false, or neither true nor false. Several of my classmates describe this as a four-valued logic, and some go further, claiming that because Nāgārjuna rejects all four alternatives when discussing the ultimate nature of things, he must be committed to true contradictions or to a rejection of the law of non-contradiction.

I am struggling to see how this can be right, and I would like to understand how the tetralemma is actually supposed to function. If it is a genuine logic, how should the four alternatives be formalized — as truth values, as modal operators, or as something else? And if all four are denied, does Madhyamaka not end up affirming the negation of the entire tetralemma, which looks like a fifth position and, worse, a self-undermining one? More generally, I would like to know whether the catuṣkoṭi should be read as a formal logical system at all, and, if not, what philosophical work its fourfold negation is doing in the Madhyamaka account of emptiness.
