I'm an infra engineer, not a philosopher, so go easy on the jargon. I've been thinking about how my team decides what 'caused' an outage. The drill: something breaks, we roll back the most recent deploy, and if the bug goes away, the postmortem says the deploy caused the outage. Done. We never say the outage was caused by the server being on, or by the network existing, or by the deploy pipeline running, even though all of those were also true and, in some but-for sense, necessary. Meanwhile our experiments team does A/B tests: flip a feature flag, watch the metric, and if it moves they say the flag caused the movement. I get that in practice you need to blame something actionable, but Im curious what picture of causation these practices commit us to. Are we implicitly saying causes are the things you can actually intervene on, so that establishing causation just is flipping a switch and watching what changes? Or is the rollback just a cheap way of checking a counterfactual ('if this deploy hadn't shipped, the bug wouldnt have happened'), in which case the background stuff should count as causes too and our postmortems are being inconsistent? Do the incident practice and the A/B testing practice presuppose the same theory or different ones? And if we actually had to swap the underlying theory, what would the postmortem template have to change?
