So I've been reading Hume's essay on miracles (not for a class, just got curious after a twitter argument lol) and I reconstructed what I think is his argument, and now I'm stuck because it seems to prove way too much. Here's how I'd formalize it:

(1) You should proportion your belief to the evidence.
(2) For any event you hear about through testimony, you should believe it happened only if the testimony is stronger evidence than the background evidence against it.
(3) A miracle is a violation of a law of nature. Laws of nature are established by uniform, exceptionless past experience.
(4) So the evidence against any miracle is massive — it's literally all of past experience.
(5) Testimony is a kind of evidence, but we know from experience that testimony is sometimes mistaken, exaggerated, or fabricated.
(6) Therefore, for any miracle report, the evidence against it (uniform experience) will always swamp the evidence for it (fallible testimony), unless the testimony being false would itself be an even bigger miracle — which it basically never is.
(7) So we should never believe any miracle report on testimony.

Here's my problem. Replace "miracle" with "scientific discovery that overturns a well-established empirical generalization." Like, before 1896, all of past experience said that atoms don't spontaneously emit radiation. When Becquerel reported his photographic plates fogging up, that was testimony for an event that violated what looked like a law of nature. By Hume's reasoning, the evidence against it (centuries of uniform experience) should have swamped Becquerel's testimony. But obviously we shouldn't have dismissed radioactivity — the testimony was good and the phenomena was real. So premise (6) seems to rule out too much: it would block scientific progress whenever progress involves testimony about surprising findings.

I feel like there's gotta be a distinction between "violating a genuine law of nature" and "violating what we currently think is a law of nature," but Hume's argument is about how we, from our limited epistemic position, should respond to testimony. At the time of the report, we can't tell whether the reported event is a genuine miracle or just a discovery that forces us to revise our laws. So the argument still seems to say: don't believe the report. Which is bad.

Where does this go wrong? I keep going back and forth between thinking premise (3) is doing sneaky work (maybe "uniform experience" is too strong — we don't actually have uniform experience of anything, we just haven't noticed counterexamples yet) and thinking the whole thing turns on an equivocation about what "evidence against" means for laws vs. for particular events. But I can't make either diagnosis stick. Help?
