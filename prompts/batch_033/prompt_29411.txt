I teach an undergraduate course on strategic reasoning and rational choice. One of the tutorial exercises asked students to analyze Douglas Hofstadter’s Platonia dilemma and the Scientific American Luring Lottery. I am now marking the submitted answers and would like a second set of eyes on one of them. Below is the exam question, followed by one student’s answer. Please grade the answer out of 10 points, with 3 points for part (a), 3 points for part (b), and 4 points for part (c), and provide the point-by-point comments that justify the grade. I especially want the comments to identify any subtle technical errors, since a hasty grader might otherwise miss them.

Exam question:

Consider the Platonia dilemma described by Hofstadter: 20 people are told that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon, that person will receive a billion dollars; if zero or more than one send, everyone receives nothing. Communication is prohibited. Hofstadter introduces the idea of superrationality: each player should reason as if all other players, being symmetric and equally capable of following the same reasoning, will land on the same strategy.

(a) Explain whether standard noncooperative game theory with expected-utility maximizing players gives a unique prescription for how a player should act in this game.  
(b) State the superrational strategy for the 20-player Platonia dilemma, and compute the probability that exactly one telegram is sent when everyone follows it.  
(c) In the Luring Lottery, contestants may submit a single postcard bearing any positive integer number of entries, and the prize of one million dollars is divided by the total number of entries. State the best-response rule for an individual contestant when the other contestants are expected to submit T total entries. Then explain why the superrational solution to this game cannot be a simple deterministic rule according to which everyone submits the same positive number of entries.

Student answer:

(a) Standard game theory has multiple pure Nash equilibria: any outcome in which exactly one player sends and the rest do not is an equilibrium. There is also a symmetric mixed-strategy equilibrium in which each player sends with probability 1/20. Since the game has many equilibria, game theory gives no unique prescription.  
(b) The superrational strategy is to send with probability 1/20. The probability that exactly one person sends is 20 × 1/20 × (19/20)^19 = (19/20)^19 ≈ 0.377.  
(c) If others submit T entries total and I submit x entries, my expected payoff is (x/(x+T)) × (1,000,000/(x+T)). The derivative with respect to x is zero at x = T, so T is my best response. The analogous superrational strategy is therefore for every contestant to submit exactly one entry: it prevents a runaway escalation and preserves the full prize. Moreover, once everyone submits one, any single contestant who submits more would only dilute the prize and lower their own expected winnings.
