At a dinner party last month I witnessed two friends—one a neurologist, the other teaching philosophy of mind at a nearby university—lock horns over a case that neither would let go of. I have been turning it over ever since, and I would like a careful adjudication.

The case was pain asymbolia: a rare syndrome, usually following damage to the insular cortex, in which patients report that they feel pain—they can locate it, describe its intensity and quality (burning, stabbing, dull)—yet they do not find it unpleasant. They do not withdraw from the painful stimulus, they do not grimace, and they show no motivation to avoid it. When asked, they will say something like "I feel the pain, but it does not bother me."

Here is how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it. I shall call the neurologist Mira and the philosopher Daniel.

(1) Mira: The case is philosophically decisive. Pain asymbolia shows that the badness of pain—the unpleasantness, the "ouch" quality—is not intrinsic to the pain sensation itself. It is a separate component, what some researchers call the affective-motivational dimension, which happens to be yoked to the sensory-discriminative dimension in normal brains but can come apart. The implication is that pain is not essentially bad. Badness is something the brain adds.

(2) Daniel: I do not think the case shows that at all. You are moving too fast from a clinical report to a metaphysical conclusion. When an asymbolia patient says "I feel pain but it does not bother me," we have to ask what they mean by "pain." They may mean they are experiencing sensations of the same sensory type as pain—the same location, intensity, and quality profile—without it actually being pain in the full sense. Compare: if someone said "I am afraid but it does not bother me," we would not conclude fear is not essentially unpleasant; we would wonder whether they are really afraid. Why is pain different?

(3) Mira: That comparison is unfair. Fear is a propositional attitude with a cognitive component—it is about something, it involves an appraisal of danger. Pain is a sensation. The asymbolia patient is not conceptually confused; she is reporting a genuine phenomenological fact: the sensation is present, its felt character is recognisably that of pain, but the motivational oomph is absent. You are stipulating that pain includes unpleasantness by definition and then using that stipulation to dismiss the counterexample. That simply begs the question against the empirical evidence.

(4) Daniel: I am not stipulating; I am pointing out that your interpretation of the evidence already relies on a theoretical choice. You treat the patient's verbal report as transparent access to the structure of experience. But there is another reading: the patient is in a state where the normal integration of sensory and affective processing has broken down, and the resulting experience is simply not pain—it is a kind of pain-sensation analogue that lacks the essential character of pain. The fact that ordinary language lacks a word for this does not make it pain. We would not say someone with blindsight sees, even if they can guess what is in their blind field; we say they have a degraded visual capacity that is not conscious seeing. Similarly, the asymbolia patient may have a degraded nociceptive capacity that is not pain.

(5) Mira: But that is precisely the point—the blindsight comparison actually helps my case. In blindsight, the subject denies seeing anything. In pain asymbolia, the subject affirms feeling pain. She discriminates it from other sensations, she rates its intensity, she identifies its quality. The only thing missing is the "this is bad, make it stop" component. So if we take the subject's report seriously—as we should, absent some reason to think she is confabulating or confused—we have evidence of a dissociation between the sensory and the affective. And if they can dissociate, pain is not essentially bad.

(6) Daniel: Here is the deeper problem. Even if I grant the dissociation—even if I grant that the sensory and affective components are separable in the brain—you still have not shown that the sensory component, taken on its own, is pain. You have shown that there is a sensory core common to both normal pain and asymbolic pain, and that the affective component is what makes normal pain aversive. But perhaps that sensory core is not pain; perhaps pain just is the whole package—sensory core plus affective reaction—and what the asymbolia patient has is merely the sensory core. You are assuming the sensory core is the essence of pain and the badness is an add-on. I could equally say the badness is the essence and the sensory core is the vehicle.

At this point the dinner party broke up and they never resolved it. I am a novelist, not a philosopher, but this matters to me because I am working on a character with something like this condition, and I need to know what the honest philosophical landscape looks like.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether the argument is sound, partly sound but missing something, or misguided, and explain why. Then give an overall verdict: who has the stronger case, and what, if anything, are both parties missing? Please approach this as a philosopher of mind with expertise on consciousness and pain. Think carefully and resist the temptation to split every difference equally. And please say explicitly what argument or empirical finding would most likely change your mind about the central issue—where your reasoning is most vulnerable to revision.

Please write your answer in British English.
