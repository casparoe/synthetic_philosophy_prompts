I am drafting a novel whose protagonist is a philosopher of mind best known for denying that the brain is, in any sense relevant to thought, a digital computer. In an early chapter she leads a graduate seminar through a series of cases and states her verdicts flatly; I have written those verdicts as follows.

A fluent human speaker of Chinese understands Chinese. A digital computer that passes the Turing test for Chinese by running a formal symbol-manipulation program — the Chinese Room arrangement — does not understand Chinese. A thermostat does not think, notwithstanding the fact that its states track the temperature of the room. A molecule-by-molecule computer simulation of a human brain does not think, however exact; it models a brain without being one. A chess-playing computer does not think about chess.

In a later chapter she must judge a case I have not yet drafted. A patient's neurons are replaced one by one with silicon chips that reproduce each neuron's input-output behavior so faithfully that the patient's speech, conduct, and reports of experience are unchanged throughout and after the procedure. The procedure is plainly a replacement, not a simulation. Afterward the patient speaks and understands Chinese exactly as before.

I care less about which answer is correct than about which answer this character would give if she is to remain consistent with her earlier verdicts. Please work through it as follows: identify the candidate rules that fit her recorded verdicts; say what each rule predicts about the replacement case; give your best prediction of her verdict and the reasons for preferring that reading of her; and then specify a further case that would best discriminate among whichever rules the replacement case leaves open. If her prior verdicts genuinely underdetermine her answer, say what she would be committing herself to with either choice.
