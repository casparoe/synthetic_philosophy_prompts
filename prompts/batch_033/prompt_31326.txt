I am a software engineer and team lead at a medium-sized company, with no formal background in philosophy. My team owns the internal developer platform, and the same unresolved argument keeps recurring. When a developer's personal notes system, a code-search index, or an AI pair-programming assistant becomes unavailable or is withdrawn, some of us describe the loss as a minor inconvenience, whilst others describe it as if the developer has lost part of their memory or competence. We have no shared way of deciding which description is right, yet the answer changes how we set incident severity, what we promise in service-level agreements, what we treat as mandatory in onboarding, and how we handle access revocation when someone leaves. We face this kind of decision several times a quarter, usually under time pressure, and the outcome has to be defensible in writing to an engineering manager who signs off on the classification.

I have come across the extended mind thesis in popular writing, and it seems directly relevant. As I understand it, the thesis says that a cognitive process need not be confined to the skull: if something outside the body performs a function that we would unhesitatingly call remembering or thinking if it were performed in the head, and if it is coupled to the person in the right way, then it can count as part of that person's cognitive system. The right kind of coupling, on my understanding, involves the resource being reliably available and typically invoked, easily accessible when needed, trusted without being checked each time, and containing information the person deliberately put there or accepted at some earlier point.

Please write a reusable decision procedure that a platform engineer or team lead with no philosophy training can run in about twenty minutes, to classify a given developer–tool dependency into one of three categories: (1) extended cognition, where the resource should be treated as part of the person's memory or thinking for policy purposes; (2) ordinary tool, where the resource is a convenience whose loss is a productivity issue rather than a cognitive one; or (3) escalate, where the procedure cannot settle the matter and a named person must make a judgement call.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions or tests in a fixed order. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a classification, and where it must escalate. It should keep what the thesis actually says rather than collapsing into a simple frequency test. In particular, it should treat the inside/outside-the-skull distinction as irrelevant; it should distinguish reliable availability from actual trust, so that a resource used constantly but verified each time is not memory-like, and a resource trusted but frequently unavailable cannot function as memory; and it should treat information the person never endorsed differently from information they put there themselves. It should also state explicitly which distinctions you have simplified or dropped, where the procedure is likely to misfire, and what the procedure cannot do.

Then run the procedure on these three cases: (a) a developer's personal notes system, maintained daily for years, consulted many times a day, and accepted without checking; (b) an internal code-search index used a few times a week, whose results are always verified against the repository before being used; and (c) an AI pair-programming assistant whose suggestions the developer accepts almost without review, including suggestions that draw on the model's training rather than on anything the developer entered or endorsed. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer we will not like, and state plainly which questions the procedure cannot settle on its own.
