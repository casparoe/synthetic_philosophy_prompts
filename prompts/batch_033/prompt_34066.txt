Most of the AI systems that have impressed people over the last decade were produced by a single recipe: start with a network whose parameters are set more or less at random, then repeatedly adjust those parameters by gradient descent so that the network becomes better at predicting the next word in an enormous body of text, or at some similar self-supervised task; afterwards, the model is often fine-tuned on human feedback, but that stage is usually treated as alignment, not education. Turing proposed something quite different: rather than training an adult system on a mountain of data, build something like a child's mind — in his discussion, an unorganized machine, a network of simple elements with mostly random connections — and then educate it, giving it problems, correcting its errors, conversing with it, the way one would teach a child. The training-from-data recipe has plainly won in practice, and I have been trying to work out what that victory quietly commits the field to. Does the dominance of prediction over instruction presuppose a particular view of what learning is — that learning is fundamentally statistical pattern-fitting, and that understanding, if it appears, is a byproduct of prediction? Or is the choice of method merely an engineering convenience, compatible with several different philosophies of mind? Does the later human-feedback stage smuggle the teacher back in, and does that make the practice's presuppositions inconsistent? And if Turing's educational picture were taken seriously, what would actually have to change in how systems are built, trained, and evaluated? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
