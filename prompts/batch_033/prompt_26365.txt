I recently finished Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I have been turning over its central analogy ever since. I work in machine learning, and a colleague pressed the book on me with the claim that it contained a deep insight about how minds might arise from sufficiently tangled symbol systems — an insight I ought to take seriously given the systems I spend my days building.

The analogy, as I understand it, runs as follows. Gödel showed that a formal system rich enough to encode arithmetic can, via Gödel numbering, construct sentences that refer to themselves — the famous "I am not provable" sentence that is true but unprovable within the system. Hofstadter takes this as the prototype of a "strange loop": a structure that crosses levels in a hierarchy, where the top folds back onto the bottom. He then argues that strange loops of sufficient complexity, realized in neural — or potentially computational — substrates, simply are what consciousness and self-awareness consist in. The mathematical case serves, in his hands, as something between a proof of concept and a template: if self-reference can emerge in a system as austere as Principia Mathematica, then surely it can emerge in a brain — and when it does, it yields a self.

I find the picture compelling, but I worry I am being moved by an analogy rather than by an argument. I would like you to audit it. Specifically: (1) lay out the correspondence table — what in the formal-system case is supposed to map to what in the mind case; (2) identify where the mapping strains or breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic; and (3) tell me whether the claim that consciousness is a strange loop can carry its own weight once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work all along. If there is a better analogy — one that captures what Hofstadter is reaching for without importing the same distortions — I would like to hear it.
