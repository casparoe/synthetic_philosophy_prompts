I came across the following passage, and something about it struck me as possibly too tidy:

> The Frege–Geach problem is widely regarded as the central objection to expressivism, and many philosophers take it to be decisive. Expressivists hold that a moral sentence like 'Lying is wrong' does not describe a moral fact but instead expresses a non-cognitive attitude — roughly, disapproval of lying. The problem, first noted by Peter Geach and drawing on Frege's work in the philosophy of logic, is that moral sentences routinely appear in what are called unasserted or embedded contexts. Take the conditional 'If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.' A speaker who asserts this conditional is plainly not expressing disapproval of lying — she is merely entertaining it hypothetically. Yet expressivism, the objection runs, can only tell us what a moral sentence does when it is asserted outright; it offers no account of what the very same words contribute to the meaning of a larger sentence when they appear in an embedded position. Because expressivists cannot explain the compositional semantics of moral language — how the meaning of a complex moral sentence depends on the meanings of its parts — they cannot explain how moral arguments can be logically valid, since validity requires that terms keep the same meaning whether they appear asserted or unasserted. And since any minimally adequate semantic theory must be compositional, expressivism simply collapses.
