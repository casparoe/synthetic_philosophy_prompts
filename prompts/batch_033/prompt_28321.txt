My dad has late-stage alzheimers and signed an advance directive years ago saying he didn't want a feeding tube at this stage. Now he's here: he can't eat enough on his own, he picks at his food and seems to want it, and the care team says the only way to keep weight on him is a tube. His directive says no. I've been reading around the precedent autonomy stuff and there's an argument I keep building that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the exit.

(1) The whole point of an advance directive is that the person's earlier competent self has authority over what happens to the later incompetent self.
(2) If an earlier self's autonomous decision can bind a later self in the dementia case, then earlier autonomous decisions bind later selves generally.
(3) But that's not how we treat ordinary life: a 25-year-old who autonomously decides she'll never marry or never have kids is not bound at 45, and we'd say the 45-year-old gets to decide, not the 25-year-old.
(4) So the authority can't come from the earlier self's autonomy alone. It has to come from something about the later self: either he's not the same person anymore, or he's the same person but no longer competent.
(5) If he's not the same person, then the directive is one person making medical decisions for a different person, which we'd never allow just because someone wrote a document while they were healthy.
(6) If he's the same person but incompetent, then why are his current wishes weightless? We try to respect the preferences of incompetent people all the time, and my dad seems to want the food when it's in front of him.
(7) So either the directive doesn't really bind when the later self objects, or any earlier self gets to own any later self, and the 25-year-old's decision should bind the 45-year-old too.

I'd rather keep the directive binding then give up on my dad's earlier wishes, but I don't see how to stop the slide to (7). Which step is the bad one, and what do I have to give up to reject it?
