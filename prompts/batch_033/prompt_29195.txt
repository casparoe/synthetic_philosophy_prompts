I've been going through some decision problems a friend sent me and I'm trying to figure out what principle, if any, my choices add up to. Here's what I'd pick, just going on gut:

Situation 1:
Option A: $1 million, guaranteed.
Option B: 89% chance of $1 million, 10% chance of $5 million, 1% chance of $0.
I pick A. The thought of walking away with nothing when I could have had a million is unbearable, even if it's only a 1% risk.

Situation 2:
Option C: 11% chance of $1 million, 89% chance of $0.
Option D: 10% chance of $5 million, 90% chance of $0.
I pick D. The odds of winning are so close that the bigger prize swamps the tiny difference in probability.

Situation 3:
Option E: 50% chance of $200, 50% chance of $0.
Option F: $100 for sure.
I pick F. I'll take the sure thing.

Situation 4:
Option G: 0.001% chance of $10 million.
Option H: 0.002% chance of $5 million.
I pick G. Both are basically lottery tickets, so I go for the bigger prize.

Situation 5:
Option I: 50% chance of $200, 50% chance of $0.
Option J: $100 for sure.
I pick J, same as in Situation 3. But now my friend says: what if each option only happens if a coin lands heads (50%), and otherwise you get nothing? So:
Option I': 25% chance of $200, 75% chance of $0.
Option J': 25% chance of $100, 75% chance of $0.
And weirdly, I want to switch to I'. The certainty of the $100 was what made J attractive, and now that its gone, the extra upside of I' pulls me in.

Situation 6:
Option K: 80% chance of $400, 20% chance of $0.
Option L: $300 for sure.
I pick L.

Situation 7:
Option M: 20% chance of $400, 80% chance of $0.
Option N: 25% chance of $300, 75% chance of $0.
I pick M. Even though the ratio of probabilities is the same as in Situation 6 (4:1), I flip. When the probabilities get low, I chase the higher amount.

So: is there a coherent rule that gets all seven of these? I've heard that "expected utility theory" says my choices in 1 and 2 contradict eachother, and that 5 and the pair 6/7 are also problems for it. But I don't know the alternatives. Is there some other theory of rational choice that fits all of them? If not, which of my verdicts is the hardest to defend, and what does the best available principle say about a case I didn't list: a choice between a 0.1% chance of $1 billion and a 0.2% chance of $500 million?
