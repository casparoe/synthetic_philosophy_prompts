A friend of mine, Lena, and her brother-in-law Marcus went at each other over dessert a few weeks ago, and I keep replaying it. Neither of them has studied philosophy; they got there through a lottery ticket and a book preface, and I couldn't tell who was winning.

Lena began with a fair lottery: a million tickets, one winner. The night before the draw, she said, it's perfectly reasonable to say of your own ticket, "it won't win." But the same reasoning applies to every ticket, one by one, so you'd be led to say that each of the million tickets won't win while also knowing that one of them will. That looks like a contradiction. Her conclusion was that the reasonable thing must be weaker: you shouldn't say you believe your ticket will lose, only that it's overwhelmingly likely to lose.

Marcus said that can't be the whole story, because the same structure appears in a case where almost no one wants to give up the beliefs. Take an author who has just finished a careful nonfiction book. She has checked every sentence and believes each one is true, yet she writes in the preface that she is sure the book contains at least one error. That has the same shape: a long list of individual beliefs, plus the belief that at least one of them is false. The author seems perfectly reasonable. So if we're fine with her, maybe the lottery holder is fine too.

Lena replied that the two cases aren't really the same. The author has a separate reason for each sentence — a source she checked, a fact she verified. The lottery holder has no reason that singles out his own ticket; his only reason is the blanket fact that almost all tickets lose. A reason that applies identically to every ticket, she said, isn't a reason to believe anything about this ticket in particular.

Marcus answered that the line between specific and blanket reasons is doing too much work too quietly. An author writing a long book might, after each sentence, have only the blanket thought that she is careful and most of her sentences are fine. Would she then be irrational to believe each sentence? Or take a lottery in which each ticket does have a little specific history — say each one was stamped by a machine known to misfire occasionally, and you have inspected yours. Would it suddenly become acceptable to believe of each ticket that it loses? If the answer flips that easily, he said, the distinction Lena drew can't be carrying the real weight.

Lena said maybe the lesson is that "belief" is the wrong unit entirely. We should think in terms of degrees of confidence. You can be 99.9999 percent confident your ticket will lose, and being that confident about each ticket doesn't force you to be confident that all of them lose. The contradiction only appears because we talk as if belief were an on-off switch.

Marcus said that move has its own cost. The author in the preface doesn't merely have a high degree of confidence that her book contains an error; she'll tell you outright that she believes it, and that belief is one of the most honest things she says about her own work. If we stop talking about belief and talk only about degrees of confidence, we lose the ordinary notion of believing something — the notion we rely on when we say someone believes their spouse is faithful, or believes the bridge will hold. He said that's too high a price to pay just to dissolve a lottery puzzle.

They went back and forth like this for a while, and neither would concede.

Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move — who is right about which specific point, and what are both of them missing? Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
