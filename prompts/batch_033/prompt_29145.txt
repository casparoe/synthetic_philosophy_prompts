Last weekend I was at a bar with two friends from my old econ study group, and they got into it over the dollar auction. You know the setup: someone auctions off a dollar bill, highest bidder wins and pays their bid, but the second-highest bidder also has to pay their bid and gets nothing. Bidding starts at a nickel. The famous result is that rational players can get sucked into bidding way past a dollar, each trying to avoid losing their sunk bid.

What started the fight was that Liam said the dollar auction is basically a parlor trick — it's cute but it doesn't actually tell you anything deep about real-world escalation, because in real life people aren't that stupid. Maya pushed back hard and said it's exactly the opposite: the dollar auction reveals a structural feature of escalation that operates even when everyone is perfectly rational, which makes it *more* disturbing, not less.

Here's roughly how it went:

Liam: "The whole thing depends on the two bidders being idiots who can't see two steps ahead. In any real situation, someone would just stop. The fact that the model says they rationally *shouldn't* stop just means the model is broken."

Maya: "That's the whole point though — each individual step *is* rational. If you've already bid 95 cents and I bid a dollar, I'm facing a certain loss of 95 cents if I quit versus a chance to break even if I bid $1.05. The rationality at each step is airtight."

Liam: "Sure, if you pretend the previous bids are recoverable. But they're gone. The question at every point is just: do I want to throw *more* money into this hole? And the answer is no. Calling that 'rational' because you're comparing it to a loss you've already taken is exactly the sunk cost fallacy."

Maya: "It's not the sunk cost fallacy. The sunk cost fallacy is when past costs shouldn't affect future decisions *because they don't change future outcomes*. But here, quitting *does* change the outcome — it turns your bid into a pure loss. Bidding again gives you a chance to win the dollar and offset it. The past cost is directly relevant to the payoff structure."

Liam: "Only if you assume the other guy will definitely keep going. If I credibly signal I'm done after this round, the game ends."

Maya: "But credible signaling is exactly what the game shows you *can't* do, because once you're in, the other player knows your incentive is to keep bidding. Your 'I'm done after this' claim isn't credible because they know you'll face the same logic again next round."

Liam: "Which just proves my point — the model assumes away everything that lets real people escape traps. Reputation, precommitment, just being willing to eat a loss and walk away. If your model of rationality says walking away is irrational, your model is wrong."

Maya: "I don't think you're engaging with the actual claim. The claim isn't 'real people always escalate.' The claim is that escalation isn't just a psychological failure — it's built into certain payoff structures. That means you can't fix it just by telling people to be more rational. You need to change the structure."

At this point they noticed I'd been quiet and asked what I thought. I said I needed to think about it and changed the subject, but I've been turning it over since.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I'm especially interested in whether Maya is correct that the dollar auction shows escalation is structural rather than psychological, or whether Liam is right that the model's assumptions are so stripped-down that it can't support that kind of conclusion. And if they're both partly right, where exactly does each argument start to go wrong?
