In a conversation about how to live with a serious illness, a friend keeps reaching for some version of the modern Stoic slogan — "you can't control what happens to you, only how you respond to it" — and uses it to close off any further discussion of trying to change the external circumstances. I find the sentence slippery enough that I can't tell what I would be conceding if I accepted it or what I would have to deny if I rejected it. Treat that slogan as the object of analysis and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making. In particular, vary the readings along at least these dimensions: (a) what counts as "how you respond" — occurrent judgments, emotions, settled dispositions, outward actions, the direction of attention; (b) what kind of claim the "can't" is — a claim about human psychology, about the causal structure of the world, or about what it is worth trying to change; and (c) whether the slogan is meant descriptively or as a normative injunction. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and say how it relates to the classical Stoic distinction between what is up to us and what is not. Then identify which reading or readings would actually license my friend's use of the slogan to rule out trying to change the circumstances, and which readings would make that move illegitimate.
