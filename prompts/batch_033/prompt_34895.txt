I'm a philosophy professor prepping a unit on medieval Islamic philosophy and I've got a slightly weird idea I want to run by someone who knows their stuff. I want you to act as an expert on both Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism and on philosophy of film — take your time with this, maybe start by laying out what you take the core commitments of Al-Ghazālī's view to be, and then work through the film scene by scene.

Here's the thing. Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism about causation is famously hard to get students to take seriously. You know the view: God is the sole cause of every event, there's no necessary connection between what we call "cause" and "effect," fire doesn't burn cotton because of some power in the fire — God creates the burning at the moment of contact. The world is recreated moment by moment, and what looks like a causal chain is just God's habit. It's a radically different picture of causation than the Humean one students usually encounter, and they tend to dismiss it as medieval obscurantism before they've really understood it.

I rewatched Denis Villeneuve's *Arrival* (2016) last week and it hit me: the film's treatment of time and causation has a lot in common with the occasionalist picture — or at least it feels that way. Louise acquires the heptapod language and starts experiencing time non-linearly. She knows the future, including her daughter's death, and that knowledge shapes her present choices. The arrow of causation gets weird: future events seem to cause present ones, and the distinction between cause and effect starts to dissolve. The heptapods themselves treat time as something where the whole shape is visible at once, not a sequence of cause and effect.

What I want from you is a careful reading of *Arrival* through the lens of Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism. Specifically:

- Where does the film genuinely illuminate the occasionalist picture? Are there scenes that dramatize something like the moment-by-moment recreation of the world, or the idea that there's no necessary connection between one event and the next?

- Where does the film *resist* the reading? Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is theistic — God's will is the only genuine cause, and the "habits" of nature are sustained by God's continuous choice. The heptapods aren't God, and the film's non-linear time seems more like a metaphysics of eternalism (block universe) than a theistic occasionalism. Where does the analogy break, and what does that break tell us about what occasionalism is actually committed to?

- And here's what I'm really curious about: does the *emotional* register of the film add something to the philosophical debate that Al-Ghazālī's texts alone don't? The film makes you *feel* the non-linear causal structure through Louise's grief — she chooses to have her daughter knowing she'll die young. Al-Ghazālī's texts are rigorous but they don't exactly make you cry. Does that emotional experience put pressure on occasionalism in a way abstract argument doesn't, or does it mislead students about what the view is actually saying?

I want to use this comparision in class to get students past the "that's just crazy" reaction, so I need to know where the analogy works and where it's going to confuse them. Be honest about where the film and the philosophy don't fit together — I don't want a forced reading, I want a genuine critical engagement with both.
