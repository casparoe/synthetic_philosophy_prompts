I spent most of Sunday evening arguing with a friend about what it takes for consent to be invalid. She holds the simple view that any deliberate lie told in the course of obtaining consent automatically vitiates it, and that any real pressure does the same; I kept insisting that the standard factors — deception, coercion, and incompetence — each have internal structure, and that a lie or a push only vitiates consent when it actually bears on the decision in the right way. We stalled because neither of us could produce a clean case in which a genuine lie and genuine pressure are both present and yet the consent remains valid. I would like you to construct that case for me; I plan to bring it back to her and, later, to a reading group on the ethics of consent.

Build a realistic one-paragraph scenario — a workplace, a hospital, or a financial setting, not a trolley problem and not a sexual encounter — in which one party, A, obtains valid consent from another party, B, even though A tells B a real lie that B believes (not a joke B sees through, not a mere omission), and B consents while under real pressure coming from A that B genuinely feels. The case must not cheat: the consent has to come out valid through the structure of the situation, not by stipulation, so you will need to show that B is competent, that the pressure falls short of coercion, and that the lie is not about anything material to B's decision. A case that simply announces that the consent is valid anyway would fail; the lie and the pressure have to be doing whatever work they do, and the validity has to fall out of the standard tests for the three factors.

After the scenario, walk through each factor in turn. State the lie and explain what makes a fact material to a consent decision, and why this fact is not material for B; state the pressure and explain why it is an offer or a situational constraint rather than a threat, and why B retains an acceptable alternative; and state why B's competence is intact. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the most work — I suspect it is that the two vitiating factors are rationally inert with respect to B's actual reasons for consenting — and say what the verdict would become if that feature were removed. Finish with how a defender of my friend's simple view would attack the case, and how you would answer that attack. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: state the verdict and the feature that secures it in one line. Then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
