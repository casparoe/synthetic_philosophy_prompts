Act as a moral philosopher working on aggregation, population ethics, and decision theory. I recently had a long argument with a friend about the torture-versus-dust-specks problem, and I came away defending the view that a single severe torture is worse than any number of dust specks — where a dust speck is a brief, real but barely noticeable discomfort, and each speck falls on a different person. I want you to stress-test this view, not defend it. Begin by brainstorming the strongest possible considerations against it, then organize them into a ranked map of what would count against my position: which arguments, formal results, and possible cases should move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Distinguish carefully between considerations that would show the view is false and considerations that would only reveal its costs or force me to qualify it. Also say which of these a committed defender of my view would themselves concede as counting against it, and which they would dismiss as question-begging or as changing the subject. Finish with an honest assessment of whether the view is insulated — whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Reason from cases and principles only; do not reach for empirical studies or citations.
