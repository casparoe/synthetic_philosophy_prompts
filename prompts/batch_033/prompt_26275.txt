I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether Dennett's view of the self as a center of narrative gravity makes grief incoherent. My friend thinks it does: if a person is an abstraction rather than a persisting thing, then mourning them is like mourning the plot of a novel you've finished reading — it mistakes the story for something that was there all along. I pushed back, but I'm not sure I won the argument, and now I have to give the eulogy.

The person who died — my older sister — was the one who introduced me to that view in the first place. She found it liberating, not deflating: she used to say that being a center of narrative gravity meant you could revise the story without betraying some fixed essence. She was forty-seven. Pancreatic cancer. She asked me to speak at her memorial, and she was explicit that she didn't want anyone pretending she was watching from somewhere. But she also didn't want a philosophy lecture. The room will be mostly family and her colleagues from the architecture firm — perhaps a hundred people, many of whom loved her fiercely and believe in an afterlife she rejected.

Please write the eulogy. It should do the following: honor her as the specific person she was, give the mourners something genuine to hold onto, and stay faithful to the view of selfhood she actually held — without using jargon, without naming any philosopher, and without turning into a defensive justification of her beliefs. The speech should run about five minutes when read aloud.

After the speech, include a section called "Commentary" of roughly 300 words. It should address where the eulogy takes interpretive liberties with the underlying philosophical view, which aspects of that view it leans on that are genuinely contested among philosophers of mind, and which parts are broadly settled among people who know the topic. I want to know where I am on firm ground and where I am not — please do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
