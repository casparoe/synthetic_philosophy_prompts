I keep running into this move in utilitarianism-adjacent arguments where someone treats pleasure and pain like entries in a single accounting ledger: pains are debits, pleasures are credits, and the only thing that matters is the net balance. The picture does real work, because it licenses the conclusion that any amount of suffering, however intense or unevenly distributed, can in principle be made up for by enough pleasure somewhere, maybe in other people or other possible lives. A friend whose into negative utilitarianism says the ledger is exactly where the reasoning goes wrong: he thinks beyond some threshold of agony, pain stops being tradeable against pleasure at any exchange rate, so the two aren't fully commensurable. He calls it lexical threshold, the idea being that avoiding extreme suffering outranks any amount of pleasure as a separate tier, the way a word starting with A comes before every word starting with B no matter how long the B-word is.

I'd like you to audit the ledger analogy the way you'd audit an argument. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of credits, debits, the balance, the currency, and the accountant? Then walk through where the mapping breaks. I can already see some candidate disanalogies: in accounting the entries belong to a single owner, debits and credits are fungible, and a positive net balance seems like an unambiguously good state of the ledger. Which of those features is the analogy quietly importing into ethics, and which of them actually matter for the intended conclusion that enough pleasure can compensate for intense suffering? Finally, tell me whether the compensation conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and wether there is a better analogy for how moderate and extreme suffering relate to pleasure.
