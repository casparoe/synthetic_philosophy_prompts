I am giving the eulogy at my mother's funeral. She was a professor of Chinese and comparative philosophy whose central intellectual commitment was to Confucian role ethics — the view, developed from the Analects and defended in contemporary form by Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont Jr., that persons are not autonomous individuals who choose their relationships but are constituted by the roles they inhabit (daughter, mother, teacher, friend, and so on), and that the ethical life consists in cultivating and virtuously performing those roles rather than in following abstract rules or calculating consequences.

She lived this philosophy. She was not warm in the way American culture expects of a mother, but she was unfailingly dutiful, and she understood love not as a feeling but as a practice of sustained, disciplined attention to the people whose lives were entangled with hers. She was also a demanding teacher, a fierce intellectual controversialist, and, in her later years, someone who struggled with the loss of the roles that had defined her — first when she retired, and then when illness made her dependent on others in ways she found humiliating.

The audience will include her colleagues and former students, who will catch every philosophical reference; my siblings, who have complicated feelings about being raised by a woman who treated parenting as a role to be inhabited correctly rather than as an outlet for spontaneous affection; her grandchildren, who knew her mainly as a frail and sometimes difficult old woman; and several of her intellectual adversaries, whom she debated publicly and sometimes harshly.

I need a eulogy — about ten minutes when spoken — that honors her thought without turning the funeral into a lecture, that is honest about who she was (she would have regarded false sentimentality as a failure of respect), and that lets her Confucian commitments do real structural work in how the speech handles grief, memory, and gratitude. In particular, the eulogy must find a way to speak about loss that does not treat her death as the annihilation of an autonomous self — because she did not think of herself, or anyone, in those terms — and yet does not offer the cheap comfort that she "lives on in our memories," which she would have dismissed as a platitude. The speech should show, rather than argue, what it means to mourn someone who understood personhood as irreducibly relational.
