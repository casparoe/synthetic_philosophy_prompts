I'm taking a seminar on intergenerational justice and we've been fighting about discounting for two weeks, and a classmate gave me an argument that seems to prove too much and I can't find the exit. It goes:

(1) Pure time preference — discounting a benefit just because it arrives later — is no more rational than discounting a benefit just because it arrives to someone far away. The date on the calender isn't a morally relevant fact.
(2) So when we evaluate policies, we should set the pure-time-preference term in the discount rate to zero.
(3) Setting that term to zero means future wellbeing enters our evaluation with the same weight as present wellbeing.
(4) Therefore the social discount rate should be zero, or near enough.
(5) With a zero discount rate, even a tiny perpetual benefit to the far future has enormous present value, so almost any present cost is worth paying to secure it — climate, asteroids, pandemics, you name it. We'd be required to near-impoverish ourselves for people we'll never meet.
(6) That can't be right.
(7) So (1) must be false: some pure time preference is rationally mandatory.

I want to keep (1), and my instinct is that the bad step is somewhere around (3) or (4), but I can't articulate why. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If (1) survives, what should someone who rejects pure time preference actually say about the social discount rate?
