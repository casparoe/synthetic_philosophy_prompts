I work as a software engineer at a mid-size tech company. A few months ago, a discussion in our company Slack about IQ differences and genetics got heated and unproductive — people were using "heritability" to mean about six different things, and the thread ended with everyone annoyed and nobody clearer. Our CTO asked me to give a thirty-minute lunch-and-learn talk called "What Heritability Actually Means," since I'd been reading about behavioral genetics in my spare time and he figured I'd at least know the definition. The audience will be engineers, designers, and product people: smart but no biology or statistics beyond what they half-remember from college, and some of them arrive with strong views they're not entirely aware they hold.

I know the material reasonably well. I understand that heritability is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population attributable to genetic variance in that population, that it says nothing about any individual, that an estimate is specific to a particular environment at a particular time, and that high heritability does not imply a trait is immutable or that environmental interventions would be ineffective. But knowing these facts is not the same as knowing how to sequence them so that people actually absorb them rather than nodding along and then reverting to their old usage the next day.

I need a teaching plan. What has to come first so that everything else makes sense? Which single example should carry the whole talk — I was thinking height, but I'm open to being talked out of it, and if you do recommend height I want to know what about it makes it the right vehicle and what about it I need to be careful about. What misconceptions should I expect the audience to form, and at exactly which points in the sequence should I head each one off? (I'm already worried about the "heritable = genetically determined" slide and the "within-group heritability tells you about between-group differences" move, but I'm sure there are others I haven't anticipated.) What should I explicitly refuse to get into at this level and say so upfront? And what's one question I can pose near the end — not a trivia question, but something that would reveal whether someone has actually understood the concept rather than just learned to repeat the definition? Please write your answer in American English.
