Read Stanley Kubrick's film *Dr. Strangelove* — in particular, the Soviet Doomsday Machine — through the lens of Derek Parfit's hitchhiker case and the broader philosophical debate about rational irrationality (the idea that it can sometimes be rational to make oneself unable to act on one's future rational self-interest).

Parfit's hitchhiker, stranded in a desert and dying of thirst, must persuade a passing motorist that he will pay a promised reward once he reaches safety, even though at that later moment he will have no self-interested reason to pay. A purely rational hitchhiker would renege, and the motorist knows this, so the motorist will not stop. The hitchhiker would therefore benefit from being the sort of person who keeps promises even when it is costly — from being, in a certain sense, irrational. The Doomsday Machine in *Dr. Strangelove* is a state-level analogue: a device that makes nuclear retaliation automatic and irrevocable, removing the human capacity to choose not to retaliate, precisely in order to make the threat credible. And yet the film is a satire, and the machine contributes to the destruction of the world.

Please provide a reading of the film — especially the Doomsday Machine and the scenes in which it is discussed — through this philosophical lens. Address the following, but do not feel bound by them as a checklist:

- Where does the film dramatize, with fidelity, the logic of Parfit's hitchhiker: that a threat or promise is credible only if the agent is unable to renege, and that it can therefore be rational to constrain one's future choices?
- Where does the film complicate or satirize that logic? In particular, what does the film show about the difference between an individual hitchhiker's self-binding and a nuclear state's self-binding — differences of scale, secrecy, accident, and the autonomy of subordinates?
- Does the film ultimately vindicate or condemn the rationality of such commitment devices? Is the Doomsday Machine's failure a contingent one (it was kept secret; it was triggered by a rogue general) or a necessary one (any such device invites catastrophe)?
- What would Parfit's analysis say about the positions taken by Dr. Strangelove and General Turgidson in the War Room? Does Strangelove's enthusiasm for automated deterrence embody a coherent version of rational irrationality, or does the film expose a flaw in the concept itself?
- Are there respects in which the film adds something to the philosophical debate that the standard cases and arguments do not capture — something about the phenomenology of living under such devices, or about the relationship between rationality and madness?

Conclude with an assessment of whether the analogy between the hitchhiker and the Doomsday Machine holds, and what the film teaches us that the philosophical literature, taken alone, might miss. Please write the answer in American English.
