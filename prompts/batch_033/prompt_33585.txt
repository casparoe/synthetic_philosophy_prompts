Just got back from a three-hour argument with a friend about whether me going vegetarian actually matters, and I feel like we just ran laps around the same track. You know the shape of it: one less burger doesn't shrink the number of cows the industry raises, so maybe my choice is literally doing nothing. He comes back with the expected-utility line — there's some tiny probability I'm the threshold buyer and my expected harm reduction is real. I say that feels like a math trick to make a zero-impact action look nonzero in the spreadsheet while in the actual world my lunch changed nothing. Neither of us budged.

And this isn't just us — this debate has been stuck since at least Glover and Parfit in the 70s, through Kagan and Nefsky and all the rest. Everyone knows all the moves: expected utility of crossing a threshold, what-if-everyone-did-that, complicity in a harmful practice, the idea that morality isn't just about causal consequences but about the kind of agent you are. And yet nobody seems to actually change their mind. Including me, including him, three hours later.

What I want isn't a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of the stall. Here are my guesses, and I want you to tell me which one is actually doing the work or whether it's some mix:

(a) It's a disguised empirical disagreement — if we actually knew how responsive supply chains are to marginal demand changes (for meat, for emissions, for voting), one side would have to give. The problem is just that the data is really hard to get.

(b) It's a conceptual fight about what "making a difference" even means. My friend means expected causal impact. I mean something closer to actual causal necessity — like, in the world as it actually unfolds, was my action in the story of why things turned out one way rather than another? Those two notions just come apart in large-scale cases and we don't have a shared concept to argue about.

(c) It's a deeper normative clash between act-level and practice-level evaluation. Some people think moral assessment attaches to individual acts and their consequences; others think it attaches to participation in a practice. And those frameworks just don't have a common currency — it's like one side is asking "was this act good?" and the other is asking "is this the kind of thing a good person does?"

(d) It's not a disagreement of the sort that argument can settle — it's a temperament thing, or bedrock intutions about whether morality is fundamentally about changing the world or about being a certain kind of person, and no chain of reasoning is going to reach across that gap.

So: which is it? And given your diagnosis, what would actually unstick this? A better empirical model of supply chain responsiveness? A conceptual distinction we haven't drawn clearly enough? A formal result from decision theory that forces both sides to accept the other's framing? Or is the honest answer that this is one of those debates where the starting points are far enough apart that no amount of argument will bridge them — and if so, what do you do with a disagreement like that instead of just having the same fight forever?
