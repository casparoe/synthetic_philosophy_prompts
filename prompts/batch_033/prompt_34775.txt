I have come to accept a Rawlsian view of religion in public life: coercive political power is legitimate only if it can be justified to citizens by public reasons that people with different reasonable comprehensive doctrines could accept, so a case for a fundamental political decision that rests solely on religious doctrine is never sufficient—though religious reasons may be offered in public debate provided that suitable public reasons are supplied as well. What would count against this view? I want the strongest objections sorted by how decisive they are, from fatal to merely awkward: cases in which public reasons genuinely run out or underdetermine a momentous decision, the charge that the asymmetry between religious and secular comprehensive reasons is itself sectarian, the worry that the standard of reasonableness begs the question against religious citizens, and the suspicion that the proviso has already been shaped to absorb every apparent counterexample. I would also like an honest account of which of these a committed Rawlsian would concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss as misunderstandings, plus an assessment of whether the refined position risks anything at all—and if not, whether that insulation is a defect.
