I'm putting together a small collection of philosophical short fiction for a reading group I co-organize—we've been working through just war theory this semester, and I've found that the arguments, however carefully laid out, keep sliding off me in a way I don't like. The debate I can't stop thinking about is the moral equality of combatants. The traditional position, defended by Michael Walzer in *Just and Unjust Wars*, holds that soldiers on opposing sides possess equal moral permission to kill one another in combat, regardless of whether their side's cause is just, provided they observe the rules of *jus in bello*—distinction, proportionality, and so on. The revisionist challenge, pressed most prominently by Jeff McMahan, rejects this symmetry outright: if your war is unjust, you have no right to kill the soldiers who are justly defending against you, full stop. I understand the moves on both sides intellectually, but I want to know what the problem feels like at the level of lived experience—what it does to someone to inhabit either position when they're the one holding the weapon.

I'd like you to write a short story that dramatizes this tension without resolving it. The setting: a near-future conflict, one side clearly the aggressor (a resource grab, thinly justified), the other defending its territory. The story follows two soldiers—one from each side—who encounter each other during a temporary ceasefire, perhaps in a neutral field hospital, at a water station, or during a prisoner exchange. The aggressor's soldier is thoughtful and privately unsettled by his country's actions, yet he believes he is morally permitted to fight because he is protecting his unit and following the rules of engagement to the letter. The defender's soldier struggles with whether she can regard him as a moral equal, or whether his participation in an unjust war taints every act he commits, even those tactically indistinguishable from her own. The philosophical arguments should surface through the characters' interaction—what they say, what they cannot bring themselves to say, the silences between them—rather than through anything that reads like a position paper. Neither character should be a mouthpiece. I want the reader to finish the story unsettled, with the problem felt in the gut. Aim for somewhere around 2,000 words.
