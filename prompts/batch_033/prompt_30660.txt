I'm a pharmacovigilance scientist at a mid-size pharma company. Every month or so a new cluster of adverse event reports lands on my desk, and I have to decide whether it's the same mechanism as something we've already seen or a genuinely new safety signal — and, just as often, whether two clusters that look alike are "the same thing" or two different things. The stakes are high: if they're the same mechanism, we do one label change or maybe pull the drug; if they're different, we handle them separately, and getting it wrong either wastes millions or leaves a real safety signal unaddressed. I usually have about half a day per comparison before presenting to our safety review board, working from case narratives, preclinical data, and epidemiology — nobody on my team has a philosophy background, and the board signs off on whatever I recommend. The part I keep getting stuck on is what "same mechanism" even means here: same biological pathway, same risk factor, same clinical effect? I've read a little philosophy of science and I gather people disagree about whether mechanisms should be individuated by their parts and organization, by the phenomenon they produce, or by what interventions they're invariant under — but I dont know how to turn that into a test I can actually run.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for classifying two anomalies as "same mechanism," "different mechanism," or "can't tell yet." I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the call is really about what we care about rather than a fact about the biology. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) two clusters of liver injury with the same enzyme-elevation pattern, one mostly in high-dose patients and one mostly in patients with a pre-existing liver condition; (2) two clusters of the same skin rash, one starting within a day of the first dose and one starting after months of continuous use; (3) a cluster of headaches and a cluster of fatigue that turn out to share the same prescribing pattern and onset timing but have different symptoms. Tell me where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — for instance, telling me two clusters are different mechanisms even though a single warning would cover both, or the reverse — and say explicitly what the procedure can't do.
