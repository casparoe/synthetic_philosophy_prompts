I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on what routine clinical pain measurement quietly commits medicine to. The practice I have in mind is this: hospitals and pain clinics across the United States ask patients to rate their pain on a single 0-to-10 numeric scale, record that number as a fifth vital sign, and use movement in that number as the standard test of whether a treatment worked. I've been reading about pain asymbolia, the neurological condition in which patients can identify a stimulus as painful, locate it, and describe its quality as sharp or burning, yet insist it does not bother them and show none of the usual grimacing, withdrawal, or distress. Some patients on morphine, or after certain brain surgeries, report a related thing: the pain remains at the same intensity, but it no longer troubles them in the way that seemed to matter. If such reports are coherent, the single number may be running together two things that can come apart: pain's sensory intensity and its unpleasantness, or badness.

I want the feature to reconstruct the implicit theory of pain's badness that the one-number practice presupposes. Which rival theories of what makes pain bad (evaluative, representational, imperativist, or however the live options are best labeled) does the practice quietly rule out, and with which is it merely compatible? Is the practice genuinely in tension with pain asymbolia and the morphine-style dissociations, or can a defender of the single number accept those cases and still say the scale is the right clinical instrument?
