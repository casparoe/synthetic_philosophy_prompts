I'm running a philosophy reading group for lawyers — smart people, no formal philosophy background — and next month we're doing Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment.' I've got one evening, maybe two hours. I've read the paper three times; what I need is a teaching plan. What has to be on the board before I even say 'reactive attitudes'? I need the dependency structure: what's the idea the whole paper turns on that they won't arrive with, and what do I have to lay down first so that idea doesn't sound like a dodge?

Then the carrying example. I know Strawson uses the guy stepping on your hand, but I'm worried that example makes it too easy — it collapses into 'he did it on purpose so I'm mad' and they'll miss the deeper point about the inescapability of the participant stance. What example should I build around instead, and what does it need to have that the hand-stepping one lacks?

Misconceptions: I'm betting they'll think Strawson is just saying 'we can't help feeling these things so determinism is irrelevant,' which makes him sound like he's giving up on justification entirely. Another one I expect: conflating the objective attitude with being objective (like, impartial) rather than seeing someone as an object of treatment. Where in the sequence do I head each of these off — before they form, or let them form and then undermine them?

And at the end, I want one question that tests whether they've actually got the participant/objective distinction, not just whether they can paraphrase the paper. Something where a wrong answer sounds plausible if you've only half got it.
