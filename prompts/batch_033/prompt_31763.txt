Since I retired from a career in an experimental laboratory and began reading philosophy, I have kept noticing a pattern in epistemological arguments for which I have no name, though I suspect the specialists have one. It occurs when a source of information is made to certify itself. Consider a technician who reads a value off a newly installed pressure transducer, forms the belief that the pressure is 4.2 bar, and then treats the fact that the transducer reported 4.2 bar when the pressure was 4.2 bar as evidence that the transducer is reliable. Or consider me glancing at a printed page, judging that I can read the words, and then treating my having read them correctly as evidence that my eyesight is functioning properly. Each step, taken alone, seems entirely innocent; the whole, taken together, looks circular. Yet people who would never accept a vicious circle in an argument apparently regard this as a legitimate way of answering doubts about the source itself.

I would like to know whether this configuration has an established name. If it does, what exactly is the phenomenon, how is it different from a plain circular argument or from the old problem of the criterion, and is the consensus among epistemologists that it is a fallacy to be avoided or an unavoidable, perhaps even acceptable, feature of our epistemic situation? If it has no established name, I would be grateful for a precise definition and a term, along with boundary cases that show where the phenomenon begins and ends. A bare label would not be of much use to me; I am after the carving.
