I'm trying to get my views on distributive justice into consistent shape before I present them at a workshop, and I'd appreciate an audit. Here's where I've landed:

(1) I'm a luck egalitarian about the currency and scope of distributive justice: an inequality between persons is unjust if and only if it is traceable to brute luck — circumstances beyond the agent's control, such as native endowments, family background, or unforeseeable misfortune — rather than to genuine choices for which the agent can reasonably be held responsible.

(2) I believe the brute luck / option luck distinction is principled and can be drawn without committing to libertarian free will. The relevant notion of responsibility is compatibilist: it requires that the agent had adequate information, faced no coercion or manipulation, and possessed the normal capacities for practical reasoning at the time of choice. The background fact that an agent's capacities and preferences were themselves shaped by brute luck (genetics, upbringing) does not, on my view, undermine responsibility for the choices that issue from them.

(3) I believe a just society owes every citizen an unconditional social minimum: adequate healthcare, housing, nutrition, and an income floor sufficient for a decent life. This minimum is owed regardless of how the citizen came to need it — whether through bad brute luck, bad option luck, or straightforward imprudence.

(4) I believe the abandonment objection — the charge that luck egalitarianism would leave the imprudent to die in the gutter because their misfortune traces to choice rather than luck — is overblown. A properly elaborated luck egalitarianism can avoid that result without abandoning its core commitment to holding people responsible for their choices.

(5) I believe that when a person with full information, adequate reasoning capacities, and no coercion voluntarily declines to insure against a known risk, and the risk materializes, that person has no claim of justice to be compensated for the resulting loss. The loss lies where it falls.

(6) I believe that a person born with a severe congenital condition — bad brute luck if anything is — has a claim of justice to resources that mitigate its effects, and that this claim is stronger in kind, not merely in degree, than whatever claim (if any) the person in (5) might have.

I want to know whether these six commitments form a consistent package. Please go through them and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension, and what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency if any is needed. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each, say exactly what would have to give and what the philosophical cost of giving it up would be.
