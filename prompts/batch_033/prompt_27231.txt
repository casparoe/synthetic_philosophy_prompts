Henry Sidgwick's decades-long involvement with psychical research — he co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 and served as its first president — is often treated as a puzzling or even embarrassing footnote to an otherwise rigorous philosophical career. Yet Sidgwick himself appears to have seen the two projects as connected. In the concluding chapter of The Methods of Ethics, he argues that practical reason yields two equally authoritative but potentially conflicting principles — rational egoism and utilitarianism — and that this dualism of practical reason can be resolved only if the universe is morally ordered in a way that ultimately aligns individual happiness with universal duty. Such an order would plausibly require personal survival after death, making evidence for post-mortem existence directly relevant to the foundational problem of his ethical system.

Write an essay assessing whether Sidgwick's psychical research was a rationally motivated extension of his ethical project or a separate pursuit that his philosophical commitments cannot vindicate. Weigh, in particular, the following considerations. On the continuity side: the dualism genuinely seems to need an empirical postulate about the structure of the universe, and investigating survival claims through the best available empirical methods — as Sidgwick attempted, however imperfectly — looks like taking that need seriously. On the discontinuity side: the dualism is arguably a conceptual problem about the architecture of practical reason, not one that empirical evidence about mediums could settle; moreover, Sidgwick's interest in the paranormal predates the publication of the Methods, and one might argue that his standards of evidence in psychical research fell short of the rigor he demanded in ethics. Reach and defend a conclusion — do not end on a non-committal note.
