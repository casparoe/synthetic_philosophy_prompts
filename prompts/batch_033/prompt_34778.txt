Exam question:

Explain the target of Wittgenstein's private language argument, and reconstruct the reasoning by which the argument is supposed to rule out that target. Your answer should address all of the following: (i) why the diarist's 'S' fails to be a word with a genuine use; (ii) the role of the beetle-in-the-box passage; and (iii) the precise scope of the conclusion — in particular, whether the argument shows that a language spoken by only one person is impossible, that sensations are not private, or neither.

Student's answer:

The private language argument is directed against the idea of a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know — in particular, to the speaker's own private sensations. Wittgenstein develops the point through the diarist who resolves to write 'S' in a diary whenever a particular sensation recurs, intending 'S' to name that sensation. The problem is that the diarist has no way to distinguish using 'S' correctly from merely seeming to himself to use it correctly. If the only check on his use is his memory or impression that the same sensation has occurred again, then whatever seems right to him will count as right, and that is no standard of correctness at all. A word's correct use therefore requires an independent standard, and Wittgenstein takes the only available standard to be public criteria — the observable circumstances and behaviour in which the word is taught, applied, and corrected. The beetle-in-the-box passage makes the same point. Each person has a box, and no one can look into anyone else's box; the object inside therefore cannot serve as a shared standard and drops out of consideration. This shows that we can never really know what another person's sensation is like, since each of us has access only to his or her own box. The argument therefore shows that a private language is impossible. It further shows that sensations themselves are not private objects, since the standards for applying sensation words are public, and that any genuine language requires a community of speakers; a language spoken by only one person is impossible.
