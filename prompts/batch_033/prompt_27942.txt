I've been working through Augustine's writings on lying — both the early *De Mendacio* and the later *Contra Mendacium* — and I find myself unable to dismiss his position the way I expected to. As I understand him, he holds that every lie is sinful without exception: a lie is a false statement made with the intention to deceive, and the wrongness of the act is located in the willful mismatch between what one believes and what one says, not in the harm it causes. This rules out even the so-called "merciful lie" — lying to save a life, to protect an innocent person from a pursuer, to spare someone devastating news. Augustine is fully aware of how harsh this is and does not soften it; his answer to the pursuer-at-the-door case is that one must either remain silent or find a way to speak truthfully, and if that means someone dies, the moral cost falls on the murderer, not on the truth-teller.

I am not a theist, so the theological framework — that lying imperils one's eternal soul, that truth-telling is obedience to God, that martyrdom is preferable to sin — does not carry weight with me directly. But I find the underlying structure of the view compelling in a secular register: the idea that there is something intrinsically corrupting about deliberately misrepresenting reality to another mind, that this wrong-making feature is not commensurable with the harms and benefits that consequentialist reasoning weighs, and that a norm against lying cannot survive if we grant ourselves exceptions whenever the stakes feel high enough. I am trying to figure out whether this position is defensible once the theology is set aside, or whether the theology was doing all the work — and, if it is defensible, what it would actually take to dislodge it.

Treat Augustine's absolutism about lying as a live position I am considering adopting in a non-theistic form, and tell me what would count against it. I want the arguments, cases, theoretical costs, and empirical considerations that should move me, sorted from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward. Distinguish between objections that a committed Augustinian would concede as genuine problems for the view and objections they would dismiss as question-begging or beside the point. And give me an honest assessment of whether the position, once carefully stated, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or simply the shape that normative theories about act-types tend to take. Give me exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
