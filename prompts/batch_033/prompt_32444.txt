I'm hoping you can help me think through something that's been troubling me for a few weeks now. My closest friend from university — we studied biochemistry together, she's now a paediatric nurse and one of the most level-headed people I know — went on a silent retreat at a Benedictine monastery in the spring. She'd been struggling badly: her eight-year-old daughter was diagnosed last year with a rare autoimmune condition, and the specialists had been increasingly pessimistic about the trajectory. She isn't someone who was especially religious before all this; she'd describe herself as vaguely spiritual at most. But during the retreat she spent hours each day in prayer, and when she returned home, her daughter's condition had improved so markedly that her consultant used the phrase "I don't have a medical explanation for this." They've since run every test they can think of, and the results are baffling. My friend is quietly convinced that a miracle occurred — that her prayers were answered.

Here's my difficulty. She told me all of this over a long evening in my kitchen, not proselytising, just recounting what happened with the same careful, undramatic tone she'd use to describe a shift at the hospital. She wasn't asking me to believe her; she was telling me because I'm her oldest friend and it mattered to her that I know. But I could tell she wanted me to take it seriously, and I found I couldn't — not really. I'm a research scientist, and my entire intellectual formation tells me that when someone reports an event that violates the known laws of nature, the rational response is to withhold belief, no matter how trustworthy the source. I've since looked into this and found that David Hume makes essentially this argument: that the uniform testimony of experience in favour of natural law will always outweigh the testimony of any individual witness to a miracle, because it is always more probable that the witness is deceived or deceiving than that a law of nature has been suspended. And yet, when I try to apply that reasoning to this particular case, it feels thin and unsatisfying. I know this woman. I've seen her stay calm in emergencies, admit when she doesn't know something, and never exaggerate. The Humean calculus seems to treat her as just another potentially unreliable reporter, when in fact my whole history with her gives me reasons to trust her that don't fit neatly into a probability estimate.

So my question is: am I being irrational in wanting to give her testimony more weight than Hume's framework would allow? Is there a way of thinking about the epistemology of testimony — particularly testimony from people we know intimately — that Hume's argument against miracles simply doesn't capture? Or is this just sentiment getting in the way of clear thinking, and I should accept that the most I can do is respect her sincerity while privately believing that a natural explanation, even an unknown one, is vastly more likely?
