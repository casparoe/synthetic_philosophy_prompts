The Frege-Geach problem is a challenge for metaethical expressivism, which is the view that moral judgments don't express beliefs or describe facts but instead express non-cognitive attitudes like approval or disapproval. The problem gets its name from Peter Geach, who drew on Frege's work about the context principle to raise it against R.M. Hare's prescriptivism, but it generalizes to all forms of expressivism.

The basic issue is this: if "lying is wrong" just expresses disapproval of lying, then what does it mean when it appears in an embedded context, like the antecedent of a conditional? Take "If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong." The speaker isn't expressing disapproval of lying here — they might not even think lying is wrong. So the expressivist seems forced to say that "lying is wrong" means one thing when asserted and something else when embedded. But then in an argument like "Lying is wrong; if lying is wrong then getting your brother to lie is wrong; therefore getting your brother to lie is wrong," the term "lying is wrong" is being used equivocally, and the argument isn't valid. So the Frege-Geach problem shows that expressivism can't account for the logical validity of moral arguments.

Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism is the most influential response. Blackburn's idea is that we can "earn the right" to talk as if there are moral facts and moral truths, even though at the fundamental level there aren't any — moral discourse is still essentially expressive. His specific solution to the Frege-Geach problem is to model moral arguments in terms of practical commitments among attitudes rather than logical relations among propositions. If you accept the conditional and you accept the antecedent, your attitudes commit you to accepting the consequent — otherwise you have what Blackburn calls a "fractured sensibility," which is a kind of practical incoherence. So validity in moral reasoning is really about whether a combination of attitudes hangs together, not about truth-preservation. This lets the expressivist keep all the surface features of moral language — embedding, negation, even truth-talk — while maintaining that moral judgments are fundamentally expressions of attitude.
