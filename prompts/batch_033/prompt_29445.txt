(1) The backward induction argument for the centipede game — the argument that rational players with common knowledge of their rationality will defect at the very first move — is deductively valid given the standard specification of the game (perfect information, common knowledge of rationality, the payoff structure, and the fact that the number of rounds is finite and known to both players).

(2) Common knowledge of rationality is a strong idealization, but it is not one that game theory can simply jettison. Without something like it, game theory loses its capacity to make determinate predictions across a wide range of strategic situations, and the whole project of equilibrium analysis becomes unmoored.

(3) If I were actually sitting across a table from another intelligent and reasonable person, playing the centipede game for real money, I would not defect at the first move. I would cooperate for at least several rounds. And I do not believe this would be irrational of me, nor do I believe it would be a mistake.

(4) The backward induction paradox is not merely an artifact of idealized assumptions that fail in practice. It reveals a genuine and deep tension within our concept of practical rationality itself — a tension between the forward-looking, maximizing reasoning that backward induction formalizes and the kind of reasoning that makes mutually beneficial cooperation stable and intelligible.

(5) The surprise examination paradox and the backward induction paradox are fundamentally different in structure. The surprise exam paradox turns on self-referential epistemic reasoning (the students reasoning about their own future states of knowledge), whereas the backward induction paradox involves no such self-reference; it is purely a matter of iterated strategic reasoning between distinct agents, each reasoning about the other.

(6) A satisfactory resolution to the backward induction paradox must preserve the action-guiding character of rational choice theory. It is not enough to say that backward induction is technically correct but that real agents should not follow it. A theory of rationality that systematically instructs agents to do what they plainly should not do has failed precisely as a theory of rationality.
