So I've been kicking around an essay idea for the magazine and I could use a philosophical gut-check before I pitch it. The piece would read Fight Club — the Fincher film, though I've read the Palahniuk too and might draw on both — through Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. Quick refresher on the Dennett side: he argues the self isn't a thing, not a Cartesian ego humming away in the pineal gland, but an abstraction, like a center of gravity. It's a useful fiction we spin when we tell the story of a person's life — the "I" is just the posited source of a narrative, not a substance you can isolate on a brain scan. And Fight Club seems to be dramatizing exactly this. The Narrator has no name, no stable identity, he literally can't sleep, and then he generates Tyler Durden — a self with all the coherence and swagger his own narrative center lacks — who turns out to be him, or a split-off gravitational well of his own psyche. The film even ends with the couple holding hands watching the world collapse, as if two narrative centers are fusing into something like a self, or trying to.

But here's what I'm wrestling with. Dennett's view is fundamentally deflationary and kind of cheerful about it — the self is real in the way a center of gravity is real, which is to say it's not nothing, but it's not a thing, and that's fine, we can get on with our lives. Fight Club, on the other hand, treats the discovery that your self is a fabrication as something traumatic and world-shattering. The Narrator's breakdown isn't a philosopher calmly accepting that the ego is an abstraction; it's visceral, bloody, and involves shooting himself in the face to kill off the fictional self he made. So I'm trying to figure out: does the film actually vindicate Dennett's picture, or does it show that Dennett's picture, even if true, is psychologically unlivable? Is the self-as-narrative-gravity something you can know and still function, or does the knowledge destroy you — and if so, what does that say about the theory?

I want the reading to be honest to both the film and the philosophy — not just using the film as a pretty illustration, and not forcing Dennett into scenes that don't support him. Which scenes would you put at the center of the analysis, where does the reading hold up best, and where does the film resist it or complicate it in ways a good critic has to admit? Also, I'd like you to say how confident you are in the reading overall and flag the one point you're least sure about — the bit where you're really going out on a limb and a skeptical editor would push back hardest.
