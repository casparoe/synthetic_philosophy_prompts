I'm organizing a two-day interdisciplinary workshop called "The Architecture of Suffering," bringing together philosophers of mind, affective neuroscientists, and pain clinicians. I'm giving the ten-minute opening remarks on the first morning, and I want the speech to do real philosophical work while keeping everyone in the room — the clinicians who've never read a word of David Bain or Nikola Grahek, and the philosophers who've been arguing about pain asymbolia for a decade.

Here's the structural idea I want the speech to build. Pain asymbolia — the condition (most often associated with insular cortex lesions) in which patients can identify noxious stimuli as painful, locate them, and describe their intensity, yet report no unpleasantness and show no withdrawal or motivation to escape — is the closest thing we have to a natural experiment dissecting suffering apart from sensation. It forces the question: is the badness of pain baked into the sensation itself, or is it a separable evaluative layer that the brain constructs and that can, in principle, come apart? The workshop exists because the answer to that question ramifies into clinical practice (whose pain are we treating, and what exactly are we treating it for?), into neuroscience (what systems realize the "ouch" versus the "this is bad"?), and into ethics (what makes a state count as suffering?). The speech should use pain asymbolia not as a niche clinical curiosity but as the wedge that opens all of that up.

Practically: the speech should make the clinicians feel the phenomenon is clinically real and strange rather than a philosopher's thought experiment, and it should make the philosophers feel the framing is precise enough that they're not wincing. Humor is welcome but not required. The closing should set up the sessions that follow without turning into an agenda recitation.

Please also include, after the speech, a brief note — a short paragraph or two — on where the speech takes liberties with the empirical or philosophical literature or leans on a contested reading of pain asymbolia. Some people in the room hold that the sensory and affective components of pain are dissociable in this way; others think the asymbolia cases are over-interpreted. I want to know which claims in the speech sit on which side of that line, and where I'm presenting as settled something that is genuinely contested among people who know the topic.
