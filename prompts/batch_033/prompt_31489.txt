I'm working on a longform piece about the science of well-being and the philosophical assumptions built into that research. A lot of the empirical work on happiness — the kind that gets translated into policy recommendations and self-help — seems to presuppose some version of hedonism: that well-being is fundamentally about pleasure and the absence of pain. But I keep running into a philosophical objection I want to understand before I write: the heterogeneity problem. The basic worry is that pleasures don't seem to form a unified kind. The pleasure of a warm bath, the pleasure of solving a crossword clue, the pleasure of watching your child take her first steps, the pleasure of revenge, the pleasure of a heroin high — these feel different, they involve different cognitive and affective processes, and it's not obvious they share any single phenomenal quality or functional role. If "pleasure" is just a grab-bag label for experiences we happen to evaluate positively, then hedonism about well-being starts to look either vacuous (well-being is whatever you like) or false (because it's really the liking, not pleasure as a unified phenomenon, that matters).

Before I dive into the literature on how hedonists respond to this — attitudinal theories, phenomenological accounts, motivational views — I want to know what I should be asking of any candidate theory. What are the desiderata that a theory of pleasure must satisfy to handle the heterogeneity problem adequately? I want the full specification: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the empirical facts from psychology and neuroscience it must respect, and the theoretical roles "pleasure" is supposed to play in a theory of well-being that it cannot simply abandon. For each desideratum, tell me the case or consideration that motivates it. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect might be in tension, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out weaker or evasive responses. If you think the heterogeneity problem is overblown — that is, if you think the desiderata themselves reveal that the demand for a unified account of pleasure is misplaced — I want to hear that too, but only after you have laid out the list.
