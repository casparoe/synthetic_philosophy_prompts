I've been working my way through some of the classics in philosophy of language—retirement project, keeps the grey matter ticking over—and I've ground to a halt on a passage in Frege's essay 'The Thought' (1918). I spent forty years in the lab, so I'm used to things that yield to patience and careful measurement, but this has got me proper stuck. I'm reading the Geach translation, and there's a bit where he distinguishes the thought from the assertion of the thought, and he says something like this (I'm paraphrasing, my German is non-existent):

In an indicative sentence we must separate the thought—the sense—from the act of putting it forward as true. The same thought can appear in a sentence that is not asserted at all. When we say 'if the accused was in Rome, then he did not commit the murder,' neither the antecedent nor the consequent is asserted; each is merely expressed. The thought that the accused was in Rome is the very same thought that would be asserted if one said simply 'the accused was in Rome,' but here it is not asserted. The assertoric force attaches only to the whole conditional.

Now, I've read around the secondary literature enough to know that this is supposed to be the starting point for the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism—the idea that moral sentences can't express non-cognitive attitudes and still function logically in unasserted contexts like conditionals. But I'm struggling to see exactly how the passage does the work its supposed to do. As I understand it, Frege is talking about truth-conditional content—thoughts that have truth-values—and saying that the same thought can be asserted or unasserted. The expressivist, by contrast, says that the meaning of a moral sentence is the attitude it expresses. So why can't the expressivist just say the same thing Frege says, but with attitudes instead of truth-conditional thoughts? That is, an attitude can be expressed without being held—you can express disapproval of lying in the antecedent of a conditional without actually disapproving. Why isn't that a perfectly adequate response? I feel like I'm missing something important about the role of logic in Frege's picture. What is it about the way Frege sets things up in this passage—the thought as something that can be the bearer of logical relations independently of whether it is asserted—that makes the expressivist's analogous move unavailable to her? Or is the problem not really in this passage at all, and I'm looking in the wrong place?
