I have no legal training, but I have been trying to work out what I mean when I say that something is law. I have been testing my reactions against particular cases, and they pull in different directions. I would like to know whether there is a single principle that captures my reactions, or nearly captures them, and which of my reactions it would force me to abandon.

Here are the cases as they strike me.

1. Under a dictatorship, a woman denounces her husband to the authorities for privately criticizing the ruler, relying on a statute that makes such criticism a capital crime. He is sentenced to death and narrowly survives. After the regime falls, a court convicts her and, in doing so, declares that the statute was never law because it was so contrary to basic morality. I share the court's moral condemnation of what she did, but I do not think the statute was never law. It seems to me it was law — monstrous law — and I am uneasy with a court saying otherwise, even though I understand why it wanted to.

2. A properly enacted statute requires ordinary citizens to assist in returning escaped slaves. I think this was law, and I also think a judge should have refused to enforce it. I can hold both of those thoughts at once without much strain.

3. A decree issued by the ruler of a regime directs the extermination of an entire minority. Here my reaction flips: I find it hard to call this law at all. Saying it was law feels wrong in a way that goes beyond saying it was unjust. But I cannot see a principled difference between this and case 1.

4. A military junta seizes power illegally and governs through courts and police. The rules it issues are generally obeyed. I think those rules are law while the junta governs, despite the illegal way it came to power.

5. A bill passes the legislature but is never signed by the head of state, as the constitution requires. I do not think it is law. Procedure seems to matter to me.

6. A commercial custom about who bears the cost when goods are damaged in transit has been followed by merchants and enforced by courts for generations, though no legislature ever enacted it. I think it is law.

7. A criminal statute forbids "unpatriotic activities" without saying what those are. I do not think this is law, or at least not something a court may use to convict anyone.

8. A statute requires doctors to report undocumented patients to the immigration authorities. It was properly enacted and remains on the books, but it is widely and openly disobeyed, and the government has not enforced it in years. I think it is still law, though a dead letter in practice.

I notice two things that bother me. Cases 1 and 3 seem to be the same kind of thing, yet I treat one as law and the other as not law. And cases 6 and 8 seem asymmetric in a way I cannot justify: long practice can make something law, but long disuse does not seem to unmake something that was enacted. The half-formed principle I keep circling is that whether something is law has to do with how it was made and whether it is actually in force, not with whether it is just. But that idea sits badly with case 3, and case 7 suggests that bare enactment is not enough either.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of competing principles, that makes the best sense of these reactions? Please state it precisely, say which of my cases motivates each part of it, apply it to two or three further cases I have not listed, and tell me plainly which of my reactions it cannot accommodate and why I should reconsider that reaction.
