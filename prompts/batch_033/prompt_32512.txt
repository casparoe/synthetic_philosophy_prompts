Last term I watched a tutorial descend into an argument about whether a strategy that isn't dominated by any pure strategy can still be dominated by a randomisation. Half the room treated mixed strategies as a bookkeeping trick; the other half insisted the mixed-strategy notion was the real one, and I couldn't make the point land without just asserting the definition. I'm teaching the same material again this term to a third-year game theory class for philosophy and economics students — about 22 people, one 80-minute seesion — and I want to do it differently. The thing I want them to feel, before I put anything formal on the board, is that allowing dominance by a mixed strategy genuinely changes what a rational player should rule out, and that the iteration has real bite: once a strategy disappears because of a mixture, eliminations open up that weren't available before, and this only works if they think through what others know about others' rationality.

Design a classroom game with rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (points or sweets are fine), plus a debrief. Arrange it so that participants who stick with a strategy that is fine against every pure reply keep doing worse than people who mix, and so the group notices the cascade itself rather than being told. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto pure-strategy dominance, mixed-strategy dominance, iterated elimination, and the difference between everyone being rational and everyone being rational while knowing that everyone is rational and knowing... Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about what the activity can't reproduce about the formal notion (for instance, whatever the full rationalisability concept captures that a one-off classroom game with novices can't).
