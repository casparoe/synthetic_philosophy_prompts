I'm drafting a novel where one of the main characters is a labor lawyer who, over the course of the book, comes to believe that basically no contract signed under capitalism is genuinely consensual. I didn't want her position to just be a vibe — I wanted her to have an argument she can actually deliver, something tight enough that the other characters can't just dismiss it. So I sat down and wrote one out. Problem is, the argument I built doesn't just take out employment contracts. It seems to take out marriage vows, medical consent forms, dinner invitations, everything. If it works, valid consent is basically impossible. That's obviously too much, but I've been staring at it for a week and I can't find the exact place it goes wrong, and I need to know because whether the argument can be stopped before it eats the whole world determines whether her arc ends in insight or in nihilism.

Here's the argument as she'd give it, cleaned up:

(1) For consent to be valid, the consent-giver must have a genuine alternative — an option they could realistically take without facing costs that no one should have to bear just for refusing.
(2) An alternative is not genuine if the costs of taking it exist only because of background conditions the consent-giver did not choose and cannot individually escape.
(3) A worker who "consents" to dangerous or degrading conditions under threat of destitution faces an alternative — destitution — whose costs exist only because of background economic arrangements she did not choose and cannot individually escape. So by (1) and (2), her consent is invalid.
(4) The reasoning in (2) and (3) doesn't depend on any special feature of employment or money. If it invalidates consent there, it invalidates consent in any case where the costs of refusing are set by unchosen background conditions.
(5) Every choice anyone ever makes is structured by background conditions they didn't choose — their biology, their upbringing, their social position, the options their society happens to make available, the fact that they need food and shelter and other people.
(6) So by (4) and (5), in every choice situation, the alternative to consenting involves costs set by unchosen background conditions.
(7) Therefore, by (1), (2), and (6), no one ever has a genuine alternative in the sense required for valid consent.
(8) So valid consent is impossible — or at least so rare that it can't do the work we normally ask it to do.

I can feel that something slipped, probably around (4) or the move from (3) to (4). It feels like there's an equivocation on "background conditions" — maybe between conditions someone is actively exploiting and conditions that are just the shape of the world. But I can't make that precise without accidentally carving out a loophole that also lets the sweatshop owner say "hey, poverty's just a background condition, I'm not threatening anything, the worker can always leave." And I'm also not sure the problem isn't earlier, in what "genuine alternative" is supposed to mean in (1).

Give me a diagnosis as a JSON object with these fields:

- "flawed_step": a string identifying the earliest step or inference where the argument goes wrong (e.g., "premise (2)", "the inference from (3) to (4)", "premise (1)").
- "what_it_gets_right": a string explaining why that step looks plausible and what cases it handles correctly.
- "what_it_gets_wrong": a string giving the precise error — equivocation, scope shift, false generalization, smuggling in a hidden condition, whatever it is.
- "cost_of_rejecting_it": a string describing what you lose if you deny that step. Which real cases of invalid consent become harder to explain?
- "can_the_argument_be_salvaged": a string saying whether, once you fix the flawed step, a weaker but still interesting version of the argument survives, or whether the whole thing collapses.
- "what_my_character_should_believe": a string giving the strongest defensible version of her position after the diagnosis.
