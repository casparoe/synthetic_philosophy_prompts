In my philosophy of language seminar we have been working through some material from the School of Names, and the sentence 'a white horse is not a horse' keeps being invoked as though it were self-evidently important. The other day my tutor used it to dismiss an objection to his claim that the meaning of a kind term such as 'horse' is not exhausted by the properties by which we recognise horses; he said the paradox shows that the objection rests on a confusion. I can see that the sentence is being asked to do genuine argumentative work, but I cannot tell what it actually asserts.

Could you set out the distinct precise claims that 'a white horse is not a horse' might be making, ranging from the trivial to the radical? Please present the readings as a menu, and for each one give a brief verdict on whether it is plausible, false, or merely terminological. It would be helpful to distinguish readings that turn on whether the 'is' expresses identity, predication, or class inclusion; on what kind of thing the terms 'white horse' and 'horse' are supposed to pick out; and on what happens to the extension of a term when a qualification such as 'white' is added. Finally, please say which reading or readings would actually support my tutor's use of the sentence against the objection, and which plausible readings would not.

I am not asking for historical or textual detail, and no references are needed. Please write your answer in British English.
