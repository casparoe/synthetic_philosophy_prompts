I'm a retired biochemist — spent most of my career on enzyme kinetics, so I'm comfortable with a decision tree but pretty new to moral philosophy. Lately I've been reading around in the repugnant-markets and commodification literature, and I keep feeling like the important distinctions are sitting right there in the arguments but nobody has turned them into something a working committee can actually run.

Here's the situation. I sit on the ethics committee of a regional hospital system. We meet monthly, and about every other month a proposal lands on our table that raises the commodification question: paying living kidney donors a fixed amount set by the state, reimbursing funeral expenses for the families of deceased donors, paying healthy volunteers more than a token amount for phase I drug trials, letting a company buy plasma from our patients. The rest of the committee is clinicians, a lawyer, and a hospital chaplain — smart people, but they haven't read the philosophy and they mostly go on gut reaction. Our recommendations go to the hospital CEO, so whatever I say has to be defensible in plain English. The stakes are high in both directions: block something and maybe lives are lost, approve something and maybe the public stops trusting the system. I usually have about an hour to prepare, and I'm the closest thing the committee has to a philosopher.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the actual philosophical worries about commodification and repugnant markets, that I can run on a proposal before each meeting. I want the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human because its a values call rather than something the theory can settle. Then run the procedure on three of the proposals I mentioned, including one where it comes out against something I would personally have approved, and tell me where you expect it to misfire. No reading list — I need the checklist.
