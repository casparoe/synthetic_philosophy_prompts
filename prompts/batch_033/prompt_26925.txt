In my aesthetics course, we have been discussing the puzzle of aesthetic testimony—the apparent oddness of forming aesthetic beliefs purely on the word of others. I have been thinking about how this puzzle is reflected in actual academic practice. Consider the norms of art history as a discipline: a doctoral student writing on Rothko is expected to spend time in front of the paintings themselves; relying solely on reproductions and written descriptions would be seen as a serious methodological flaw. Yet a historian of the French Revolution is not expected to have witnessed the events she studies, and no one faults her for relying on documents and secondary sources. What theory of aesthetic knowledge does this asymmetry presuppose? Does the practice commit art history to the view that aesthetic properties can only be known through direct acquaintance, or are there other ways to make sense of it? And if we were to reject that view—if we held that aesthetic knowledge can be transmitted by testimony in roughly the way factual knowledge can—what would art history pedagogy have to change?
