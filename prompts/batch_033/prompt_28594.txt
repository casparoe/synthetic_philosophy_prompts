The substantivalist–relationist dispute about spacetime has run from Newton's rotating-bucket argument against Leibniz, through Mach's critique, and into the modern hole-argument literature, and it has an odd profile: the physics has been settled for a century, the formal results are accepted on all sides, and yet the ontology remains as contested as ever. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, not another survey of the positions.

Let me lay out the dialectic as I understand it. Newton argued that the concave surface of the water in a rotating bucket has to be explained by true rotation, and that this rotation cannot be rotation relative to any ordinary material body, since the water's surface becomes concave even at the moment it is at rest relative to the bucket; so there must be absolute space, or at least a privileged standard of non-acceleration. Leibniz replied that all motion is analyzable in terms of changes in the distances between bodies, and that absolute space is empirically idle and offends the principle of sufficient reason: there would be no reason for the entire material world to be situated here rather than a few feet over, so its placement cannot be a real feature of the world. Mach then turned the bucket around, suggesting that the water rotates relative to the fixed stars, and that inertia might be a function of the large-scale distribution of matter. In the modern literature, the pressure on substantivalism comes mainly from the hole argument: if the points of the differentiable manifold exist independently of the metric field, then a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside some region and nontrivial inside it maps one solution onto another that agrees with the first outside that region; if the manifold points are real, these count as distinct possible worlds, and determinism fails because the state outside the region does not fix the state inside. Substantivalists have responded by going "sophisticated" and denying that such diffeomorphically related models represent genuinely distinct possibilities, which to relationists looks like surrendering the view the substantivalist set out to defend.

Here is what puzzles me. Everyone in this debate accepts general relativity, everyone accepts the mathematics of the hole argument, and no one expects a new experiment to settle whether spacetime points exist. Yet sophisticated substantivalism and relationism remain live, opposed positions, and each side continues to write as though the other is making a straightforward mistake. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about physics and not exactly about words, but about what an ontology of motion is supposed to do: substantivalists take the explanatory work done by positing spacetime — giving inertia a subject, making sense of absolute acceleration — as evidence for an entity, while relationists take the same work as evidence that the theory needs richer relations rather than a new kind of substance. But I can also imagine diagnoses on which it is a verbal dispute about the word "exists," or a disagreement about whether theoretical posits require direct observational evidence, or a difference about which facts may be left brute.

Diagnose this impasse. Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two research programs answering different questions under one heading? Where does the real hinge lie — in views about explanation, about the status of unobservables, about identity across models, or somewhere else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the question, a formal result one side has failed to absorb, a reformulated question both sides could actually answer, or an honest argument that the dispute is ill-posed and no progress should be expected?
