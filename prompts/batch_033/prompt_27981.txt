A colleague presented the following argument in a workshop, and I have been unable to settle on which premise to reject. The argument seems to prove that there is no morally significant difference between lying and merely misleading—a conclusion that strikes me as false, but I cannot locate the error.

(1) If two acts are identical in all morally relevant respects, then they are morally on a par: neither is worse than the other, and if one is permissible then so is the other.

(2) What makes lying pro tanto wrong is that the speaker intentionally induces a false belief in the hearer, thereby subverting the hearer's capacity to act on accurate information about the world.

(3) In paradigm cases of misleading—for instance, stating a carefully worded truth one knows the hearer will misinterpret, or exploiting a false conversational presupposition without correcting it—the speaker also intentionally induces a false belief in the hearer and subverts the hearer's capacity to act on accurate information to the same degree.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), misleading is not morally less wrong than lying.

(5) But there are clear cases where misleading is permissible and lying would not be. Consider a host who, when asked by a guest she dislikes whether he is welcome at next week's party, replies 'I shall be away that weekend' rather than 'You are not welcome'. The reply is true, and the host has not lied, yet the guest forms a false belief about the host's attitude. Many judge this permissible, whilst a direct lie ('Of course you are welcome') would be impermissible. If (4) is true, this pattern of judgements cannot be correct.

(6) So either premise (2) is false—lying is wrong for some reason beyond intentionally inducing false belief—or premise (1) is false—two acts can differ morally even when identical in the respects that make one of them wrong—or the intuitive verdict about cases like (5) must be surrendered.

I am most reluctant to give up (5); the cases feel robust across variations. But I also find (1) hard to deny without opening the door to a kind of moral arbitrariness I cannot make sense of. That leaves (2) as the most likely candidate, yet I cannot see what the extra wrong-making feature of lying could be—something about the speech act of assertion itself, perhaps, or about the relationship between speaker and hearer that a lie damages in a way that misleading does not.

Where does the argument go wrong? If you reject a premise, what does that rejection commit you to, and are you prepared to pay that price?
