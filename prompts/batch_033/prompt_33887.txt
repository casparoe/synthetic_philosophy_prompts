i'm teaching an undergrad moral philosophy seminar, about 15 students, 75 minutes. we're doing promises — hume, scanlon, the speech-act people — and i want them to feel the puzzle before they read anything. 

design a game i can run in class. here's the rough idea: students pair up and make promises to each other, but under systematically different conditions. maybe some pairs make a verbal promise in private, some write it down and sign it, some make it in front of a witness, some do a ritual (handshake, whatever). the promise should be something like 'i'll bring you a coffee next session' or 'i'll save you a seat' — real but low stakes. then, between sessions, i email everyone with a reason they might want to break the promise (a scheduling conflict, a better offer, whatever). next session, we see who kept their promise and who didn't, and more importantly, we debrief on *why* they felt bound or didn't.

the point is for them to notice that the bindingness of a promise isnt reducible to any single feature — not the words, not the expectations, not the social cost. the debrief should map what they say onto the philosophical positions without me lecturing. they should generate the categories.

tell me: the exact rules, the timeline across two sessions, how to debrief, what predictably goes wrong (some students will just keep all promises because they're nice, some will break all of them because they're edgy), and which philosophical position the activity is unfair to (i suspect the speech-act view gets shortchanged since the low stakes make the performative aspect feel unserious).
