I work in the provenance office of a mid-size university museum, and right now our acquisition and restitution reviews are basically vibes. We handle three or four new objects a month, plus every couple of years a claim or a journalist poking at something we've held for a century. The stakes are uneven: nobody's going to jail over most of these, but we've already had one ugly repatriation fight and the director is now paranoid about the next one, and anything we flag has to go to the director and general counsel to sign off. I've been reading Nozick's entitlement theory — the idea that a holding is just only if it was justly acquired in the first place and then passed along through just transfers, and that past injustice calls for rectification, with the Wilt Chamberlain point that you can't judge a distribution just by looking at the pattern it ends up in. I want to turn that into a procedure my office can actually run on any object that comes in or gets claimed: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no, don't acquire" or "yes, clear it," and where it has to hand the file to a human because it's now a values question rather than a fact question. We can't do archival research on every transfer back to creation — that would take months per object — so the procedure also has to say how far back the chain has to be checked and what to do when the records just don't exist, which is alot of the time.

Then run the procedure on three cases. One: a mid-19th-century funerary object that a colonial administrator took from a site, later sold by his heirs at auction, bought by a dealer, and donated to us in the 1950s; the source country's government has now asked for it back. Two: a contemporary print bought last month directly from the artist's gallery with a standard invoice and a clear paper trail. Three: a painting we received in 1947 from a refugee family that may themselves have acquired it under pressure during the war, but there's no documentation either way. For each, show the step-by-step working, flag the judgment calls, and make sure at least one case ends with the procedure telling us something we don't want to hear. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do — where the theory itself runs out and a person has to decide.
