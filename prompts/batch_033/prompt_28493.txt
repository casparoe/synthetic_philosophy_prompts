You are an expert in mathematical logic and the philosophy of mind, fully at home with Gödel's two incompleteness theorems, their standard proofs, and the Lucas–Penrose debate about whether they refute computationalism about the human mind. Before you answer, spend some time brainstorming candidate defeaters, then sort them and present the strongest version of each.

Here is the position I hold: Gödel's incompleteness theorems, correctly understood, have no direct consequences outside formal mathematics. In particular, they show nothing about whether the human mind is or is not a computational system; they impose no in-principle limitation on what a machine could do; and every anti-mechanist argument that invokes them requires substantial further premises that, rather than the theorems, carry the philosophical weight. What would count against this view?

I want a map of the view's exposure, not just a list of objections. Which formal results, arguments, empirical findings, or thought experiments would have to be true for my position to be false? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and mark which are live and which are only logically possible. In particular: what would a Lucas–Penrose style argument have to establish, and which of its assumptions — about human consistency, about our ability to recognize the truth of our own Gödel sentence, about the relevant notion of mechanism — would have to hold for it to count against me? What should a defender of my view concede genuinely counts against it, and what should they dismiss as a misunderstanding of what the theorems say? Finally, say honestly whether my view is insulated from refutation by its insistence that the theorems are merely facts about formal systems, and if it is, whether that is a defect or the correct way to draw a boundary. For what it is worth, I would be moved most by a precise technical argument and least by appeals to the felt experience of grasping a mathematical truth.
