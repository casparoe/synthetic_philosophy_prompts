I'm writing an essay on Hofstadter's 'Person Paper' and I'd love some feedback before I finalise it. The essay's meant to be a critical look at whether the central analogy of the satire actually works as well as it's usually taken to. Here's the current draft:

---

**Does Hofstadter's Person Paper Persuade, or Just Preach to the Choir?**

Douglas Hofstadter's 'A Person Paper on Purity in Language' is rightly celebrated as one of the cleverest pieces of philosophical satire in recent decades. Written under the pseudonym William Satire, it constructs a world in which the word 'person' and its compounds — chairperson, spokesperson, craftsperson — are used to refer exclusively to white people, while Black people are designated by a separate vocabulary ('individuals', or in some contexts simply excluded from the scope of the generic altogether). The reader is walked through a series of increasingly absurd linguistic contortions, all presented in the reasonable tone of a newspaper columnist defending ordinary usage, until the parallel with gendered language becomes inescapable. The intended effect is that anyone who has ever defended 'he' as a generic pronoun, or 'man' as a term for humanity, will recognise their own arguments mirrored back at them and feel the force of the objection from the inside.

The essay is brilliant, and I don't want to deny that. But I've been worrying about whether it actually has the persuasive power it's usually credited with, or whether it mostly works on readers who are already sympathetic to the feminist critique of language. My worry has two parts.

First, there's a question about the analogy itself. The argumentative force of the satire depends on the reader finding the racialised language obviously absurd and offensive, and then noticing that the same structure applies to gendered language. But the analogy between race and gender in this context isnt perfect, and the differences might matter. In the world of the Person Paper, the term 'person' has been deliberately co-opted to exclude a particular group — it's presented as a kind of linguistic power grab. But the history of 'man' as a generic term is messier than that. In Old English, 'man' genuinely was the unmarked term for a human being, with 'wer' and 'wif' marking the male-female distinction. The narrowing of 'man' to mean specifically 'adult male human' happened gradually and without anything like the deliberate exclusion the satire depicts. A defender of generic 'he' or generic 'man' could reasonably point out that the historical trajectory is different, and that this weakens the analogy. The satire works by making the linguistic practice seem like an obvious injustice, but if the injustice in the gender case is less clear-cut — if it's more a matter of accumulated usage patterns than anyone's active decision to exclude — then the analogy might not carry the weight Hofstadter wants it to.

Second, and relatedly, I wonder whether the satire's effectiveness depends on the reader already accepting that language can encode and perpetuate injustice in a way that matters. If you come to the essay already thinking that concerns about sexist language are overblown, you might simply accept the racialised language as absurd and offensive but deny that the gendered case is parallel — not because of any specific disanalogy, but because you dont think language works that way in the first place. The satire doesn't give you an argument for why language matters; it assumes you already feel it. And if you don't feel it, you can just shrug and say 'well, the racial case is different because race and gender are different.' The essay, in other words, might be a perfect illustration of the position it's attacking — but an illustration isn't the same thing as an argument.

I don't think either of these worries is fatal to the essay. The Person Paper isn't trying to be a deductive proof; it's a piece of satire, and satire is allowed to work by making you feel the absurdity of a position rather than by constructing a watertight argument. But I do think there's a real question about who the essay is for. If it's for people who already get it, it's a delight. If it's for people who don't, I'm not sure it does much to change their minds — and it might even backfire, because the racial analogy can feel like an accusation of bad faith, which tends to make people defensive rather than reflective.
