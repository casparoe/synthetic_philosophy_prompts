I'm a retired biochemist who's been reading philosophy for about two years now, so please correct me if I've got this wrong.

The paradox of tragedy — why we enjoy art that makes us feel sad or afraid — seems to me like it dissolves the moment you think about it in terms of evolved function. We're social primates who need to learn about threats, losses, and moral violations without actually experiencing them. Fiction is a low-cost simulation environment. The sadness we feel during King Lear is genuine, sure, but it's a cost we pay for the benefits: catharsis, insight into human nature, the aesthetic pleasure of Shakespeare's language. Net expected utility is positive, so we choose to watch. That's not a paradox, that's just a tradeoff.

But philosophers have been puzzling over this since Aristotle, and I doubt they're all just confused about cost-benefit analysis. So what's the actual puzzle? Is the claim that the sadness *itself* is somehow pleasurable, not just that it's outweighed by other pleasures? Because if that's the argument, I don't see how it's coherent — sad is sad, pleasure is pleasure, they're different qualia. Or is the real paradox something else entirely that I'm not seeing?
