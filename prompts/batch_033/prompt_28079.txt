I've come to accept that pure time preference—discounting a benefit or harm solely because it lies further in the future—is not a legitimate normative input, but a bias to be debiased. On my view, the only permissible discounting is for uncertainty, opportunity costs, and similar epistemic or instrumental factors; the bare passage of time itself should carry no weight. I want to know what would count against this. What observations, arguments, formal results, or possible cases should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward? In particular, which of these would a committed defender of temporal neutrality concede as counting against the position—for example, implications for choices involving the very distant future, the analogy between temporal and spatial partiality, the dependence of discounting on personal identity over time, or puzzles that arise once infinite horizons are allowed—rather than dismissing as mere demandingness objections? Finally, has the position been qualified in ways that insulate it from any such evidence, and if so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind?
