I have been trying to reconstruct the rule behind a colleague's use of "life-changing" in what she insists is a strict sense, not a loose way of saying that something matters. Her verdicts are consistent, and I have kept a list. She counts these as life-changing: having a first child; falling in love for the first time; losing one's hearing as an adult; moving permanently to a country whose language and customs one does not yet know; and entering a monastic order for life. She counts these as not life-changing: laser eye surgery after decades of wearing glasses, on the grounds that the person already knows what clear sight is like; and taking a new job in the same field in a nearby city, on the grounds that the person would be the same individual with the same concerns at a different desk. She has also said that merely acquiring a new skill would not be enough.

A new case has come up: an adult who has had no sense of smell since birth is deciding whether to undergo a treatment that would give her a normal sense of smell. Predict how my colleague will classify this case. I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her record just as well, which of her earlier cases already tells against one of those rules, and what further case would best settle which rule she is really using.
