I write about science and technology for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a piece on the conceptual side of AI safety. In my reading, I came across a recent book for a lay audience that summarizes the field's central problem like this:

"Alignment is the problem of getting advanced AI systems to do what their designers intend. The field's founding nightmare is the paperclip maximizer: a system given a harmless objective that it pursues so single-mindedly that it converts everything, including us, into paperclips. Researchers now usually divide the problem in two. Intent alignment means making a system carry out the instruction it was actually given. Value alignment means making a system act on human values rather than merely on stated goals. Most people in the field regard value alignment as the harder and more fundamental task, because human intentions are too vague and shifting to serve as a specification. A third strand, corrigibility, asks that a system allow itself to be corrected or shut down. But corrigibility is best understood as a safety net; if value alignment were solved, a system would do what is right, and the shutdown problem would disappear."

I do not have the technical background to know how much of this to trust, and I would rather not repeat its errors in print. Take the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a researcher working on these questions would accept, which are simplifications that would draw a wince, and which are outright mistaken. In particular, does the final claim — that corrigibility is only a fallback and that solving value alignment would make the shutdown problem disappear — follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does it rest on a confusion?
