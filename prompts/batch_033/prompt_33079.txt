I have been teaching decision theory for over a decade now, and I keep returning to a puzzle that I cannot shake. The two-envelope problem has a way of humbling you: the setup is simple enough for a first-year undergraduate to grasp in under a minute, and yet a line of reasoning that seems to follow from nothing more than symmetry and the definition of expected value produces a conclusion that is manifestly absurd. I want to lay out the argument as cleanly as I can and then ask you to tell me where it goes wrong — not by name-dropping a solution from the literature, but by identifying the precise step at which the reasoning fails, explaining why that step looked innocent, and making clear what someone who resists your diagnosis would have to give up instead.

Here is the situation. You are shown two sealed envelopes and told, truthfully, that one contains exactly twice as much money as the other, though you are not told which is which. You select one envelope but do not open it. Now consider: let the amount of money in your chosen envelope be X. The other envelope therefore contains either X/2 (if you happened to pick the larger amount) or 2X (if you happened to pick the smaller). Since your initial choice gave you no information that could break the symmetry, each of these two possibilities appears equally likely. The expected monetary value of switching envelopes is therefore (1/2)(X/2) + (1/2)(2X) = (5/4)X, which is strictly greater than X. So you should switch. But here is the trouble. Once you have switched, you now hold the other envelope. Call its contents Y. The very same reasoning applies to it: the envelope you left behind contains either Y/2 or 2Y, each with probability one-half, and the expected value of switching back is (5/4)Y, which is greater than Y. So you should switch again. The argument appears to endorse an endless cycle of switching before you have so much as peeked inside either envelope — and to do so on purely a priori grounds. That cannot be right.

I have my own diagnosis, but I am more interested in yours. Identify the step at which this reasoning fails, explain why that step looked plausible enough to have survived scrutiny for as long as the puzzle has, and spell out what the alternative positions cost. If your diagnosis involves the way probabilities are assigned to the two possible contents of the unopened envelope, be precise about what an alternative assignment would look like and how it blocks the paradox without simply stipulating that the two possibilities are not equiprobable. I am particularly interested in whether you take the problem to be fundamentally about probability, about the representation of utility, or about something in the logical form of the argument itself — an equivocation, a scope error, an illicit substitution — and I want to know what your diagnosis commits you to elsewhere in decision theory.
