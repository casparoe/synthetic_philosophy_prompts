Population axiology has been in a long impasse. The total view implies the repugnant conclusion; average and critical-level views avoid it only by making the value of adding a life depend on the existing population's average or on a threshold, which yields its own strange results; and person-affecting views avoid some of these problems but struggle to account for choices that trade off the number of lives against their quality, including choices involving merely possible lives. The standard arguments now repeat without moving anyone. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not factual and not simply a clash of brute moral intuitions, but a disagreement about which formal adequacy conditions an axiology is required to satisfy, with each side implicitly prioritising different constraints such as avoiding the repugnant conclusion, respecting dominance, or honouring neutrality intuitions. Diagnose the structure of the impasse: are the parties answering different questions under one heading, treating different cases as data, or resting on bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? And given that diagnosis, say what would count as progress, or whether none should be expected.
