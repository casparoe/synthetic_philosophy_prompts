I spent my career in evolutionary biology and only started reading philosophy after I retired. A buddy from my reading group lent me a book — something on rationality and game theory, I didn't catch the author — that summarizes Parfit's hitchhiker case like this:

"Parfit asks you to imagine you're stranded in the desert. A driver pulls up. You promise to pay him a hundred bucks once you're safely in town. But the driver is rational, and he knows you're rational too. He thinks: once we're in town, a rational person has no reason to actually hand over the money — the ride is already done, paying is pure loss. So he doesn't pick you up. Parfit's point is that it can be rational to make yourself irrational: if you can bind yourself to keeping promises even when it's against your interests, you become the kind of person drivers will stop for. Rationality sometimes requires abandoning rationality. This shows that standard decision theory is incomplete, because it can't handle cases where being rational makes you worse off."

Something about this rubs me the wrong way but I'm having trouble articulating exactly what. It feels like the summary slides from 'it's useful to have certain dispositions' to 'rationality demands irrationality' without earning that jump, and the bit about decision theory being incomplete seems like a big conclusion to hang on one thought experiment. But I don't know the literature — I've only read a bit of Parfit and none of the commentary on this case — so I can't tell whether my unease is tracking real problems with the summary or just my own ignorance. Can you go through it and tell me which parts a careful philosopher would accept, which parts are subtly misrepresented, and whether the conclusion about standard decision theory actually follows from what's accurate in the summary?
