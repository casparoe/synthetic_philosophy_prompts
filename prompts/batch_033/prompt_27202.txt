Clinical diagnosis in psychiatry is organized around a two-part test that I want to understand philosophically. A condition counts as a mental disorder only if, first, some psychological or biological mechanism is not working as it is supposed to — not merely unusual, but malfunctioning — and, second, that malfunction causes clinically significant distress or impairment in the person's life. The practice treats the two prongs very differently. The second is openly evaluative: whether someone's state is distressing or impairing depends on what their society expects of them, what work they must do, and what a bearable life is taken to require. The first, by contrast, is presented as a factual, scientific matter, as if "working as it is supposed to" were something a biologist could read off the organism. I am trying to work out what theory of health and disease this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that dysfunction is a value-free biological fact, or does the first prong quietly import norms about what a human mind is for? Is the two-part structure coherent, or is it an unstable compromise between two rival theories? And what would have to change in diagnosis, insurance reimbursement, and disability determinations if the practice committed itself fully one way — either treating every harmful condition as a disorder regardless of mechanism, or treating every mechanism-failure as a disorder regardless of harm? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined.
