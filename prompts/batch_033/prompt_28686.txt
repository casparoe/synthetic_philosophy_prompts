Before I read any more arguments about whether a digital mind can be wronged, I want the specification first: write the desiderata that a theory of moral status must satisfy to be adequate for digital minds — emulations, reinforcement-learning agents, language models, and the intermediate cases people actually argue about. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Cover at least the following, and add whatever I have missed: the cases the theory must get right, including a system that reports pain while lacking any reward signal, a system with a reward signal that reports nothing, a copy of a patient, a patient run at a hundredth of normal speed, a patient whose computation is suspended and resumed, and two systems merged into one; the distinctions the theory cannot collapse, including being a moral patient versus being a moral agent, having moral status versus having more of it, a state that is realized versus one that is merely simulated, direct versus merely derived status, and whatever separates behaving exactly as if one suffers from actually suffering; and the results from computer science and biology it must respect, such as the substrate-independence of computation and multiple realizability. For each desideratum, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if, for example, substrate-neutrality collides with a requirement of biological implementation, or if requiring that copies preserve status conflicts with requiring that status attach to a particular token. Finally, say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular theories — speciesism, substrate chauvinism, sentientism, functionalism about welfare, and any others you would place on the list — and give a brief scorecard. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria and the cases behind them are what I am after.
