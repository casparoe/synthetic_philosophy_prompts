I'm writing a paper for an upper-division philosophy of mind seminar, and I'd like you to help me work through the argument. The topic is the causal exclusion argument—broadly, the claim that if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, then mental properties risk being causally idle, since the causal work is already done at the physical level. What I find interesting is that the literature seems to split along a fault line that rarely gets made fully explicit: whether the argument presupposes a "production" or "generative" conception of causation, where causes produce their effects, as opposed to a counterfactual or difference-making conception, where causes are whatever makes the difference to whether the effect occurs. It seems to me that the exclusion argument hits much harder under a production model—if the physical already produces the effect, the mental has nothing left to produce—but that under a counterfactual model, mental causation might be on much firmer ground, since mental properties can make a difference to outcomes even if the underlying physical properties are sufficient. I'm not entirely sure this is right, though, and I'd like to see it argued through carefully.

Please weigh the considerations on both sides—don't just assert that one framework solves everything. In particular, address the objection that even under a difference-making account, we still face the question of whether mental properties make a difference over and above their physical realizers, or whether the counterfactual dependence of behavior on mental states is simply inherited from the counterfactual dependence of behavior on the physical states that realize them. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it, but acknowledge the strongest argument against your position and explain why you think it ultimately fails.
