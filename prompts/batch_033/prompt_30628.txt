A professor in an introductory metaphysics course summarized the literature on dispositions, finks, and masks to me roughly like this: "The classic analysis of dispositions is the simple conditional: an object is disposed to M in circumstances C just in case, if it were in C, it would M. Finks and masks are the standard counterexamples. A fink removes the disposition when the trigger arrives — a live wire attached to a safety device that makes it dead the instant it is touched, so the conditional is false even though the wire was disposed to conduct. A mask leaves the disposition in place but blocks its manifestation — a fragile glass packed in foam that would not break if struck. The standard repair, due to Lewis, requires the object to possess an intrinsic causal basis for the disposition and to retain it through the triggering circumstances; this is usually said to handle the fink cases. Masks are often treated as finks of a sort, with the interference merely coming from outside the object, so the same repair is thought to cover them as well. The upshot usually drawn is that the simple conditional analysis fails and dispositions therefore cannot be given any conditional analysis at all; we should treat them as primitive intrinsic powers." I suspect the last step moves too quickly and that finks and masks are being run together, but I don't trust my ear. Which parts of this summary would a philosopher working on dispositions accept, which parts conflate distinct claims, and does the final conclusion about primitiveness follow from the counterexamples as described?
