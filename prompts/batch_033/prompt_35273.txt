I've been persuaded by the superrationality thesis in decision theory — roughly, the view that in symmetric strategic interactions where each agent knows the others are reasoning in the same way and using the same principles, the rational choice is the one that yields the best outcome on the assumption that all agents will reason identically and thus arrive at the same decision. On this view, cooperating in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between equally sophisticated reasoners isn't a mistake but a requirement of rationality, because the alternative — defecting — guarantees a worse outcome once you recognize that your counterpart, following the same reasoning, will defect as well.

I want to know what would count against this view. What formal results, what plausible counterexamples, what alternative equilibrium concepts, or what empirical considerations should make me reconsider? I'd like the defeaters ranked from those a committed superrationalist would concede as genuine problems to those they would dismiss as question-begging, and I'd like an honest assessment of whether superrationality can be distinguished from related ideas — evidential decision theory, Kantian universalization, team reasoning — in a way that gives it independent content, or whether it collapses into one of them under scrutiny.
