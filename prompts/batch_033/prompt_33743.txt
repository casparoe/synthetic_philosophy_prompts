I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about truth — not in some grand, hand-waving way, but about what we are actually doing when we call a claim true. By the end of it I found myself defending a roughly deflationary picture, and I think I held my ground, but I also noticed that I was doing the thing where you keep adjusting the view so that nothing quite lands as a counterexample. I want to step back and ask the question I probably should have asked myself before the argument started.

Here is the position as I understand and endorse it. The truth predicate does not pick out a substantive property — it is a logical device for semantic ascent. Its entire function is captured by the equivalence schema: the proposition that p is true if and only if p. When we say that a theory is true, or that everything someone said is true, we are not describing those propositions as bearing a relation to reality, or as cohering with one another, or as being ideally justifiable. We are using "true" to endorse them, to generalize over them, or to take a stance toward them without having to enumerate them. Truth has no hidden nature awaiting philosophical discovery. Correspondence, coherence, pragmatic, and epistemic theories of truth are all trying to answer a question that does not need answering — or they are answering a different question altogether and calling it a theory of truth.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for the standard objections that deflationists already have rehearsed replies to — the generalization problem, the normativity of truth, the success argument, the problem of blind ascriptions, the role of truth in embedded contexts. I know those debates. What I want is a map of the view's genuine exposure: which considerations, if they turned out a certain way, would actually threaten deflationism, and which ones only look like threats from outside the view?

Specifically: what would have to be true — about language, about logic, about cognition, about the role truth plays in our practices — for deflationism to be wrong? Which of these are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones? What would a deflationist herself have to concede as counting against her position, and what would she legitimately dismiss as begging the question or as demanding that truth be something it isn't? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. And I would like an honest assessment of whether deflationism has been qualified and insulated to the point where nothing can count against it — and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, or whether it is exactly what we should expect from an account of a concept as basic and thin as truth.
