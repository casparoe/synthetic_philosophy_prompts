I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the better part of a year reporting on disputes among families, surrogates, and hospital teams about the force of advance directives written long before a patient developed advanced dementia. The position I've come to defend is this: the authority of an advance directive is not all-or-nothing but tracks the degree of psychological continuity between the person who signed it and the person now in the bed, so that in cases of radical discontinuity the directive should be treated as strong but defeasible evidence of the earlier self's wishes rather than as a standing command that silences the later self's present preferences. I'm giving grand rounds at a teaching hospital, and the audience will be clinical ethicists, palliative-care physicians, geriatricians, and two health-law faculty members. They are not interested in personal-identity metaphysics for its own sake; they are interested in whether my view gives them a workable, defensible, and legally safe way to handle the cases that keep them up at night. Most of them accept the standard autonomy-based justification for advance directives, worry about paternalism, fear legal exposure, and have watched surrogates freeze at three in the morning. My argument, compressed: the usual justification for binding advance directives is respect for the autonomy of the person who made them; autonomy's authority over a later time depends on there being a single continuing subject whose life is being steered; when dementia or another condition has produced deep changes in values, memory, and character, that continuity has thinned; therefore the directive's claim weakens in proportion, and in the most discontinuous cases the later self's settled present preferences should normally prevail where they can be reliably discerned.
