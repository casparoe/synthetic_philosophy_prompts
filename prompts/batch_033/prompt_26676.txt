I'm a retired computational neuroscientist — spent my career modeling cortical microcircuits — and I've gotten into philosophy of mind over the last few years. I'm trying to get my own views straight on which systems are conscious and which matter morally, and I'd like someone to stress-test the package before I bring it to my reading group.

Here's what I think:

(a) Substrate independence. If you implement the same causal structure in silicon, in a giant lookup table, or in a brain, you get the same conscious experience. The computation is what matters, not the stuff.

(b) Functionalism. Phenomenal states just are functional states of the right kind. There's no extra ingredient beyond the causal role.

(c) Gradualism about consciousness and moral status. Consciousness comes in degrees — a dog has less than a human, a bee less than a dog — and moral status tracks those degrees. You owe more to a chimp than to a mouse.

(d) Not everything is conscious. Thermostats, rocks, and purely feedforward networks aren't conscious at all, not even a tiny bit. You need some kind of recurrent or self-modeling architecture to get any consciousness off the ground.

(e) The conscious/non-conscious boundary is vague. There's no sharp cutoff, just like there's no sharp cutoff for "bald." But there are clear cases on both ends.

(f) Same computation, same moral status. If two systems run the exact same computation, they get the same moral consideration. No bonus points for being made of meat.

I want to know: does this set hang together? Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are on a first pass, and what the cheapest revision would be. Rank the tensions by severity — I want to know what I should actually worry about versus what just needs a clearer formulation.
