Here is an argument I encountered in a seminar on Wittgenstein's On Certainty. It seems to show that all rational inquiry is ultimately irrational, which can't be right. I need help finding the flaw.

(1) Any inquiry must hold certain propositions fixed — propositions that are not up for question in that inquiry. Wittgenstein calls these hinge propositions: they are what make the inquiry possible, like "there is an external world" or "my memory is generally reliable."

(2) Hinge propositions cannot be supported by evidence, because any evidence one could offer for them would already presuppose them. You cannot gather evidence that there is an external world without already relying on the existence of an external world to gather it.

(3) If a proposition is not supported by evidence, then believing it is not rationally justified.

(4) So hinge propositions are not rationally justified.

(5) If the foundational commitments of an inquiry are not rationally justified, then the inquiry itself rests on unjustified commitments.

(6) A practice that rests on unjustified commitments is itself irrational.

(7) Therefore, all rational inquiry is ultimately irrational.

This conclusion is absurd, yet I cannot see which premise to reject. I am most reluctant to give up (3), since it seems like a basic principle of epistemic rationality, but I am also reluctant to accept that our most fundamental commitments are irrational. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the way out involves saying that hinge propositions are justified in some non-evidential way, what would that way be, and does it avoid letting in wishful thinking and faith as equally "justified"?
