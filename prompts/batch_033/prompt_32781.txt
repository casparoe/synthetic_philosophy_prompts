I've been collecting a colleague's verdicts on knowledge ascriptions, and I want to predict how she'll rule on a new case. In every case so far, the evidence is the same: the subject has consulted a posted railway schedule, which is almost always right but occasionally wrong, for the proposition that the train leaves at 6:15. Her verdicts:

- A subject with nothing riding on catching the train, checking out of idle curiosity: she says the subject knows the train leaves at 6:15.
- A subject whose job depends on catching the train, with the same schedule-based evidence: she says the subject does not know the train leaves at 6:15.
- A subject who would suffer only a moderate inconvenience from missing the train: she says the subject knows the train leaves at 6:15.

The new case is a third-person ascription. A speaker with nothing at stake, in a relaxed conversation, says of a traveler — whose job depends on catching the train, and who has the same schedule-based evidence — that the traveler knows the train leaves at 6:15. Would my colleague call the speaker's statement true or false? Which rule is she implicitly running: attributor-contextualism, subject-sensitive invariantism (pragmatic encroachment), or some other rule that fits her earlier verdicts equally well? Say what each candidate rule predicts about the new case, give a committed prediction with reasons, and name the one further case that would most cleanly settle which rule she is using if the prediction is disputed. End with a line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for your predicted verdict and rule.
