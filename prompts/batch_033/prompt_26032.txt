A friend and I have been going back and forth on Kavka's toxin puzzle and he laid out an argument that seems to prove way too much, and I can't spot the mistake. Quick refresher on the puzzle: an eccentric billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make you sick for a day. The money depends only on whether you have the intention at midnight — not on whether you actually drink it. So at midnight you have every reason to form the intention, and tomorrow you'll have no reason to carry it out.

Now his argument:

(1) You can rationally intend to do something only if, when the time comes to do it, you'll have sufficient reason to actually do it. Otherwise you're just self-deceived about what you'll do.
(2) In the toxin case, at midnight you know that tomorrow you'll have zero reason to drink the toxin — the money's already decided, and the drink just makes you sick.
(3) So you can't rationally intend at midnight to drink the toxin.
(4) Same logic applies to any situation where the benefit of intending comes before the act, and the act itself is purely costly.
(5) That means it's irrational to intend to: carry out a threat after the other party has already complied, keep a burdensome promise when the other side has already performed and can't punish you, punish defectors in the last round of a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, or even stick to your diet when the cupcake is right there and your past self already got the benefit of having intended.
(6) But we don't think people in those situations are irrational — in fact we often admire them as doing something distinctively right.
(7) So premise (1) must be false, and our whole picture of what makes an intention rational needs rebuilding.

He says the upshot is that rationality can require you to act against your all-things-considered judgment at the time of action, which seems nuts. I want to resist but I can't figure out which step to attack. Tell me where this goes wrong, and if you also think (1) has to go, what you'd replace it with. I want a committed answer — don't just lay out the options, take a stand and defend it.
