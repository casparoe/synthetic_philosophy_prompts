Question: "Parfit's hitchhiker is often taken to show that it can be rational to make yourself irrational. Reconstruct the argument as Parfit gives it, and then evaluate: does the case actually establish that conclusion, or does it show something weaker?"

Student answer:

Parfit asks us to imagine you're stranded in the desert, dying of thirst. A driver comes along and offers you a ride to town, but only if you promise to pay him $100 once you arrive. You promise sincerely. But here's the problem: once you're safely in town, you have no self-interested reason to pay. You've already gotten the ride, and paying $100 just makes you poorer. The driver, knowing this, won't pick you up unless he believes you really will pay. So to get the ride, you need to be the kind of person who keeps promises even when it's not in your immediate self-interest. The conclusion is that rationality requires you to make yourself irrational: you must cultivate a disposition to pay, and then when you arrive in town, you follow through on that disposition—even though the act of paying, considered on its own, is irrational. This is a genuine case of rational irrationality because the irrational act (paying when you don't have to) is required by the rational disposition (being a promise-keeper), which in turn was rationally adopted.

The case is structurally identical to the prisoner's dilemma. In both cases, you do better if you can commit to a cooperative move, but rational self-interest at the moment of choice pushes you toward defection. What Parfit shows is that the rational strategy is to change your own preferences so that you value keeping promises for their own sake. This refutes the standard economic view that rationality is just about satisfying your given preferences. It shows that preferences themselves can be rationally evaluated and that the rational agent will sometimes adopt preferences that lead to actions that, by their original lights, were irrational.

I think the argument is sound but its scope is limited. It only works when you face a one-shot interaction where your reputation isn't at stake. If you're going to be in the same town as the driver, you might pay to maintain your reputation, and then the case collapses into ordinary long-term prudence. So the hitchhiker case only proves anything if we stipulate a genuinely one-off encounter with no reputational effects. Parfit is aware of this and builds it into the example. Still, the conclusion stands: there are cases where you should make yourself irrational, and this tells us something deep about the nature of rationality itself.
