I'm taking a course on formal epistemology this term, and we've been going through the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. I find myself a bit stuck on something, and I was hoping you could help me think through it.

So here's the thing. On the face of it, these two paradoxes look like they're doing basically the same thing. In both cases you've got a set of beliefs each of which seems individually rational to hold, but the conjunction of them is inconsistent — or at least would be, if rational belief were closed under conjunction. In the lottery case, I rationally believe of each ticket that it will lose, but I can't rationally believe that all tickets will lose. In the preface case, I rationally believe each claim in my book, and I also rationally believe that at least one claim is wrong, but the set {p1, p2, ..., pn, ¬(p1 ∧ p2 ∧ ... ∧ pn)} is inconsistent. So both paradoxes seem to put pressure on the idea that we can straightforwardly conjoin our rational beliefs.

But my lecturer keeps insisting that the two paradoxes are importantly different, and that a solution to one might not carry over to the other. I've been trying to work out exactly what the structural difference is, and I'm not fully satisfied with my own attempts. Here's what I've got so far: in the lottery case, the evidence for each belief is purely statistical — for each ticket, the probability of winning is 1/n, and that's all I've got. In the preface case, the evidence for each individual claim is whatever specific evidence supports that claim, and the evidence for the preface statement is inductive (authors make mistakes, I'm an author, etc.). So the lottery case is about statistical evidence generating a sort of arbitrariness problem, whilst the preface case is about the tension between first-order and second-order evidence. But I'm not sure this really captures the difference, or wether it matters for how we'd resolve each paradox.

Could you compare the two paradoxes in some depth? I'm particularly interested in whether there's a genuine structural difference that means we should treat them differently, or whether the lottery paradox is really just a special case of the preface paradox (or vice versa). And if there is a difference, what does it imply about the prospects for a unified theory of rational belief?
