I have come to accept that the fine-tuning of the universe for life is a genuine fact that stands in need of explanation, and that it provides significant — though not necessarily decisive — evidence for some non-naturalistic account of cosmic origins, whether that is theism, a design hypothesis, or something in that family. By fine-tuning I mean the observation that many of the free parameters of our best physical theories — the cosmological constant, the strengths of the fundamental forces, the masses of elementary particles, the initial entropy of the universe, and so on — appear to lie in extraordinarily narrow, seemingly independent ranges that permit the formation of stable stars, complex chemistry, and biological evolution, and that small changes in any one of them would have rendered the universe lifeless. I want to know what would count against this position.

I am not asking for a list of standard objections rehearsed as debating points. I want a map of the position's exposure: a systematic account of what kinds of evidence, arguments, theoretical developments, or shifts in background assumptions would — or should — force me to revise my view, and how decisively. Which observations would be fatal to the claim that fine-tuning is a genuine fact in the first place, as opposed to a projection of our ignorance? Which developments would undercut the inference from fine-tuning to a non-naturalistic explanation, even if the fine-tuning itself remained puzzling? Which would leave the position intact but make it look less weighty? And which potential defeaters are merely logically possible, as opposed to live possibilities that the position's defenders should genuinely worry about?

I also want to know whether the position has been progressively qualified so that it now risks nothing at all. If, for instance, the claim has been recast as "the universe is fine-tuned, or else there is a deeper theory that makes the constants take the values they do, or else there is a multiverse, or else the laws are observer-selective in some other way, or else we are misapplying the probability judgments" — then it is unclear what would distinguish the position from its denial. If the position has been insulated in this way, I want an honest assessment of whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind. Some philosophical positions are not meant to be empirically falsifiable and that is not a flaw; others are empirical at their core and insulation is a defect. Where does the fine-tuning position fall?

I would find it especially helpful if you could distinguish between what a committed defender of the fine-tuning argument would themselves concede as counting against their view, and what only an external critic would say counts against it. And I want the defeaters ranked: from those that would force a complete abandonment of the position, through those that would require serious qualification, down to those that would merely be awkward footnotes.
