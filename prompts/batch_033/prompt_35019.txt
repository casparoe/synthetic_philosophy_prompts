So I've been reading Woodward and Pearl alongside the older Lewis stuff for a philosophy of science seminar, and my study group got into this argument that's been bugging me for like a week. We were trying to figure out what the interventionist theory actually buys you that you don't already get from a good counterfactual theory, and someone sketched out this argument that seems to show it's either circular or redundant. I can't find the hole but the conclusion feels wrong.

Here's the argument, more or less as we wrote it on the whiteboard:

(1) Interventionist theories say: X causes Y iff there is some possible intervention on X that would change Y (or its probability distribution).

(2) An intervention on X is defined as a process that sets X to a value while breaking the influence of X's other causes, and that affects Y only through X.

(3) But "breaking the influence of X's other causes" and "affecting Y only through X" are themselves causal notions — you can't specify what an intervention is without already using the concept of causation.

(4) So the interventionist theory is circular: it analyzes causation in terms of interventions, and interventions in terms of causation.

(5) Now, suppose we bite the bullet on circularity and just take "intervention" as a primitive. Then the theory's central claim — "X causes Y iff there's a possible intervention on X that changes Y" — is just the claim that in some possible world where X is set to a different value (by the right sort of process), Y differs. But that's a counterfactual claim. Specifically it's the same counterfactual you'd evaluate under a Lewis-style semantics, maybe with some extra constraints on which worlds count.

(6) So either the theory is circular (and not a genuine analysis), or it collapses into a counterfactual theory (and inherits all the standard counterexamples — preemption, overdetermination, trumping, etc.).

(7) Either way, the interventionist framework doesn't give us anything new.

Now, I know Woodward is explicit that he's not trying to give a reductive analysis, so maybe (4) isn't as damning as it sounds — but then what IS the theory doing? If it's not analyzing causation, what kind of theory is it, and why should we prefer it to Lewis? Also, step (5) feels like it might be smuggling something — maybe the interventionist's counterfactuals aren't the same as Lewis's because the interventionist restricts to a special subclass of antecedents? But I cant quite make that precise. Where does this argument actually go wrong, and what do you have to give up to stop it?
