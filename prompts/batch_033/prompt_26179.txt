I've been working through David Lewis's modal realism — the view that possible worlds are concrete entities, just as real as the actual world, causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another, with "actual" functioning as an indexical. I find the view genuinely attractive: it delivers a reductive account of modality, properties, propositions, and counterfactuals all at once, and it does so without positing primitive modal facts or unanalyzed modal operators. The theoretical payoff seems enormous.

But I'm trying to figure out how exposed the view actually is, and I don't mean just whether it strikes people as strange. I want to know what would have to be true — what observations, what arguments, what formal results, what consequences the view is committed to — for modal realism to be false, or at least rationally untenable. I'm interested in the full map of its vulnerability: which defeaters would be fatal (the view is incoherent, or it collapses into a rival), which would be deeply damaging but perhaps survivable with revision, and which are merely awkward bullet-biting costs that a committed modal realist can shrug off. I'd also like to know which of these defeaters Lewis himself took seriously and addressed, which ones he dismissed, and whether any of his replies have come to look inadequate in hindsight.

In particular, I want to understand the status of the epistemic objection — that if other worlds are causally isolated from us, we can't have knowledge of them, and so modal realism undermines modal knowledge rather than explaining it. Is that a fatal problem, or does it rest on a demanding conception of knowledge that a modal realist can reject? And I want to know whether counterpart theory is an essential part of the package: if counterpart theory turned out to be untenable for de re modality, could modal realism survive by adopting some form of trans-world identity, or would that break the reductive story? Finally, I'd like an honest assessment of whether modal realism has been insulated by qualifications — whether its defenders have, over time, retreated to a version of the view that risks nothing at all, and if so, whether that's a problem for a metaphysical theory of this kind or just par for the course.
