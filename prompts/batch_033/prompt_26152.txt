"The problem of old evidence, due to Clark Glymour, is that Bayesian confirmation theory seems unable to account for how evidence that we already know can confirm a theory. In standard Bayesianism, E confirms H iff P(H|E) > P(H). But if E is old evidence—meaning we already know it to be true—then P(E) = 1. And if P(E) = 1, then P(H|E) = P(H) by the probability axioms, so no confirmation occurs. This is a problem because in actual science, old evidence often does confirm theories: the classic example is that the anomalous perihelion advance of Mercury was known for decades before Einstein's general relativity, yet it's widely regarded as strong confirming evidence for GR.

One proposed solution is the counterfactual approach, associated with Howson and Urbach. The idea is that we should evaluate confirmation not using our actual credence P(E) = 1, but using what our credence in E would be if we didn't already know it. So we conditionalize on the counterfactual situation where E isn't yet known, and ask whether P(H|E) > P(H) in that scenario. This seems to capture the intuition that Mercury's perihelion really does support GR—it's just that we need to imagine ourselves back before 1915 to see it.

But there's a problem: how do we determine what our credence in E would be if we didn't know it? We can't just forget that we know E. This is the 'problem of logical omniscience' in a different guise. So the counterfactual approach doesn't fully solve the problem.

A better solution is Daniel Garber's. Garber argues that old evidence can confirm a theory when we learn that the theory entails the evidence. On his view, we should assign P(E) < 1 even for old evidence, because we should always allow some probability that our observational judgments are mistaken. When we then learn that H entails E, this new logical knowledge raises P(H) because it shows that H successfully predicts something we weren't entirely certain of. This solves the problem without needing counterfactual credences."
