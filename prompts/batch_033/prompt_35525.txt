I've been teaching metaethics for two decades, and I've watched the debate over the Frege–Geach problem settle into a pattern that I find increasingly frustrating. The problem, as you know, is that if expressivism is right — if moral sentences like "lying is wrong" express conative attitudes rather than beliefs with propositional content — then it's mysterious how such sentences can embed in logically complex contexts. "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong" seems to be a valid conditional, but in the antecedent "lying is wrong" occurs unasserted: the speaker is not expressing disapproval of lying, merely supposing it. So what does the antecedent contribute to the inference? Expressivists have responded with increasingly elaborate constructive programs: Blackburn's commitment-theoretic account of logical validity, Gibbard's fact–plan world semantics, and more recent hybrid and relational approaches. Critics have replied that each proposal either smuggles propositional content in through the back door or fails to secure the right logical properties. After forty-plus years of this, the moves on both sides are thoroughly catalogued, and yet neither side concedes anything.

I want you to diagnose what kind of impasse this is. Here are some hypotheses I've considered, but I'm not settled on any of them:

(a) The disagreement is ultimately verbal: both sides agree on all the facts about how moral language actually works, and the dispute is only about whether the resulting theory deserves the label "expressivist" or "realist."

(b) It's a methodological disagreement about what a semantic theory must deliver: critics demand a compositional semantics that assigns semantic values to moral terms and derives the truth conditions of complex moral sentences, while expressivists think that demand already begs the question by assuming moral sentences have truth conditions in the first place.

(c) It's a substantive disagreement about the data: expressivists and their critics have different pre-theoretic intuitions about which inferences involving moral vocabulary are valid, or about what speakers are doing when they make moral judgments, and there is no neutral way to adjudicate between those starting intuitions.

(d) The question itself is ill-posed, because it presupposes a sharp distinction between cognitive and conative mental states that we have independent reason to reject — and the impasse is a symptom of that deeper mistake.

Which of these, if any, best captures what's going on? Or is there a better diagnosis I haven't considered? And given your diagnosis, what — if anything — would count as progress? A new formal result? A distinction nobody has drawn? An empirical finding about ordinary speakers' inferential practices? Or should we expect the impasse to persist indefinitely, and if so, what does that tell us about the project of metaethics?
