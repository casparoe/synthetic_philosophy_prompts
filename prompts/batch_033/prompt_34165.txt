I have been preparing a unit on free will and moral responsibility for an upper-level course, and I have spent the past several weeks inside the Frankfurt-case literature, which now looks to me like a debate that has been running for over half a century without any real movement. The original strategy is familiar: describe an agent who acts on her own, uncoerced, and whom we would hold responsible; add a counterfactual intervener who would have made her perform the very same action had she shown any sign of doing otherwise; and conclude that she could not have done otherwise, yet is responsible, so the principle of alternative possibilities is false. Incompatibilists have replied that the cases either presuppose determinism, or covertly preserve some residual alternative possibility in the agent's deliberation or intention, or rest on intuitions that are themselves in dispute. Compatibilists have responded with ever more elaborate cases meant to block each proposed alternative possibility. The strange thing is that both sides now agree on the mechanics of every case, each side has a standing reply to every move the other makes, and yet nobody's considered judgment has shifted. A further wrinkle is that many incompatibilists have since conceded that moral responsibility may not require alternative possibilities at all, holding instead that what matters is whether the agent is the ultimate source of her action; if that is right, the cases may be refuting a premise that the most interesting versions of incompatibilism no longer need.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse persists because the participants are not answering the same question. One side seems to be doing the analysis of ability: what "could have done otherwise" means, and which powers or possible worlds it ranges over. Another side seems to be doing the metaphysics of responsibility and sourcehood: what the actual sequence of an agent's mental life must be like for her to deserve praise or blame. If that is correct, the Frankfurt literature would be a case of two research programs passing each other under a single slogan, and the genuine disagreement would be about which question, if either, is prior. But I am not confident; it may instead be a clash of bedrock intuitions about artificial cases that no argument can reach, or a hidden disagreement about what a successful counterexample to a principle must look like, or about how much weight case-intuitions should carry against systematic theoretical commitments.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who wins. Is it verbal, substantive, or a mismatch of questions? When the two sides disagree about a Frankfurt case, are they really disagreeing about the analysis of "can," the conditions of moral responsibility, the dialectical standards for counterexamples, or the evidential weight of intuitions about outlandish cases? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, an agreed taxonomy of senses of "ability," empirical work on folk judgments, or an argument that the question was ill-posed from the start? Please engage the positions as they are actually defended, but do not let this turn into a survey of the literature.
