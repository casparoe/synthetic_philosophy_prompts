I'm a journalist working on a long-read about why some academic debates seem to run forever without resolution, and I've been looking closely at the judgment aggregation literature. The discursive dilemma — the discovery that majority voting on a set of logically interconnected propositions can produce a collectively inconsistent set of judgments even when every individual voter is perfectly consistent — was identified decades ago and formalized rigorously in the years since. We now have a family of impossibility theorems, a taxonomy of escape routes (premise-based voting, conclusion-based voting, distance-based methods, relaxing independence conditions), and a mature formal apparatus. And yet the central philosophical question seems as unsettled as ever.

Here is how the impasse looks to me. On one side are those who take the discursive dilemma to reveal something deep about the very possibility of group rationality: that there is an irreducible tension between respecting the judgments of individual members and ensuring that the group speaks with a single, logically coherent voice. On this view, the impossibility results are not just technical curiosities — they show that group agency is, in a precise sense, a fraught notion, and that any attempt to treat a collective as a rational agent in its own right will run into structural problems. On the other side are those who see the whole thing as a problem of institutional engineering: different contexts call for different aggregation rules, the impossibility results only bite under conditions that no real-world institution needs to satisfy, and talk of "group rationality" is either metaphorical or reducible to the rationality of the procedures we design.

Both sides are looking at the same theorems. Both sides agree on the formal facts. So what kind of disagreement is this? Is it a verbal dispute about what it means to call a group "rational"? Is it a normative disagreement about which features of individual rationality a collective decision procedure must preserve? Is it a disagreement about the domain of the problem — one side doing metaphysics of social ontology, the other doing normative institutional design — such that they are not really contradicting each other at all? Or is there a hidden empirical bet about how actual institutions function that neither side has made explicit?

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, supported by what each side actually argues, and an honest answer about what, if anything, would move it. If the impasse is genuine, say why and what that tells us about the limits of formal work in social philosophy. If it is illusory, say what distinction would dissolve it and let both sides recognize they were never really in conflict.
