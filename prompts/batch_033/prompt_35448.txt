I've been thinking about the Ship of Theseus — the puzzle where a ship has every plank replaced one by one over the years, and you're supposed to say whether it's still the same ship. I keep noticing people reach for this as an analogy when they talk about countries, and I want to know how much weight it can bear.

The argument I hear goes like this. Over two centuries, a country replaces its entire population, rewrites its laws, reshapes its institutions, and sometimes even shifts its borders. But we still call it the same country. So — just as the ship remains the Ship of Theseus — a nation's identity isn't tied to any particular set of people, laws, or borders. And from this people conclude that worrying about "the real France" or "the real America" is a mistake, or that constitutional change can't threaten national identity, or that preserving a national character is a confused project.

I want an audit of this analogy. What corresponds to what? Are the citizens the planks, the constitution the keel, the name the figurehead? Where does the mapping break? The ship changes plank by plank under a single continuous process; a country can undergo revolution, secession, occupation — changes that are sudden, contested, and not obviously the same process at all. The ship's identity is a puzzle about physical objects; a country's identity matters to the people inside it, who are themselves the parts being replaced. The ship doesn't have a view about whether it's still the same ship; the country's inhabitants do, and their views partly constitute the answer. And in the original puzzle, the old planks can be reassembled into a second ship that also claims to be the original — but what would the analogue of that even be for a country? A government in exile? A restored ancien régime?

Do these disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support? If the mapping is weaker than it looks, does the conclusion — that we shouldn't worry about preserving any particular national features — survive, or was the analogy carrying it all along?
