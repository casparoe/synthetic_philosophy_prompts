So I'm trying to work through McTaggart's "The Unreality of Time" and I'm getting stuck on how one part of the argument is supposed to land. Here's the passage I've been staring at (this is from the 1908 paper):


"Since distinctions of the first class are permanent, they might be held to be more objective, and to be more essential to the nature of time. I believe, however, that this would be a mistake, and that the distinction of past, present and future is as essential to time as the distinction of earlier and later, while in a certain sense, as we shall see, it may be regarded as more fundamental than the distinction of earlier and later. And it is because the distinctions of past, present and future seem to me to be essential for time, that I regard time as unreal."


The bit that keeps tripping me up is that last move. I can follow the broad shape of the paper: he gives us the A-series (past/present/future) and the B-series (earlier/later), says time needs both, then argues that the A-series generates a contradiction because every event has to be past, present, and future but those are incompatible determinations. The obvious response is "well it's not all three *at the same time*," and McTaggart says that move just pushes the problem back and creates either a vicious circle or an infinite regress.

Fine, but this passage comes before all that. He's already signalling that the conclusion—time is unreal—follows specifically from the A-series being *essential* to time. What I can't get clear on is whether he's actually entitled to that conditional. Is he making the flat claim "if the A-series is essential to time, then time is unreal" (in which case the later contradiction argument is the proof of that conditional), or is he saying something more ?nuanced—like, the A-series is *more fundamental* than the B-series, and *that* additional thesis is doing work in the contradiction that I'm missing? Basically, why wouldn't a B-theorist just look at this passage and say, "Fine, I reject the antecedent—the A-series isn't essential, the B-series alone is enough for time, problem dissolved"? Is there something in the text that's supposed to block that move before the contradiction argument even gets off the ground, or is the whole weight of the paper really resting on the regress argument later on?

If you could walk me through what exactly "more fundamental" is doing here—and whether McTaggart ever actually justifies that, or just asserts it—I'd appreciate it. Keep it under 500 words if you can, I'm trying to get a tighter handle on it.
