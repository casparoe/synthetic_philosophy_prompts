I run a small science-and-ideas blog and I want to put together one of those "which theory do you actually hold?" quizzes for my readers — this one on costly signaling and the handicap principle. The idea is to sort people by what they think about concrete cases, not by which label sounds good.

The positions I need in the mix: (1) the strong Zahavi view — signals are honest *because* they're costly, and that's the main story across the animal kingdom; (2) the Grafen strategic equilibrium view — costs enforce a separating equilibrium but they have to be paid in the same currency as the trait being advertised; (3) the index signal view — many signals are honest because they're physically or physiologically tied to the trait, no strategic cost required; (4) the common-interest view — when senders and receivers share goals, signals can be cheap or free and still honest; (5) the sensory exploitation view — signals evolve to hijack preexisting receiver biases and honesty is at best a side effect. If there are logically possible positions nobody defends (e.g., "all signals are costly handicaps including honeybee dances"), note them and say why they're empty.

I want seven or eight questions. Each should be a concrete case — peacock tails, gazelle stotting, honeybee waggle dances, nestling begging, frog calls, human luxury goods, and at least one case where the signal might actually be dishonest or manipulative — with three answer options that pull apart the different theoretical commitments. No question should just ask "do signals need to be costly?"

Include a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions, and flag honestly which positions your quiz can't cleanly separate (I suspect Grafen vs. Zahavi might blur on some cases). Write it so someone who's never heard of Amotz Zahavi can take it and come out with a result that actually reflects their intutions about how signaling works.
