I've been reading about split-brain patients for a school project on consciousness, and I've gotten stuck on a philosophical debate that seems to have been going in circles for decades. The experimental facts are well established: when the corpus callosum is severed, the two hemispheres can be presented with different stimuli and respond independently — the left hand might pick up an object the right hand denies seeing, or a patient might verbally report seeing nothing while their left hand draws what was shown to the right hemisphere. But when philosophers ask how many conscious subjects a split-brain patient is, they disagree radically. Some, like Thomas Nagel, argue that there is no whole-number answer — that the unity of consciousness can break down into something that isn't exactly one mind or two. Others, like Derek Parfit, treat the split-brain case as evidence that personal identity and conscious unity are matters of degree, not all-or-nothing. Still others insist that the patient is a single conscious subject whose access to information is merely fragmented, or that there are two distinct streams of consciousness. The experimental data haven't changed dramatically since the 1960s and 70s, yet the philosophical disagreement persists.

My question is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive disagreement about the nature of consciousness that could in principle be settled by better evidence or a sharper concept, or is it a verbal dispute about what we mean by "a conscious subject"? Are the different sides operating with different criteria for what counts as one mind, and if so, does that make the question ill-posed? My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is really about whether "conscious subject" is a natural-kind term that carves nature at its joints, or a pragmatic label we apply based on functional integration — and that the two sides are talking past each other because they never make this background assumption explicit. But I'm not sure if that's right, and I'd appreciate a diagnosis that engages with what each side actually says.

I'd also like to know what, if anything, would count as progress — a new experiment, a conceptual distinction everyone could accept, or something else. Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure about.
