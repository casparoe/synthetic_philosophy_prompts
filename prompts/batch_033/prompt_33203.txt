I've been teaching Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" alongside Camus's The Stranger this semester, and I keep going back and forth on what the novel actually shows about the reactive attitudes. On one reading, Meursault is someone who fails to exhibit the normal reactive attitudes — he doesn't feel grief, remorse, or indignation — and the novel's trial scene shows society insisting on treating him as a participant in the moral community even when he seems to have opted out of it. That would be a kind of vindication of Strawson: the participant stance is not something we can simply set aside, and the court's outrage at Meursault's failure to feel the right things is itself a reactive attitude that proves Strawson's point about their inescapability. On another reading, though, Meursault's emotional flatness is closer to what Strawson calls the objective stance — he views his own life, and even his own crime, with a kind of detached indifference that makes him more like a psychological case than a moral agent. And if Meursault is genuinely incapable of the participant reactive attitudes, then Strawson's framework would say that he is not a proper target of them either — which makes the court's condemnation of him a kind of moral mistake, or at least a category error. But then the novel's ending complicates both readings: Meursault's explosive outburst at the chaplain, where he finally feels something like defiant affirmation, might be his first real entry into the participant stance, right at the moment he is about to be executed for failing to occupy it.
