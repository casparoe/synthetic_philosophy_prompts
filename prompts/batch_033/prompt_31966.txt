I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a paper on the points of contact between pragmatist theories of truth and the metaphysics of fiction, and I want to center the paper on a close reading of Jorge Luis Borges's "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" through the lens of the Peirce–James divergence on truth.

The broad thought is that the story literalizes something distinctive about pragmatist truth, but I'm unsure whether it does so as vindication or as warning. On Tlön, as Borges describes it, a radical Berkeleyan idealism is taken as common sense: the world has no substance independent of perception, and the languages of its hemispheres lack nouns entirely, treating reality as a flux of mental events and adjectival qualities. More strikingly, the Tlönian concept of the hrönir describes objects that duplicate and elaborate themselves through expectation and desire — the more insistently a lost object is sought, the more detailed the duplicate that turns up in its place; thought exerts a kind of ontological pressure. By the story's end, Tlön's imaginary encyclopedia has begun to supplant our own world's categories — its artifacts materialize, its history is taught in schools, its metaphysics quietly colonizes ordinary thought. The narrator watches this with a mixture of fascination and quiet horror.

What interests me philosophically is that the story seems to stage a collision between a Peircean picture of truth and a Jamesian one, and I can't tell where Borges lands. On a broadly Peircean reading, truth is what the community of inquiry is fated to converge upon in the long run — truth is essentially public, tied to the method of science and to what would be believed at the ideal limit of investigation, not to any individual's expedient belief. One could read the spread of Tlön as a dark parody of this: a global community does in fact settle into a new consensus, and reality reorganizes itself to confirm that consensus, but the mechanism is not inquiry — it's repetition, enchantment, the sheer accrual of descriptive detail in a fabricated encyclopedia. On a Jamesian reading, by contrast, truth is made in the course of experience: an idea becomes true by proving itself useful, by having practical cash value, by working for the believer. And Tlönian metaphysics does work for its adopters: it reshapes the world until the world fits. The hrönir would be the Jamesian account of truth rendered visible, truth as a process of making rather than a terminus of convergence.

Yet the story is plainly dystopian. The narrator's final confession — that he continues his solitary translation of an English work into a Tlönian language, while the world around him slides into Tlön's categories — reads less like complicity than despair. So here is what I want to work through: is Borges indicting pragmatist truth as such, or only a certain version of it, or does the story expose a vulnerability internal to pragmatism that philosophical argument alone misses? In particular, does Tlön dramatize the worry that if truth just is what the community converges on, and if convergence is something that can be engineered — by encyclopedias, by institutions, by fictions repeated until they become infrastructure — then pragmatism contains no resources to distinguish genuine inquiry from mass self-deception? And is the Jamesian picture, with its more individualist and voluntarist account of truth-making, more or less vulnerable to this dystopian reading than Peirce's communitarian one? I could also imagine the story cutting the other way: that Borges shows us something Peirce gets right that James does not, precisely because the horror of Tlön's takeover is the horror of a convergence that bypasses genuine inquiry, and the Peircean framework has the conceptual tools to say why that isn't truth at all.

I'd like a reading that takes the story's details seriously — the hrönir and their degrees of elaboration, the language without nouns, the encyclopedia as a technology of world-making, the narrator's own seduction by Tlön's elegance — and uses them to pressure the Peirce–James distinction, rather than simply mapping story elements onto philosophical positions. I'm also interested in where the story resists the pragmatist lens: are there features of Borges's metaphysics that pragmatism cannot accommodate, and that therefore mark a genuine limit of the reading? The paper needs to be faithful in both directions — it shouldn't reduce the story to an illustration of a philosophical quarrel, and it shouldn't distort the philosophy to make it fit the fiction.
