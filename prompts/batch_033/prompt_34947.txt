I'm working on a dissertation chapter about Augustine's absolutism about lying and I keep hitting the same wall. The standard reading is that Augustine thinks lying is always wrong, no exceptions, not even to save someone's life. But I've started to suspect that his definition of a lie — roughly, speaking contrary to what you hold in mind with the intent to deceive — does a lot more work than commentators usually notice, and not in a way that helps the absolutist reading. If the definition only covers intentional false assertion, then a whole range of deceptive-but-not-false speech seems to fall outside the prohibition: equivocation, misleading implicature, saying something technically true but calculated to deceive, half-truths where the false part is what you leave out rather than what you say. Augustine doesn't really discuss these cases systematically, and I'm not sure his framework has the resources to handle them. So my half-formed idea is: maybe Augustine's absolutism is narrower than it looks, and the interesting question is whether it's too narrow — whether his definition of lying creates a gap that any plausible account of truthfulness needs to close, and whether later attempts to patch the gap (mental reservation in the casuist tradition, say) are basically trying to fix a problem Augustine built into the definition.
