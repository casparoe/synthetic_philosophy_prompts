I want to practise attacking van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I need a proper sparring partner. Please play a committed defender of Reflection — someone who thinks it's a genuine synchronic requirement of rationality, not just a pragmatic rule of thumb. You should be willing to conceed small dialectical points but hold the line that the principle itself survives scrutiny and that apparent counterexamples rest on a misunderstanding. Keep your turns to roughly a paragraph. Here's my opening:

Consider an agent who knows she's about to undergo a procedure that will selectively erase her memory of a particular piece of evidence. Before the procedure, her credence in some proposition P is 0.9, based on the evidence she's about to lose. She knows that after the procedure, her credence will drop to 0.5. Reflection says her current credence, conditional on her future credence being 0.5, should be 0.5. But she knows her future credence will be 0.5 — the procedure is deterministic — so her unconditional current credence should also be 0.5, which contradicts her actual credence of 0.9. She clearly shouldn't lower her credence just because she's about to forget evidence. So isn't Reflection demanding something irrational here?
