The dagger soliloquy in Macbeth (Act 2, Scene 1) is often invoked in debates over the cognitive penetration of perception — the thesis that cognitive states such as beliefs, expectations, fears, and desires can directly shape perceptual experience, rather than only shaping the judgments one makes about what one sees. But the scene is usually waved at rather than worked through, and I would like a reading that works through it from both the literary and the philosophical side. On his way to murder Duncan, Macbeth has a vivid visual experience of a dagger floating before him; he tries to grasp it and his hand closes on nothing; he draws his actual dagger to compare; and the apparition then seems to become covered in blood where a moment before it was clean, a change he attributes to the deed he is about to do shaping what his eyes present to him.

I am preparing to teach the scene alongside the cognitive penetration literature and want to know how honestly the pairing holds up. Specifically: (1) Set out the distinctions the debate needs — cognitive penetration proper as against post-perceptual judgment or interpretation, and veridical perception as against illusion and hallucination — and locate the scene within them. (2) Take seriously the objection that the dagger cannot be a case of cognitive penetration at all, because there is no dagger: the experience is a hallucination, not a perception of a real object, so there is no perceptual state there to be penetrated. Is that objection sound, or does it draw the line between penetrating a percept and generating one in a way the scene puts pressure on? (3) The play stages something like an internal experiment: Macbeth tests sight against touch and against the real dagger in his hand, and the content of the vision alters as his anticipation of the killing sharpens. What does this dramatize about whether a person could tell from the inside that their perception is cognitively shaped — and what does it suggest that way of drawing the distinction misses? (4) End by saying what the scene adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not. Most of the literature runs on belief- and expectation-driven cases; Macbeth's is driven by intention and desire, and the dagger does motivational work, making the contemplated act present to the eye before it is performed. Does the play show something about that motivational role that the standard examples flatten out? And where does the scene resist the philosophical reading, such that an honest critic has to say so?
