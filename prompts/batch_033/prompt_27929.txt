I'm preparing a graduate seminar on theories of truth and the Liar paradox. I want to present the major approaches in order of how convincingly they handle the strengthened Liar — the sentence that says of itself that it is not true — without generating a revenge paradox. (A revenge paradox is a new paradoxical sentence formulable within the approach's own machinery, showing the original Liar hasn't been fully tamed.)

The six approaches I'm covering:

1. Tarski's hierarchy: truth predicates come in levels; a sentence at level n can only be called true or false by a predicate at level n+1.
2. Kripke's fixed-point construction: sentences can be neither true nor false if they are ungrounded; the truth predicate is partially defined.
3. The dialetheist approach: the Liar is both true and false; some contradictions are true and must be accepted.
4. Revision theory: truth is a circular concept whose extension is defined by a revision rule; the Liar oscillates rather than settling.
5. Contextualism about truth: the extension of the truth predicate shifts with context, so the Liar gets different evaluations depending on the context of utterance.
6. Deflationism: truth is not a substantive property; the Liar reveals a logical problem with the truth predicate's syntax, not a deep metaphysical puzzle.

Please rank these six from best to worst on the single criterion of resisting revenge paradoxes. For each, give a score from 0 to 10 (10 = no revenge paradox arises) and explain in a paragraph what the revenge problem looks like for that approach and how well it holds up. Then, in a closing section, identify which structural feature of the top-ranked approach does the real work, and say whether any lower-ranked approach could be modified to incorporate that feature without simply collapsing into the top-ranked approach.

Structure your response as:

- Rankings at a glance (a short table: approach, score out of 10, and a one-sentence justification)
- Detailed evaluations (one paragraph per approach, in ranked order from best to worst)
- The decisive feature (a paragraph on what separates the winner from the rest, and whether the gap can be closed by modifying a runner-up)
