I've been turning over a question about psychiatric labels for a while now, and I can't find a principle that squares with all my gut reactions. I'd like you to work through it with me. Please take the role of a careful philosopher of psychiatry -- think through the cases step by step, brainstorm alternative principles before committing, and don't settle on the first one that fits most of the data. I want the best overall account, even if it means overturning some of my verdicts.

Here are the cases and what I think about each:

1. A college student can't focus, loses things constantly, and feels like a failure. A therapist diagnoses ADHD. The student learns about executive function, gets medication and accommodations, and starts to understand their struggles as a wiring issue rather than a character flaw. They finish their degree and feel more in control of their life than before. Verdict: the diagnosis clearly helped.

2. A woman in her early twenties is moody, impulsive, and has turbulent relationships. A psychiatrist diagnoses borderline personality disorder. Before the diagnosis, she thought of herself as going through a rough patch and expected to grow out of it. Afterward, every emotional swing becomes "a BPD episode," every fight becomes "splitting," and she starts reading about how people with BPD are manipulative and treatment-resistant. She withdraws from friends, her sense of herself as a person worth knowing collapses, and she gets worse. Verdict: the diagnosis clearly harmed her.

3. A man has panic attacks. His doctor says it's panic disorder. The label is a relief at first -- it has a name, it's a real thing, it's treatable. But over the next year, he starts monitoring his body for any sign of arousal, and sensations he used to ignore now trigger full attacks because he interprets them as "the panic disorder acting up." His panic attacks become more frequent than before the diagnosis. Verdict: the diagnosis helped and then hurt; the net effect feels negative, but I'm not fully confident.

4. A teenager gets in trouble at school -- arguing with teachers, breaking small rules, refusing to comply. A psychologist diagnoses oppositional defiant disorder. The school starts treating him as a disciplinary case with a clinical label, and the kid, overhearing adults talk about his "ODD," starts acting the part, almost proud of it. He gets suspended repeatedly. His mother later says the label was the worst thing that happened to him. Verdict: the diagnosis clearly harmed him.

5. A middle-aged man has been depressed for years without knowing it -- he just thought life felt heavy for everyone. A diagnosis of major depressive disorder makes him cry with relief. He does therapy, takes an antidepressant, and recovers. Years later, he says the depression was something he had, not something he was, and the diagnosis was essential to making that distinction. Verdict: the diagnosis clearly helped.

6. Two friends have similar symptoms -- periods of high energy and grandiosity alternating with crashes. One is diagnosed bipolar II and starts mood stabilizers; she stabilizes and builds a career. The other is diagnosed with complex PTSD from childhood abuse; she does trauma therapy and also stabilizes, though more slowly. Both credit the diagnosis with saving them, but they needed different ones. Verdict: the right diagnosis helped each of them; a swapped diagnosis would have harmed both.

7. A man in his forties is diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder. He had always known he was different but had no framework for it. The diagnosis lets him stop trying to force himself to be neurotypical, find community, and reorient his life around his actual strengths and needs. Verdict: the diagnosis clearly helped.

8. A woman in her thirties goes to therapy for grief after a miscarriage. The therapist diagnoses "adjustment disorder." The woman feels that her grief has been pathologized -- turned into a clinical condition with a time limit -- and she becomes angry and mistrustful of therapy. She stops going and doesn't seek help when she later develops what looks like actual depression. Verdict: the diagnosis clearly harmed her.

What I'm trying to figure out is whether there's a principle that explains which diagnoses help and which harm, and why. I can feel a pattern but I can't state it. It seems to have something to do with whether the label gives the person a story that makes sense of their experience without taking it over, whether it separates the problem from the self or fuses them together, and whether the label carries a social meaning that the person can resist or that steamrolls them. But these are vague gestures, not a principle.

If you can find a principle that captures all eight verdicts, I want to hear it. If not, tell me which verdicts I should reconsider, and why the principle that saves the rest is worth the sacrifice. Also, please apply your principle to a few cases I haven't thought of: what would it say about a diagnosis of "prolonged grief disorder" (a proposed diagnosis for grief that lasts beyond what clinicians consider normal), about a teenager who proudly adopts a Tourette's diagnosis as an identity, and about someone who is misdiagnosed with schizophrenia but the misdiagnosis is caught and corrected within a month?
