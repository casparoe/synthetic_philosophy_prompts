I serve on the Intergenerational Equity Working Group, a standing advisory body to our national infrastructure planning agency. We have been asked to recommend a single social discount rate to govern cost–benefit analysis for all public projects with expected impacts beyond fifty years — flood defences, nuclear waste storage, rail corridors, reforestation programmes. The working group is split, and the chair has asked each member to circulate a short written opinion ahead of our vote next month.

The division is essentially philosophical. Several members, mostly from economics backgrounds, argue that a positive pure rate of time preference is descriptively accurate — people do discount future utility — and that democratic policymaking should respect revealed preferences. They favour a discount rate in the range of 3–4%, which would make benefits beyond a century essentially weightless in present-value terms. Another faction, drawing on the argument that pure time preference is ethically indefensible, insists the pure rate should be zero or near-zero, with discounting driven only by expected growth in consumption (if we assume future generations will be richer) and by existential risk. They point out that any positive pure rate implies that a life saved a hundred years from now is worth less than a life saved today, which they regard as straightforwardly objectionable. A third, smaller group wants us to sidestep the philosophical question entirely by adopting a declining discount rate schedule, but others have pushed back that this merely relocates the problem rather than solving it.

Please draft the written opinion I should circulate. You are an expert in discounting theory and intergenerational ethics who has advised similar bodies before. Before writing, take a moment to think through the strongest version of each side's case and what a defensible compromise might look like. The opinion should recommend a concrete discount rate (or a range, or a multi-tier approach), give the reasoning, and engage seriously with the strongest arguments on the losing side rather than dismissing them. The audience is not philosophers — it is engineers, economists, and civil servants who need to implement whatever we decide. The opinion should be actionable: someone reading it should know what number to plug into a spreadsheet and why.
