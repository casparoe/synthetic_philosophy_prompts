I've been going down a rabbit hole on the epistemology of markets — the idea that prices aggregate dispersed information, that trading reveals private knowledge, and that the market is in some sense a distributed inference engine. In the course of this, I came across a Substack post that summarizes the no-trade theorems in a way I found simultaneously clarifying and suspiciously sweeping. The author writes something like (I'm reconstructing from memory, but I've kept the structure):

"The no-trade theorem, due to Milgrom and Stokey, shows that purely informational differences can never generate trade among rational agents. The reasoning is beautifully simple: if I know you're rational and I see you offering to sell me a risky asset at some price, I should infer that you have private information suggesting the asset is worth less than that price. But then I update my own valuation downward, and the trade collapses. The same logic applies symmetrically if I try to sell to you. The upshot is that the only trade we should ever observe in a rational market comes from non-informational motives — hedging, liquidity shocks, differences in risk tolerance, or straightforward irrationality. It's a formalization of the intuition that you can't make money trading against someone who knows as much as you do and thinks as clearly as you do; the market is a mechanism for punishing epistemic arrogance. A corollary is that speculative bubbles shouldn't exist under rationality, because a bubble requires a buyer who believes the asset is overvalued but expects to flip it to a greater fool — and the no-trade theorem says that kind of reasoning can't get off the ground if everyone is rational and everyone knows that everyone else is rational."

I've been around the rationalist blogosphere long enough to recognize this as a cousin of both the efficient market hypothesis and Aumann's agreement theorem, and I've seen it deployed to argue that active investing is a fool's errand and that prediction markets are close to epistemically optimal. But I've also heard that the actual theorems require a fairly demanding set of conditions — common priors, common knowledge of rationality, and, crucially, that the initial allocation is already Pareto efficient — and that the results are narrower than the way they get used in blogospheric arguments. I'd like someone to go through this summary and tell me: which parts would a theorist who works on these models sign off on, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and whether the jump from the formal theorem to the claim that speculative bubbles shouldn't exist under rationality is actually licensed by the math or requires additional premises the blog post is quietly smuggling in.
