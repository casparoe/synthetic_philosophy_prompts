In recent years, bioethicists have debated Julian Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence, which holds that prospective parents, when choosing among possible future children they could bring into existence, have a moral reason—perhaps even an obligation—to select the child with the best expected life prospects, insofar as such information is available. Defenders of the principle are careful to distinguish it from the coercive, state-directed eugenics programs of the twentieth century: procreative beneficence operates through individual choice, not government mandate, and concerns the selection of which possible person to bring about rather than the elimination or sterilization of existing people. Yet I find myself unable to locate a stable conceptual boundary here. If we grant that some possible lives are, from the standpoint of prospective parents, better candidates for being brought into existence on genetic grounds, does this not entail a judgment that certain genetic profiles are simply less valuable—less worth living—than others? And once that judgment is accepted as a legitimate basis for reproductive decision-making, what stops it from filtering into social attitudes, institutional policies, and eventually the kind of systematic discrimination against the genetically disadvantaged that liberal societies have spent decades trying to dismantle? I am not asking whether Savulescu intends this outcome; I am asking whether the logic of his principle, once set in motion, can be contained.
