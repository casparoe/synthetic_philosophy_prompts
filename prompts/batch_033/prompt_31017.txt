I am preparing a unit on instrumental rationality and want to make sure I frame Kavka's toxin puzzle correctly before presenting it to my students. In the case as I understand it, an agent is offered a very large sum of money if, at midnight tonight, she genuinely intends to drink a harmless but nauseating toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money will be paid before she has to drink, and she knows this, so she also knows that by tomorrow she will have the reward in hand and no reason to undergo the discomfort.

My working gloss has been that the puzzle is fundamentally a problem for expected utility theory. Forming the intention tonight seems to have positive expected utility, since it is the necessary condition for receiving the money and costs nothing in itself, while carrying out the intention tomorrow has negative expected utility, since it produces only discomfort. That looks like a single normative theory issuing contradictory prescriptions about one and the same agent. I have therefore been tempted to tell my students that the puzzle's upshot is either that expected utility theory must be abandoned as a general account of practical rationality, or that rationality can require a kind of local self-deception—getting oneself to believe, however briefly, that one will drink so that the intention can honestly be formed. I would be grateful for comments on whether this framing is sound. In particular, does the toxin puzzle really function as a counterexample to expected utility theory, or is its lesson better located in the norms governing intention itself—and if the latter, what exactly is that lesson?
