I train police detectives in witness interviewing, and I keep running into the same problem. We teach them all the standard stuff about not asking leading questions, but the deeper issue — the one the training manuals never quite get to — is that what a witness percieves in the first place might already be shaped by what they believe or expect. I was talking to a friend in a philosophy department and she mentioned this idea of "cognitive penetration of perception" — that your backround beliefs, your concepts, even your moods can literally alter what you see, not just what you infer from what you see. And that got me worried, because if that's right, then by the time a witness sits down with one of my detectives, their perceptual memory isn't some pristine recording — it's already been through several rounds of top-down reshaping, and the interview itself could add more.

So here's what I'd like. I want a practical procedure — a checklist or a decision tree — that a detective can actually use when interviewing an eyewitness, designed to minimize the effects of cognitive penetration at every stage: before the interview starts, during the initial free recall, and during the follow-up questioning. I want it structured as questions to ask yourself in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and points where the procedure says "stop, don't go further" or "hand this to a supervisor." The procedure should be something you can run in thirty to forty minutes with a scared witness who just saw something terrible, not a lab protocol. It should flag the points where the science is genuinely unsettled and the detective just has to make a judgment call.

Then walk me through the procedure on two scenarios: one where the witness is a civilian who saw a shooting, and one where the witness is a fellow officer who was on scene for an arrest that went wrong. I want to see where the procedure gives different guidance for the two cases — because the cognitive penetration worries might be very different for an officer who has years of threat-detection training and a whole set of professional expectations about how these situations unfold.

Also, please be honest about where the cognitive penetration hypothesis itself is contested among philosophers and psychologists. I don't want a procedure that assumes the strongest version of the thesis is settled fact if reasonable people in the field disagree about whether perception is actually penetrable in the relevant way, or about which kinds of cognition can penetrate which kinds of perception. If some steps in the procedure only make sense on one side of that debate, mark them as such.
