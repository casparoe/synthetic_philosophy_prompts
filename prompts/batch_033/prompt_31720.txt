I've been running a reading group on decision-theoretic paradoxes, and last week we spent three hours on the two-envelope problem. I left more confused than when I arrived, and I'm hoping you can help me pinpoint where the reasoning goes off the rails.

Here's the setup. You're shown two sealed envelopes, identical in appearance. You're told that one contains twice as much money as the other, but you're not told which is which. You pick one envelope, and before you open it, you're offered the chance to switch. The standard argument for switching runs: let the amount in your chosen envelope be X. The other envelope contains either X/2 or 2X, each with probability 1/2. The expected value of switching is (1/2)(X/2) + (1/2)(2X) = 5X/4, which exceeds X. So you should switch. But notice that this argument didn't require you to open your envelope — it tells you to switch even before looking. Worse, once you've switched, the same reasoning applied to your new envelope tells you to switch back, and so on forever. The two envelopes are symmetric before opening, so a line of reasoning that demands an endless cycle of switching seems to have gone wrong somewhere.

We reconstructed the argument as follows: (1) For any possible amount X in your envelope, the conditional probability that the other envelope contains 2X rather than X/2 is 1/2. (2) If (1), then the expected value of switching conditional on X is 5X/4. (3) If the expected value of switching conditional on X exceeds X for every possible X, then you should switch regardless of what you observe — including when you observe nothing. (4) Therefore, you should always switch. (5) But the situation before opening is symmetric: by parallel reasoning, you should also switch back, and so on ad infinitum. (6) A rational choice procedure cannot both require endless switching and respect the symmetry of the setup. (7) Therefore, one of the premises must be false.

We couldn't agree on which premise to reject. Some said (1) is the culprit — that the probability isn't 1/2 for every X, because that would require an impossible prior distribution over the possible envelope amounts. Others said (3) is too strong, because expected-value reasoning breaks down when the relevant expectations are not well-defined. A third group argued that the whole framing is wrong and the real issue is that "X" doesn't refer to a fixed quantity across the two branches of the expected-value calculation. I'm not asking you to solve the two-envelope paradox in full — I know there's a literature. What I want is a diagnosis of this specific argument. Tell me which premise or step you would reject, explain why it seemed plausible, and spell out what someone who rejects it has to give up elsewhere. If you think the argument is valid and the real cost is accepting some surprising consequence, say that instead — but then tell me what that consequence is and whether it's really so bad.
