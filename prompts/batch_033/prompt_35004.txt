I was recently promoted to lead an internal investigations unit — we examine allegations of misconduct inside a large organization — and I have been trying to build a shared framework for how my team evaluates evidence. Over several months of reviewing cases, I have noticed a recurring confusion in how we talk about what grounds a belief, and I do not have the vocabulary to diagnose it properly.

It seems to me that we rely on four fundamentally different kinds of warrant for a belief, and that the differences among them matter for how we weigh claims, how we challenge one another's conclusions, and where our reasoning is most vulnerable. But our training materials and procedures recognize only two categories — "direct evidence" and "circumstantial evidence" — and that coarse division is causing mistakes.

The four kinds I keep observing:

1. What an investigator perceives firsthand: they were in the room, they witnessed the exchange, they held the document and read it themselves.

2. What an investigator derives by reasoning from facts already established: connecting separate pieces of information, drawing an inference, eliminating alternative explanations by logic.

3. What an investigator accepts on the word of a person who is in a position to know: a whistleblower's account, a supervisor's description of events they oversaw, a colleague's report of a conversation they participated in.

4. What an investigator recognizes because one situation is relevantly like another: "this follows the same pattern as the Henderson matter," "this email has the structure of the ones we saw during the 2019 cover-up."

In practice, my team uses all four constantly. But our procedures keep trying to collapse kind 3 into kind 2: they say testimony is only as good as your inductive evidence that the source is reliable, so it is just a special case of inference from known facts. I can see the philosophical motivation for that reduction, but it does not match how we actually work — we routinely treat a credible first-person report as grounding belief in a way that is not merely a shortcut for running a reliability calculation, and forcing the reduction leads my people to dress up their reliance on testimony in inferential language that obscures what they are actually doing. Kind 4 creates a different problem: case-based recognition gets dismissed as "just intuition" or "pattern-matching" and is treated as subjective, even though my strongest investigators depend on it, and when I press them they can often articulate the relevant dimensions of similarity — but the recognition reliably arrives before the articulation does.

I am trying to determine whether there is an established philosophical framework that treats these as genuinely distinct and irreducible ways of knowing — not merely different sources of propositions that all get evaluated by the same downstream logic. I have a vague recollection that Indian epistemology draws a classification along these lines, but I do not know the terms, whether the mapping is exact, or whether any Western tradition makes a comparable distinction.

Specifically, I would like to know: (a) is there a named view, in any philosophical tradition, that carves epistemic warrant roughly this way; (b) if so, what are the kinds called and how are they defined; (c) where does my fourfold distinction align with the canonical version and where does it diverge; and (d) if no such view exists, what should I call these four kinds and what are the boundary disputes I should expect when I introduce the terminology to my team? I am not satisfied with a bare label; I need the carving — what each kind is, what it is not, and where the edges are.

- "named_view": the name of the view or framework, or null if none fits.
- "tradition": the philosophical tradition it belongs to, or null.
- "canonical_kinds": an array of objects, each with "name" (the technical term), "translation" (a short English gloss), and "maps_to_my_kind" (which of my four kinds, 1–4, it corresponds to, or "none" if a canonical kind has no counterpart in my list).
- "fit_quality": one of "exact", "close", "partial", or "poor".
- "mismatches": an array of strings describing where my fourfold distinction diverges from the canonical version.
