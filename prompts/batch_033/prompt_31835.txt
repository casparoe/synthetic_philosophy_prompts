I run a reading group that's been working through the free will literature, and we're about to hit Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment." The group is about a dozen people — mostly grad students and a few advanced undergrads, a mix of philosophy and STEM — and we have a two-hour session. I've been reading rationalist blogs for years, and one thing I've internalized is that if people encounter a view only as a text to analyze, they'll treat it as a position to be refuted rather than as a claim about their own experience. I want the opposite here: I want them to feel the core Strawsonian move before they read a word of it, so that when they encounter the essay they recognize it as a description of something they've already lived.

The idea I want them to encounter experientially is this: the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, hurt feelings — are not theoretical beliefs about the presence of libertarian free will that we update when we learn about determinism. They are constitutive of what Strawson calls the participant stance: the stance we take toward other people when we relate to them as persons rather than as objects to be managed or treated. We can suspend a reactive attitude in a particular case when we receive an excuse (he didn't mean to; she was under duress) or an exemption (he's a child; she's not in control of her actions), but we cannot suspend the whole set of reactive attitudes toward everyone on the basis of a general theoretical truth like determinism. To do so would be to abandon the participant stance entirely and adopt the objective stance toward all persons — and that is both psychologically impossible and, if we tried, would mean giving up interpersonal relationships as we know them.

I want a structured activity that makes them run into this themselves. No lecture up front; I want the activity to generate the experience, and then the debrief names what happened. The activity should have four moments:

1. They experience a genuine reactive attitude toward a specific person, in a scenario vivid enough to actually produce the feeling (or at least its imaginative analogue).
2. They encounter an excusing or exempting condition and feel the attitude shift — or fail to shift, depending on the condition — and notice the difference between excuses and exemptions.
3. They encounter a generalized deterministic explanation of the same action and discover that it doesn't shift the attitude in the same way, even if they think it should.
4. They try, on instruction, to adopt the objective stance toward everyone involved — including themselves — and discover what it costs and whether they can sustain it.

The debrief should map each moment onto the Strawsonian framework: participant stance vs. objective stance, excuses (internal to the participant stance) vs. exemptions (move someone to the objective stance), and why the truth of determinism doesn't function as either — it's not an excuse (it doesn't show the action was uncharacteristic or unintentional) and it's not an exemption (it doesn't show the agent is abnormal or incapacitated). The debrief should also be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: Strawson's argument is partly normative — it's about whether we *ought* to suspend the reactive attitudes given determinism, not merely whether we can. A classroom game can only get at the psychological inescapability and the phenomenology of the participant stance. Say what the facilitator needs to supply to bridge that gap, and flag where a smart incompatibilist in the room will push back and what the right response is.

Give me a fully specified, runnable activity: the exact scenario cards or prompts, the instructions for each phase, the rating or recording scheme, the phase-by-phase timeline, and the debrief script. I want a committed design with definite choices — not a brainstorming list of possible activities, not a menu of options. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (there are always at least two failure modes) and how to handle each one in the moment.
