Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about something that's been bothering me for years, and I'm hoping someone can help me think through it. Here's the pattern: I'll decide, in a calm and reflective moment, that I want to get up early tomorrow to exercise before work. I genuinely mean it — I lay out my clothes, I set the alarm, I go to bed early. Then the alarm goes off at six, and I think: I could get up, but I'd rather sleep another hour. And I turn it off. Later I regret it. The same thing happens with saving money, sticking to a writing schedule, eating better — I make a plan that I endorse wholeheartedly when I'm not in the grip of the temptation, and then when the temptation is right in front of me, I choose against the plan. And I do this over and over.

My friend's argument — and we went back and forth on this for two hours — is that I'm misunderstanding myself. He says there's no deep mystery here: when I turn off the alarm, I am simply revealing what I actually prefer at that moment. The person who set the alarm the night before is, in effect, making a prediction about what the morning person will want, and that prediction keeps being wrong. The morning person wants sleep more than exercise. So the rational thing, he says, is either to accept that I'm the kind of person who prefers sleep and stop beating myself up about it, or to set up some kind of external commitment device — but not to talk as if there's some truer self that's being betrayed by a weaker one. He says the whole idea of "weakness of will" is confused: you always do what you most want to do in the moment you act, and calling it weakness is just a story you tell afterward to avoid admitting that your preferences shifted.

I feel like he's missing something important, but I couldn't quite pin down what it was. It seems to me that the fact that I reliably regret the choice, and reliably make the same plan again, suggests that my preferences aren't just shifting neutrally — there's a systematic pattern where the near-term reward looms disproportionately large and then shrinks once it's no longer immediate. But my friend would say that's just a description, not a critique: preferences that depend on temporal proximity are still preferences. Who's right? And if I am right that something genuinely irrational is going on, what exactly is the nature of the irrationality?
