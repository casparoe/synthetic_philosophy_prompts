I've been rewatching Christopher Nolan's Tenet (2020) ahead of a film-and-philosophy series I'm helping to organize, and I keep coming back to how much of its plot machinery rests on a single physical idea: that reversing an object's entropy is what it means to reverse its direction in time. In the film, "inversion" is a technology, apparently sent back from the future, that runs a person's or object's entropy backward, so that bullets fly back into guns, an inverted man walks backward through a world that unwinds around him, and the climactic threat is a device that would invert the entropy of the entire planet, which a future civilization hopes will let it undo the damage of the present. I'd like a serious reading of the film through the philosophy of the thermodynamic arrow of time — not a debunking, and not an "it's just a movie" pass, but an assessment of where the film is actually tracking the physics and where it is using entropy as metaphor while appearing to do physics.

Specifically, I'd like you to sort the film's use of entropy against the real conceptual landscape: Boltzmann's statistical account of irreversibility, the low-entropy initial condition of the universe (what philosophers call the Past Hypothesis), the point that the arrow is a gradient of an ensemble rather than a fundamental law of the microphysics, and Loschmidt-style reversibility worries. Where does Tenet track those ideas, and where does it trade on confusions — for example, treating entropy as a local property of a single bullet that could be flipped independently of its environment, or treating a globally inverted arrow as something that could be switched on and lived through rather than a fragile statistical condition that contact with the surrounding world would immediately undo? The film half-notices the interaction problem, I think, in details like inverted characters needing their own inverted air and inverted fire freezing rather than burning; I'd like a judgment on how much it actually gets right and whether it seems to know the difference between the parts it is using as physics and the parts it is using as metaphor. It also ties inversion to a peculiar fatalism, as if acting against the world's arrow means the outcome is already fixed even as one deliberates; I'd like to know whether that follows from anything in the physics or is an independent commitment the film smuggles in. Finally, I want the residue report in the other direction: is there anything about the arrow of time that the film dramatizes — the first-person experience of a locally reversed arrow, the fantasy of running the tape backward to undo ecological damage — that the physics and the philosophy papers leave out, and that a discussion group would lose by staying at the level of the literature?
