Over the past term my philosophy of mind reading group has been doing the extended mind debate, and I've hit the the same wall every week. The same moves keep coming around: Clark and Chalmers's parity principle, Otto and his notebook, the claim that the notebook would count as cognitive if it were surgically implanted, the reply that a notebook still lacks the right kind of content or the right kind of processing. Nobody in the group disagrees about what Otto can and can't do, or what Inga can and can't do; we all agree that there's no empirical fact at stake about their behavior. And yet half the room keeps concluding that Otto's memory literally extends into the notebook, and the other half keeps concluding that the notebook is just a tool, and after a couple months nobody has moved.

My hunch, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that this isnt really a debate about minds at all. It looks to me like a disagreement about what the word 'cognition' is supposed to be doing: whether it's meant to pick out a natural kind with a mark (something like original, nonderived content, or the right kind of internal processing), or whether it's just a loose term for whatever plays the relevant functional role. But it could also be a disagreement about what an explanation in cognitive science has to do — whether it should be personal-level and functional, or subpersonal and mechanistic — with each side treating their explanatory standard as the obvious one.

I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict for one side. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two different questions being run under the heading 'where does the mind stop'? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or should we expect the debate to keep running forever, and why?
