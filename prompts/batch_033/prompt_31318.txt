im writing the final for my upper-level philosophy of physics seminar and want one essay question on the hole argument that cant be answered by reciting the standard summary. the course went through the argument carefully, so the question should force students to say exactly which commitments generate the problem, what general covariance contributes, and why the quick conclusion that substantivalism is false is too strong. 90 minutes, one question, 20 marks total.

Question: The hole argument is often summarized as showing that substantivalism about spacetime conflicts with determinism in general relativity. That summary runs together two claims: (i) spacetime points exist independently of the metric and matter fields, and (ii) two models related by a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside a region but not inside it represent distinct possible worlds. Write an essay that does three things. First, state precisely which package of commitments drives the argument, and reconstruct how it goes — keeping the manifold and the metric field distinct, and making clear the role of general covariance. Second, explain why the argument does not force us to abandon substantivalism altogether, but only a particular way of being a substantivalist. Third, say what a sophisticated substantivalist must claim about representation to restore determinism, and what philosophical cost that position pays, if any. An A answer will say exactly where determinism enters, and will state the sophisticated position without collapsing it into relationism.

Model answer (about 600 words):

A model of general relativity is a triple (M, g, T): M is the manifold, the set of spacetime points; g is the metric field, which gives the points their geometric properties; T stands for the matter fields. Substantivalism, in the form the hole argument targets, says M exists independently of g and T, and that the individual points of M are real in a strong sense: it matters which point has which metrical properties. Such a substantivalist denies Leibniz equivalence and treats two models that differ only in which points play which roles as representing different possible worlds.

General covariance, or diffeomorphism invariance, says that if (M, g, T) is a solution, then for any diffeomorphism d, (M, d*g, d*T) is also a solution, where d*g and d*T are the fields dragged along by d. This is a symmetry of the theory: it does not move the points, it moves the fields over the points.

Now take a model with a Cauchy surface Σ and pick a region H to the future of Σ — the hole. Choose a diffeomorphism d that is the identity on Σ, on the past side, and outside H, but is nontrivial inside H. Apply it. The result is a second model that agrees with the first everywhere up to and including Σ, and agrees outside H, but inside H it assigns the metric and matter properties to different points than the first model does. Since the equations are diffeomorphism invariant, the second model is just as much a solution as the first.

If the substantivalist insists these two models represent distinct possible worlds — because the same points have been given different properties — then the laws, together with the complete state on Σ, fail to fix what happens inside H. The same initial data are compatible with two different continuations. That is precisely a failure of determinism. So the argument shows that strong, point-individualist substantivalism plus general covariance yields indeterminism.

But it does not show that substantivalism itself must go. The offending assumption is the combination of two things: that points are bare individuals with identity independent of the fields, and that diffeomorphism-related models represent distinct worlds. The substantivalist can keep the manifold while denying the second claim. That is sophisticated substantivalism. It says the physical content of a model is not given by which particular point has which property, because points have no primitive identity over and above the metric and matter fields defined on them. Diffeomorphism-related models are different mathematical presentations of one and the same physical world, much as two coordinate descriptions present one geometry.

The price is that the substantivalist must give up haecceitism — the view that the bare identity of points is physically meaningful — and with it the intuitive picture of spacetime as a container whose points are there anyway before the fields arrive. But the position is still substantivalist rather than relationist, because it does not reduce spacetime to relations among material events. It keeps the manifold and the metric as real, but denies that a point's identity is something over and above the fields. Restoring determinism costs the substantivalist the bare points, not the spacetime.

Marking scheme (20 marks total):

(4) Correctly sets up the model (M, g, T) and keeps the manifold and the metric field distinct throughout.
(3) States the role of general covariance precisely: a diffeomorphism maps any solution to another solution.
(4) Reconstructs the hole construction: a diffeomorphism that is the identity on and below a Cauchy surface and outside the hole, nontrivial inside, so the two models agree up to Σ but differ in H.
(4) Locates determinism: if the two models count as distinct possibilities, the same data on Σ have two different continuations inside H.
(3) Says what the argument does and does not establish: it refutes the package of point-individualist substantivalism plus denial of Leibniz equivalence, not substantivalism as such.
(2) Gives the sophisticated substantivalist fix and its cost: diffeomorphism-related models represent the same world; points lack primitive identity; this is anti-haecceitist but still substantivalist, not relationism.

What seperates an A from a B: an A answer never treats the metric as if it were the manifold, can say that the diffeomorphism drags the fields over the same set of points without flinching, and states the sophisticated view's fix as a claim about representation rather than as the slogan that there are no points. A B answer typically blames general covariance itself for the trouble, says the two models differ mathematically rather than representationally, or concludes that sophisticated substantivalism is just relationism under a new name. Another common B move is saying determinism fails because the equations are underdetermined, rather than because distinct possibilities share the same initial data.
