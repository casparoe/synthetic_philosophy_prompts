A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether the decision to have a child can be rational. She defended a version of the transformative experience argument — the one associated with L. A. Paul — and concluded not only that parenthood cannot be rationally chosen, but that a great deal of ordinary life cannot be either. I found that conclusion unacceptable, yet after two hours I still could not locate the step where her reasoning goes bad. I have written the argument out as carefully as I can reconstruct it, and I would like a diagnosis.

Here is the argument:

(1) A decision is rational only if the agent can, in advance, evaluate what the possible outcomes would be like from the inside and how much value the agent would attach to those outcomes after they obtain.

(2) Some experiences are transformative in the relevant sense: undergoing one changes the agent's preferences, values, or self-conception in ways that cannot be fully known before the fact. Having a child is the central case.

(3) For a transformative experience, the agent cannot satisfy the condition in (1): they cannot know from the inside what the outcome would be like, and they cannot know what they would value after the transformation, because the person doing the valuing after the fact would no longer be the person now doing the choosing.

(4) Therefore, by (1) through (3), deciding to have a child cannot be a rational decision — at least not when the decision is based on what the agent expects the experience to be like for them.

So far this is just the familiar argument, and I could accept it as a local result about an unusual kind of decision. But my friend did not stop there:

(5) Nearly every significant life decision has a transformative element. Taking a new job changes your routines, your social world, and often your sense of what you are good at and what matters to you; moving to a new city exposes you to ways of living you could not fully anticipate; entering a serious relationship alters your preferences in ways you cannot predict in advance. Even many small decisions — trying an unfamiliar food, reading a novel, taking up a sport — shift your sensibilities in ways you could not have fully foreseen.

(6) If the reasoning in (1) through (4) shows that parenthood cannot be rationally chosen, then the same reasoning shows that these other decisions cannot be rationally chosen, since in each case the agent faces some degree of the same epistemic gap.

(7) Therefore, by (5) and (6), almost no significant life decision — and a great many ordinary ones — can be rational.

(8) A theory of rational choice that condemns nearly all of the decisions people actually make as irrational has proved too much; whatever is going wrong in those decisions, it cannot be that they all fail as rational choices.

(9) Therefore, at least one of the steps in (1) through (7) must be rejected.

I am not sure where to resist. I want to say there is a difference in kind, not merely in degree, between becoming a parent and taking a new job — but I worry that this is special pleading for a distinction the argument's own logic erases. I am also tempted to reject (1), since it seems to make rationality require a kind of first-personal foresight that ordinary decision-making never has; but if I give up (1), I need to know what conception of rational choice I am left with, and whether it still distinguishes deciding well from deciding on a whim.

Which step should I reject? If the argument is valid, which premise is the least costly to give up, and what does giving it up commit me to? If there is a flaw rather than a false premise, identify the step where it occurs and explain why that step looked sound.
