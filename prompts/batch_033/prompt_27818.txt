I've been in the foundations of quantum mechanics trenches for a while now, and the Born rule problem in many-worlds has started to feel less like a research program and more like a philosophical stalemate. Here's what I mean. Everyone agrees on the formalism: unitary evolution, decoherence, branching. The question is why we should expect to observe outcomes with frequencies matching the Born rule. The decision-theory camp (Deutsch, Wallace) says rational agents in the multiverse should bet as if the Born rule holds, so probability enters via practical rationality. The envariance camp (Zurek) tries to derive the Born rule from symmetry properties of entangled states. The self-locating uncertainty camp (Sebens and Carroll, maybe Vaidman) says you should be uncertain about which branch you'll end up in, and that uncertainty should follow the Born rule. And then the skeptics say all of these either assume what they're trying to prove or rely on a notion of probability that makes no sense when all outcomes happen.

My question isn't who's right. It's: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "probability" — some people meaning objective chance, others meaning credence or betting odds? Is it a disagreement about what counts as a legitimate derivation, where the two sides have different standards of success and neither can convince the other because there's no shared standard to appeal to? Or is there a hidden empirical question here — like whether the branches are all equally real, or whether decoherence really solves the preferred basis problem — that's doing the work underneath?

I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, grounded in what the different camps actually argue. And then tell me: given the diagnosis, is there anything that could actually move this? A new theorem, a conceptual distinction, an experiment (what would it even look like?), or should we just accept that this is where the disagreement lives permanently?
