I've been reading the alignment side of LessWrong for years, and after the dozenth post about learning human values from demonstrations I still keep getting stuck on inverse reinforcement learning. The pitch, as I understand it, is that you watch someone's behavior and infer the reward function they're approximately optimizing, and that this gives you their preferences — maybe even their values. But this sounds exactly like revealed preference theory from economics, and I thought the whole lesson of that literature was that choices alone don't pin down a unique utility function: any number of preference orderings are compatible with the same behavior once you allow for constraints, unobserved costs, mistakes, or caring about something you didn't measure. I get that modern IRL doesn't assume perfect optimality — people use maximum entropy models where action probabilities are softmax in expected return — but that just changes the likelihood; a softmax policy is invariant to adding a constant to all returns, so the data still underdetermine the reward. Since IRL is just revealed preferences with function approximation, and revealed preferences can't recover a unique utility, what's the point of using IRL to learn human values for alignment?

I've spent alot of time trying to taboo the words "preference" and "value" and just look at the math, and the math seems to say that demonstrations only pin down a behavior-equivalence class of rewards, with everything else coming from the prior. So why do people treat IRL as if it recovers what humans actually want, rather than re-describing what they did? I'm sure I'm missing a step, but I can't find it.
