In the department lounge last week, two of my colleagues got into an extended argument about the problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory. They asked me to referee; I found I could not do it justice on the spot, so I later wrote down their exchange as faithfully as I could. Here it is, lightly compressed and numbered for ease of reference.

(1) Ellen opened: The problem of old evidence is close to fatal for Bayesianism. In the standard apparatus, evidence E confirms hypothesis H just in case P(H | E) > P(H). But when E is already known before H is formulated, P(E) = 1, and then P(H | E) = P(H), so E cannot confirm H at all. Yet the history of science is full of cases in which a theory was accepted precisely because it accounted for data known long beforehand; the advance of Mercury's perihelion confirmed general relativity even though the anomaly had been catalogued for decades. A theory of confirmation that cannot represent such episodes is missing something central.

(2) Marcus replied: That is too quick. The natural fix is to evaluate the theory against a counterfactual prior, one that subtracts the evidence or imagines an agent who does not yet know it. Ask how plausible the hypothesis would have been had the old evidence not been in hand. If general relativity makes the perihelion data antecedently likely and its rivals do not, then the data confirms the theory, old or not. The problem is an artifact of insisting on the agent's actual, evidence-soaked probabilities.

(3) Ellen: But "subtracting" evidence from a probability function is not a well-defined operation. Once you know E, there is no unique rational way to redistribute your credences as if you did not; different subtractions yield different degrees of confirmation, and nothing constrains the choice among them. You have replaced a real difficulty with a free parameter. Worse, the original question was whether the evidence, as we actually possess it, supports the theory. Your account answers a different question: whether it would have supported it in a state of ignorance no actual scientist was ever in.

(4) Marcus: The free-parameter charge has some force, but there is a deeper response. The standard formalism assumes logical omniscience, and that assumption is what creates the problem. Suppose we allow the algebra to include propositions about logical and derivational relations — for example, the proposition that the theory entails the observed anomaly. Learning that entailment is a genuine epistemic event even when both the theory and the data are already in hand, and it can raise the probability of the theory. Old evidence confirms through logical learning, not through the evidence proposition itself. This is not an ad hoc patch; it is the removal of an idealization we already knew was false.

(5) Ellen: But that relocates the difficulty. If the agent knows the theory and knows the data, and the entailment is a logical fact, then a logically omniscient agent would already know the entailment as well. Your proposal works only by making the agent logically non-omniscient, and then you owe us an account of the priors over these entailment propositions. Where do they come from, and what constrains them? Without that, you have traded one free parameter for another — and a more obscure one.

(6) Marcus: Every Bayesian model needs priors, so pointing to priors is not an objection to my proposal in particular. And the relevant agents, actual scientists, are precisely not logically omniscient; someone had to do the derivational work connecting the theory to the anomaly, and before that work was done the entailment was not known. Priors over entailment propositions can be tied to how much of that derivational work has been completed, so the free-parameter worry, while not baseless, is less damaging than you suggest.

(7) Ellen: Notice what has happened, though. To get old evidence to confirm, you have added an apparatus of logical relations and degrees of derivational progress, and the confirmation now occurs because a probability shifts when a logical fact is learned, not because the data bears on the theory in the usual way. That looks less like a vindication of the original idea that confirmation is a relation between evidence and hypothesis than a replacement of it.

(8) Marcus: But the "usual way" was never the whole of confirmation. Bayesianism has always been an account of how rational credences change under new information, and the new information here is the recognition of a logical connection. You are faulting the solution for not being the very theory we found wanting. That is not a defect; it is the shape any solution must take.

At that point I had to leave for a meeting. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange as it stands.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. The issue (roughly 150–200 words). State precisely what the problem of old evidence is and what is at stake between the two positions.
2. Move-by-move adjudication (roughly 900–1,100 words). Go through moves (1) through (8) in order. For each, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify the rating.
3. What both parties are missing (roughly 250–350 words). Identify the most important consideration neither side has adequately addressed — for instance, a distinction they have run together, a reframing of the problem, or an unnoticed cost of the proposed solutions.
4. Overall verdict (roughly 150–200 words). Say who is more right overall, and explain how much that verdict depends on what one wants a confirmation theory to do.
