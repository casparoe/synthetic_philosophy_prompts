I'm trying to get my head round Arrow's impossibility theorem and want a poem where the form does the arguing, not just the words. You're a philosopher of social choice who also writes formal verse. Write a pantoum about the theorem. The pantoum's interlocking repeated lines should enact the failure of independence of irrelevant alternatives: the same phrase — some third candidate, an irrelevant alternative — keeps coming back in a new stanza and quietly changing the social ranking, even though it shouldn't matter. The opening stanzas should set up the four conditions as the reasonable promises of a fair rule for aggregating ranked preferences among three or more alternatives; the middle stanzas should let the repetition do the damage, with each return of the irrelevant alternative tipping the result; and the final quatrain, which in a pantoum brings back the poem's first and third lines as its second and fourth lines, should land as the theorem's conclusion — you can't keep all four promises, and if you insist on the first three, a dictator is what's left. It should be accurate enough that someone who teaches Arrow would recognise the theorem without wincing: no fudging the quantifiers, no pretending rank-order methods escape it. Before writing, brainstorm which lines you want to recur, and think carefully about wich condition the poem ultimately blames; work through the alternatives seperately so the poem commits to one.
