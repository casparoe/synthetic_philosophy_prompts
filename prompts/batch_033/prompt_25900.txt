My philosophy of language professor has given us her verdicts on whether the following assertions were proper — that is, permissible according to whatever norm governs assertion:

1. A speaker asserts that a restaurant is open, having checked its hours online that morning. The restaurant is open. Verdict: proper.

2. A speaker asserts that it will rain tomorrow based purely on a hunch, with no evidence either way. It rains. Verdict: improper.

3. A speaker asserts that a defendant is guilty based on circumstantial evidence that most reasonable people would find insufficient to convict. The defendant is in fact guilty. Verdict: improper.

4. A speaker asserts that her train is delayed based on an announcement she overheard from a colleague she knows to be a compulsive liar. By chance, the colleague was telling the truth; the train is delayed. Verdict: improper.

5. A speaker asserts that the milk in the fridge is still good, having smelled it and found no sign of spoilage. The milk is good. Verdict: proper.

Next week's case: A speaker asserts that the university library is open on Sundays, having checked the library's website that morning. The website is normally reliable and displays the library as open. The library is in fact open. Unbeknownst to the speaker, the website suffered a glitch that day and was displaying last year's Sunday hours; by coincidence, the hours have not changed, so the displayed information was correct.

Predict her verdict on the library case. Identify which norm of assertion best explains her pattern of verdicts so far — the knowledge norm, the justification norm, or another candidate — and say which of her earlier verdicts rule out the alternatives. If more than one norm fits her record, say which one you think she is running and why, and tell me what further case would settle the matter. I want a committed response: take a definite position and defend it.
