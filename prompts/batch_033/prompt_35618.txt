ok so i just had this long arguement with a friend about the problem of old evidence and i walked away thinking it's actually a way bigger deal than most people admit. the basic thing is: if you already know some piece of evidence E, then P(E) = 1 for you, so P(H|E) = P(H), and old evidence can't confirm anything. but that's obviously nuts — einstein literally used the known anomaly in mercury's orbit to confirm general relativity, and nobody thinks that was illegitimate. so bayesian confirmation theory seems to just get a central case wrong.

my friend kept bringing up the standard fixes — garber's move where you learn logical relations instead of the evidence itself, or the counterfactual approach where you ask what your credence would've been if you didn't already know E. but i'm not convinced any of them work without begging the question or just relocating the problem.

so here's where i'm at: i hold the view that the problem of old evidence is a genuine, deep problem for bayesian confirmation theory and that the standard responses don't actually solve it. what would count against this? i want the evidence or arguments that should move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be. and i want an honest assessment of whether i've set this view up so that it can't be falsified — like, am i just going to dismiss any proposed fix as inadequate by definition? if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it fine?
