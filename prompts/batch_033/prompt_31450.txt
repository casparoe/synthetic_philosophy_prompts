Two members of my philosophy reading group, Elena and Samir, got into a protracted argument about Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," and neither would budge. I followed the back-and-forth but couldn't sort out who was right at each stage. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

Elena: The dialogue demonstrates that deductive rules cannot be justified. The Tortoise accepts the premises of a simple argument and then refuses the conclusion. When Achilles adds the corresponding conditional as a further premise, the Tortoise accepts that too and still refuses to detach the conclusion. Trying to force the inference by adding more conditionals just generates an infinite regress. So modus ponens has no non-circular foundation — you can't justify it without already using it or something like it.

Samir: That's exactly backwards. The regress only arises because Achilles makes a fundamental mistake: he treats a rule of inference as if it were an extra premise. The Tortoise claims to accept "If A and B then Z" but still won't accept Z. But a being that genuinely accepted that conditional and accepted A and B yet refused Z simply doesn't understand "if." The lesson is that rules of inference are not premises, and treating them as such is a confusion — not a skeptical discovery.

Elena: You're actually making my point. You just said the Tortoise can't be forced to detach by any premise — which is precisely to say that the inferential act itself is not reducible to any propositional content. That act is what we can't justify. You've just relocated the problem from "the rule can't be justified" to "the rule isn't the kind of thing that could be justified by premises." But that's the same limitation: we can give no propositional justification for the inferential move itself.

Samir: No, it's a different claim. You're demanding that a rule of inference be justified the way a belief is — by being supported by further premises. But rules are not beliefs. Asking for a premise that forces detachment is like asking for a rule of grammar that forces you to apply it. The demand is misconceived, not unanswered. The Tortoise's challenge is pseudo-profound because it conflates the acceptance of a conditional with the adoption of a rule.

Elena: I don't think that rescues deduction from the problem. If modus ponens is a rule we follow as a matter of practice or convention, then other communities could follow different rules and be equally "right" by their own lights. You've traded justification for rule-relativism. The Tortoise's challenge still stands: can we show that someone who follows a different inferential practice is making a mistake, rather than just not sharing our conventions?

Samir: That's not relativism. Some rules are constitutive of the concepts involved: you can't count as meaning "if" by "if...then" unless you treat modus ponens as valid for it. So there's no coherent alternative community that uses "if" with a different rule and means the same thing. The Tortoise isn't offering a rival logic — he's claiming to accept the conditional while failing to act on it. That's not a different practice; it's an incoherent performance.

Elena: Even if I grant that, notice what you've conceded: you've given up the idea that the correctness of modus ponens can be demonstrated to someone who doesn't already accept it. You're saying we can only justify deduction from inside a practice that already uses deduction. That's exactly the limited, circular result I started with — you've just dressed it up in constitutive-rule language.

Samir: I'd accept "from inside" but not "circular." We can show that modus ponens preserves truth by truth-table reasoning, and the fact that this reasoning itself uses modus ponens isn't a defect — no one here was doubting modus ponens. The Tortoise isn't doubting it either; he's pretending to accept the premises and refusing to perform. That's a performance problem, not an epistemological one. The dialogue doesn't show that deduction is ungrounded; it shows that accepting a rule is not a propositional attitude.
