I am working through Galileo’s Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina of Tuscany (1615), and I am trying to understand the argumentative function of one of the early quotations from Augustine. Here is the passage:

These men would perhaps not have fallen into such error had they but paid attention to a most useful doctrine of St. Augustine’s, relative to our making positive statements about things which are obscure and hard to understand by means of reason alone. Speaking of a certain physical conclusion about the heavenly bodies, he wrote: “Now keeping always our respect for moderation in grave piety, we ought not to believe anything inadvisedly on a dubious point, lest in favor to our error we conceive a prejudice against something that truth hereafter may reveal to be not contrary in any way to the sacred books of either the Old or the New Testament.”

I want help figuring out exactly what work this passage is doing in Galileo’s larger argument. Is Galileo using Augustine merely to scold his opponents for overconfidence, or is he laying down a stronger principle: that when a natural claim has not yet been demonstrated, interpreters of Scripture should avoid binding Scripture to a particular physical doctrine? Does the Augustine quotation imply a division of labor between the book of nature and the book of Scripture, or only a counsel of epistemic caution? I would also like to know whether, in saying this, Galileo is already presupposing the view—later made explicit in the letter—that Scripture speaks in a manner accommodated to ordinary appearances and to the understanding of common people, and how much of that later view is genuinely contained in this early quotation. Please don’t turn this into a general summary of the letter; I’m interested in the precise logical relation between this quotation and the argument Galileo subsequently builds about the authority of Scripture in purely natural matters.

As you answer, please separate what is settled about the text and its context from what remains genuinely contested among historians and philosophers who study Galileo, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the scholarly consensus.
