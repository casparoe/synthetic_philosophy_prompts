I keep coming back to a one-liner that gets used in my philosophy of religion reading group, and I've realised I don't know what it's actually claiming. We're working through Hume on miracles, and whenever someone mentions a present-day miracle report — a cancer remission, a resurrection story, that sort of thing — one member shuts the discussion down with "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence." She says it as though it's the whole argument, and nobody pushes back.

I want you to act as an epistemologist who knows Hume's essay on miracles inside out, and to think carefully before you write. Start by brainstorming all the different precise principles that slogan could be expressing — from the boringly true to the obviously false, and everything in between — before you settle on your list.

Then lay the readings out as a menu. For each one: state the precise principle; say what "extraordinary" and "evidence" would have to mean for that reading to work; say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false; and say what it would imply for the kind of miracle testimony Hume was discussing. Which of these readings actually support dismissing testimony out of hand the way my friend does, and which don't? I'd also like you to tell me which reading is the most charatable reconstruction of what she probably means, and whether Hume's own argument in the essay commits him to that reading or to something weaker — and if the slogan on its best reading does no work beyond Hume, or is just a crude stand-in for a more careful claim, I want that said plainly.

When you answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question.
