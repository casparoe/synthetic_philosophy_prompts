The exam question was: "Does the conceivability of philosophical zombies show that physicalism is false? Explain the argument, identify the premise most in need of defense, and assess one serious objection to that premise."

The student wrote:

"The zombie argument runs as follows. A philosophical zombie is a creature physically identical to a normal human being but with no phenomenal consciousness, no felt experience, nothing it is like to be it. If zombies are conceivable, and if conceivability entails possibility, then zombies are possible. And if zombies are possible, physicalism is false, because physicalism says that once all the physical facts are fixed, all the other facts, including the facts about consciousness, are fixed too. A zombie world has the same physical facts as ours but different phenomenal facts, so physicalism would be false in such a world.

The premise most in need of defense is the bridge principle, that conceivability entails possibility. This is not generally true, as Kripke showed. We can conceive that water is not H2O; someone could coherently think that the clear drinkable stuff in lakes and rivers is an element. But given that water is in fact H2O, there is no possible world where water is not H2O. So conceivability does not entail possibility, full stop. The water case and the zombie case are on a par here: both are scenarios we can apparently conceive of, and both are ruled out by the actual nature of things. So the zombie argument fails at the bridge premise for the same reason.

The usual reply is to restrict conceivability to ideal conceivability, where a scenario counts only if it survives ideal rational reflection and not just if nobody notices a contradiction. That helps, but it does not rescue the argument, because ideal conceivability is still an epistemic notion, and an epistemic notion cannot establish metaphysical possibility. Chalmers's distinction between primary and secondary conceivability is meant to address exactly this. Primary conceivability is conceivability of a scenario considered as actual, and secondary conceivability is conceivability given the way the actual world is. Chalmers concedes that primary conceivability only gives us epistemic possibility, and he admits that the zombie scenario is at best primarily conceivable, so on his own view the argument delivers only epistemic possibility, not the metaphysical possibility that physicalism needs to be false."
