Me and a couple of others run a metaethics reading group and we've spent three meetings watching the expressivist and the truth-conditionalist talk past eachother about the Frege–Geach problem. We want to make them write something they'd both actually sign, instead of another round of "but what does boo mean." So please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between an expressivist about moral language (moral sentences express desire-like attitudes rather than beliefs or propositions) and a truth-conditional descriptivist (moral sentences have propositional content and truth conditions like ordinary declaratives). Both of them accept the surface data, they're writing in good faith, and neither is allowed to redefine the other's position.

The document should include:

- A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. This should cover the uncontroversial embedding data — negation ("stealing is not wrong"), conditionals ("if stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong"), disjunction, questions, and attitude reports ("Alice thinks stealing is wrong") — plus the claims that meaning is compositional, that "stealing is wrong" means the same thing asserted as it does embedded, and that the obvious modus ponens argument from "stealing is wrong" and "if stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong" to "getting your little brother to steal is wrong" is valid in the ordinary sense. Nothing in this section should be something either side would quietly hedge.

- Then a disagreements section (a table is fine) where each side's position is stated in wording that side would accept. So the expressivist's row shouldn't say moral sentences lack meaning, and the descriptivist's row shouldn't say expressivists just conflate force and content.

- Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. I expect at least these to show up: (i) the negation problem — what state or attitude does "stealing is not wrong" express, and does that explain why it clashes with "stealing is wrong"; (ii) mixed sentences like "if stealing is wrong, then grass is green" — can the expressivist assign a single attitude to the whole conditional without smuggling truth conditions back in; (iii) whether an expressivist account of validity as incoherence among attitudes can match the extension of ordinary logical validity for moral arguments, including ones with non-moral premises.

- For each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about what that case shows, written down in advance so nobody can move the goalposts after the fact.

- Finally, say which parts of the disagreement, if any, are merely verbal — but don't pretend the whole dispute is verbal, because it isn't.
