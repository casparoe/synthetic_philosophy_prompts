At a literary festival last autumn I found myself seated between two acquaintances—Helen, a critic, and Marcus, a retired philosopher—who had both just read the same debut novel and spent the better part of an hour arguing about whether it was any good. Helen maintained that the book was plainly bad: its central character never acted, the plot turned on a coincidence she called unforgivable, and the prose was, in her phrase, competent only in the way a departure-lounge announcement is competent. Marcus replied that this was autobiography rather than criticism—a record of how one reader's taste happened to be struck—since there is no fact of the matter about whether a novel is good.

Helen answered that this was exactly the position she took Hume to have dismantled in his essay on the standard of taste: beauty is felt rather than demonstrated, yet some judgements are still better than others, because they issue from critics who possess strong sense and delicacy of sentiment, which practice improves, comparison perfects, and prejudice corrupts, and because the convergent verdict of such critics over time constitutes the standard. Marcus said the proposal was circular: either a true judge is identified by some independent test, in which case one still needs a reason to think that test tracks goodness, or a true judge is simply whoever's verdicts endure, in which case the standard only restates the fact that some verdicts endure. Helen replied that the test was not empty, and that the capacities involved were real: she recalled the story Hume borrows from Cervantes, in which two tasters detect leather and iron in a fine wine and are laughed at until a key on a leathern thong is found at the bottom of the cask. The alternative, she said, was the absurdity that a schoolboy's verdict on King Lear counts for as much as a lifetime's considered judgement. Marcus said the absurdity could be conceded without granting a standard: we can still argue, point to features the other has missed, and change one another's minds, but persuasion is all there is, and the test of time is only the sediment of persuasion plus institutional power. Helen asked what then remained of the idea that a judgement could be wrong. Marcus said, quietly, that perhaps nothing did.

As a novelist I had reason to care about the answer, so I kept out of the argument at the time; I have since been unable to settle who was right about what. Would you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move—say where each argument succeeds, where each fails, and what both of them are missing—and then, if it still makes sense to do so, say who is more right overall?
