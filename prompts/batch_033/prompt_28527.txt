I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm several months into reporting a feature on the free will debate. The piece is meant to be argumentative rather than a survey, so I need to state a position of my own, and after interviews with philosophers on both sides I've found myself settling into a package of views. Before I commit to it in print, I want a rigorous consistency audit from someone who works in this area.

Here is where I've landed. (a) I'm a compatibilist: determinism is compatible with moral responsibility. (b) I think Frankfurt-style cases succeed against the principle of alternative possibilities — an agent can be morally responsible even if she couldn't have done otherwise, because what matters is the actual sequence rather than alternative possibilities. (c) But I also think manipulation cases (the covertly engineered values, the hypnotist, and so on) show that purely current-time actual-sequence conditions like reasons-responsiveness or mesh between first- and second-order desires are not sufficient: provenance matters. (d) So I hold a historical compatibilism: responsibility requires that the agent's springs of action have the right kind of history, not merely the right present structure. (e) Still, I don't think manipulation arguments succeed as arguments for incompatibilism. The manipulated agent is disanalogous to an ordinary determined agent in a way that matters — roughly, her springs of action were inserted by another agent's intentional interference, whereas an ordinary determined agent is shaped by a diffuse natural and social process that isn't anyone's doing. (f) I think the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation — are the right starting point for a theory of moral responsibility. (g) Finally, I think the luck objection is fatal to event-causal libertarianism, and that agent-causal libertarianism is obscure rather than false.

Please act as a professional philosopher working on free will and moral responsibility and give me an audit rather than a lecture. Start by mapping which commitments presuppose, motivate, or sit uneasily with which others; then identify the tensions, rank them by severity, and distinguish the ones that are genuine from the ones that dissolve on inspection. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. I'd rather you be specific about which of (a) through (g) has to move than be told that the whole package is defensible from some point of view.
