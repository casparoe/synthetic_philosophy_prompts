I've been turning over an argument that came up in a discussion group on the ethics of deception, and I find myself unable to locate the mistake — if there is one — without giving up something I'd rather keep. I'm hoping for a careful diagnosis.

The argument purports to show that there is no morally significant difference between lying and merely misleading. It runs like this:

(1) If lying and misleading differ morally in some pair of cases that are matched for all downstream consequences, then the difference must be intrinsic to the acts themselves — it cannot be explained by what the acts cause.

(2) The intrinsic difference between lying and misleading is this: the liar asserts a proposition she believes to be false, while the misleader asserts a proposition she believes to be true but deliberately arranges for her audience to draw a false inference from it.

(3) For this intrinsic difference to carry moral weight, it must matter either from the standpoint of the speaker's agency (something about what she does, or the quality of will her action expresses) or from the standpoint of what the hearer is owed (something about the right the hearer has that is violated).

(4) From the speaker's standpoint: in both cases the speaker acts with the same ultimate end — to induce a false belief in the hearer — and deliberately selects means to that end. The choice between a false assertion and a true assertion paired with a false implicature is a choice of mechanism, not a difference in the quality of the will. If I intend your death and choose poison over a blade, I have not committed a lesser wrong; I have simply chosen a different instrument. Why should the instrument of deception be any different?

(5) From the hearer's standpoint: the hearer is left with a false belief either way, and that belief was produced through the speaker's deliberate action. Moreover, it is far from clear that a hearer who discovers the deception would feel less wronged by the misleader than by the liar. "What I said was technically true" is not a defense that most people would accept from someone who intentionally tricked them, and it is not obvious that they should.

(6) Therefore, there is no intrinsic moral difference between lying and misleading. Whatever considerations make a lie wrong apply with equal force to a parallel act of misleading.

(7) But this conclusion seems unacceptable — it proves too much. Most of us have the strong intuition that if one must deceive, misleading is the less bad way to do it. Our practices reflect this: perjury requires a false statement under oath, not merely misleading testimony; many people who would not lie to a partner or friend still permit themselves to mislead; and the distinctive sting of having been lied to — as opposed to having been cleverly misdirected — seems to track something real. If the argument is sound, all of this is a mistake.

I am most reluctant to surrender (7), but I cannot see which of the earlier steps is vulnerable, or what rejecting it would cost. Is the argument valid but unsound — and if so, which premise should be abandoned, and what does abandoning it commit one to about the ethics of assertion, the norms of conversation, or what we owe one another as speakers and hearers? Or is there a subtler problem: an equivocation, a scope shift, or a step that looks valid only under a reading the premises do not support?
