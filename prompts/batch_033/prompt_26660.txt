I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of death, and I have noticed a recurring structural problem in the first few weeks. The Epicurean slogan "Death is nothing to us" gets invoked early — by me, by the students, by Lucretius — and the discussion immediately polarizes. Half the room treats it as a sophistical trick that collapses under the slightest pressure; the other half treats it as a deep insight they cannot quite formulate. I have come to suspect that both reactions are responding to quite different claims that have been conflated under a single sentence, and that my own grasp of what the slogan actually asserts is less precise than it ought to be.

I want to get clearer about what, exactly, "Death is nothing to us" could be claiming. The slogan seems to admit of readings along several axes. There is a metaphysical claim about what death is or is not: that death is not an event in one's life, that there is no subject for death to be bad for, that post-mortem non-existence is not a state one occupies. There is an epistemological claim: that we can have no well-founded beliefs about what being dead is like, or that we systematically misrepresent death when we try to think about it. There is a normative or evaluative claim: that death is not an evil, that it is irrational to fear death, that one's own finitude provides no reason for regret or for action. And there is a comparative claim: that our attitudes toward prenatal and post-mortem non-existence ought to be symmetric.

These are plainly not the same claim, and they do not all stand or fall together. What would be useful to me is a systematic disambiguation: a menu of the distinct precise theses that the slogan might be taken to express, organized by the kind of claim each is making, with an assessment of which are trivial (true but doing no interesting work), which are substantive and defensible, which are probably false, and — most importantly — which are the ones that actually need to be true for the Epicurean argument to succeed, that is, for the conclusion that death is not an evil for the one who dies. I would also like to know which reading represents the strongest version of the argument, and whether that reading survives the deprivation account and the asymmetry objection. My plan is to use this analysis to restructure the first three weeks of the seminar so that students encounter the slogan with these distinctions already in hand, rather than discovering them only through the unproductive polarization I have been seeing.
