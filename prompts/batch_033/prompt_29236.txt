I spent my career in evolutionary biology, and only in retirement have I turned to philosophy in a sustained way. I recently read Nabokov's *Lolita* for the first time — late to it, I know — and I have been turning it over in light of something I have been reading in aesthetics: the debate about whether a work's moral character bears on its aesthetic value. The novel seems almost engineered as a test case for that debate. Humbert Humbert is a predator, and the novel never lets the reader forget the damage he does, yet the prose is so extraordinary that one finds oneself — I found myself — half-seduced into his way of seeing. The book appears to be, at one level, about precisely this: whether beauty can excuse, or at least aesthetically redeem, what is morally unredeemable.

I would like a reading of *Lolita* through the lens of the philosophical positions on this question — autonomism (the view that moral defects are not as such aesthetic defects), ethicism (the view that a moral defect in a work is an aesthetic defect when it is relevant to the work's aesthetic aims), and any other positions you take to be live in the debate. Specifically:

- Which scenes, structural choices, or features of the narration would an autonomist point to as evidence that the moral horror and the aesthetic achievement occupy separate registers, and which would an ethicist point to as evidence that the moral horror is internal to the aesthetic experience and cannot be bracketed?
- Does the novel itself take a position on this question — that is, does it dramatize an argument, through Humbert's aestheticism and its consequences, about whether aesthetic and moral evaluation can be kept apart?
- There is a further question about separating the work from the artist. Nabokov famously insisted that *Lolita* has no moral message and that art exists in a realm beyond moral concern. Does the novel's own treatment of Humbert's relentless aestheticizing — his tendency to turn Dolores into a textual object — undercut or complicate that insistence?
- Finally, what does the experience of reading *Lolita* add to the philosophical debate that the standard arguments in the journals do not capture? I am not asking for a summary of the novel — I have read it — but for what the novel reveals, or makes felt, that a paper arguing for ethicism or autonomism might miss. Where does the novel resist being reduced to an illustration of either position, and what does that resistance tell us?
