I'm a journalist working on a long-form piece about corporate disclosure, and I keep running into the same defense. A company will reveal information that is technically accurate but carefully incomplete — for example, stating that a product passed all required safety tests while omitting that it failed several tests the law does not require — and then the spokesperson will say, "We never lied; everything we said was accurate," as if that settles the question of wrongdoing. I am convinced this move is doing argumentative work it has not earned, but I want to be able to say exactly what work that is.

Lay out the distinct precise claims that this one-liner could be expressing, from readings so weak they are compatible with almost any view to readings strong enough to settle the question of wrongdoing. For each reading, say whether it is close to tautological, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and say whether it actually supports the conclusion that the company's disclosure was not morally or professionally objectionable. In particular, separate the readings along two axes: what the speaker is claiming about the act itself — for example, that no false statement was made, that no one was actually deceived, that no duty to volunteer further information was violated — and what the speaker is claiming about the moral evaluation of the act — for example, that only lying is wrong, that misleading is always less wrong, or that a hearer who could have asked better questions has no complaint. Where does the move from "it was not a lie" to "it was not wrong" require an extra premise, and which premise is doing the questionable work? I would also like the answer to flag which readings depend on a definition of lying and which depend on a substantive moral claim about deception, so I can tell when a spokesperson is sliding between the two.
