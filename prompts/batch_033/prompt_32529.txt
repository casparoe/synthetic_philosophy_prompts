I spent thirty years as a research biologist—molecular stuff, mostly—and I'm used to thinking about causal chains: you perturb a system, you trace the effects downstream, you quantify the uncertainty. So when I started reading philosophy last year, consequentialism seemed like the natural home for me. But the more I read about cluelessness (Lenman, Greaves, that whole literature), the more I realize my own judgments about when consequences matter and when they don't are a bit of a mess. So I'm trying to do what I used to do in the lab when the data was confusing: lay out the cases and see if there's a pattern.

Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them—and if the answer is that some of my verdicts have to go, say which ones and why.

1. I'm at the grocery store choosing between two brands of pasta. One is slightly cheaper. I buy it. I have no idea what the long-run causal consequences of this choice are—it might affect the profits of two companies, which might affect hiring decisions, which might affect someone's life trajectory, and so on. I feel zero obligation to even try to think about any of that. The immediate effect (saving a dollar) is enough.

2. I'm deciding whether to have a second child. The downstream causal effects are enormous—a whole human life, plus all the effects that person will have on others, plus their descendants, rippling out for generations. I genuinly cannot predict the net sign of these effects. But I don't feel paralyzed. I feel like I'm allowed to just decide based on whether I want another child, our family's wellbeing, resources, and so on. The cluelessness doesn't seem to matter here either.

3. I'm on a hiring committee for a tenure-track position. We have two finalists. One seems slightly stronger on research, the other slightly stronger on teaching. I think about which choice will produce better outcomes for students, for the department, for the field. I know there are downstream ripples I can't trace—maybe the person we reject goes somewhere else and mentors a future Nobel laureate, who knows. But I don't feel like this cluelessness lets me off the hook. I feel like I should try to estimate the expected consequences as best I can and choose accordingly.

4. I'm considering whether to donate to a charity that distributes bed nets vs. one that does cash transfers. The evidence on which does more good per dollar is messy and contested. There are also long-run effects on local economies, population growth, political institutions, etc. that are genuinely hard to estimate. I feel like I should do my best with the available evidence and choose the one that seems more cost-effective, even though I'm deeply uncertain. The cluelessness bothers me but doesn't make me think the whole exercise is pointless.

5. I'm walking to work and can take the scenic route through the park or the direct route along the road. Choosing the park route might, by some chaotic butterfly-effect chain, cause or prevent a car accident somewhere. I have literally no idea. I feel no obligation to consider this. I just take the park route because it's nicer.

6. Here's the one that trips me up. A government is deciding whether to fund a large infrastructure project (say, high-speed rail) vs. a broad public health intervention. Both have cost-benefit analyses with very long time horizons, both have speculative effects on economic growth, technological development, social cohesion, and so on. The uncertainties are huge and the causal chains are long. I want to say: you still have to do the analysis and make the best decision you can. But I also recognize that the difference between this case and the pasta case is arguably just one of scale and tractability, not a difference in principle. And I'm not sure that's a satisfying place to land.

So: is there a principle that captures all of these? Something about when we're permitted to ignore downstream cluelessness and when we're obligated to wrestle with it? Or are my verdicts inconsistent, and I need to give one up? If so, which one, and what does the repaired view look like? Also, please separate what's genuinely settled in the literature from what's still contested—I'm new enough to this that I can't always tell when an author is defending a consensus view and when they're staking out a controversial position.
