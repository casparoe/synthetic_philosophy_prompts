Consider the game Nomic. In its initial form it consists of a body of numbered rules. Some of these rules specify how the game is played and won; others specify how the rules themselves may be changed: players propose rule-changes, vote on them, and adopt them according to procedures that are themselves among the rules. The amendment procedures apply reflexively, so the rules governing amendment can themselves be amended, and even the rule that defines winning can be changed, provided the procedures currently in force are followed. Some rules are initially designated immutable, but that designation is itself a rule, and the system contains procedures through which even it can be altered.

Philosophers of law have sometimes treated Nomic as a toy model of a legal system that contains its own criteria of validity. The puzzle is this. When a rule authorizes changes to itself, or when a rule's validity is conferred by a procedure that is itself valid only under that very rule, we seem to be caught in a self-referential circle. Does this yield a genuine paradox of legal self-reference — a legal analogue of the liar, or an infinite regress of justifications — or is the appearance of paradox merely that, an appearance that dissolves once we distinguish levels of rules, acts performed under rules, and the temporal stages of an amendment?
