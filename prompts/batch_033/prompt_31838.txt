A guy in my decision theory reading group keeps cutting off my point about whether two rational forecasters can start with different priors on an election and still both be reasonable, by saying "rational agents can't agree to disagree." I suspect that one-liner is smuggling in a lot of different claims. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing? I can think of at least the narrow theorem about common priors plus common knowledge of each other's posteriors, the stronger common-prior doctrine that all rational disagreement is really just private information, the modeling convention that says you assume a common prior to keep the math from exploding, and the normative claim that once I learn you disagree I ought to update toward you. There are probably more. For each reading, tell me whether its a theorem, a convention, a substantive empirical claim, or just false, and which of them would actually get him from "we still disagree after talking it through" to "one of us is irrational." I want a menu of readings I can take back to the group, not a defense of your favorite one.
