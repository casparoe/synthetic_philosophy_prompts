I'm writing an essay that reads Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* through the debate over moral testimony, and I'd like your reading rather than a summary of either the novel or the philosophical literature. The novel is narrated by Stevens, an English butler who, looking back on his career, describes how he made Lord Darlington's judgments his own: he repeatedly treats his employer's views about politics and character as authoritative, and he suppresses his own unease about Darlington's growing sympathy for Germany and his dismissal of two Jewish maids. By the end, the reader has to weigh whether Stevens's life was emptied by a failure of nerve, by a social role that made deference a virtue, or by something about the very idea of taking one's moral beliefs from another person.

Here is the philosophical lens. Pessimists about moral testimony hold that there is something distinctively problematic about forming moral beliefs on someone else's say-so: moral knowledge, unlike knowledge of train schedules or plumbing, cannot simply be passed from one person to another without loss, because the recipient lacks the understanding or first-hand appreciation that makes the belief properly their own. Optimists reply that this exaggerates the difference between moral and non-moral testimony, and that deferring to those with better judgment is often exactly what a modest agent should do.

I'd like you to give a detailed reading of the novel as an extended test of the pessimist thesis. Where does Stevens's deference look like a case of moral testimony gone wrong, and where does the novel resist that diagnosis—for instance, by suggesting that his problem is self-deception, snobbery, or a professional ethic of service rather than testimony as such? Does the novel capture something about why moral deference corrodes an agent that the standard arguments about understanding and autonomy miss, or does it ultimately show that the pessimist has mistaken one character's pathology for a general truth about moral belief? Be willing to say that the novel and the theory pull apart rather than forcing them into agreement.

After you give the reading, tell me where your interpretation is most likely to fail and what would change it: which alternative reading of Stevens, or which version of the optimist's reply, would force you to revise your verdict.
