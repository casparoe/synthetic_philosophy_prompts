I'm TAing an undergrad decision theory course and I'm writing the grading key for a short-answer question. I'd like a point-by-point assesment I can work from, not just a general impression. The question I set was: "Suppose you're offered the same favorable bet over and over: each round you stake some fraction of your current bankroll, a win returns your stake plus b times your stake, and a loss costs you the stake. (Win probability p, loss probability q = 1 - p.) Explain what the Kelly criterion tells you to stake, why people gloss it as maximizing the geometric mean or long-run growth rate of wealth, and whether it deserves to be called the uniquely rational way to size bets."

One student wrote: "The Kelly criterion says to stake the fraction f = (bp - q)/b of your bankroll each round. The usual argument is that this maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth. Since log wealth adds up across rounds, maximizing expected log wealth is equivalent to maximizing the long-run growth rate of wealth: over n independent rounds, (1/n) log W_n converges to E[log(1 + fX)] by the weak law of large numbers, so the f with the highest E[log] grows fastest with probability 1. This also means Kelly maximizes expected wealth after enough rounds. Full Kelly avoids ruin in the sense that it never stakes the whole bankroll, so a single loss can't wipe you out. Some people say Kelly is too aggressive, but that criticism can't be right: the criterion follows from the axioms of expected utility theory once you adopt log utility, and since any rational agent must have a concave utility function, log utility is the canonical choice for everyone."
