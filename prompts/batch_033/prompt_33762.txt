I was diagnosed with generalized anxiety disorder about six months ago, and one of the strangest things about the experience has been noticing that when my anxiety spikes, the world doesn't just feel more threatening — it perceptually looks different. Stairs seem steeper, distances longer, neutral faces more hostile. My therapist says this is well-established: emotion and cognition shape perception. But when I started reading the literature, I discovered that whether cognition can genuinely penetrate perception is one of the most stubbornly stalled debates in cognitive science, with sophisticated experimental programs on both sides and no convergence after decades.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, not a verdict on who is right. My working hypothesis, from a non-specialist's reading, is that the two sides operationalize "perception" differently: impenetrabilists mean early sensory processing, which is close to stipulatively encapsulated, while penetrabilists mean conscious perceptual experience, which almost trivially admits cognitive influence. If that's right, the debate is largely verbal. But I've also encountered the reply that this is too quick — that there is a genuine empirical question about whether top-down signals modulate processing within the visual system proper, prior to post-perceptual judgment, and that experiments can in principle settle it. Which diagnosis is better supported by what each side actually argues? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new experimental paradigm, a conceptual distinction, a formal model, or something else?
