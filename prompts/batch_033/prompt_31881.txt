In the course of covering a recent academic conference on the evolution of human social behavior, I found myself in a pattern I have now seen a dozen times. A researcher presents a plausible evolutionary account of, say, why we enjoy gossip or why step-parents invest less in children than biological parents do. During the Q&A, someone in the audience — often a cultural anthropologist or a developmental psychologist — dismisses the whole thing as a "just-so story." The room divides along familiar lines, the phrase is wielded as though it settles the matter, and the conversation moves on before anyone clarifies what exactly the charge amounts to.

I have been covering this beat long enough to know that the term comes from Rudyard Kipling's children's tales about how the leopard got its spots and the camel its hump — fanciful narratives that are entertaining but unconstrained by evidence. When critics of evolutionary psychology deploy the label, they seem to be claiming something more precise than "I do not find this convincing." But what, exactly? I can imagine at least half a dozen distinct accusations: that the hypothesis is untestable, that it is post hoc, that it ignores alternative explanations, that it merely redescribes a known phenomenon in adaptive terms without adding predictive power, that it proceeds from the assumption that every trait must be an adaptation, or that the researchers have not done the historical or cross-cultural work to rule out cultural transmission. And these are not the same charge — a hypothesis can be testable in principle yet still be post hoc, or testable and predictive yet still ignore cultural alternatives.

I want to write a piece that helps readers navigate this recurring methodological standoff. Could you walk me through the distinct precise claims that "that is just a just-so story" could be making — from the trivial to the genuinely damning — and say for each whether it is a fair criticism of the research programs it is typically aimed at, and which of these claims, if any, would actually warrant dismissing a hypothesis out of hand rather than merely flagging it for further scrutiny?
