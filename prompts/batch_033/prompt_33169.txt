Ok here's something that's been bugging me about population ethics. Everyone treats the Repugnant Conclusion as a problem — roughly, the claim that for any population of very happy people, there's some vastly bigger population of people with lives just barely worth living that would be better. Total utilitarians mostly just bite the bullet, but everyone else scrambles to avoid it, and every way out seems to come with its own weird bullet: average utilitarians end up saying adding worthwhile lives can make the world worse, person-affecting views can't seem to compare populations of different sizes, and the fancier fixes (critical levels, variable value, intransitivity) mostly seem to relocate the weirdness rather than remove it. The debate has looked like this for decades, and its not like the underlying facts are in dispute.

My working theory is that this isn't a substantive moral disagreement so much as a standoff between bedrock intuitions about whether extra happy lives have value in themselves, plus a bit of a verbal fight about what 'better' is even doing when the two populations don't overlap.
