Two colleagues in my department got into a long argument yesterday about whether a mutual friend can rationally decide to have a child. I'll reconstruct the exchange as best I remember it.

(1) Elena said the friend can't make a rational decision, because to decide whether the outcome of having a child is better than the outcome of not having one, she'd need to know what it's like to be a parent — and she can't know that until she is one. The experience changes what she values, so she can't assign credences to outcomes using her current values.

(2) David replied that this can't be right, because it would mean almost no major life decision is rational — changing careers, emigrating, getting married — and we'd have to conclude that nearly everyone acts irrationally nearly all the time. That's a reductio.

(3) Elena said that the reductio might just be true: standard decision theory may simply break down for these cases. But she added that having a child is especially radical because it changes your very self — not just your circumstances but what you care about, what you find fulfilling.

(4) David said that testimony from other parents is evidence. Most people who have children report being glad they did, and we can observe their lives and see whether they seem happy. That's exactly the kind of information rational agents use under uncertainty.

(5) Elena answered that testimony doesn't solve the problem, because the person giving the testimony is the transformed self. The fact that the post-transformation self is glad doesn't tell the pre-transformation self whether the transformation is worth undergoing. The pre-transformation self might not share the post-transformation self's values at all.

(6) David said that's no different from any decision that affects your future self. You save for retirement even though your future self will have different preferences; you take a job that will change your tastes. If we refused to make decisions whose outcomes would be evaluated differently by different selves, we'd be paralyzed.

(7) At that point they noticed I'd been listening and asked me to weigh in, but I couldn't do it on the spot. Please adjudicate move by move: rate the strength of each of their points, say who has the better of each exchange, and give a final verdict on who is more right overall — and what, if anything, both are missing.
