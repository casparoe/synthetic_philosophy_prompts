In a graduate seminar on international security, a classmate repeatedly invoked the old maxim "If you want peace, prepare for war" to shut down a debate about whether a state should enlarge its nuclear arsenal, shorten its launch decision times, and advertise a greater willingness to escalate early in a crisis. Whenever anyone pushed back, he simply repeated the slogan more slowly, as though it were self-evidently decisive. I am convinced the sentence is doing real argumentative work for him, but I cannot tell exactly what that work is, because the words are elastic enough to cover several very different claims.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this maxim might be used to assert? I am especially interested in the range from fairly weak readings — something like "a credible capacity to retaliate can sometimes deter an attack" — to much stronger readings, such as "any increase in military capability or readiness makes armed conflict less likely, everywhere and always." For each reading, I would like to know whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical claim, or close to false; and, crucially, which of the readings would actually be needed to support the specific conclusion he was drawing about expanding an arsenal and adopting a more aggressive crisis posture. Are there also readings under which the slogan, far from licensing his conclusion, actually cuts against it once one considers arms-race dynamics or the risk of accidental or inadvertent escalation?
