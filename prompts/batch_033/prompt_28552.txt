I keep coming back to the Dutch book argument for probabilism, and I've been defending a pretty strong version of it in a reading group. The version I like says: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there's a package of bets, each of which looks fair to you on its own, that together guarantee you loose money no matter how the world turns out; and the fact that you're vulnerable to this is supposed to show that your credences are irrational. I want to know how much pressure this argument can actually take before it stops being a justification and becomes just a vivid story.

So here's the position I'm asking you to attack: the Dutch book argument shows that rational degrees of belief must satisfy the probability axioms, and the sure-loss vulnerability is doing real justificatory work, not merely dramatizing an inconsistency we already had independent reason to reject. What would have to be true for this to fail? I want a map of the load-bearing assumptions — the package principle (that accepting each bet commits you to the whole set), the idea that the bets are each evaluated individually, that the payoffs are in money and utility is linear over the stakes involved, that being immune to sure loss is what rationality consists in, that the agent's future self honors the bets diachronic books depend on, and so on — and for each one, what a rational agent or a world would have to look like for the assumption to give out. Which of these are live escape routes and which are merely logical possibilities? I also want an honest take on the broader worry that Dutch books diagnose incoherence without explaining why coherence is rationally required, and that accuracy-based arguments have maybe taken over the justificatory role. Would a committed Dutch book person concede that as counting against them, or dismiss it as a different question? I'd also like to know whether the Dutch book framing predicts anything about real agents that a purely accuracy-based story doesn't. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether the view ends up insulated by qualifications — and if so, whether that insulation is a problem. Personally I'd find a realistically described agent who violates the axioms and yet seems obviously rational more persuasive than a formal counterexample, but I'm not sure whether that's a fair demand.
