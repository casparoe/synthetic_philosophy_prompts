I teach a graduate seminar on collective agency and social ontology, and we have been working through the discursive dilemma—sometimes called the doctrinal paradox—at some length. The standard illustration runs as follows. A three-judge appellate panel must decide a contract dispute that turns on two issues: whether a valid contract was formed, and whether it was breached. The court's doctrine says the plaintiff prevails only if she wins on both. The judges vote:

- Judge A: valid contract (yes), breach (yes) → plaintiff wins.
- Judge B: valid contract (yes), breach (no) → plaintiff loses.
- Judge C: valid contract (no), breach (yes) → plaintiff loses.

If the panel aggregates votes premise-by-premise, the plaintiff wins on contract 2–1 and on breach 2–1, so she should win the case—yet a majority of the same judges (B and C) would vote to reject her claim if asked for the bottom line directly. The paradox is clean and familiar.

But real multi-member courts do not, in fact, resolve this by picking one aggregation method and sticking with it. What they do instead—and here I am thinking especially of the U.S. Supreme Court but also of intermediate appellate courts—is let each judge vote on the disposition and then write separately on the reasoning, with the result that we routinely get plurality opinions: a majority converges on the outcome but no single rationale commands majority support. Lower courts are then left to extract binding precedent under something like the Marks rule, which treats the narrowest opinion concurring in the judgment as controlling. That rule has no deep philosophical justification that I have ever seen; it is a practical fix, and a contested one at that.

I want my students to see that these are not merely procedural accidents. They reflect substantive—and contestable—philosophical commitments about what a collective judgment even is. So here is the question I would like a committed answer to, one that takes a definite position and defends it rather than surveying possibilities. What theory of group judgment does the actual practice of outcome-first voting with separate rationale-voting presuppose? Does it presuppose, for instance, that a collective agent exists only at the level of the verdict and not at the level of the reasons that support it, or that consistency is required of individual judges but not of the court as a body? Is that presupposition defensible? And if we instead insisted that a collective judgment must be consistent all the way down—premises and conclusions alike—what would have to change structurally in how multi-member courts operate? I am less interested in what the case law says than in what the practice implicitly commits us to, philosophically, and whether we should own up to it or revise the practice. Please write in American English.
