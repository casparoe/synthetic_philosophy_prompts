I'm applying for a one-semester research fellowship in early modern philosophy and need to turn a half-formed idea into a two-page proposal. The idea concerns the place of self-interest in Leibniz's moral psychology. On the standard reading, Leibniz is pulled in two directions. On one side, he holds that the will always follows the intellect's representation of the greatest apparent good, and that all striving is ultimately for the agent's own perfection and happiness. On the other side, he characterizes justice as benevolence governed by wisdom and love as taking pleasure in another's well-being, and he denies that morality reduces to prudence. The long-standing critical worry is that the first commitment collapses the second into enlightened self-interest: if I benefit another only because doing so increases my own perfection or pleasure, then my real object is my own good, and the other person figures merely as an occasion for it. But I suspect the standard debate assumes a clean separation between self-regarding and other-regarding motivation that Leibniz's account of pleasure, perfection, and love is designed to undercut. The real question, then, is whether Leibniz can draw a principled line between moral motivation and mere prudence, or whether he only gets the line back by relying on theological premises about the harmony of interests in the best possible world.
