I'm a novelist working on a book whose central character undergoes a procedure that gradually replaces all of her memories, dispositions, and character traits over the course of a year, while her body remains biologically continuous throughout. The novel is told in close first person, and the philosophical question of whether the narrator at the end is the same person as the narrator at the beginning is meant to be genuinely unsettled — not just for the reader, but metaphysically. In working out the rules of the story world, I've ended up with a set of views about persistence and identity that I'd like pressure-tested before I commit the novel's structure to them. I'm not a philosopher by training, so I may be using terms loosely; please hold me to their standard meanings and tell me if I'm inadvertently contradicting myself.

Here is where I've landed:

(1) I am an endurantist about persons. A person is wholly present at each moment she exists, not spread out across time as a series of temporal parts. When I say "she is the same person," I mean numerical identity — one and the same entity — not merely qualitative similarity.

(2) I hold that psychological continuity is the criterion of personal identity over time: what makes a person at t₂ numerically identical to a person at t₁ is the right kind of overlapping chain of psychological connectedness — memories, intentions, beliefs, character traits, and so on — regardless of whether the body is the same.

(3) I accept the "only x and y" principle: whether a later person y is identical to an earlier person x depends only on facts intrinsic to x and y and the relations between them, not on what happens to any third party. If x = y, that fact cannot be undermined by the existence of someone else who also stands in the same relations to x.

(4) I think personal identity is always determinate. For any case, there is a fact of the matter about whether A is the same person as B, even if we lack epistemic access to it. There are no vague objects and no vague identity facts.

(5) I think the first-person perspective carries metaphysical weight. There is something it is like to be a particular person, and facts about what it is like for me are not fully captured by third-person descriptions of psychological states. This is connected to why I find the question of my character's survival urgent in a way that is not just about which description we apply.

I can feel that some of these are pulling against each other, but I cannot articulate exactly where the problems are. Go through these and identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each, say what would have to give to resolve it. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to stabilize the set — and what it would cost the novel.
