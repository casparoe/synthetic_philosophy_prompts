I chair the finance and capital projects subcommittee of my university's board of trustees. Last fall I heard the dollar auction described for the first time, and I have not been able to stop seeing it in the decision now before us. For eighteen months the university has been competing with a peer institution to be selected as the host of a federally funded research center. The selection process has taken on the structure of an auction: each round requires both finalists to make additional, non-recoverable commitments—search consultants, site surveys, matching funds for laboratory space, salary top-ups for named investigators—and only the eventual host receives the center. We have completed three rounds. Our commitments so far total roughly $4.3 million, while our own analysis puts the center's total expected value to the university, after operating costs and including reputational and recruitment benefits, at about $5 million. The funding agency has indicated that the next round will be the last. The provost now asks us to approve a fourth round of commitments totaling $1.8 million. He argues that refusing now would guarantee that the $4.3 million was spent for nothing, while approving keeps our bid alive against a rival that has committed a comparable amount and faces the same arithmetic. Several trustees reply that this is precisely the escalation of commitment the dollar auction was meant to expose, and that the only defensible move is to stop before the loss deepens.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame—authorize the fourth round, authorize it with conditions, or decline and withdraw—and it should honestly engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than caricature it. The full board will vote on the basis of this memo, so it needs an actionable bottom line and reasons a trustee with no background in decision theory could defend, not a general essay on sunk costs.
