The debate over whether behavioral criteria — from the Turing test onward — are sufficient for attributing mind has been running for over seventy years without, it seems, much movement. Both sides have their canonical moves: the behavioral sufficiency camp points to the problem of other minds and asks what more there could be to having a mind than passing the tests we use to attribute minds to one another; the opposite camp offers thought experiments (Searle's Chinese Room, Block's Blockhead) in which a system passes every behavioral test but is intuitively mindless, and takes those intuitions as data. The same arguments and counterarguments have been recycled for decades, and the debate seems to have the structure of a stalemate in which neither side can land a decisive blow on the other's terms. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is. Is it a disagreement about the metaphysics of mind — about what mental states are — or about the epistemology of other minds, or about the semantics of mental terms (what we mean by 'understand', 'think', 'conscious')? Or is it, as some have suggested, a clash of methodological commitments about what counts as an explanation and what kind of evidence thought experiments can provide? Support your diagnosis by examining what each side treats as its central, non-negotiable datum, and then say what, if anything, would count as progress. If your diagnosis is that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, identify the questions and say whether either is worth pursuing on its own.
