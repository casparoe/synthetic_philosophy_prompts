I am a retired geologist who has recently taken up political philosophy, and I have become preoccupied with a question that sits upstream of every other question in democratic theory: the boundary problem, the problem of saying who should belong to the demos before we ask how the demos should decide. My professional habit is to want distinctions sharp enough to be tested, and this debate resists that, so I would like to see it staged properly.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent proponents of opposing views on the boundary problem. One defends the all-affected-interests principle: roughly, that anyone whose interests are meaningfully affected by a collective decision should have some standing in the process that makes it. The other defends the all-subjected principle: roughly, that the demos should be composed of those who are subject to the coercive enforcement of the decision, or of the legal order under which it is made. Have the characters respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another, and have them work through at least one concrete case, such as a coastal city whose flood-control project shifts water toward a neighboring district, or a wealthy state whose climate policy raises energy costs for workers abroad. They should also engage the familiar difficulties: the all-affected principle seems to make the demos indefinitely wide, while the all-subjected principle seems to make coercion bear a moral weight that may be incidental to why someone has a claim to be heard; and both face a regress worry about who decides who counts as affected or subjected. The dialogue need not resolve; its point is to show why each position remains attractive under the other's best objections. Please format it as an exchange with clear speaker labels, and do not include citations or bibliography.
