I've been reading philosophy blogs and forums for years — LessWrong, some academic philosophy blogs, the occasional Reddit thread that doesn't immediately decend into chaos — and I keep noticing a pattern that I can't put a name to. It shows up across completely different debates: free will, the existence of the self, whether moral facts are real, even some arguments about God. The pattern has four moves, and once you start seeing it you can't unsee it.

Here's what I mean. Someone takes a position on one of these topics — say, "the self exists as a unified thing." Their critic points out that if you try to locate this self, you can't find it independent of thoughts, memories, sensations; it seems to depend on them, so it can't be a fundamental thing. "Okay," the original person says, "then the self doesn't exist — it's an illusion." But now the critic says: in calling it an illusion, you're still relying on some concept of what a self is; you can't deny it without invoking it. "Fine, the self both exists and doesn't exist — it's a useful fiction that's real in its effects." But that's just a contradiction, and now you're not saying anything coherent. "Alright, then the self neither exists nor doesn't exist — it's beyond categories." But that sure sounds like a claim about the self, and if it's beyond categories, why are we using language to make claims about it at all? I've seen this exact four-pronged structure — exists, doesn't exist, both, neither — deployed in debates about free will (libertarian, hard determinist, compatibilist, mysterian), about abstract objects, about whether there are moral facts. Sometimes it's used sincerely as a refutation of all possible positions; other times it reads as a rhetorical trap that makes it impossible to say anything at all without being shown inconsistent. I get the sense there's a whole tradition that takes this structure seriously, maybe in Indian or Buddhist philosophy, but I don't know the vocabularly.

What I'm after is: does this four-pronged argument structure have an established name, and who has developed it most systematically? I want to understand whether it's meant as a tool for refuting all views (in which case, does it refute itself?) or as something else entirely — a way of pointing at something that can't be captured in any of the four positions. If there are multiple traditions that use versions of it, I'd like to know how they differ in what they do with it. And if the nearest established concept doesn't quite match what I'm describing, I want to know exactly where the mismatch is. I'm not just looking for a label; I want the carving.
