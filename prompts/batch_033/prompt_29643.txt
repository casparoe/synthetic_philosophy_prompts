A colleague and I have been arguing for months about how deep the Moloch problem goes, and I want to locate the disagreement precisely rather than keep trading examples. My credence is around 0.7 on the following claim: competitive dynamics that produce collectively self-defeating outcomes — multipolar traps, races to the bottom, arms-race equilibria — are an irreducible feature of social life, in the sense that you cannot fully explain them away by appealing to individual bad values, mistaken beliefs, or correctable institutional failures. Her credence is around 0.3; she thinks traps are real but largely derivative of things we can fix without solving coordination problems de novo.

I want your own credence on that same claim, but more importantly I want the decomposition that produces it. Please assign a probability to each of the following sub-questions, with a sentence of justification, and then show how they combine:

(1) What fraction of the major social outcomes we collectively lament — environmental degradation, arms proliferation, the attention economy, cost escalation in positional goods, regulatory capture — are best modeled as Nash equilibria of competitive games, rather than as straightforward expressions of the preferences or values of the most powerful actors?

(2) Of those competitive equilibria, what fraction are genuine traps: cases where there exists an alternative equilibrium that every participant would prefer, but no unilateral deviation can reach it?

(3) Of those genuine traps, what fraction would dissolve if all participants had accurate shared information about the game they are in and the payoffs?

(4) Of the traps that survive better information, what fraction would be resolved by institutional changes that are feasible given existing political and economic constraints — that is, changes someone with power could actually implement and sustain?

(5) For the residual — traps not soluble by better information or feasible institutional redesign — are they fundamental (the structure of the situation makes a preferred equilibrium unreachable even in principle, absent an external coordinating authority that doesn't exist) or do they ultimately trace back to incompatible values that no amount of structural tinkering could reconcile?

Combine these into your overall credence on the claim, and tell me which term carries the most weight in the gap between my 0.7 and her 0.3. Finally: what empirical finding or conceptual argument would shift your own number the most, and in which direction?
