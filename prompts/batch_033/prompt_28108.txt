I'm giving a talk next month at an AI safety workshop arguing that when we design AI systems to make morally-loaded decisions, we should build them to reason roughly as deontologists — following rules, respecting side-constraints, treating certain acts as impermissible regardless of consequences — rather than as utilitarians. The audience is mostly ML engineers and technical alignment researchers who default to expected-value reasoning and think of ethics as basically "maximize utility, figure out the utility function later." There will also be a couple of philosophers in the room who are sympathetic to consequentialism.

My core argument, roughly: (1) any AI system we build in the forseeable future will be operating under severe uncertainty about the downstream consequences of its actions, and deontological constraints are more robust under radical uncertainty than utility calculations; (2) side-constraints are easier to specify, verify, and maintain under distribution shift than a global utility function; (3) in multi-stakeholder settings, deontological rules make an AI's behavior more predictable and contestable by the humans affected, which is a practical necessity for real deployment.
