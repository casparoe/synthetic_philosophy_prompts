I serve on the honorary degrees committee at my university, and we're considering a nominee who has me deeply conflicted. The nominee is a former pharmaceutical CEO, now a major philanthropist. Thirty years ago, his company discovered that one of its widely prescribed drugs had a rare but fatal side effect — roughly one in ten thousand patients would die from it. An immediate recall would have bankrupted the company, which at the time was a small firm with a promising pipeline, including a drug in late-stage trials for a pediatric cancer that had no other treatment. He made the call to delay the recall by eight months, long enough to get the cancer drug approved and secure the company's finances. During those eight months, roughly forty people died from the side effect who would have lived if the drug had been pulled immediately. The cancer drug went on to save thousands of children's lives. The CEO has always been open about this decision; he says he chose to sacrifice forty to save thousands, and that anyone who calls it wrong is refusing to do the moral arithmetic.

Several of my colleagues on the committee see this as a textbook case of courageous leadership: he made a hard choice, took responsibility, and the numbers speak for themselves. The nomination package even uses the phrase "the willingness to do what was necessary." But I can't shake the feeling that there's something fundamentally wrong about honoring a man who looked at forty people and decided, deliberately, that they would die so that others might live — even if the arithmetic works out. I've tried to articulate this to myself, but I keep getting tangled: am I just being squeamish about the kind of calculation that any serious leader has to make? Or is there a principled reason to distinguish what he did from genuinely tragic choices where the bad outcome is foreseen but not intended as a means? I'm supposed to vote in two weeks, and I'd like to think through whether my unease is philosophically defensible or just a reluctance to face hard realities.
