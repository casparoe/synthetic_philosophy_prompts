I teach an undergraduate course on modality and I want to spend a session on the debate between counterpart theory and transworld identity, using Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life as the anchoring text. The film's premise, as I understand it, is that an angel shows George Bailey what his town would have been like had George never existed: Bedford Falls becomes Pottersville, and the people George loves become coarser, unhappier, or dead. Please read the Bedford Falls/Pottersville sequence through the lens of this debate. Does the film's moral argument depend on treating the Pottersville versions of Mary, Uncle Billy, and the town as literally the same individuals existing in another possible world, or would counterpart relations of similarity be enough to make the film's point? Would a committed counterpart theorist and a defender of strict transworld identity disagree about what the angel actually shows George, and would either find the film's modal metaphysics coherent? I want a reading that takes both philosophical positions seriously and does not simply use the film as decoration; it should be willing to say where the film quietly helps itself to an assumption the debate cannot settle.

A further wrinkle I want addressed: George observes the no-George world as an outsider, and in that world he seems to have no counterpart at all, because he was never born. Does this make the film a counterexample to the counterpart-theoretic treatment of de re modal claims, or does it show only that George's case is unusual? Also consider whether the film's famous thesis that a single life has far-reaching effects on many others is better captured by counterpart theory, by transworld identity plus a kind of subtraction from a shared world-history, or by neither. I would like the reading to end by saying what the film contributes to the philosophical debate that a bare argument does not, and where its cinematic presentation of absence may mislead.
