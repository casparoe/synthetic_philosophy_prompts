I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of psychiatry, and I'd like a decision tree that sorts readers into positions on what psychiatric categories are. The tree should have five or six leaves. The positions that must appear are: strong biological realism (psychiatric categories are natural kinds, like chemical elements, and looping effects are epistemologically irrelevant noise), Hacking-style dynamic nominalism (categories are real but interactive — they change the objects they classify through looping effects, and this looping is central to what they are), strong social constructionism (categories fabricate their objects and would not exist without the classificatory practice), a dimensional view (the categories are arbitrary cut-points on continuous dimensions of real variation, so there are no discrete kinds), and a deflationary view that treats the categories as clinical instruments and regards the realism question as misplaced.

Build the tree from the logical space first: identify the claims on which these positions come apart, phrase each as a concrete question someone can answer without knowing the labels, then map the positions onto the answer combinations. Flag any logically possible combination that has no defenders and say briefly why — is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored? Also flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate with the questions you've chosen, and say what further question would split them.

The questions should not be transparently about metaphysics. For instance, instead of asking "are you a realist?", ask something like: "Suppose a biomarker for major depression is discovered — a blood test with high sensitivity and specificity. Would this settle, once and for all, the question of what depression is? Or would it leave something important out?" Each branching question should force a choice the positions genuinely disagree about, and the answer options should be phrased in ordinary language, not in terms of the philosophical positions themselves. Make at least one question turn on a concrete example of a looping effect — for instance, the way receiving an autism diagnosis can reshape a person's self-understanding and life narrative — and ask whether that phenomenon bears on what kind of thing the category is.
