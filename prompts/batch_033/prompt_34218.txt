I've been covering the replication crisis and the collapse of public trust in institutions for a few years now, and it keeps pushing me back toward a question that's older than it looks. The Euthyphro dilemma asks whether the gods love the pious because it's pious, or whether it's pious because the gods love it. But the structure of the problem isn't really about gods — it's about any authority you might consult to settle a question. When does the authority's say-so make something the case, and when does the authority merely register, well or badly, what was already the case independently? I've been collecting my own snap judgments on cases across different domains, and I'd like to know whether there's a principle that captures the pattern they seem to trace — or whether the pattern is an illusion and I need to start overturning verdicts.

Here are the cases, as neutrally as I can state them, along with what my gut says.

1. **Chess arbiter.** A player claims a draw by the fifty-move rule. The arbiter checks the scoresheet and declares the game a draw. My gut: the game is a draw because the arbiter declared it. Before the declaration, it wasn't a draw in the game, even though the conditions were met — the draw exists in the game only once the arbiter says so. The arbiter's say-so is constitutive.

2. **Strep test.** A doctor swabs my throat, runs a rapid test, and tells me I have a streptococcal infection. My gut: I have a strep infection because the bacteria are in my throat. The doctor's say-so doesn't make it true; it reflects an underlying biological fact. The doctor's authority is epistemic — they're a reliable tracker of something that's already there.

3. **Statutory interpretation.** The Supreme Court rules 6–3 that a vague provision of the Clean Air Act means X. Before the ruling, sophisticated lawyers disagreed about whether it meant X or Y. After the ruling, my gut says: legally, it means X. The Court's say-so settles what the law is, not merely what we think it is. The authority here feels constitutive, not epistemic.

4. **Parent on stealing.** A parent tells their eight-year-old that stealing is wrong. The child asks why, and the parent says "because I said so." My gut: stealing is wrong independently of the parent's say-so. The parent is doing their best to track a moral fact that's already there. The parent's authority is epistemic — or at most, it's a legitimate shortcut for a child who can't yet grasp the underlying reasons.

5. **Friend on offensiveness.** I tell a joke at a dinner party. Later, a friend whose judgment I trust tells me the joke was offensive. My gut says: the friend is probably right, and I shouldn't have told it. But I'm not sure whether the friend's say-so is evidence of an independent fact about the joke (that it was offensive) or whether offensiveness is partly constituted by reasonable people taking offense — in which case my friend's reaction helps make the joke offensive, rather than merely detecting it. This case feels slippery in a way the strep test doesn't, and I can't articulate why.

6. **Public health reversal.** In early 2020, a public health agency announces that masks are not necessary for the general public. Six weeks later, the same agency announces that masks are necessary. My gut: masks were necessary all along — the agency was wrong the first time and corrected itself the second. The agency's say-so doesn't constitute the necessity; it tracks it, imperfectly. The agency's authority is epistemic and fallible.

7. **Pope ex cathedra.** The Pope declares, speaking ex cathedra, that Mary was assumed bodily into heaven. For a believing Catholic, my gut says: the declaration makes it the case that this is a doctrine of the faith — the Pope's say-so is constitutive within the practice. For me, as a non-Catholic, the Pope's say-so is just one religious leader's opinion and carries no constitutive force at all. This suggests that whether an authority is constitutive depends on whether you're inside the relevant practice. But that can't be the whole story, because I'm not a lawyer, and I still think the Supreme Court's say-so is constitutive for me.

8. **Dictionary and "literally."** A major dictionary adds a new entry for "literally" as an intensifier, citing widespread usage. My gut: the dictionary isn't making "literally" an intensifier; it's reporting that it already is one. The dictionary's authority is purely epistemic — it tracks usage. But I also know that people appeal to the dictionary to settle arguments about what words mean, and at some point descriptive authority shades into normative authority. I can't pin down where.

9. **Hospital ethics committee.** A hospital ethics committee reviews a novel treatment protocol and votes that it is ethically permissible to proceed. My gut: the committee's say-so doesn't make the protocol permissible — it either is permissible on the merits or it isn't. But I also feel the committee's judgment carries more weight than a random bystander's, and I'm not sure whether that's just because the committee is more likely to be right (epistemic) or because institutional approval is itself part of what makes a medical protocol permissible in a regulated context (constitutive). This case feels like it straddles the line.

10. **Divine command and the binding of Isaac.** God commands Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac. If God commanded it, was it right to obey? My gut says no — killing an innocent child is wrong, and no command, not even a divine one, can make it right. But this means I think moral standards constrain even divine commands, which pushes me toward the "God commands it because it's right" horn. In that case, though, divine commands seem redundant as a source of moral knowledge — and they also seem to lose their normative force, since I'd be evaluating them against a standard I already have. That makes me wonder whether I'm smuggling in a commitment I haven't examined.

11. **Fashion.** The editor of Vogue declares that skinny jeans are out and wide-leg trousers are in. My gut: in the world of fashion, wide-leg trousers are in because the editor says so. There's no independent fact about trouser silhouettes that the editor is tracking. The authority is purely constitutive. But interestingly, I don't feel any pressure to wear wide-leg trousers — which suggests I don't take myself to be "inside" the fashion practice. And yet I do take myself to be inside the legal practice, even though I'm not a lawyer. What marks the difference?

I'm after a principle — or a small set of candidates — that can sort these cases into the epistemic and the constitutive and explain why the hard ones feel hard. In particular, I want to know: what feature of a domain, or of my relationship to it, determines whether an authority's say-so makes something the case or merely tracks it? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the best principle force me to abandon? I'd also like the principle to speak to a case I haven't listed: a professional historian declaring that a particular event happened. Is the historian's authority epistemic, constitutive, or something in between?
