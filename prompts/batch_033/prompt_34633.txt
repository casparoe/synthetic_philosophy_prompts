I'm giving a talk at my department's ethics workshop in a few weeks, and honestly I'm a bit nervous. The topic is standing to blame — specifically, I'm defending the view that hypocrites lack the standing to blame in a way that makes their blame defective *as blame*, not just rude or unfair. This has gotten weirdly personal for me lately because a senior colleague who's done some pretty shady stuff in the past decided to publicly call me out over a minor screwup, and I've been trying to figure out what exactly made it so galling. It wasn't just that I disliked being criticized — I think there was something genuinely wrong with the blame itself.

Anyway, my argument is roughly this: blame isn't just a judgment that someone did wrong; it's a kind of communicative act that presupposes certain things about the blamer's relationship to the norm they're invoking. When you're a hypocrite, one of those presuppositions fails, and the blame misfires in something like the way a promise misfires when you don't intend to keep it. So it's not just that the hypocrite is being inconsistent or unfair — their blame literally doesn't do what blame is supposed to do.

The audience is my department: mostly analytic philosophers, with a strong Kantian contingent (like, people who think the moral law applies regardless of who's doing the talking), a couple of Strawsonians who think about blame in terms of reactive attitudes, and one person who works on the semantics of normative language and will probably have something to say about presupposition failure. Plus my senior colleague — the one I mentioned — will probably be in the room. He's not on the workshop roster but he sometimes shows up.
