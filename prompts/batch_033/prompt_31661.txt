I am a philosopher who chairs a small interdisciplinary research group with no formal hierarchy, and we are revising our internal procedures for decisions in which any single member could, on their own initiative and without consulting the others, take an action — releasing a working paper, sharing a sensitive dataset, or writing to an outside body in the group's name, for example — that would be costly to reverse and would bind the whole group. Please give the five most important implications of the unilateralist's curse for how we should design those procedures. I am particularly interested in what the curse implies about the difference between decisions that are unilateral as a matter of official policy and those that are merely unilateral in practice, and about the role of common knowledge among members in triggering or blunting the curse. Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Please separate the implications that are settled structural consequences of the curse from those that are genuinely contested among people who have worked on it, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
