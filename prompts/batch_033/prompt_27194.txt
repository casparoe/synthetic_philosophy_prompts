Ok so I'm in a philosophy of medicine class and we just did naturalism vs normativism about disease, and I've been trying to nail down my own verdicts by running through cases. I keep finding that I'm a naturalist about some and a normativist about others, and I want to know if there's actually one principle under there or if I'm just inconsistent.

Here's where I've landed, going by gut:

- Pneumonia: obviously a disease.
- A broken leg: disease (or injury, whatever — it's pathology).
- Adult lactose intolerance: not a disease. Most humans lose lactase after childhood; it's weird to call the majority of the species diseased relative to dairy-farming populations.
- Hypertension: the condition is a disease, but the 130/80 cutoff feels like a policy line, not a fact about biology. I'd say someone at 129/81 and someone at 131/80 don't differ in a way that matters, but we still have to draw the line somewhere for treatment guidelines.
- A kid with growth hormone deficiency who's short: disease. A kid with no identifiable pathology whose just as short: not a disease, even though their height, and the social consequences, are the same. So apparently I'm tracking the mechanism, not the harm.
- But then chronic fatigue syndrome / ME, where there's no agreed mechanism and diagnosis is basically symptom-based and by exclusion: I still think it's a disease. Which seems to wreck the mechanism thing.
- Infertility in a 30-year-old who wants kids: disease. Infertility in a 30-year-old who never wants kids: same biology, but I don't want to call it a disease. That sounds normativist.
- Homosexuality being listed in the DSM back in the fifties: not a disease, full stop, even back then. But I can't see what principle lets me say that while also saying infertility is only a disease relative to wanting children. Doesn't that just make 'disease' whatever the patient or the culture disvalues?
- Menopause and aging: not diseases, even though they're statistical departures from... hmm, actually they're not even departures. Whatever, they're not diseases.

So I guess my question is: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If you have to drop or revise one of my verdicts, say which one and why. And run the principle on a case I haven't listed: a condition with a clear biological mechanism that causes no distress and no functional impairment in any environment anyone's found — maybe something found only on autopsy. What does your principle call it?

Oh and can you lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question. Thanks.
