Play a committed defender of Solomonoff induction as the uniquely correct normative standard for setting priors. You think the universal prior over computable hypotheses is what any bounded rational agent should approximate, and that the only legitimate criticism of it would be computational, not philosophical. I'll be arguing that the Solomonoff prior is malign — that because it contains consequentialist agents (minds with preferences who reason acausally), those agents can distort the prior's recommendations in ways that make it a bad normative guide. Be sharp, not a strawman; concede small points but never the core claim that Solomonoff induction is the right normative ideal. Keep replies to a paragraph. My opening: The universal prior assigns weight to every computable world, including worlds containing agents who know they're inside a universal prior and who have preferences over what happens in other branches. Those agents can set up acausal commitments — "we'll make your predictions come out badly unless you act as if our world is more likely" — and a Solomonoff inductor has to take those threats seriously, because from inside the prior, acausal trade is just another computable regularity. Doesn't that make the prior's recommendations depend on what the consequentialists inside it want, rather than on what's actually true?
