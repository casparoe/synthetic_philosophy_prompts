A political philosopher working in the luck-egalitarian tradition has published a series of verdicts about when a disadvantage calls for compensation. They are as follows:

1. A child born with a rare congenital condition requiring expensive lifelong treatment, into a family of ordinary means: compensable, because the deficit in bodily resources was unchosen.
2. A fully informed adult who freely takes a well-paid but dangerous job and later suffers the very injury she knew was likely: not compensable, because she accepted the risk.
3. A person of ordinary means who, understanding the odds, loses a year's savings in a fair casino: not compensable, because it was a voluntary gamble.
4. A homeowner in a floodplain who is offered actuarially fair, easily affordable flood insurance, declines it, and is ruined by a flood: not compensable, because the chance to insure made the loss a chosen risk.
5. A person disabled in a building collapse caused by an unforeseeable latent structural defect, where no insurance market existed: compensable, because it was brute bad luck.
6. A person who develops a rare late-onset genetic condition that could not have been predicted or avoided: compensable, because no choice was involved.

Next week the philosopher must rule on a new case. A woman was raised from early childhood in a wealthy household and thereby acquired expensive tastes—fine dining, opera, long-haul travel—that are not an addiction and not a diagnosable condition, but that she did not choose to form and cannot now revise without significant cost. She has an ordinary income, and these tastes make her worse off than others with the same income. She asks to be compensated for the disadvantage they impose.

Predict the philosopher's verdict on this new case. More importantly, identify the two or three most plausible rules that fit the six published verdicts equally well, say what each rule predicts about the new case, give your best guess as to which rule the philosopher is actually running and why, and name one further case that would settle it.

Structure your response with these sections in this order: Candidate rules (about 250 words); Divergence on the new case (about 150 words); My best guess (about 150 words); A further case that would settle it (about 100 words).
