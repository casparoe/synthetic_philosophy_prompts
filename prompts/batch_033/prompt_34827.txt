I keep noticing a pattern in how people assign moral blame to different ways of causing false beliefs, and I'm trying to find out whether the philosophical literature has a name for the cluster of issues it raises — or whether the pattern is even recognized as real.

Here are two central cases:

1. A colleague asks whether I've read her draft. I haven't, but I say "Yes, I read it over the weekend." I've lied.

2. A colleague asks whether I've read her draft. I haven't, but I say "I spent the whole weekend thinking about the issues you raised." She infers, reasonably, that I read it. I haven't said anything false, but I've deliberately caused her to form a false belief.

Most people I ask — and I've asked enough to see a pattern — judge case 1 more harshly, even when I stipulate that the downstream consequences are identical: same false belief, same disappointment later, same damage to trust. The asymmetry isn't confined to social politeness. It persists when I vary the stakes: medical disclosures, financial negotiations, courtroom testimony. Something about the bare false assertion seems to attract a distinct kind of condemnation that the merely misleading true statement does not.

But the boundary turns out to be unstable in ways that interest me more than the central cases. Consider:

Case 3: The same words as case 2, but I deliver them with a theatrical wink to a third party, so that my insincerity is obvious to an audience but not to my colleague. Some people say this is now a lie, because the wink cancels the social meaning of the words. Others say it's still merely misleading, because the words themselves remain true.

Case 4: I say "Yes, I read it" but in a tone so flat and robotic that I'm confident my colleague will detect sarcasm. She doesn't. She takes it literally. Is this a lie? People who focus on the words say yes; people who focus on the intended communicative effect say no.

Case 5: I'm selling a used car. The buyer asks about the engine. I say "It was serviced at the dealership six months ago" — true. I omit that the service was for a catastrophic defect the dealership couldn't fix, and the car has been sitting in my garage ever since. The words are true, the implication is false, and the omission does a lot of the work.

Case 6: I say "The engine was serviced at the dealership six months ago" knowing that, in my community, this is conventionally understood as an assurance that the car runs well. I'm relying on the convention to do the misleading. If the convention is robust enough, does my statement become a lie in that community even though the words are literally true?

I'm trying to get clear on two things. First, is there an established name for the position that this moral asymmetry between lying and misleading is real and important — that is, that there is a morally significant difference between asserting a falsehood and merely implicating one? And second, is there a name for the broader set of puzzles about where the boundary falls, especially the cases where conventional meaning, speaker intention, and audience inference come apart? I'm aware that philosophers distinguish lying from misleading, but I want the specific term for the view that the distinction carries moral weight, together with the boundary problems that challenge it. If the literature has a standard term for the contrary view — that there is no moral difference — I'd like that too.

I'm not just after a label. I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are. If the nearest named concept fits only imperfectly, I want to know exactly how my description differs from it and whether the difference is philosophically significant.
