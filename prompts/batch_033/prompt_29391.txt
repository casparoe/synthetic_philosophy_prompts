A colleague keeps arguing that the mind–brain relation is just the software–hardware relation: the same program can run on many different machines, and likewise the same mental state can be realized in many different physical systems. He uses the analogy to license two conclusions: that psychology can proceed without worrying much about neuroscientific detail, and that a silicon system with the right functional organization would literally think rather than merely simulate thinking. I suspect the analogy is doing more argumentative work than it can carry. Audit it. Set out the mapping explicitly—program, hardware, compilation, input/output, portability, and so on—then identify where the mapping breaks down, separating the breakdowns that matter for his two conclusions from harmless disanalogies. What features of software does the analogy quietly import into the mental—discreteness, clean interfaces, substrate independence, copyability—that may not belong there? Lead with your bottom line—your overall verdict on the analogy and on his two conclusions—in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Finish by saying whether the two conclusions survive the audit and whether some better analogy would support them more honestly.
