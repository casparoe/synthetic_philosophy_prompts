I'm a PhD student in statistics; my work is mostly on Bayesian hierarchical models and model-comparison methods. Lately I've been reading philosophy of science on the side, and I keep running into the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory. As I understand it, the core difficulty is this: if evidence E is already known — if your credence P(E) equals 1 — then by Bayes' theorem P(H | E) = P(H), so old evidence can never confirm a hypothesis. But scientists routinely treat long-known phenomena as evidence for new theories: the perihelion advance of Mercury was documented decades before Einstein used it to support general relativity, and the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass was known for centuries before it became evidence for the equivalence principle. So either Bayesian confirmation theory is missing something, or confirmation by old evidence needs to be redescribed in a way that does not show up in the formalism.

For each major move in the philosophical debate — the counterfactual approach, the logical-learning approach, and any other solution you take to be important — tell me what it looks like when expressed in the language of Bayesian statistical practice. Be precise: if a philosophical move corresponds to, say, replacing the actual prior with a counterfactual one, or to conditioning on a meta-level proposition about the model rather than on the data, say so explicitly.
