I've been trying to get clear on what separates mechanical rigidity from genuine flexibility — the distinction Dennett highlighted with the Sphex wasp, which will repeat its whole burrow-inspection routine from scratch if you move the cricket a few inches, and will do so indefinitely without ever catching on. I have verdicts on a range of cases, but I can't tell whether they add up to a coherent view.

Here they are:

The Sphex wasp itself. Verdict: pure mechanism. No flexibility, no agency worth talking about.

A chess grandmaster playing blitz. Most moves come automatically from pattern recognition built over decades. But if you present a genuinely novel board position, the player adapts — they're not locked into a fixed routine. Verdict: flexible, not Sphexish, even though much of the processing is unconscious and automatic.

My uncle reaching for his phone every few minutes. He doesn't decide to; he doesn't notice he's doing it; if you point it out he'll say "oh, right" and then do it again two minutes later. Verdict: Sphexish. He's a full human being with capacities he isn't deploying, but in this behavior he might as well be the wasp.

A recovering alcoholic at a wedding who feels the pull of the open bar, hesitates, and walks past it. This took effort and I want to say it's a paradigm of non-Sphexish action — genuine self-governance.

A large language model answering a subtle philosophical question. It's doing next-token prediction, which is as mechanical as anything could be. But its outputs track the question, adjust to counterarguments, and it can correct itself when you point out an error. I'm torn: it feels more flexible than the wasp, but I'm not sure it's flexible in the way that matters.

Someone deep in a conspiracy theory who treats every piece of counterevidence as further confirmation of the conspiracy. They're reasoning — drawing inferences, making distinctions — but within a perfectly sealed system. Verdict: Sphexish, despite the surface intelligence.

A novelist who realizes, halfway through a draft, that a character she's been writing for months wouldn't actually do what she'd planned, and who changes the plot to honor that discovery. Verdict: the opposite of Sphexish. Genuine responsiveness to something beyond the existing routine.

I can feel tensions among these verdicts. The language model and the chess grandmaster seem hard to distinguish in principle — both are trained systems producing outputs that adapt to novel inputs — but my gut treats them differently. And the conspiracy theorist and the novelist are both following something through to its conclusion, yet I judge them oppositely.

Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? If no principle gets them all, which ones should I overturn, and why? And what does the best principle say about a case I haven't listed: someone who adopted a decision procedure years ago — say, "I will always take the job that pays more" — and follows it without reconsideration, even when the circumstances have changed in ways that the original procedure was never designed to handle?
