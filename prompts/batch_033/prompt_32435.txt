I just got out of a long argument with my friend about revealed preference and I'm still trying to figure out what his actual position is. He's an economist and he insists you can only tell what someone prefers by what they actually choose — not by what they say they prefer. But I kept throwing edge cases at him and he kept making exceptions, and by the end I had this list of his verdicts but I can't tell what rule he's running.

Here's what he said:

- Someone buys the cheaper of two basically identical products. He says: they revealed a preference for the cheaper one, full stop. Even if they tell you they preferred the expensive one, the choice is what counts.
- Someone orders a salad when their friends are watching but a burger when they're alone. He says: the salad choice doesn't reveal their real preference — social pressure is an external constraint so the choice doesn't count as revealing.
- Someone buys a gym membership in January and never goes. He says: they revealed a preference for having the option to go, not for actually going. You have to look at what was actually chosen, which was the membership contract, not the exercise.
- Someone picks a dominated investment because they didn't read the prospectus. He says: this doesn't reveal their preference among the investments — they were uninformed, so the choice isn't informative about their investment preferences.
- Someone chooses a risky career, fails, and later says they regret it. He says: the choice still revealed their preference at the time. Regret doesn't undo it.

So here's the new case we didn't get to: someone donates to a charity after seeing one of those really manipulative ads — starving kids, sad music, the whole thing — and a week later, after thinking about it, they say they feel like they were emotionally manipulated and they don't actually endorse having made that donation. What would my friend say? Does this donation reveal a preference or not?

I'm less interested in the answer itself than in what rule he's implicitly running, what other rules would fit his verdicts just as well, and which of those rules would give different answers on the charity case. And if his verdicts are consistent with more than one rule, what's a furthur case that would definitively tell them apart?
