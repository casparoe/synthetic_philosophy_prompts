I've been thinking about self-referential rules — rules that refer to themselves, either to regulate their own amendment, to entrench themselves, or to declare their own status — and I'm trying to figure out whether there's a principled way to sort the coherent ones from the incoherent ones. I'm a law student, so some of these cases come from constitutional theory, but what I'm really after is the underlying logic, not the doctrine of any particular jurisdiction. My gut verdicts keep pulling me in different directions, and I want to know whether there's a single principle behind them or whether I have to live with a messier picture.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can give them. (1) A statute says "this statute may be repealed by a two-thirds majority of the legislature." This seems entirely unproblematic. It's just a rule laying out its own repeal conditions — self-reference of the benign, bookkeeping sort. (2) A constitutional provision says "this provision is not amendable under any circumstances." I find this puzzling but not paradoxical. It seems coherent for a community to decide, at the founding moment, that some things are off the table forever; but it also feels like a kind of metaphysical cheat — the founders are legislating for people who never consented. My verdict: coherent, but normatively suspect. (3) A rule says "this rule is invalid." I'm confident this is paradoxical in something like the way the Liar is. If it's valid, it says it's invalid; if it's invalid, it's a nullity. I think the right resolution is that it's simply invalid — a failed speech act, not a rule at all — but I'm not fully sure. (4) A club's bylaws include: "Any rule of this club may be amended by a majority vote of the members." The members then vote, by majority, to amend that rule to read: "Any rule of this club may be amended by a two-thirds majority vote." I think this is fine. The amendment was valid when made, and the rule now requires a supermajority going forward. (5) Same club, same starting rule. The majority votes to amend it to read: "Any rule of this club may be amended by a majority vote, except this rule, which may only be amended by unanimous vote." I think this is invalid. It feels like the majority is trying to use its power to irrevocably give up its power to change the amendment rule itself, and the original rule didn't authorize self-entrenchment of that kind. But I can't articulate why (4) is fine and (5) isn't, since both change the threshold for future amendments. (6) A statute says "this statute takes precedence over any conflicting statute, including any statute enacted in the future." I think this is legally toothless — the next legislature can just repeal it, the earlier statute's claim to precedence notwithstanding. Not paradoxical, just a rule that can't achieve what it attempts. (7) A rule of evidence says "this rule may be amended, but any amendment that makes it easier to admit hearsay is void." I think this is fine. It restricts the content of permissible amendments, not the amendment power itself. This feels different from (5) in a way I can't quite articulate. (8) A constitution says "the amendment procedure in Article X may itself be amended by the procedure in Article X." I think this is perfectly fine — the standard case, and nearly every real constitution does this. (9) A constitution says "Article X, which sets out the amendment procedure, may not itself be amended." I think this is coherent but striking — it freezes the amendment procedure forever. Not paradoxical, just a substantive choice with consequences I'm not sure the founders fully grasp.

I have a tentative principle I've been toying with, but it doesn't quite work. The principle is: a self-referring rule is valid if and only if it does not attempt to remove from future rule-makers a power that the rule itself was created under. The idea is that rule-makers can change the substance of rules going forward, but they can't use a self-referring rule to permanently alter who gets to make rules, or by what procedures. That would explain why (4) is fine — the majority used its majority power to raise the threshold, but didn't eliminate majority governance — and why (5) is not — the majority tried to remove the very power it was exercising. But the principle doesn't cleanly handle (2) or (9), and I'm not sure what it says about (6).

What I'd like from you: propose a principle (or a small set of them) that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely. Run it through all nine cases, explaining for each why it yields the verdict it does. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and why I should revise those rather than the principle. And give me verdicts on a few cases I haven't thought of — ideally ones that would cleanly distinguish your principle from my tentative one. I want a committed answer that takes a definite position and defends it, not a survey of the options.
