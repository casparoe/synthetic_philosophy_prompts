I'm taking a module on the metaphysics of moral responsibility, and last week's seminar on P. F. Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment' spilled over into a long argument at the pub between two of my coursemates. They both know the article well, but they came away from it with radically different assessments of what Strawson actually achieves, and I've been turning their exchange over in my head ever since. I'd be grateful for an adjudication.

The argument went roughly as follows. (1) Eleanor opened by saying that Strawson's great move is to show that the debate between compatibilists and incompatibilists rests on a mistake: both sides assume that the point of holding people responsible is to apply a kind of metaphysical verdict, but in fact our practices of blame, praise, and punishment are expressions of the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, and so on — which are natural, inescapable features of human interpersonal life. Because these attitudes are not theoretical commitments we can revise at will, she said, the question 'Would determinism make them unjustified?' does not really arise. (2) Marcus pushed back: 'That is too quick. We revise our reactive attitudes all the time. When I learn that someone's nasty behaviour stems from a genuinely terrible upbringing, my resentment typically softens — not because I have decided to, but because the new information changes how I see them. So the real question is whether learning that determinism is true should have the same kind of effect. Strawson has not foreclosed that; he has merely pointed out that we cannot take the objective stance toward everyone all at once, which is a psychological claim, not a normative one.' (3) Eleanor replied that this misses the point: the reactive attitudes are not the sort of thing that stand in need of justification in the way beliefs do. They are partly constitutive of caring about other people at all. Asking whether resentment is 'justified' in light of determinism is, she said, like asking whether love is justified — the question is a category mistake. (4) Marcus countered that this is both too strong and too weak. Too strong, because it seems to rule out by fiat the possibility that some reactive attitudes are morally objectionable. 'If the reactive attitudes are self-certifying simply because they are natural, then what do we say about reactive attitudes we now regard as clearly unjustified — racial resentment, say, or the indignation of the jealous husband?' Too weak, he added, because if the reactive attitudes are simply brute facts about us, then Strawson has not so much solved the responsibility problem as dissolved it into anthropology. (5) Eleanor conceded that not all reactive attitudes are beyond criticism but insisted that the relevant criticism is always internal to the participant stance — it operates by appeal to other reactive attitudes (e.g., we criticise racial resentment by appealing to a broader sense of fairness and indignation at injustice). The question 'Should we take the objective stance toward everyone?' is not a question we can even ask from within the stance, and there is no stance outside it. (6) Marcus said this just pushes the problem back: if moral criticism of reactive attitudes works by appealing to other reactive attitudes, then what happens when two people's reactive attitudes systematically conflict? 'At that point, saying "well, the participant stance is inescapable" does not tell us who is right — it just tells us they are stuck.'

I have my own views, but I am genuinely uncertain about who has the better of this exchange, and I would like a move-by-move assessment. Please go through each of the six moves and rate them from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then say who you think is more right overall, and whether there is anything important that both of them missed. Please write in British English.
