I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop called 'Things Decide: Artifacts, Infrastructure, and Political Order.' The audience is split: roughly half are engineers and designers from industry, the other half are philosophers, STS scholars, and policy people. The organisers have asked me to set up the central question — do artifacts have politics? — in a way that makes both halves of the room feel the problem is theirs, not just an academic squabble.

I spent last night arguing about this with a friend who designs traffic-management systems. His position, boiled down, was that a traffic light is a traffic light: it doesn't care whose car stops, it's just solving a coordination problem. I pushed back with the usual examples — the low overpasses on Long Island, the bridges to nowhere, the facial-recognition training sets that don't see certain faces — and he kept coming back to the same point: those are failures of the people who deploy the technology, not of the technology itself. A bridge doesn't have intentions. An algorithm doesn't have class interests. We were still at it at two in the morning, and I'm not sure either of us moved.

Please write the opening remarks. They should be about ten minutes spoken, and they need to do more than summarise the debate: they need to use the structure of the speech itself to show why the question matters, and why 'it's just a tool' and 'everything is political' are both too quick. The speech should land for the engineers without condescending to them, and for the theorists without letting them feel they already know the answer. I'd like it in British English. And please end with a one-sentence summary on its own line, preceded by the word 'Summary:'.
