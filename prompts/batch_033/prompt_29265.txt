I'm currently taking a course on contemporary contractualism, and we've spent the last few weeks on the aggregation problem — the difficulty Scanlon's view has in explaining when we can add up many small claims to outweigh a large one. Since contractualism assesses principles by asking whether any single individual could reasonably reject them, it seems to rule out aggregating across persons: the fact that many people would each suffer a tiny loss doesn't seem to give any one of them a reason strong enough to reject a principle that protects someone else from a devastating loss. And yet some cases seem to cry out for aggregation. I've been trying to get clear on my own verdicts before we turn to the proposed solutions in the literature, and I'm hoping you can help me extract a principle from them — or tell me which ones I need to give up.

Here are the cases and my intuitive verdicts:

1. **Rescue (one vs. five).** A lifeguard can save either one drowning swimmer or five, all strangers with no special claims. Verdict: save the five. This feels as close to bedrock as anything in ethics.

2. **Transmitter room.** To broadcast the World Cup final to millions, a technician must stay in a transmitter room where the equipment causes her severe pain for the duration. There is no other way to broadcast, and the viewers have no idea she is suffering. Verdict: impermissible. No number of football fans' enjoyment can justify inflicting severe pain on one person.

3. **Painkiller triage.** A clinic has a fixed supply of a painkiller. Option A: give it all to one patient with a severely painful condition (8/10 pain, lasting a week). Option B: divide it into small doses for 200 patients each with a mildly painful condition (2/10 pain, lasting a week). The drug is equally effective per dose in either use. Verdict: give it to the 200. The aggregated relief of many mild pains outweighs the relief of one severe pain.

4. **Organ harvesting.** A surgeon can kill one healthy, unconsenting patient to harvest organs that would save five patients who will otherwise die. Verdict: impermissible. The numbers don't matter here — you cannot kill one to save five.

5. **Safety guard.** A factory can install a guard on a machine, which would prevent one worker from losing a hand each year (a near-statistical-certainty over time). The guard slows production, costing each of 500 workers roughly ten minutes of leisure per day. Verdict: the factory must install the guard. The risk of a severed hand is not outweighed by an aggregate of minor inconveniences.

6. **Vaccination program.** A childhood vaccine prevents a mild week-long illness in 10 million children annually but causes a severe adverse reaction (lifelong disability) in roughly 1 in 100,000 recipients. Verdict: proceed with the program. The aggregated prevention of mild illness across millions justifies imposing a small risk of severe harm on a few.

The pair I can't reconcile is case 2 and case 3. In both, we're weighing one person's severe suffering against many people's mild discomfort or disappointment. Yet I want to forbid aggregation in the transmitter room and permit it in the triage. I've considered a few candidate distinctions but none of them seem to work across all six cases:

- Maybe the difference is that case 3 involves the relief of suffering while case 2 involves the provision of a positive benefit. But then case 6 also involves preventing suffering (the illness), and I aggregate there — so that can't be the whole story.
- Maybe it's about whether the many are being used as a justification to harm the one (case 2) versus a situation where someone will suffer no matter what and we're just choosing who (case 3). But case 5 is about preventing a harm by imposing costs, and there I refuse to aggregate — the one large claim wins.
- Maybe it's about the kind of claim: in case 1, all parties have claims of the same type (risk of drowning), so aggregation is fine, while in case 2 the claims are of different types (pain vs. enjoyment). But then case 3 involves claims of the same type (pain) at different magnitudes, and I aggregate, while case 5 involves claims of different types (bodily integrity vs. leisure), and I don't — which might actually fit. But case 6 involves claims of different types (risk of disability vs. mild illness) and I do aggregate, so the pattern breaks.

I'm going in circles. What I'd like from you is: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. For each clause of the principle, tell me which case motivates it. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should revise that verdict or revise the principle. Finally, test the principle against a case I haven't considered: suppose a government can either fund a treatment that saves one person per year from a rare fatal disease, or fund a public health measure that prevents 10,000 people per year from developing a mild but chronic skin condition (itchy, unsightly, but medically harmless). What does the principle say, and is that the right answer?
