I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether the self is anything more than a useful fiction. My friend took the Dennettian line: the self is a center of narrative gravity — an abstraction we posit to make sense of the stories we tell about a human life, but not a thing with independent existence. The analogy is meant to do real work. Just as a center of gravity is not a material object and no one would go looking for it with a microscope, the self is not a Cartesian res cogitans and we should stop treating it as one. Just as a center of gravity is perfectly real in its own way — a real abstractum that figures in real explanations — the self is real enough for practical purposes without being a substance. My friend used this to conclude that questions about the true self, or about personal identity over time as a deep metaphysical matter, are misguided in much the way that asking where a center of gravity goes when an object is destroyed is misguided: the question dissolves once you see what kind of thing you were asking about.

I find the analogy compelling, but I am not sure how much weight it can bear, and the argument left me wanting a more systematic audit. I would like you to lay out the analogy in full detail. What maps onto what — the physical object, its mass distribution, the center of gravity itself, the calculations that use it, the fact that the center of gravity can lie outside the object, the fact that it survives changes in the object's shape? Where does the mapping break down in ways that matter for the conclusion my friend wants to draw? Does the analogy quietly import features of centers of gravity that the self lacks — or features of selves that centers of gravity lack — and do those imports carry the argument rather than merely illustrate it? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive, or was the analogy doing more work than it can sustain? And if a better analogy is called for, what would it be?
