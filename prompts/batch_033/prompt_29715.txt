So I had this long argument last night with a mate of mine about whether the evidence ever really forces a scientific theory. Her whole case rested on the curve-fitting picture, and she was basically defending underdetermination of theory by evidence with it. She kept saying: give me any scatter of data points and I can draw infinitely many curves through them, so for any theory that fits the evidence there are always countless rivals that fit it just as well. From that she went all the way to the conclusion that theory choice is never forced by the evidence itself — simplicity, elegance, conservatism, all that lot are doing the real work, and calling one theory better supported is a bit of a con. I spent ages trying to push back, becuase the maths bit is obviously right as far as it goes, but every time I said something — like the rival curves stop agreeing once you extrapolate past the data, or real data have error bars and a theory has to account for the noise, not just pass through the points — she just said new observations become new points and you can draw a new infinity of curves through those too. It felt like she had an answer for everything, but I still dont think her conclusion follows.

What I'd like is an audit of the analogy itself, not just the conclusion. Lay out what corresponds to what — the data points, the curve, the goodness of fit, the gaps between points, the supposed true curve, the extrapolation beyond the last point (a table is fine). Then say where the mapping actually breaks. Which features of curve-fitting is she quietly importing into science that science doesn't have, and which disanalogies don't matter for the use she's making of it? In particular I'd like to know whether the infinity of rival curves really shows what she thinks it shows, or whether most of them are ruled out by something other than bare fit to the current data — and whether the ones that survive are the ones doing any damage to her conclusion. And at the end: how much of her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — is she right that the evidence never uniquely forces a theory, or was the analogy carrying more than it can bear? If there's a better analogy for what evidence actually does in science, tell me that too.
