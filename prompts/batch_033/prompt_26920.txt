I spend my time thinking about functions that map inputs to labels, and about the difference between a compact rule that fits a dataset and a lazy learner that judges each new case by its nearest neighbors. I want you to translate the debate between moral generalists and moral particularists into that vocabulary. As I understand them, the generalist holds that sound moral judgment is underwritten by a finite set of general principles, while the particularist holds that judgment is properly aimed at the particular case and that principles are at best rough summaries or pedagogical crutches. Restate the two positions as claims about the right kind of model class for moral cases — something like a low-complexity decision rule versus something like instance-based or exemplar-based reasoning — and state carefully what the particularist's holism about reasons (the idea that a feature that counts in favor of an act in one context can count against it in another) becomes under the translation. Then give me the residue report: which claims carry over cleanly, which had to be distorted, and which have no counterpart in the vocabulary at all. Do not rig the translation so that the target framework wins by default. I want to know, in particular, whether the distinction between predicting a judgment and justifying it survives, and whether 'morality is not codifiable' is the same claim as 'no compact model generalizes well from the available cases.' Is this debate ultimately a dispute about the right model class, or does the residue show that one or both sides were never in the business of learning from cases at all?
