I'm working on a paper about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness and I've got a sketch of an argument I'd like feedback on. Here's the core idea:

The illusionist says that phenomenal consciousness — the kind that involves "what it's like"-ness, qualia, etc. — doesn't exist. When we introspect and think we're detecting these private, ineffable, intrinsic qualitative properties, we're wrong. There's no such thing. What we're really doing is reacting to our own cognitive states in a way that generates a false belief that there's something extra.

Here's my worry. It seems like illusionism can be read two ways, and each reading has a problem.

Reading 1: Illusionism as a metaphysical claim. The claim is that there are no qualia, no phenomenal properties, nothing that answers to the philosophical concept of phenomenal consciousness. But if you're already a physicalist about the mind, you already think there are no non-physical qualia. The hard part — the part illusionism is supposed to solve — is explaining why it seems to us that there are. So the metaphysical claim alone doesn't seem to do much work; it just restates physicalism.

Reading 2: Illusionism as an epistemological/psychological claim. The claim is that introspection is radically misleading in a specific way: it generates a systematic illusion of phenomenal properties. This is the interesting reading. But now the burden is to explain how introspection works such that it produces this illusion so reliably and so vividly. And here's the problem: most theories of introspection (even deflationary ones) don't predict this kind of systematic error. If introspection is just a matter of our cognitive systems accessing and representing their own states, why would that access mechanism generate a persistent illusion of something that isn't there? Compare: our visual system generates illusions because it's optimized for speed and pattern recognition under resource constraints. What's the analagous story for introspection?

So the dilemma is: either illusionism is a metaphysical claim that collapses into physicalism without adding much, or it's a substantive psychological claim that needs a detailed error theory for introspection that nobody has really provided.

I'm worried this is too quick. Am I missing something? Do illusionists have a clear way of splitting the horns? And am I being unfair in how I cast the second horn — is there actually a decent story about introspective illusion that I'm overlooking? Also, when you respond, please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
