I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology and I help run a small departmental podcast. We've got this guest coming on next month — she's a philosopher of science whose been defending something like Dennett's idea of "free-floating rationales": the claim that there can be genuine reasons why things are the way they are (why a trait evolved, why a market equilibrium holds, why a cultural practice persists) without any agent having or representing those reasons. The "design without a designer" move is pretty familiar to our audience, so I don't want to spend time rehashing that. What I want to get at is trickier: once you grant that natural selection can produce the appearance of design, what does calling the selection pressures "reasons" actually buy you? Is it more than a metaphor? And if the reasons are real but unowned, what kind of thing are they — are we committing to some spooky normative realism about reasons, or is there a deflationary story that keeps everything respectably naturalistic?

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions that push on this? Here's what I'd like covered: at least one question on how free-floating rationales differ from ordinary causal explanations (if "the reason the giraffe has a long neck is to reach high foliage" is just a redescription of the selection history, why not stick with the causal story?), one on whether the view forces you into realism about reasons or whether you can stay comfortably naturalistic, and one on whether free-floating rationales work differently in cultural evolution versus biological evolution — like, once you've got actual minds in the loop imitating and transmitting practices, does the "free-floating" part start to look different? Our guest has a habit of deflecting hard questions by saying "the reasons are real, they're just not anyone's reasons," so for each question I'd love a follow-up that anticipates that move and pushes past it. Also, if you could throw in one question near the end that asks what would actually count as evidence against the view — what kind of discovery would make her abandon free-floating rationales — that'd be great. I want to end on something that makes the position feel falsifiable rather than unfalsifiable, because honestly that's where I get stuck when I try to pin down what the view rules out.
