I'm giving a twenty-five-minute talk with a long Q&A afterward at a workshop on the metaphysics of time, and I want to be ready for the question period rather than just the talk. My thesis is a reconstruction of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — the argument that tries to get conclusions about what is real from the relativity of simultaneity in special relativity — and I claim both its friends and its critics overshoot. It does not by itself establish the block universe, and it certainly does not establish determinism; but it does put a dilemma to any moving-spotlight view. Either the spotlight's "now" is frame-relative, in which case there is no unique present and the view has quietly collapsed into something like a B-theory, or it is frame-invariant, in which case the view is committed to a privileged inertial frame that nothing in the physics selects. The part of the talk I expect to be weakest is my claim that the argument can be made to work without the transitivity-of-realness premise that the usual reconstructions build in, and without conflating "is real at some time in some frame" with "is real simpliciter."

The audience will not be friendly in the usual way. It includes two philosophers of physics who know this argument far better than I do: one is a committed eternalist who thinks the argument is basically sound and will press me on why I stop short of the block universe; the other is an A-theorist who thinks the whole debate is a verbal dispute about two senses of "real." There will also be several metaphysicians who are agnostic but allergic to arguments that look like physics doing metaphysics by fiat, and one relativist physicist who objects to the word "frame" as if frames were physical things. I have read enough about map–territory confusions and about words like "real" and "exists" doing hidden work that I want to know, for each objection, whether it is a genuine problem or a verbal one, and how to say so without sounding evasive.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I am actually going to get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them; for each, a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest; what I should concede up front and what I should hold; and the one question I should be hoping nobody asks. Don't write the talk itself — I want the exchange afterward.
