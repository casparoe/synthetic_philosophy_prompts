I am a philosophy professor preparing to teach a single eighty-minute session on Plato's views on drunkenness to advanced undergraduates in a course on Ancient Political Philosophy. They have read the Republic and the Symposium, but not the Laws, and this session is their first encounter with the Laws' treatment of wine and the symposium. I want you to act as an expert on Plato's late political philosophy and to plan my session for me. Do not explain the material to me; I already know it. Instead, I need a teaching plan: the conceptual architecture of the session.

Think carefully about the dependency structure of the ideas before you lay out the sequence. What must be in place before the core argument of Laws Books 1 and 2 can land? The students will arrive knowing the Symposium's contrast between Socrates' imperviousness to wine and Alcibiades' drunken disorder; they will also know the Republic's suspicion of appetite and its call to rational self-mastery. The pivot the Laws makes — that controlled drunkenness can be a positive instrument of moral education, a pharmacological test of character — will strike them as contradictory to the Plato they think they know. I need the session to make that pivot feel like a genuine philosophical development rather than a contradiction or a retraction.

Your plan should specify all of the following:

1. The order in which the ideas must be introduced, with a justification of what depends on what. Include the moment at which the students should first encounter the specific proposals of the Laws (the supervised drinking party, the sober symposiarch, the "test of the wine" as a tool for civic virtue), and explain why that moment cannot come earlier.

2. The one extended example or scenario that should carry the argument through the whole session — something that makes the Athenian Stranger's pedagogical logic vivid and intuitive before the students learn to resist it. Tell me what this example must have and what it must lack if it is to do the work.

3. The misconceptions the students will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Among the misconceptions I expect you to address are: the assumption that the Laws is simply more authoritarian than the Republic and therefore its endorsement of drinking is a puzzle; the conflation of the Athenian Stranger's argument with a modern harm-reduction or "safe space" logic; the reading of the "test of the wine" as a mere surveillance mechanism; the tendency to slide from "Plato thinks drunkenness reveals character" to "Plato thinks drunkenness reveals the true self" — a Romantic notion that is not in the text; and the idea that the elderly, whose drinking Plato also discusses, are simply exempt from the test. You may identify additional ones, and you should explain why each is a distortion of the text's logic.

4. What I should openly refuse to explain at this level and why — what would require a deeper dive into the Laws' political psychology, its theology, or its anthropology that would derail a single session, and how I should signal that refusal without making it sound like a weakness of the argument.

5. A single question I can pose in the final five minutes to check whether the central idea has genuinely landed rather than been merely understood on the surface. The question should not be an exegetical quiz; it should require the student to deploy the logic of the session in an unfamiliar direction.

The deliverable is the plan, not the lecture itself. Do not suggest readings. The plan must work with what the students already have in their heads from the Republic and Symposium, plus whatever I can sketch on the board.
