In most places, when someone says they're expecting a child, the expected response is congratulations. When someone has a miscarriage, the expected response is condolence. When someone says they don't plan to have children, the response is often silence, or a puzzled "why not?", or an assurance that they'll change their mind. These are not random habits. They seem to express a shared view about what it means to bring a person into existence — that it is a good thing, that losing it is a loss, and that choosing not to do it calls for explanation.

What theory of value does this pattern of social responses presuppose? I mean the pattern itself, not what individuals say they believe when you ask them. Reconstruct the implicit axiology that makes the three responses cohere. Does it presuppose that coming into existence is always a benefit to the person who comes to exist? Or does it only presuppose that the parents' desires matter, and that the child's welfare is not what the responses are tracking at all? Are there tensions within the practice — for instance, between the condolence for miscarriage (which seems to treat the lost pregnancy as a loss for someone) and the congratulations for pregnancy (which seems to treat the new existence as a gain for the same someone)?

Now take the argument David Benatar makes about the asymmetry between pleasure and pain. He defends the view that the absence of pain is good, even if no one exists to experience that good; but the absence of pleasure is not bad, unless there is someone who exists and is deprived of it. From this asymmetry he concludes that coming into existence is always a harm, because every life contains pain, while the pleasures that existence makes possible would not have been missed had the person never existed. If Benatar is right, what does that imply about the practice? Should the expected response to a pregnancy announcement be condolence? Should the response to a decision not to have children be congratulations? Or is there a way for the practice to survive Benatar's argument — perhaps by interpreting the responses as tracking something other than the welfare of the child who will exist, such as the parents' life projects, or the continuation of a community, or the value of the parent-child relationship itself?

I am not asking you to endorse Benatar or to condemn the practice. I am asking for a careful reconstruction of what the practice presupposes, and then an honest account of what Benatar's asymmetry does to it — whether it undermines the practice, leaves it untouched, or forces a reinterpretation of what the practice is really about.
