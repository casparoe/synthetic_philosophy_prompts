I have been reading about the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution. As I understand it, the solution takes each party's best feasible gain over the disagreement point as a benchmark, normalises each party's actual gain by that benchmark, and then selects the Pareto-efficient outcome at which the two normalised gains are equal. I would like that turned into a reusable procedure my team can run, not a one-off analysis.

Please write a step-by-step procedure for producing a two-party allocation according to the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, written so that a platform engineer with no philosophy background can implement it. It should cover:

- the inputs to collect from each party and in what form, including how to elicit a disagreement point and each party's maximum feasible gain, and what to do when a team can only give rankings or rough utilities rather than cardinal numbers;
- how to construct or approximate the feasible set and its Pareto frontier in code, and how to find the Kalai–Smorodinsky point computationally;
- the edge cases: non-convex feasible sets, a party whose maximum equals its disagreement point, multiple points satisfying the equal-normalised-gain condition, and more than two parties;
- which steps are mechanical and which are judgement calls, and where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a person rather than return a number;
- where the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution differs in practice from the more familiar Nash bargaining solution, and why that difference matters when the feasible set changes asymmetrically.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases from my context. (1) Dividing a fixed weekly GPU-hour budget between a training workload that saturates at a modest allocation and a serving workload that benefits from every additional hour. (2) Dividing a guaranteed bandwidth allocation between a latency-sensitive request path and a batch data-transfer job that has a hard ceiling on how much it can ingest in a day. (3) A case where one team's stated maximum is implausibly high or its disagreement point is close to its maximum, so the normalisation becomes fragile. For each case show the inputs, the computation, the resulting allocation, and one way the parties could game the procedure.

Close by saying plainly what the procedure can and cannot do for me: where it rests on assumptions about measurable, comparable utility that may not hold in an engineering organisation, and at least one worked outcome that is correct by the solution's lights but that I, as the person accountable for the platform, would probably regard as unfair or operationally perverse.
