I'm a journalist who covers technology and ideas, and I've spent the past few months embedded with a team building an AI coaching system — the kind of product that uses behavioral data and language models to help people make decisions about their careers, relationships, and life direction. They've hit a philosophical logjam that I need help diagnosing.

The case that's stuck them involves a test user, a 29-year-old lawyer who's been using the system for eight months. She has consistently told the system that her goal is to make partner by 35 and that she wants to optimize for career prestige and financial security. She's articulate and unwavering about this. But the system's model of her, built from her messaging patterns, sleep and activity data, voice-tone analysis from coaching sessions, and the free-text journal she writes inside the app, tells a sharply different story. It predicts with high confidence that if she follows the partner track, she'll be significantly less satisfied at 40 than she would be on an alternative path — one that involves a shift toward public-interest law and makes space for the fiction writing she's been steadily abandoning. Her journal entries about work are administrative and flat; her entries about the writing group she left are vivid and grieving. The model also flags that the phrases she uses to justify the partner track closely mirror language her father uses in the family group chat.

The engineering team has split into two camps. One camp argues that the system should take her stated preferences at face value and help her achieve them. She's a competent adult; she knows what she wants; overriding that is paternalism, no matter how sophisticated the model. The other camp argues that the entire point of the system is to help people see themselves more clearly than they can unaided — and if the data reveals a gap between what a user says she wants and what would make her life go better by her own deeper standards, surfacing that gap is exactly what the system is for. They've been deadlocked for a month, and the product deadline is approaching.

I can see that this isn't merely a product disagreement. It seems to touch a deep tension in how we think about respecting autonomy when you can model someone's future self better than they can. But I lack the philosophical vocabulary to name the problem or to judge whether there's a principled resolution. Is there one? And if so, what would it look like to operationalize it in a system that actually has to ship — not in a thought experiment, but in code?
