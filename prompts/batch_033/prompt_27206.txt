I've spent a fair bit of time on forums like LessWrong and the EA Forum, where philosophy of language comes up in discussions about whether language models 'refer' to anything, or whether an AI's concepts can be said to 'latch onto' real-world kinds. In that context, I've seen Putnam's Twin Earth argument invoked dozens of times, usually in passing and usually to support the claim that meaning isn't in the head. Wanting to get clearer on what the argument actually establishes, I picked up a popular introductory book on philosophy of language — the sort of thing pitched at undergraduates or autodidacts — and was struck by how strongly the author summarised the takeaway. Here is the passage, which I've reproduced from memory as accurately as I can:

"Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment is the classic argument for semantic externalism, the view that what our words mean is fixed by the world, not by what goes on inside our minds. Putnam imagines a planet that is a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of Earth, except that the clear liquid in its rivers and oceans has the chemical composition XYZ rather than H₂O. In 1750, before the development of modern chemistry, an Earthling named Oscar and his counterpart on Twin Earth have exactly the same mental images, beliefs, and sensations associated with the word 'water.' Yet Putnam argues that Oscar's word 'water' refers to H₂O, while Twin Oscar's word refers to XYZ. Since their psychological states are identical but the meanings of their words differ, meaning must be determined partly by their physical and social environments. Putnam took this to refute the traditional empiricist view, inherited from Locke and Hume, that words get their meanings from the ideas in speakers' heads."

Something about the final move — from 'the two Oscars refer to different substances' to 'meaning must be determined by the environment' — feels too quick to me, as though the author is running together reference and meaning or overstating how general the argument is. But my grasp of the territory is patchy: I can tell something bothers me, but I can't confidently say what's wrong and what's fair. Could you perform a careful audit of this summary? For each claim in the passage, I'd like to know whether a sympathetic reader of Putnam — not a hostile critic, but someone who understands the argument in detail — would endorse it as stated, qualify it, or reject it. I'm especially interested in whether the author's conclusion ('meaning must be determined partly by their physical and social environments') follows from the thought experiment as Putnam actually constructed it, and whether the summary silently generalises a point that Putnam only meant to make about a restricted class of terms. If there are distinctions Putnam draws — for instance, between reference and meaning, or between different components of meaning — that the summary collapses, please flag them and explain why they matter, particularly for the kinds of debates about AI and 'grounding' that got me interested in the first place.
