I am a novelist at work on a book centred on a populist political movement, and I have become stuck on a conceptual problem that is affecting the coherence of my characters. The phrase "the will of the people" appears throughout my draft—invoked by different characters to justify overturning a lost election, to demand a binding referendum, to denounce constitutional courts as anti-democratic, and, in one scene, to defend a policy that has been polling at around thirty percent. I have come to suspect that the phrase is doing several quite different kinds of work in these passages, but I lack the analytical vocabulary to distinguish them. Could you take the phrase "the will of the people" and lay out the distinct precise theses someone might be asserting when they appeal to it? What kind of entity is "the people," what does it mean for them to have a "will," and how would one in principle determine what that will is on each reading? For each candidate precisification, I would like to know which of my characters' moves it would actually license—and whether the reading itself holds up under scrutiny, or collapses into something hollow once made explicit. I should add that my aim is not to debunk populism but to write characters who are intellectually serious, so I am looking for the strongest defensible versions of these claims, not straw men.
