I am preparing for a graduate seminar on early modern political thought, and I keep getting tangled up in the structure of Pascal’s argument about justice and force in the Pensées, especially in the fragment often numbered Lafuma 103 / Brunschvicg 298. What I want is a precise conceptual reconstruction of the moves in that fragment, not a historical or biographical gloss. In particular, it seems to move from the claim that justice and force each give a reason for obedience, but of different kinds, to the claim that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical, and then to a disquieting conclusion: since justice is subject to dispute while force is easily recognizable and undisputed, we could not make the just strong, so we made the strong just. I am confused about what exactly that last move amounts to, and whether Pascal is explaining, justifying, or debunking the identification of justice with force.

Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Each point should isolate and explain one distinct conceptual step or shift in the argument. I especially need clarity on the following: the difference between being justly followed and being necessarily followed; what work the contrast between disputability and recognizability does; how the transition from “one could not make what is just strong” to “one made what is strong just” is supposed to follow; whether this treats justice as a name for effective force or as a separate standard the strong can borrow; and whether any residual normative content of justice survives the argument or is dissolved by it.
