Two people in my decision-theory reading group got into it the other night over Pascal's mugging, and I'm honestly still not sure who to side with. The setup they were using was this: a stranger stops you on the street and claims to be running a simulation from outside our universe. He says that if you hand over your wallet, he'll use it to create 10^30 happy lives, and he admits the probability he's telling the truth is one in 10^20. By ordinary expected-value math the expected payoff is still enormous, so the math still say you should pay. The argument went like this. (1) Ana said the whole thing is a reductio of expected-value reasoning: no one in their right mind pays, so something in the framework has to go. (2) Ben replied that nothing has to go; the conclusion is just that a tiny credence in a big enough payoff swamps ordinary costs, and if that feels wrong it's a fact about your credences, not a defect in the math. (3) Ana came back that you can rerun the argument with any finite probability and any finite payoff, so unless you assign literally zero to every outlandish claim, the mugger can always name a number big enough to make paying look rational—and assigning zero to every such claim has its own problems. (4) Ben said that's why a reasonable agent needs either a bounded utility function or some penalty on very small probabilities, otherwise decision theory is hostage to whatever the mugger says next. (5) Ana said a bound is just an ad hoc patch: nobody can say where the bound goes or why, and whatever number you pick, a mugger can come in just under it. They went round and round after that and neither gave ground.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each numbered move, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and why; then say what the whole exchange is missing that would actually settle whether to hand over the wallet, and if you had to pick, who is more right overall.
