I've been thinking about how we ought to form beliefs when we know the objective probabilities of things. The idea seems straightforward enough: if I know that a fair coin has a fifty per cent chance of landing heads, then I should be fifty per cent confident that it will land heads. If I know that a die has a one-in-six chance of showing a four, then I should be exactly one-sixth confident that it will show a four. This feels like a basic norm of rationality — your degree of belief should simply track the known chances.

But I keep running into a problem that I cannot seem to resolve. Suppose I know that a coin is fair, so the objective chance of heads on the next toss is fifty per cent. But suppose I also happened to see the coin land, and I know it came up heads. In that case, my confidence that it landed heads should obviously be a hundred per cent, not fifty per cent. The principle seems to break down whenever I possess extra information about the particular outcome.

One might reply: well, in that case you do not merely know the chance — you also know the outcome. So the principle only applies when you are ignorant of the outcome. But that seems to drain the principle of most of its content. When do we ever know the objective chance of something without also knowing other facts that bear on it? If I know a coin is fair, I typically know that because I have examined it or been told about it — and those are themselves pieces of information that go beyond the bare chance. If I know that a medical treatment has a seventy per cent success rate, I almost certainly know other things about the patient that might push my confidence above or below seventy per cent. The principle seems to retreat into a narrow range of artificial cases where we know the chance and nothing else whatsoever — and even there, I am not sure such cases actually exist.

So here is the question I keep circling back to: is there a way to state the idea — that we should match our beliefs to known chances — that genuinely works once we admit that we nearly always have additional information, or is the principle simply mistaken? I go back and forth. On the one hand, it seems as though there must be something right about it, because otherwise what would it even mean to say a coin is fair? On the other hand, I cannot see how to formulate the principle without it either being false in the cases that matter or being so hedged about with qualifications that it no longer tells you anything definite at all.
