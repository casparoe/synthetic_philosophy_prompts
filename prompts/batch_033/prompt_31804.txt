I've been working through the Hart–Fuller debate in my philosophy of law seminar and I'm starting to think the whole thing is stuck in a way that's not just "it's a hard problem." We read Hart's "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" and then Fuller's reply, and then we did the back-and-forth about the grudge informer cases and the Nazi law problem, and honestly by the end of it I felt like both sides had made their points sixty years ago and everyone's just been restating them ever since. Hart says there's no necessary connection between law and morality — a law is a law if it passes the rule of recognition, even if it's monstrous. Fuller says there is a necessary connection because law has an internal morality of its own: his eight desiderata for lawmaking, like publicity, non-retroactivity, clarity, and so on, and a system that systematically violates them doesn't just produce bad law, it fails to produce law at all. Hart comes back and says those are just efficacy conditions for a legal system, not morality proper — like how a knife has to be sharp to be a good knife, but sharpness isn't a moral quality. And then it all just... sits there. Every time I think one side has landed a decisive point, the other side has already absorbed it into their framework and nothing actually moves.

What I want to know is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about what counts as "morality" — where Hart is using a narrow sense (morality as substantive right and wrong) and Fuller a broader one (morality as any normative standard internal to a practice)? Is it a genuine factual or conceptual disagreement about whether legal systems that violate Fuller's principles actually collapse as legal systems, or whether subjects can still identify law in such systems? Is it a disagreement about what we should want from a concept of law — Hart wanting analytic clarity and resistance to ideology, Fuller wanting a concept that explains why law is worth having and when it deserves fidelity? Or is it something deeper, like a difference in philosophical method (Hart doing ordinary-language conceptual analysis, Fuller doing a kind of purposive functionalism) that can't be adjudicated from neutral ground? I have a hunch the two sides are just answering different questions — Hart is asking "what makes a rule a legal rule" and Fuller is asking "what makes a legal system worthy of fidelity" — but I'm not sure if that's right or just a cheap way to avoid picking a side. And if it is right, I want to know why anyone thought they were disagreeing in the first place.

Give me a committed diagnosis. Don't just lay out the options and shrug — tell me what you think the disagreement actually is at bottom, what (if anything) would count as progress given that diagnosis, and whether there's a side you think has the better of it. Write it in American English.
