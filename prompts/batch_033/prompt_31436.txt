Question:

Explain the difference between epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment about knowledge (the view also called subject-sensitive invariantism or interest-relative invariantism). Use the standard bank case to illustrate. Address all three of the following: (a) what each view says about the truth of the subject's first-person ascription that he knows the bank will be open on Saturday, in the low-stakes and high-stakes versions of the case; (b) whether each view treats knows as context-sensitive in meaning; and (c) whether the two views diverge in their predictions about third-person knowledge ascriptions.

Student's answer:

In the bank case, a subject considers on a Friday whether the bank will be open on Saturday. In the low-stakes version, little turns on this, and he claims to know that it will be open. In the high-stakes version, something important turns on it, and the same subject with the same evidence is unwilling to claim that he knows it will be open. The contextualist explains this by saying that the word knows picks out different epistemic standards in different contexts of utterance. In the low-stakes context the standards are low, so the subject's self-ascription is true. In the high-stakes context the standards are high, so the same ascription is false. On this view, knows works like an indexical such as I or here: its content shifts with the context of the speaker. Pragmatic encroachment, by contrast, holds that the meaning of knows is fixed, but whether a person knows depends partly on practical factors, namely how much is at stake for the subject given his evidence. In the high-stakes version the subject fails to know because the practical cost of being wrong is too high relative to his evidence, so the ascription is false; in the low-stakes version he knows, so the ascription is true. Thus the two views make the same predictions about the truth of ordinary knowledge ascriptions and differ only at the level of semantic theory. One further consequence is that contextualism implies that there is no fact of the matter about whether the subject knows, whereas subject-sensitive invariantism preserves a fact of the matter but lets it depend on the subject's practical interests.
