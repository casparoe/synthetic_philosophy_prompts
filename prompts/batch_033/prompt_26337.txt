Expected value fanaticism is the implication of standard expected utility theory that one should be willing to sacrifice any finite amount of certain value for a sufficiently tiny probability of an astronomically large payoff. For instance, if you assign a one-in-a-googol chance to an outcome you value at ten to the power of a googol utils, the expected value of pursuing that outcome exceeds the value of everything else you might do, so maximizing expected value demands pursuing it. Because consequentialism instructs us to maximize expected value, the theory seems to direct us to abandon near-certain interventions — say, funding malaria prevention — in favor of long-shot gambles like trying to influence the far future of the galaxy. That is the fanaticism worry: a moral theory shouldn' be so sensitive to tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs that its recommendations become impossible to take seriously.

The bounded utility response holds that utility functions are bounded above — there is some finite maximum value an outcome can have. With a bounded utility function, sufficiently small probabilities make any expected-value contribution negligible, so fanaticism is blocked straightforwardly. The cost is that we must give up the intuitive idea that for any good state of affairs we can imagine a better one; but this may be a price worth paying.

The second response rejects one or more axioms of expected utility theory, typically the Independence axiom. Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory, for example, allows agents to weight outcomes in a way that is sensitive to the relative probability of states, effectively giving less decision-weight to very small probabilities. This resolves fanaticism without needing to bound utility: even if value can increase without limit, tiny probabilities get down-weighted enough that they do not dominate deliberation. This response is attractive because it preserves the intuition that there is no upper bound on value while still avoiding fanatical conclusions.

On balance I think the second response is preferable. Bounded utility seems arbitrary — why cap value at any particular point? — whereas risk-weighted decision theory offers a principled rationale for why tiny probabilities should not drive our choices.
