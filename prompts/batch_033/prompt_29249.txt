I serve on the governing board of an interdisciplinary research institute — the kind of place that funds year-long fellowships for academics and writers to work alongside each other. Since the institute was founded in the early 1970s, every fellowship application has been subject to a rule I will call the cross-discipline requirement: a proposal must name at least one collaborator from a discipline other than the applicant's own, and proposals that fail to do so are returned unread. A historian proposing a book on eighteenth-century agriculture must find, say, a soil scientist to sign on; a mathematician working on knot theory must recruit a physicist or a biologist who can plausibly claim an interest.

A bloc on the board now argues that the requirement has outlived whatever purpose it once served and should be abolished. Their case is crisp: the rule generates paperwork rather than genuine collaboration; it wastes applicants' time on coalition-building that adds nothing to the intellectual substance of the work; and if interdisciplinary projects are genuinely stronger, they will succeed in open competition without a quota. The bloc wants the institute to fund the best proposals, full stop.

I find myself in the middle, and I have been tasked with drafting the short memorandum that will go to the full board before our vote. My difficulty is not that I think the requirement is obviously worth keeping; it is that I think we are in a poor epistemic position to judge. The requirement has been in place for over fifty years. The founding documents give only a thin rationale — a paragraph about cross-fertilisation and preventing the hardening of disciplinary silos — and nobody now can reconstruct in full why the founders chose a hard requirement over, say, an aspirational statement or a tie-breaking preference. It is possible that the requirement served, and continues to serve, functions that are not captured by anyone's current articulation of its value: shaping which researchers apply at all, setting norms about intellectual humility, creating a reputation that attracts a particular kind of scholar. It is also possible that none of this is true and that the requirement is simply deadweight. The point is that we do not know, and abolishing it is irreversible or at least costly to reverse.

This seems to me a live instance of a deeper problem that I cover in my own work: when an institution contemplates dismantling a longstanding practice whose full rationale is not recoverable, what burden of proof should the reformers bear? But I do not want to turn a board memo into a philosophy essay, and I am genuinely unsure what recommendation follows from taking the epistemic difficulty seriously.

Please draft the memorandum. It should:

1. Recommend a concrete decision — abolish, retain, or modify — and if modification, specify the revised rule;
2. Provide reasoning that engages the strongest version of the case for abolition, not a straw man;
3. Address the epistemic problem directly: does our inability to recover the original rationale give us any reason to be cautious about abolition, and if so, how much reason, and what would discharge it?

If you think I am misframing the problem — if, for instance, you think the original rationale is simply irrelevant once the current costs and benefits are on the table, or that my talk of irreversibility smuggles in a conservative presumption that itself needs defending — then say so and correct the framing. I would much rather have the terms of the debate set right than receive a recommendation that works within what might be a confused brief.
