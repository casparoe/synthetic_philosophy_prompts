I'm preparing a workshop for my department's ethics reading group on Nozick's utility monster and the broader challenge it poses to aggregative consequentialism. Rather than lecture from the start, I want to open with a sorting instrument — a "which position on the utility monster do you actually hold?" quiz — so that participants can locate themselves in the logical space before the discussion begins. The instrument should sort people by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which label they happen to prefer, and it should surface the fact that positions that sound different in the abstract can converge or diverge in surprising ways once the cases are made specific.

I need eight to ten questions, each presenting a short, concrete scenario that varies the utility monster along one or more dimensions: whether the beneficiary is a single being or a dispersed group, whether the transfers are voluntary or coercive, whether the utility differences are modest or astronomical, whether the recipient is already well-off or destitute, whether the transfer involves a rights violation or merely a distributive choice between competing claims, and whether the scenario is framed as a one-off decision or an iterated policy. Each question should offer two or three answer options that force genuine trade-offs between positions, and no question should simply ask "are you a utilitarian?" or "do you believe in rights?"

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least the following positions: classical total utilitarianism (biting the bullet), prioritarianism, egalitarianism, sufficientarianism, a rights-based or deontological rejection of the trade-off, and a diminishing-marginal-utility response that denies the monster's metaphysical possibility. If there are positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate — for example, prioritarianism and egalitarianism on certain cases, or sufficientarianism and a threshold version of prioritarianism — flag those explicitly rather than pretending the instrument distinguishes them. Also note any logically possible answer patterns that correspond to no defended position in the literature, with a brief diagnosis of why that combination is empty: is it incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? The audience is graduate students and faculty in philosophy, so the questions can assume familiarity with the basic thought experiment without explaining it from scratch. The deliverable is the quiz itself (questions and answer options), the scoring key, and the diagnostic notes on separability and empty cells.
