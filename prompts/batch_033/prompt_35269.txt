I've been going down a rabbit hole on the surprise exam paradox — you know the one: a teacher announces on Friday that there will be an exam on exactly one day next week, and that the students won't be able to know the night before which day it's going to be. The students then reason backwards: it can't be Friday, because if it hasn't happened by Thursday night they'd know it's Friday and it wouldn't be a surprise. So rule out Friday. Then it can't be Thursday, because with Friday gone, if it hasn't happened by Wednesday night they'd know it's Thursday. You keep going and all five days get eliminated. The students conclude the exam is impossible. Then Wednesday morning rolls around and the teacher hands out the exam, and it's a complete surprise. So something in the students' reasoning is broken — but what?

The thing is, this paradox has been around since the 1940s and there are now something like six or seven major families of diagnosis, and after reading a bunch of the literature I get the sense that the debate has been basically frozen for decades. The epistemic logic camp says the students' reasoning tacitly relies on a principle like the KK principle (if you know something, you know that you know it), and that principle fails in these kinds of self-referential contexts. The self-reference camp says the teacher's announcement is subtly self-refuting in the way the Liar paradox is, and the students' reasoning is actually fine — the problem is that the announcement itself can't be true. The temporal-indexical people say the reasoning ignores the fact that the students' epistemic state shifts as the week progresses, and once you model that properly the backward induction falls apart. The "simple solution" crowd says the students can't eliminate Friday in the first place because their reasoning only works if they trust the announcement, and they're in the process of undermining it. And there are a few more: people who think the whole thing is about the semantics of "surprise," people who think it's a Sorites-like vagueness problem, people who think the students' reasoning is perfectly valid and the teacher's announcement is just false in a non-obvious way.

And here's what's driving me a bit nuts: everyone in each camp seems to think their own diagnosis is transparently correct and that the other camps are just confused or missing the point. Nobody moves. The papers from the 60s and 70s are making basically the same arguments as the papers from the 2010s, with slightly fancier formalisms. After 80 years you'd expect some convergence, or at least a clear statement of what the remaining disagreement is about, but I'm not sure that exists.

So what I want isn't another solution to the paradox. I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself. What kind of disagreement is this? Is it a substantive disagreement about the logic of knowledge and self-reference, or is it a verbal dispute about what counts as being "surprised" or what "knowing" means in this sort of scenario? Are the different camps actually answering different questions — one about formal epistemology, one about the semantics of announcements, one about the pragmatics of what a teacher can coherently say — and just talking past each other under a single label? Or is one of these diagnoses genuinely right and the others are just stubbornly wrong, and if so, what's blocking consensus? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here: a new formal result, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the disagreement, or is this one of those things where the underlying intuitions are genuinely incompatible and no resolution should be expected?
