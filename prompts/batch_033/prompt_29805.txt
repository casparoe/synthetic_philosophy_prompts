So I was at this dinner party last weekend and two friends of mine — let's call them David and Mira — got into this intense argument that I'm still turning over in my head. I want you to act as a professional philosopher who specializes in ethics and adjudicate their exchange point by point. Think through each move carefully before you respond, and give me a committed answer — take a definite position and defend it, don't just lay out the options.

Here's roughly how it went:

We were talking about some disaster movie, and somehow we got onto the question of what you should do if you can rescue either one person or five people, but not both. David said obviously you save the five — five lives are more than one, end of story. Mira said she wasn't so sure, and things escalated from there.

(1) Mira: "Look, imagine you're the one person. You'd feel like your life matters just as much as anyone else's. Why should you be the one who gets left behind just because you happen to be outnumbered? Each of the six people has an equal claim on you — you're the only rescuer — so the fair thing is to give each claim equal weight. Flip a coin."

(2) David: "That's insane. You'd really flip a coin when five people are drowning on one side and one on the other? The coin gives the single person a fifty percent chance, and each of the five gets a ten percent chance. How is that equal? You're massively priviledging the one."

(3) Mira: "The coin isn't about distributing probability shares to individuals. It's about resolving a conflict of claims. Each person has a claim that you save them. These claims can't all be satisfied. The fairest resolution procedure is one that gives each claim equal consideration — and a coin toss does that. If you just save the five, you're not even considering the one person's claim; you're treating it as though it doesn't exist."

(4) David: "But that's because the one person's claim is outweighed! Five claims of equal weight outweigh one claim of equal weight. That's just what it means for claims to have weight. You're basically denying that claims aggregate at all."

(5) Mira: "I am denying that, actually. Or at least I'm denying that they aggregate in the way you're assuming. Think about it: if claims aggregate straightforwardly, then a million people each with a mild headache would have a stronger claim on a painkiller than one person with a broken leg in agony. But that seems wrong. There's something about the nature of the claim — what's at stake for each person — that matters, not just the headcount."

(6) David: "Okay, but in the rescue case, what's at stake is the same for everyone: their life. So the headcount is all that's left. Your headache example actually works against you — it shows that when the stakes are equal, numbers do the work."

(7) Mira: "No, I think it shows the opposite. The headache case shows that we can't just sum across persons like that. And in the rescue case, precisely because each person's stake is the same — their entire life — each claim is maximal in a way that resists aggregation. You can't say 'five maximal claims beat one maximal claim' because 'maximal' doesn't come in degrees. It's not like five infinities versus one infinity — it's that each person's world is all they have."

(8) David: "That's a rhetorical trick though. Lives aren't infinities; they're finite goods. And we trade them off all the time — we spend limited medical resources, we decide which roads to make safer. In all those cases we use numbers. Why is the rescue case different?"

(9) Mira: "Because in those cases we're distributing resources across a population, not choosing which person's entire existence to save. When you're the sole rescuer facing six people each of whom will die without you, you're not a policymaker allocating a budget — you're in a one-off confrontation with six individual claims on your action. That's a different moral situation."

At this point someone changed the subject, but I've been thinking about it since. Please go through this exchange move by move — (1) through (9) — and adjudicate.
