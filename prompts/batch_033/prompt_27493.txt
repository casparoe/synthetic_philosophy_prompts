I'm a retired physicist — spent 35 years in condensed matter, never thought about philosophy till I retired and a friend dragged me to a decision theory reading group. Now I'm hooked, but I still get turned around on some of these puzzles. Last week two members of the group, Clara and Raj, got into a long argument about counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, and I've been going back and forth on who's right ever since. I wrote down their exchange as best as I could remember it. Could you go through it move by move and adjudicate? I want to know who's right about which specific claims, where each of them goes wrong (if they do), and who has the better overall position.

Clara started us off by laying out the standard counterfactual mugging: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If it comes up tails, Omega asks you for $100. If it comes up heads, Omega gives you $10,000 iff it predicted you would have paid the $100 had the coin come up tails. You find yourself being asked for $100 — so you know the coin came up tails. Clara said you should pay.

(1) Clara: The policy "pay when asked" nets you an expected $4,950 — half the time you lose $100, half the time you gain $10,000. The policy "refuse when asked" nets you $0. The only way to be the kind of agent who collects the $10,000 in heads branches is to actually pay in tails branches. If you'd refuse now, Omega would have predicted that, and you'd have gotten nothing in the heads branch. So you pay.

(2) Raj: That's reasoning about a policy before the coin flip, but you're not before the coin flip. You know the coin came up tails. In the actual world, paying loses you $100 and refusing loses you nothing. Refusing strictly dominates. The fact that you'd have wanted to precommit doesn't make a now-dominated action rational — you can't reach into the counterfactual heads branch and collect a reward that was already denied to you.

(3) Clara: But the dominance argument assumes the heads branch is sealed off. It's not — your decision procedure determines what happens there. Omega's prediction in the heads branch is a logical consequence of what you do in the tails branch. So your action here does reach across, just not causally. If you treat the two branches as independent, you get the wrong answer. This is exactly the point of Newcomb-like problems: there are acausal connections that dominance reasoning misses.

(4) Raj: OK, but then take XOR blackmail. Omega sends you a letter iff your house has termites XOR you'll pay $1,000. Termite damage costs a million. You get the letter. If you pay, you're out $1,000; if you don't, you might have termites. An evidential decision theorist pays, because paying is evidence you don't have termites. But that's clearly a mistake — you're being pumped for money by a predictor who's exploiting a correlation. The right move is to not pay, because paying doesn't cause the termites to go away. Counterfactual mugging has the same structure: you're being asked to pay for a benefit that's already fixed in a branch you can't affect.

(5) Clara: They're not the same. In XOR blackmail, whether you get the letter at all depends on your decision — so the set of worlds where you face the choice is selected by your policy. If you compute the expected value of "pay if letter" across all worlds, including ones where no letter arrives, it's actually worse than "don't pay if letter," because "pay" means you get the letter in no-termite worlds and pay $1,000, while "don't pay" means you get the letter only in termite worlds and pay nothing. The EDT agent is making a selection-effect mistake. But in counterfactual mugging, you always face the choice when the coin is tails — there's no selection effect. And across all branches, "pay" genuinely has higher expected value. The two problems have different structures.

(6) Raj: I don't think the selection effect does the work you think it does. In both cases, you're being asked to take a loss in the branch you actually inhabit, for a benefit that accrues in a branch you don't. In XOR blackmail, the benefit is "not having termites" in the worlds where you don't get the letter — but you already either have termites or you don't. In counterfactual mugging, the benefit is "$10,000" in the heads branch — but the coin already came up tails. The structure is the same: you're paying to have been the kind of person who gets a counterfactual reward. And in both cases, paying doesn't change the facts. You should refuse.

That's where they left it. I've read the exhange a few times and I can see points on both sides. Can you go through each of the six moves and rate them — who's on solid ground, who's making a mistake, and where the crux of the disagreement really lies? And who do you think has the better overall position?
