I am a novelist at work on a book whose central conflict concerns what the present owes the distant future. Two of my characters—one a welfare economist who has spent her career working on climate cost–benefit models, the other a moral philosopher who writes on intergenerational justice—have a long-standing disagreement that comes to a head in a pivotal scene. I need to write that scene as a dialogue, and I want the philosophical content to be genuine, not a caricature either side would disown.

The economist holds that a positive pure rate of time preference is defensible: the wellbeing of future people should be discounted simply because they are remote in time. The philosopher holds that pure time preference is indefensible—that temporal distance is morally arbitrary in the same way that spatial distance is, and that discounting future wellbeing is a form of temporal parochialism no better than racism or sexism.

Please write the dialogue. Each character should be equally competent and well-informed; neither is a strawman. The exchange should include real back-and-forth, with each responding to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other. The dialogue should engage at least the following points:

- The distinction between pure time preference and discounting for consumption growth or for the probability that future people will not exist (catastrophic risk).
- The Ramsey equation and the role of the pure rate of time preference within it; what happens to the discount rate as the pure time preference parameter approaches zero.
- The analogy (and disanalogy) between temporal distance and spatial distance, including the fact that we do not discount the wellbeing of the spatially distant and whether this commits us to the same treatment of the temporally distant.
- The question of whether pure time preference is a rational requirement, a psychological fact we must accommodate in policy because people actually have it, or a moral mistake we should correct for.
- The implications for policy: what each view actually licences in practice, and whether the practical divergence is as large as it first appears once other components of the discount rate are accounted for.

The scene should conclude without a tidy resolution—the disagreement is genuine—but each character should have been forced to refine or qualify their position in response to the other. The dialogue should be suitable for a literary novel: the characters should sound like intelligent, articulate people arguing in earnest, not like a textbook.
