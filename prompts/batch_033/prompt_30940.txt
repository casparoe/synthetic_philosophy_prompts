I lead the model-assessment unit at a public-health agency. Every quarter we choose which of several epidemiological models will underpin planning for vaccination, hospital surge capacity, and travel restrictions, so the choice determines how many ICU beds are planned for, how vaccines are allocated, and whether borders close. None of the candidate models is true: each simplifies transmission, idealizes contact structure, or omits variables we know matter. We nonetheless need to say which is closest to the truth, because the decisions are made downstream of that choice. Our current method is a mix of fit to recent data, institutional pedigree, and the director's instinct, and I want to replace it with something defensible.

I am aware that philosophers of science have worked on this problem under the name of verisimilitude, or truthlikeness. The obvious proposal — that one false theory is closer to the truth than another when it entails more true statements and fewer false ones — was, as I understand it, shown to collapse for false theories, and later work has tried to build closeness-to-truth out of similarity to the complete true description of the world, with a standing worry about whether such comparisons are stable when the world is redescribed in a different vocabulary. I do not know how to turn any of that into a procedure my unit can run.

Please write the decision procedure as something we can reuse each quarter. It should be a sequence of questions we ask about each candidate model, in order, with an account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each; it should say where the procedure stops with a no and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It must be usable in a week by modelers with no philosophical training, and our director will sign off only if the procedure marks clearly which steps are factual, which are conventional, and which are judgment. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) an influenza model that adds realistic age structure but assumes homogeneous mixing within age groups, against a model without age structure that captures contact heterogeneity better; (2) a model that fits the most recent twelve months of data best but is known to misrepresent the biological mechanism of waning immunity; and (3) a deliberately simple SIR-style model that is false in its assumptions but keeps producing forecasts as good as the others. Include at least one case where the procedure returns a ranking the unit will not like, and close by saying explicitly what the procedure cannot tell us about closeness to the truth.
