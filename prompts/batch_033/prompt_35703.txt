A colleague in my department walked me through an argument last week, and I have been unable to find the exit. I want a diagnosis of the argument itself, not a general essay on chaos or determinism.

(1) A dynamical system is deterministic just in case its state at any one time, together with the laws governing the system, fixes its state at every other time.
(2) If a system's present state fixes its future states in that sense, then an observer in possession of the complete present state and the laws could in principle predict any future state exactly.
(3) In a chaotic system, trajectories that begin arbitrarily close together diverge exponentially: after a few Lyapunov times, two trajectories that were indistinguishable to any real measurement lie far apart.
(4) Since every real measurement carries some finite error, no observer ever possesses the complete present state of a chaotic system.
(5) Therefore, from (3) and (4), no observer can predict a chaotic system's future beyond a short horizon, even though (2) says prediction would be possible if the system were deterministic.
(6) So chaotic systems are not deterministic.
(7) But the Lorenz system — the canonical example of chaos — is specified by three ordinary differential equations with no stochastic term, and is standardly described as deterministic.
(8) Hence either the account of determinism in (1) is wrong, or determinism is false of the very systems usually cited as its clearest cases.

I am confident about (7), and I would prefer to keep (1) as a working definition rather than conclude that the Lorenz system is indeterministic. But I cannot see which other premise to reject, or where the argument first overreaches. Which step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
