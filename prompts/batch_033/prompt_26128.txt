I teach a unit on the Fermi paradox in an undergraduate philosophy of science course, and I want a taxonomy of positions to use as a handout. The taxonomy should be built as a tree where each branching point is a question a student can answer without knowing the names of positions in advance: the questions should be concrete claims or thought experiments that split the logical space, not invitations to choose a label. Build the tree from the logical space first — map out the combinations of answers the questions allow — and then locate the actually-defended positions within that space. Mark any logically possible combination that lacks defenders, with a brief note on whether it seems incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of positions the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would split them.

Start from this observation: our galaxy is roughly 13.6 billion years old and contains on the order of a hundred billion stars; a technological civilization with even modestly ambitious interstellar capabilities — traveling at a few percent of light speed and building self-replicating probes — could colonize every habitable system in the galaxy within tens of millions of years, a sliver of the available cosmic time; yet we have no convincing evidence that anyone has done so, or that anyone else is out there at all.

The taxonomy must include as terminal leaves at least: Rare Earth (or Rare Abiogenesis), the position that a Great Filter lies ahead of us, the Zoo Hypothesis, the Dark Forest hypothesis, the view that we are searching the wrong way (wrong medium, wrong wavelengths, insufficient effort), and the view that advanced civilizations are unrecognizable to us (post-biological, having migrated to other substrates or realms). You may add leaves beyond these if the logical space demands them. The order of branching questions matters — each question should genuinely separate positions that agree on all previous questions.

The audience is smart undergraduate students with no philosophy background but a willingness to think carefully. The taxonomy should be compact enough to fit on two printed pages.

As the final line of your response, write \summary{...} containing a one-sentence statement of what the taxonomy reveals about the structure of the debate.
