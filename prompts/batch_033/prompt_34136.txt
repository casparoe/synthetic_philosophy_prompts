So I work in communications at a mid-size nonprofit, and we've been overhauling our style guide to be more inclusive — pronouns, gendered job titles, the works. It's been surprisingly contentious, not in a hostile way but just lots of genuine disagreement about where to draw lines. Anyway, two of my coworkers got into this whole thing at lunch last week about Hofstadter's "Person Paper," the satirical piece where he imagines a world where "white" is the default generic term (so you get "chairwhite," "congresswhite," "freshwhite," etc.) and then someone proposes switching to "person," and the fictional letter-writer is apoplectic about how clunky and artificial "chairperson" and "spokesperson" sound. The point, obviously, is that this mirrors exactly the resistance people have to gender-neutral language in our world — and that if you'd find the race-based complaints absurd, you should find the gender-based ones equally absurd.

Marcus basically thinks the analogy is a knockout. He says it exposes that all the objections about "chairperson" sounding awkward or being a neologism are just aesthetic preferences dressed up as principle, and the satire proves it by swapping in a domain where nobody would take those objections seriously. Elena pushed back pretty hard though. Her argument was that the analogy is rigged because "man" actually has a history as a genuine generic in English — like, the "man" in "chairman" traces back to the Old English "mann" meaning "person," not the gendered "man" (which used to be "wer"). So when people resist "chairperson," part of what they're reacting to, she says, is the loss of an actual etymological pattern, not just discomfort with a marked category becoming unmarked. In Hofstadter's flipped world, "white" as a generic has zero linguistic precedent, so of course the resistance looks absurd — but that's because he's comparing a real historical usage to a completely fabricated one. Marcus came back and said etymology is a red herring; what matters is how the words function in speakers' heads right now, and right now "chairman" absolutely codes as male. She said if etymology doesn't matter then the whole piece is just a rhetorical trick that works by picking a domain with no generic precedent, which begs the question. He said she's missing the forest for the trees — the piece isn't a linguistics paper, it's about default assumptions and who gets to be the unmarked human.

They went in circles for a while and I've been turning it over in my head ever since. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I'm particularly curious whether Elena's etymology point actually does any work against the analogy, or whether Marcus is right that it's beside the point.
