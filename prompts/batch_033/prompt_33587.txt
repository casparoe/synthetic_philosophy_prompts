I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in experimental philosophy, twelve students, one eighty-minute session. Design a classroom activity in which students first serve as subjects in a minimal-pair judgment task modeled on the Knobe effect, then work in small groups to articulate what principle separates the two cases, and finally confront the aggregate pattern of their own responses before the phenomenon is named. I want the surprise to be felt rather than reported: before the judgments are tallied, each student must write down and publicly commit to a prediction about how the class will respond, and the facilitator must not introduce the name or the standard interpretation until the debrief. Specify the vignettes to use, written in your own words rather than quoted from the published studies, and design them so that one case involves a norm violation and the other a norm-conforming action with structurally similar side effects. Then give a procedure for collecting judgments without social contamination, a timeline for the eighty minutes, and a debrief that maps the class's responses to the distinction between acting intentionally and acting with foresight, the role of norm violations in producing the asymmetry, and the broader experimental-philosophy question of what folk intuitions can and cannot tell us about concepts. By the end, students should see the asymmetry as a live feature of their own judgments rather than a reported finding, and as a methodological challenge for reading concepts off intuitions. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the effect a classroom replication cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the instructor instead.
