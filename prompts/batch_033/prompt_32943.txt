I've been thinking about David Lewis's account of conventions as solutions to coordination problems. As I understand it, a convention is a regularity in behaviour that everyone follows because everyone expects everyone else to follow it, and there is at least one alternative regularity that would have served the same purpose — driving on the left rather than the right being the standard example. Lately I have been trying to see whether moral norms fit this picture. Take the practice of promising. We keep our promises partly because we expect others to keep theirs — if promising broke down as a social practice, it would be pointless to keep promises unilaterally. And promising seems arbitrary in the sense that different societies could have different conventions around what counts as a binding commitment. So promising looks like a Lewis-style convention. But if that is right, then the obligation to keep a promise is just a matter of mutual expectations and could have been otherwise — which seems to drain promising of any genuine moral force. Have I correctly applied Lewis's framework, or am I missing something about what makes a convention a convention in his sense? Please keep your answer to one paragraph of no more than 300 words.
