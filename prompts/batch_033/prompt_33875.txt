2. **Higher-order evidence has genuine normative force.** Learning that one is sleep-deprived, biased, or reasoning under conditions known to impair judgment can make it rational to reduce confidence in conclusions reached under those conditions, even when one cannot identify any specific flaw in the reasoning itself. The fact that the first-order evidence still seems to support P does not, by itself, insulate the belief from rational revision.

3. **Epistemic akrasia is always irrational.** It is never rational to be in the state of believing that P while simultaneously believing that one's total evidence does not support P. Whatever else rationality demands, it forbids this kind of structural mismatch between one's first-order beliefs and one's beliefs about what one's evidence supports.

4. **Steadfastness about peer disagreement.** When you have carefully examined the first-order evidence and reached a conclusion, learning that an epistemic peer — someone equally competent, honest, and well-informed about the same body of evidence — disagrees with you does not by itself require you to reduce your confidence. You may permissibly retain your view, even while acknowledging the peer's credentials.
