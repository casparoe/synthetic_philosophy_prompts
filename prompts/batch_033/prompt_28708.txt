Here is an argument I reconstructed from a paper on group decision-making, and I cannot find a stable way out of it.

(1) A collective body—a court, a board, a committee—is rationally required to hold a consistent set of judgments: if it judges that P and judges that Q, it must judge that P and Q.
(2) If a body is to decide democratically rather than by letting one member's view prevail, its collective judgment on any proposition should be the majority judgment of its members on that proposition.
(3) Majority judgment, applied proposition by proposition, can violate (1). Take a three-member panel deciding whether a defendant is liable under a rule making liability equivalent to the conjunction of breach and causation. Member A finds breach and causation, hence liability. Member B finds breach but no causation, hence no liability. Member C finds no breach but causation, hence no liability. The majority finds breach (A and B), the majority finds causation (A and C), and the majority finds no liability (B and C). So the panel accepts both premises and rejects the conclusion that follows from them.
(4) Therefore majority judgment is not an acceptable way for a collective body to form its judgments.
(5) And the problem is not a quirk of majority rule. The same structure—individually consistent judgments mapped to an inconsistent collective set—can be produced for any rule that treats voters and propositions even-handedly, unless the rule simply makes one member's judgment decisive. The familiar ways of restoring consistency all abandon even-handedness somewhere: a premise-based rule lets the verdict on the conclusion be fixed by the premises even when a majority of members reject that conclusion, while a conclusion-based rule ignores the members' views on the premises and can yield a verdict that contradicts what follows from the majority's views on them.
(6) So no acceptable method of aggregating judgments exists: every method sacrifices either collective rationality or democratic even-handedness.

That conclusion cannot be right. Courts and boards do form judgments, and some ways of doing it are plainly better than others. I am pulled in two directions: I would like to keep premise-by-premise majority voting, but I would also like to keep the idea that a group with settled judgments is answerable to the same consistency norms as an individual. Giving up either one seems to cost more than I can afford. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
