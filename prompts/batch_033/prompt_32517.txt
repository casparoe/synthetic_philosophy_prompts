I've been involved in a long-running discussion on one of the rationalist forums about whether compatibilism actually addresses what people care about in the free will debate, or whether it just changes the subject. Several people pointed me towards P.F. Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment' as a genuinely different approach — one that reframes the whole problem rather than picking a side in the compatibilist/incompatibilist fight. I haven't got round to reading the paper yet, but I did come across this summary in a book that's meant to introduce analytic philosophy to a general audience:

'Strawson argued that the entire free will debate rests on a confusion. Whether determinism is true or false simply doesn't matter for moral responsibility, because moral responsibility isn't a matter of having metaphysically free will — it's a matter of our natural emotional responses to one another. We feel resentment when someone wrongs us, gratitude when someone helps us, and indignation when we witness injustice. These "reactive attitudes," as Strawson called them, are deep-seated human emotions that we cannot simply switch off. Determinism can't threaten moral responsibility because it can't make us stop feeling these things; we are psychologically incapable of living without them. The only time we do suspend these attitudes is when we discover that someone wasn't really a proper target for them — for instance, if they were coerced, or if they suffer from a serious mental illness — and in those cases we shift to what Strawson called the "objective attitude," treating the person as something to be managed or controlled rather than as a participant in a shared moral life. The upshot is that incompatibilists are demanding a kind of freedom that has nothing to do with our actual practices of holding one another responsible, and their position collapses once you notice that moral responsibility is constituted by these emotional responses, not by some further metaphysical fact about the universe.'

Something about this summary bothers me, and I'm trying to work out whether the problem is with Strawson or with the summary. The appeal to psychological inevitability — "we can't stop feeling these things" — sounds like an is-ought move, and I'd expect a philosopher of Strawson's reputation to have something more subtle to say than "we're stuck with our emotions, so determinism is irrelevant." The summary also seems to suggest that Strawson simply defines moral responsibility in terms of our reactive attitudes, which would make the incompatibilist's worry disappear by fiat rather than by argument. But I haven't read the paper, and the book this summary comes from is generally reliable on other topics I know more about. I'd like to know: is this a fair account of Strawson's argument? Go through the summary and tell me which parts a careful Strawson scholar would accept, which parts are subtly distorted, and whether the overall conclusion the summary draws actually follows from what Strawson established. If the summary is unfair, what's the better version of the argument that it's gesturing at?
