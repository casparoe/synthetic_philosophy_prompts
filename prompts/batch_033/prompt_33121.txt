I work as a backend engineer at a mid-size tech company, and last week two of our data scientists got into a pretty heated argument during a sprint review that I still can't fully untangle. We ran an A/B test on a new recommendation algorithm — call it RecSys-B — against the current production one. The overall numbers looked good: users in the treatment group had about 4% higher click-through rate (significant, p < 0.01). But when Amara (one of the data scientists) broke the results down by device type, things got weird. On mobile, RecSys-B was 2% *worse* than the control. On desktop, it was also 2% *worse* than the control. Yet overall it was 4% better. The reason is that RecSys-B caused users to shift toward desktop — the recommendations were richer and people wanted to see them on a bigger screen, so the proportion of desktop sessions went way up, and desktop users naturally have higher CTR anyway.

Here's where the argument started. Amara said this is textbook Simpson's paradox and we should not ship: the algorithm is genuinely worse for users on every device type, so rolling it out would degrade the experience across the board. The overall positive number is just a compositional artifact. Marcus (the other data scientist) pushed back hard. He said Amara was misapplying Simpson's paradox. Yeah, the subgroup effects are negative, but device type is *downstream* of the treatment — the algorithm changes what device people use, and that's part of the intervention. If you control for device type, you're stripping out one of the mechanisms by which the algorithm improves engagement. The total effect is positive, and that's what matters for the decision. He said Simpson's paradox is only a paradox when you mistakenly think the disaggregated numbers tell the truer story, but here the aggregate is the right causal estimand.

Amara came back with: even if device type is downstream, the fact remains that a user sitting on their couch with their phone gets worse recommendations under RecSys-B. That's a real harm, not a statistical illusion. You can't just say "they'll switch to desktop more." What if they *can't* switch — maybe they're on a bus, or their laptop is dead? You're making the product worse for them in that moment.

Marcus replied: by that logic you'd never ship anything that has heterogenous effects. Almost any feature makes some subgroup worse off. The question is whether overall user experience improves, and it does. He also said Amara's framing implicitly treats device type as a pre-treatment covariate you should adjust for, when it's actually a mediator — and adjusting for mediators is a classic mistake in causal inference.

Amara's final shot was: fine, call it a mediator — but if the mechanism is "make the mobile experience worse so people flee to desktop," that's a pretty terrible mechanism to rely on, and we should at least understand *why* mobile degrades before shipping.

They never resolved it, and our eng manager ended up tabling the decision.
