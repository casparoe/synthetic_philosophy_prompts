Here is an argument I have been wrestling with that seems to force a conclusion I cannot accept, and I want to know which step is the culprit.

(1) Try to conceive of an object that exists entirely unconceived — without any mind perceiving or thinking about it.
(2) In the very act of trying to conceive of such an object, you are conceiving of it. Whatever you succeed in imagining is something conceived by you.
(3) So you cannot conceive of an unconceived object; the attempt defeats itself.
(4) If it is impossible even to conceive of something existing unconceived, then the idea of mind-independent existence is incoherent.
(5) Therefore, nothing can exist without a mind perceiving it — esse est percipi.

The conclusion is Berkeley's idealism, which I find hard to take seriously. Yet each step feels individually forced. If I reject (1) by saying we do not need to conceive of a thing to know it could exist, I seem to abandon the empiricist principle that our ideas of existence are grounded in what we can conceive. If I reject (2), I am denying that conceiving is a mental act that brings its object before the mind. If I reject (4), I am claiming that something can be the case even though it is literally inconceivable, which seems to license all manner of nonsense. And yet (5) seems absurd: the tree in the quad really does seem to be there when no one is looking.

Where does this argument go wrong? If it does not go wrong, what exactly am I committed to if I accept it but try to minimize the damage? I am especially interested in whether any of the standard responses — Kantian, Russellian, direct realist — actually escape the trap or merely relocate it, and what each of them has to give up to do so.
