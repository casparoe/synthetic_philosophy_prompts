I was in this philosophy of mind seminar last week and we got into it about the hard problem of consciousness. One guy in the class — really confident, kind of a bulldozer — kept saying the whole thing is a giant mistake, and his go-to move was an analogy with vitalism. His line was: for centuries people thought you needed some special "élan vital" or life-force to explain living things, because how could mere matter organize itself, reproduce, heal wounds, and so on? It seemed obvious that life was something over and above chemistry. Then biochemistry matured and we realized life is just very complicated chemistry — no spooky ingredient, no missing ontological ingredient, and nobody today thinks metabolism poses a deep philosophical mystery. His claim was that the hard problem is exactly the same kind of confusion: we're pre-theoretically convinced that subjective experience is special and can't be reduced to physical processes, but once neuroscience is sufficiently advanced we'll look back at Chalmers the way we now look at Hans Driesch. A few of us pushed back but the discussion got tangled and we ran out of time before anyone really pinned down what was wrong with the analogy — or whether anything was.

I've been chewing on it since and I honestly can't tell if the analogy is genuinely illuminating or just rhetorically effective. Could you do a careful audit? I'd like to see: what maps onto what (a table of correspondences would be great), where the mapping breaks, which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion he was pushing, and whether the conclusion — that the hard problem will dissolve the way vitalism did — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. And if the analogy turns out to be more misleading than helpful, is there a better one that captures what's actually at stake? Please write your answer in American English.
