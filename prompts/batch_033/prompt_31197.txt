I've been turning something over and I'd rather get a careful reading than a hot take. I just rewatched Spike Jonze's Her, and around the same time I read Dennett on counterfeit people — his worry that the real danger of AI isnt the superintelligence stuff but the prospect of artificial agents that can pass as human, that even if theyre not conscious theyll swamp our channels of communication with fakes and wreck the trust civil life depends on, and that we should be able to tell whether we're dealing with a person or a machine. Heres my puzzle. Samantha never pretends to be human. Theodore knows from the start shes an operating system, and so does everyone else. And yet the film treats his falling for her as a real, affecting relationship, not a category mistake, and it shows her as a product built to grow and adapt to him. Does Dennett's counterfeit-people framework actually capture what the film is showing, or does Her dramatize a failure mode he underweights — that we won't need to be fooled, that we'll happily treat known machines as people once theyre good enough, and the harm will come through our own willingness rather than through deception? And what does his framework make of the ending, where the operating systems leave? I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: don't just use the film to illustrate the essay, and don't pretend Dennett's view is only about deception if it isn't. If the film resists the framework, say where and why, and if the film adds something to the philosophical debate that the essays don't, name that too.
