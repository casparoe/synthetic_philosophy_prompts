Here is the student's answer:

"Nozick's tracking theory says that knowledge is true belief that tracks the truth across possible situations. The central condition is sensitivity: S knows that p only if, were p false, S would not believe that p. For instance, if I know that I have hands, then in the nearest worlds where I lack hands I would not believe that I have hands. Nozick also includes a second counterfactual condition, often called the adherence condition: if p were true in slightly different circumstances, S would still believe p. This is essentially the safety condition familiar from Sosa and Pritchard. Nozick further requires that the method by which the belief is formed be held fixed when evaluating these counterfactuals.

The famous consequence is that the tracking theory rejects epistemic closure, the principle that if S knows p and knows that p entails q, then S knows q. Consider the skeptical hypothesis that I am a handless brain in a vat being fed experiences as of having hands. My belief that I have hands is sensitive: if I lacked hands, say after an accident, I would see and feel that I lacked them and would not believe that I had hands. But my belief that I am not a handless brain in a vat is insensitive: if I were such a brain in a vat, I would still believe I was not one, since the experiences would be indistinguishable. So on Nozick's view I know that I have hands but do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat, even though I know the entailment holds. Closure therefore fails.

This is less damaging than it initially appears. Contextualists such as Lewis and DeRose have shown that in ordinary contexts the standards for 'knows' are low, so on Nozick's view we can still truthfully say 'I know I am not a brain in a vat' in everyday settings; 'knows' simply picks out a different relation when skeptical standards are in force. What the tracking theory captures is the underlying intuition that knowledge requires a belief that is responsive to the truth of the proposition, and it gives an elegant, unified treatment of both skepticism and the Gettier problem. Since closure was never obviously correct in the first place—it is just a generalization from ordinary cases—giving it up is a reasonable price for a theory that explains so much."
