I'm giving a paper next month at a workshop that's half philosophers of social science and half evolutionary game theorists. The thesis is that Lewis basically got the content of a convention right — an arbitrary but self-perpetuating equilibrium in a recurring coordination problem — but his mechanism for how conventions get started (precedent, common knowledge, a standing preference for conformity) can't do the origin work, so I add an evolutionary story where norms begin as local regularities that spread by imitation and payoff advantage and only later pick up the common-knowledge structure Lewis describes. My argument sketch: (1) set up a simple 2x2 coordination game with multiple equilibria; (2) argue that the Lewisian selection story already presupposes a history of interaction, so it can't explain the first run; (3) simulate a population under a replicator dynamic with local interaction and show a stable convention emerging without anyone representing the whole equilibrium; (4) claim the resulting regularity counts as a Lewis convention once agents form the relevant expectations; (5) conclude that the evolutionary process is the origin story and Lewis's account is the mature-state description, not the selection mechanism. The room includes two hardcore rational-choice people who think an evolutionary explanation either reduces to individual incentives or changes the subject, and a few modelers who think common knowledge is unfalsifiable and philosophers use the word norm too loosely. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would put them, which ones have real force, what I should concede up front, the weakest part of my argument I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
