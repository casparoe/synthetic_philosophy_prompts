Ok so a friend and I were talking about the unilateralist's curse — you know, the idea from Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg that when a bunch of people share a goal but have different private estimates of whether some action is a good idea, and any one of them can just go ahead and do it, the action gets taken if even the most optimistic person thinks it's net-positive, which can be disastrous if that person is wrong. The standard fix is supposed to be something like: don't act unilaterally, wait for consensus or at least a supermajority.

But then we started applying this to everyday moral life and the whole thing seemed to eat itself. Here's the argument we ended up with:

(1) The unilateralist's curse says: when agents share an aim but have private estimates of whether an action advances that aim, unilateral action means the most optimistic estimate wins — even if the average estimate says "don't."

(2) The natural remedy is a norm: don't act on your private estimate unless there's broad agreement (say, a supermajority) that the action is net-positive.

(3) The global community of morally serious people shares a very general aim — something like "make the world better" or "reduce suffering" — but we have massively different private estimates of which concrete actions actually do that. (Think about disagreements over AI risk, animal welfare vs. global health, systemic change vs. incremental reform, etc.)

(4) If (2) is the right response to (1), then each morally serious person should refrain from any concrete action that lacks supermajority support among morally serious people.

(5) Almost no concrete action has that kind of support. Giving to GiveDirectly vs. AMF vs. the Shrimp Welfare Project? No supermajority. Working on AI safety vs. climate change? No supermajority. Even something as basic as "donate 10% of your income" doesn't get consensus on the margin.

(6) So (4) tells us to do basically nothing.

(7) But "do basically nothing" is itself a course of action that lacks supermajority support — most morally serious people think you should do *something*.

(8) So the norm in (2) forbids itself. It's self-defeating.

(9) Therefore, either the unilateralist's curse doesn't apply to the global moral case (but why wouldn't it?), or the remedy in (2) is wrong (but then what's the right response to the curse?), or the curse isn't a real problem to begin with.

I'm stuck. I feel like something goes wrong between (1)-(2) and (3)-(4), maybe a scope slip or an equivocation on "share an aim," but I can't make it precise. Give me exactly five things — each a sentence or two — diagnosing where this argument goes wrong or which steps to reject and what it costs. No intro, no conclusion, just the numbered list.
