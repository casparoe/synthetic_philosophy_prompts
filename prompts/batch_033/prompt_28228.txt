I'm doing a podcast where I 'interview' historical philosophers — the gimmick is they've been brought to the present and have had time to catch up on everything published since they died. Next episode is Descartes, and I want to spend the whole time on his views about animal minds. You know, the beast-machine stuff: animals are automata, no souls, no consciousness, no genuine feeling. I've read the Discourse Part V and the relevant bits of the Meditations and the correspondence with More, but I need questions that actually corner him, not just let him restate the view.

Draft me eight to ten questions with follow-ups. I want at least one question on each of these pressure points:

- The argument from language: he says animals can't use language in a way that shows rational thought, but human infants and deaf-mutes can't either in the relevant sense. Why doesn't his argument overgeneralize? And if he bites the bullet on infants, I want a follow-up about whether he's really prepared to say a newborn has more mental life than an adult chimp.

- The argument from behavioral flexibility (or lack thereof): he claims animal behavior is fully explained by mechanical principles, but his own physiology — the stuff about animal spirits and the pineal gland — was pretty speculative. Does he actually have an empirical case that animal behavior is stimulus-determined in a way human behavior isn't, or is he just asserting it? Follow-up: what would he make of modern ethology — tool use in crows, octopus problem-solving, etc.?

- The suffering problem: if a dog yelps when you kick it, and Descartes says that's just a mechanical response with no inner feeling, what exactly is the difference between that and a human cry of pain? Is he committed to saying that if the mechanical story were good enough, human pain responses would also be evidence of nothing? Follow-up: does he think vivisection is morally unproblematic, and if so, is he comfortable with where that lands?

- The theological angle: he's clearly motivated by wanting to deny animals an immortal soul (so no animal afterlife problem, no animal theodicy problem). But couldn't a benevolent God give animals conscious experiences without immortal souls? What's the tight connection between having a mind and having an immortal soul, for him? Follow-up: if God can create a world where animal suffering doesn't exist because animals don't suffer, why not create a world where human suffering doesn't exist either?

- The contingency question: does his dualism actually force the beast-machine conclusion, or could a consistent Cartesian dualist grant that animals have conscious sensations but no rational soul? What's the weakest premise in his argument that he could drop while keeping the rest of his system?

And then one or two wildcard questions that go somewhere I haven't thought of. Make the follow-ups keyed to his most likely reply — I want to be ready for the obvious parries.
