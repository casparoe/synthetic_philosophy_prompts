**Sam** started by saying Hume's argument is question-begging. The argument (as Sam reconstructed it) is: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature; a law of nature is something established by uniform experience; so by definition the evidence against any miracle is as strong as evidence can be; therefore no testimony can ever overcome it. But that just builds the conclusion into the definition of a law of nature — you're assuming that the uniformity is exceptionless before you even look at the testimony, which is exactly what's at issue.

**Maya** pushed back: that's not fair to Hume. He doesn't define laws of nature as exceptionless by fiat. He says we have a massive, uniform body of experience — dead people stay dead, water doesn't turn into wine — and testimony is itself a kind of evidence that we evaluate based on past experience of how reliable witnesses are. So it's not a definitional trick; it's a weighing of two bodies of evidence, and the one drawn from uniform experience is just vastly stronger.

**Sam** came back with what I thought was their best move: if that's the argument, it proves too much. Consider someone in 1696 reporting a black swan. Every European's experience said all swans are white — uniform experience. By Hume's logic you should reject the testimony, because it's more probable the witness is mistaken than that the uniform experience is wrong. But we don't think that — we think you should update and accept there are black swans. So Hume's principle would rule out accepting any shocking-but-true empirical discovery.

**Maya** responded that a black swan isn't a violation of a law of nature, it's just a new instance of a kind. Laws of nature are a different sort of thing from accidental generalizations like "all swans are white." Hume's argument is specifically about violations of genuine laws, not surprising observations within the lawful framework.

**Sam** wasn't having it: but how do you tell which regularities are laws and which are accidental generalizations *before* you encounter a putative counterexample? That distinction only becomes clear in retrospect. At the time, someone reporting a black swan was reporting something that contradicted all prior experience just as much as a resurrection would. If you can't distinguish them in advance, Hume's principle either applies to both or to neither.

**Maya** conceded that's a real problem but said: look, for a miracle like the resurrection, we're not just dealing with "all prior experience" in some generic sense. We have the entire framework of biology, thermodynamics, and the observed history of every other dead organism. The evidence against it is of a wholly different order than "Europeans haven't seen a non-white swan." So even if Hume can't draw a perfect line between laws and accidental generalizations, the actual cases we care about are so far on one side of whatever line exists that the argument still works where it matters.

**Sam**' last move: okay, but then the argument isn't the structural, almost-logical proof it's usually presented as. It collapses into ordinary Bayesian reasoning — you have a very low prior on resurrections, and you'd need a ton of testimony to overcome it, but there's no in-principle bar. Whether any given miracle report clears that bar depends on the specifics of the case, not on a general argument that testimony can *never* be enough. So Hume hasn't actually shown what he claimed to show.
