You are a professional philosopher of mind with deep expertise in Daniel Dennett's work on consciousness, particularly the multiple drafts model and the critique of the Cartesian theater. You are also thoroughly familiar with the opposing position—the view that there is a unified locus or "finish line" in the brain where conscious experience comes together, often called Cartesian materialism. You understand both sides well enough to represent each as its own proponent would.

I want you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed Cartesian materialist and a committed multiple-drafts theorist. The two participants have spent a year arguing in good faith, and this document is what they would produce together.

Before writing the final document, think through the following: what each side genuinely means by its core claims (not what the other side caricatures it as meaning); what empirical facts about the brain both sides accept; what the real cruxes are as opposed to merely terminological confusions; what each side counts as the strongest argument against its own view; and where the remaining disagreement is sharpest and most consequential. Take your time with this.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed-upon claims—claims about the brain, about phenomenology, about what a theory of consciousness must explain, and about what both sides explicitly reject. These should be substantive, not vacuous. Avoid formulations that collapse into one side's vocabulary.

2. A disagreements table with columns: (a) the precise question at issue, (b) the Cartesian materialist's answer, phrased so a Cartesian materialist would accept the wording, (c) the multiple-drafts theorist's answer, phrased so a multiple-drafts theorist would accept the wording, and (d) whether this disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, with a brief justification for that classification.

3. A list of cruxes—specific sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, describe a concrete empirical finding, thought experiment, or argument that both sides agree bears on the crux, and state what each side predicts about it. Where possible, specify what each side would count as a genuine concession against its own position.

Take definite positions throughout—this document represents what these two specific theorists would actually sign after a year of good-faith adversarial collaboration, not a survey of all possible positions. If you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal, say exactly which part and why, and show how the remaining substantive disagreement is narrower but sharper as a result.
