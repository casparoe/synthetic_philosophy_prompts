i'm a TA for a course that's a weird mix of philosophy of biology and behavioral genetics, and i'm grading the short-answer section of the midterm. here's one of the questions and a student's answer. i want to grade it properly rather than just pattern-matching to "they know the standard heritability caveats." grade it out of 10 and give me point-by-point comments: what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong (especially anything subtle a lazy grader would miss), and what a corrected version of the wrong parts should say. dont just give an overall impression.

question (10 points): In plain terms, what does it mean to say that the heritability of a trait like height is 0.8 in a given population? What, if anything, does that estimate license us to conclude about (a) the causes of an individual's trait value, (b) how much an environmental intervention could shift the trait, and (c) the causes of a mean difference between two groups raised in the same environment?

student's answer:

Heritability is a population-level variance decomposition: the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population that's statistically associated with genetic differences among individuals. So a heritability of 0.8 means 80% of the observed variation among individuals is genetic in origin and about 20% is environmental (setting aside gene-environment correlation and interaction, which get folded in differently depending on the study design). It says nothing about any particular individual: it doesn't mean 80% of a given person's height is "caused by genes." It's also local to the population and the range of environments where it was estimated, so the estimate can change if the population or the environment changes. And high heritability doesn't imply the trait can't be changed environmentally — a trait could be highly heritable in the current range of environments and still respond to a new environmental intervention. That said, since only 20% of the variance is environmental, an environmental intervention is bounded: it can only move the trait within that 20% slice, so for a trait with heritability 0.8 there isn't much room for environmental improvement as long as the environment stays roughly as it is. Finally, heritability within populations doesn't by itself tell you why two groups differ in means, and it's a common mistake to think it does. But if the two groups are raised in the same environment, high within-group heritability does put pressure on the idea that the between-group difference is environmental: if environment accounts for only 20% of the variation within each group, the mean gap between them is unlikely to be environmental either.
