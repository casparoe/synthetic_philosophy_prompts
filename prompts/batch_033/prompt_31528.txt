Okay so I just got back from a very long walk with a friend where we argued for like three hours about ranked-choice voting and Arrow's impossibility theorem, and I realized we were talking past each other the whole time. He thinks RCV basically fixes the spoiler problem and that Arrow is just a technical curiosity; I kept saying no, the theorem means every way of aggregating preferences has to give up at least one thing you'd want. By the end it turned out he's fine giving up independence of irrelevant alternatives, and I'm more of a "preferences have to be shaped before you aggregate them" person, and neither of us had a clean name for what we actually thought. I write a small politics-and-philosophy blog and I want to turn this into one of those sorting quizzes where people find out which position they actually hold.

Please write me a quiz that sorts readers into positions on what Arrow's theorem shows about voting, based on how they answer concrete cases rather than which label they like. The audience is my blog readers: they know roughly what the theorem says but they arent social choice theorists, and a fair number of them have strong opinions about RCV. No question should just ask "do you think IIA matters?" or ask them to pick a school of thought.

Format the response in these sections, in this order:

1. How to take the quiz (about 75 words) — set it up in a friendly, non-lecturing voice.

2. The cases (8 questions) — each question is a concrete voting scenario with two to four answer options in ordinary language. Make sure to include: the classic three-voter Condorcet cycle, a spoiler entering a plurality election, an "irrelevant" candidate changing a Borda result, a case where agenda control decides the outcome, a case where one voter cares much more intensely than the others, and a case where a voter has what looks like a spiteful or self-defeating preference. Phrase everything so a reader can answer without knowing any social choice terminology.

3. Scoring key (about 250 words) — how to map answer patterns onto positions, as a points table if that's cleanest.

4. The positions (six leaves, each about 60 to 80 words) — name each one, and give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want at least these six represented: the domain restrictor (single-peakedness does the real work), the IIA-dropper (Borda/score/approval people), the cycle-embracer (majority rule is fine even when it cycles), the information enlarger (the real problem is Arrow's ordinal, non-comparable setup), the deliberative transformer (fix the preferences before you aggregate them), and the agenda-power realist (some person or body will always have tie-breaking or agenda power, and that's not the disaster Arrow makes it sound like). Rename them if you find something snappier.

5. Blind spots (about 150 words) — which positions the quiz can't cleanly seperate, and any answer combinations that nobody seems to occupy, with a sentence on why each one is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Be honest about the places where, say, the cycle-embracer and the agenda-power realist blur together.

The quiz voice can stay light, but the sorting should do the real work.
