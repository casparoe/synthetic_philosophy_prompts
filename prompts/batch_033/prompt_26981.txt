Two of my former colleagues — both, like me, retired from the physical sciences and now absorbed in philosophy — have been arguing for weeks about a single case: a long queue outside a restaurant neither of them knew, while a similar restaurant next door stood almost empty. One took the queue to be strong evidence about where to eat; the other took it to be a sign of the sort of collectively generated illusion that a reasoning individual should resist. I reproduce their exchange as faithfully as I can.

(1) The first maintained that following the queue is straightforwardly rational. Each person ahead has private information — a recommendation, a review, a favorable impression from the street — and a choice based on such information transmits it. A long queue aggregates many private signals that no single observer could gather alone; to ignore all of that in favor of one's own passing impression is not independence but arrogance.

(2) The second replied that this aggregation argument fails. Choices transmit information only when they are made independently. Once people choose a restaurant because others have chosen it, later choices cease to be independent evidence and become echoes of the first few. The length of the queue then measures its own momentum, not accumulated information.

(3) The first answered that this goes too far. Even if later choices are partly echoes, the observed history can still rationally outweigh a single observer's weak private signal. If dozens have entered one establishment and almost none the other, a person whose own hunch weakly favors the empty one may reasonably set that hunch aside: the probability that the crowded restaurant is the better one, conditional on the entire observed history, can exceed the probability that her own hunch is correct. Herding can therefore be rational, and this is no embarrassment to rationality.

(4) The second countered that this is precisely the embarrassment. If it is rational for each person to set aside a private signal, the public sequence stops incorporating new information, and the crowd can lock itself onto the worse option even though the information distributed across the group would, if pooled, favor the better one. The result is individually rational and collectively defective — and fragile, since one credible dissenter or a little public information can shatter it.

(5) The first responded that no one can be faulted for failing to use information she does not have. Rationality governs the use of one's own evidence; the fact that the group, in some distributed sense, has better information is irrelevant to the choice actually facing the person on the pavement.

(6) The second replied that a sophisticated reasoner should take this into account and discount the queue accordingly. Knowing that many in the line are there only because others are, she should treat its length as weak, autocorrelated evidence rather than as many independent signals; in many cases the rational response will be to trust her own impression more than the queue invites her to.

I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication: which claims survive scrutiny and which fail, where each side overstates its case, what the two of them are jointly missing, and which side is on balance closer to the truth. I am less interested in the particular restaurant than in the underlying question.
