A friend of mine backed me into this argument and I can't figure out where it goes wrong. We were arguing about whether there's any principled reason not to hand your wallet to a stranger with a good story and a big number, and he laid it out like this:

(1) Rational choice is taking the action with the highest expected utility.
(2) The expected utility of an action is the sum over the possible outcomes of (probability of that outcome × utility of that outcome).
(3) A stranger on the street offers to make you richer than you can imagine tomorrow if you give him five dollars now. You're almost certain he's full of it, but you can't assign the claim probability exactly zero — you can't rule out that the world is much stranger than you think, so you assign it some tiny p > 0.
(4) You might object that the fact that he's making the offer is itself evidence the offer is fake. But whatever that evidence amounts to, it's already included in p. And for any p > 0 and any finite cost c of giving him the money, there's some promised payoff U such that pU > c. The stranger can always just name a bigger number.
(5) So, by (1) and (2), giving him the five dollars is the rational choice.
(6) And the same move works for any amount — your savings, your house, whatever. There's no stopping point: whatever amount you'd hand over, a bigger number makes handing over more the rational move.
(7) That's absurd. Nobody thinks you should sign over your savings because a guy on the street promised you 3↑↑↑3 utils.
(8) So at least one of the steps above has to be false.

I want to know which step you'd reject and what you're paying for it. My instinct is to keep (1) and (2) and say the trouble is somewhere around (4), but I can't say exactly what, and I don't want to fix it by declaring the probability literally zero — that feels like it's own kind of dogmatism. I'm also worried that whatever patch I accept will force me to change how I reason about ordinary tiny-probability, huge-stakes cases — existential risks, Pascal's wager, that sort of thing. Which premise is actually load-bearing here, and what does giving it up commit me to?
