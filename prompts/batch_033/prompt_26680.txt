In clinical neurology, the standard way to determine whether a patient with a severe brain injury is conscious is behavioral: clinicians look for command-following, verbal responses, visual pursuit, or purposeful movement. Patients who show sleep-wake cycles but no behavioral signs of awareness are diagnosed as being in a vegetative state (now often called unresponsive wakefulness syndrome), and this diagnosis carries enormous consequences — it shapes decisions about pain management, life-sustaining treatment, and in some jurisdictions the withdrawal of artificial nutrition and hydration. Over the past two decades, however, researchers using fMRI and EEG have shown that a nontrivial minority of patients who appear behaviorally vegetative can modulate their brain activity in response to instructions — for instance, by imagining playing tennis or walking through their house when asked — indicating that they are in fact conscious but unable to produce motor output. Despite these findings, behavioral assessment remains the clinical and legal gold standard in virtually every country; neuroimaging is used in research settings but has not displaced bedside observation as the basis for diagnosis and decision-making.

I am trying to understand what theory of consciousness this practice presupposes. At first glance, it seems to presuppose a form of behaviorism or functionalism about consciousness — the view that consciousness just is, or is reliably indicated by, a certain kind of behavioral responsiveness. But the practice is not consistently behaviorist: clinicians do not treat locked-in patients (who are fully conscious but paralyzed except for eye movements) as unconscious, and considerable effort goes into establishing communication with them. Nor is the practice consistently functionalist in any precise sense, since the behavioral criteria are coarse and qualitative, not derived from a worked-out functional theory of what consciousness requires. And there is a further puzzle: if we have evidence that some behaviorally vegetative patients are conscious, why does behavioral assessment retain its authority? Is the implicit view that consciousness without behavioral expression does not count for the purposes that matter clinically and legally — that it is consciousness in some metaphysical sense but not in the sense that grounds moral status? Or is it simply a pragmatic compromise that does not reflect any coherent philosophical position at all?

I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of consciousness that the practice of behavioral diagnosis presupposes, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me whether the practice is philosophically coherent as it stands. If it is not, what would have to change — in the diagnostic criteria, in the legal framework, or in the underlying theory — to bring it into coherence?
