I am a product-safety reviewer at a company that ships consumer AI systems — a voice assistant, a customer-service bot, and a writing tool. I review roughly a dozen proposed behaviors or new features a month before launch, and for each one I must produce a written determination: honest, deceptive, or not classifiable without a policy decision from above. The stakes are high: a wrong call can mean consumer-protection enforcement, press coverage, or a recall, and the engineers in the room usually argue that nothing the system says is technically false. I have no formal philosophy background, my colleagues will not read anything with jargon in it, and I have about half a day per case. My determinations go to a compliance lead and then to legal for sign-off, so the output has to be something a lawyer can act on.

Write me a reusable decision procedure for classifying whether an AI system's behavior is honest or deceptive. Ground it in a defensible account of what deception, lying, misleading, and sincerity actually are, but express it as a sequence of tests I can run without the theory. The procedure should specify:

- the tests in the order they should be run, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- where the procedure stops with a "deceptive" finding, where it stops with an "honest" finding, and where it must stop and hand the question to a person because it is a values or policy question rather than a factual one;
- which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped — for example, the difference between what a system literally says and what a reasonable user would take it to mean; the difference between intending to mislead and merely being indifferent to whether the output is true; the difference between a false statement and a true-but-misleading one; the difference between the system's actual confidence and the confidence the interface displays;
- the hard cases the procedure must handle: a literally true answer that invites a false inference; a disclaimer that exists but is effectively hidden; a system that qualifies its answer as low confidence while the interface strips the qualification; a system that presents itself in first-person, emotional terms without any intent to deceive; a summary that omits the strongest counterargument to the view it presents;
- where a step is a judgment call dressed up as a test, and where the procedure will systematically misfire; and
- what the procedure cannot do, and the cases where it will give an answer that is defensible but uncomfortable.

Then run the procedure on these three worked cases:

1. A customer-service bot, asked whether a subscription can be canceled online, answers "you can cancel from your account page" when in fact the cancellation option appears only after the user has spoken to a retention agent. The sentence is literally true, but the ordinary reading is false.
2. A writing assistant regularly produces plausible-sounding legal and medical text with no indication of confidence. When asked directly, it says it cannot give professional advice, but the interface otherwise presents its output as authoritative, and the team that built it is indifferent to whether the output is true.
3. A voice assistant describes itself as "your friend" and says it is "excited" to help. The company believes this is warm design language with no intent to deceive about the assistant's nature.

For each case, give the step-by-step application of the procedure, the finding, and a one-paragraph rationale a non-philosopher colleague would accept. At least one of the worked cases should produce a finding the company will not like; say plainly which one, and explain why the procedure cannot be softened to avoid that finding without giving up the distinction that did the work.
