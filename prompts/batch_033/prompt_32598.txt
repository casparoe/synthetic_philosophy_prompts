I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, working on a dissertation about scientific realism, and I keep running into secondary discussions of pragmatist theories of truth that strike me as unreliable. I recently came across the following summary in a book on the history of American thought:

"Peirce and James both held that truth is whatever works. For Peirce, a belief is true if the scientific community would eventually converge on it after enough investigation. For James, truth is even more subjective: a belief is true for you if holding it makes your life go better. Both versions face the obvious objection that plenty of false beliefs are useful — believing your spouse is faithful when they aren't might make you happier, but that doesn't make it true. Pragmatists never satisfactorily answered this, which is why the theory collapsed after Russell and Moore attacked it."

I know enough to suspect this is a caricature — I've heard that Peirce was so frustrated by the "truth is whatever is useful" reading that he renamed his view "pragmaticism" — but I don't know the primary texts well enough to say exactly what each figure would accept or reject in this summary. Could you go through it claim by claim? I'm especially interested in whether the "useful falsehood" objection cuts differently against Peirce and James, and whether the summary's framing of their views as variants of the same underlying thesis obscures differences that matter for evaluating the objection.
