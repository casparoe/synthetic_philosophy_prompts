Not a philosopher, but I sit on a few committees and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for. Take a three-person panel deciding whether to uphold a complaint. Upholding it needs two things to be true: that the procedure was followed, and that the decision was unfair. Suppose Amy thinks procedure yes, unfairness no, so the complaint fails. Ben thinks procedure no, unfairness yes, so it fails. Cara thinks procedure yes, unfairness yes, so it succeeds. Each of them is perfectly consistent. But if you vote question by question, the panel says: procedure followed (2–1), decision unfair (2–1), yet complaint not upheld (2–1). So the group's official position is that the procedure was followed and the decision was unfair, but the complaint should fail — a combination none of the three members actually holds. I've seen the same shape in recruitment panels and ethics boards: the overall verdict contradicts the group's own answers to the sub-questions. I know this is somewhere near Condorcet's paradox, but that's about rankings cycling (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A), and here nothing cycles — it's a flat contradiction between the majority's answers to the sub-questions and the majority's answer to the overall question. Is there an established name for this specific thing, and how exactly does it relate to Condorcet's paradox and to the idea that a group can be 'rational' when its members are? I'm not just after a label; I want to know what the standard examples are, where the edges of the concept are, and whether this really shows something about groups or is just an artefact of voting on each question seperately.
