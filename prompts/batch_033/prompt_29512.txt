I direct the intake unit of a university research-integrity office. We receive about a dozen allegations of research misconduct a year — fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism — and we decide which ones warrant a formal investigation. An increasing share of the complaints come from people whose own hands are not clean: a senior scientist sanctioned for fabrication a decade ago now accusing a junior colleague of fabricating data, a department chair with a string of retractions for image manipulation accusing someone else of the same practice, a graduate student who helped carry out her supervisor's falsification and now wants to report it after a falling-out. Our current handling is ad hoc: sometimes we discount a complaint because of the complainant's record, sometimes we set the record aside entirely, and no one can say what principle distinguishes the two.

Please turn the moral-philosophical idea of standing to blame into a reusable intake procedure my analysts can run. The idea I mean is this: a person can be correct that someone did wrong and still not be in a position to blame them — where that is a different matter from being a bad witness, from having an ulterior motive, or from merely being a hypocrite. I want a procedure that tells us when a complainant's own conduct should stop us from opening an investigation, when it should not, and why.

The procedure should consist of questions to ask in order, with, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer; it should say where it stops with the outcome that we decline to open an investigation on the complaint, where it stops with the outcome that we must open one despite the complainant's conduct, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is a value judgment the procedure should not make. Be explicit about which distinctions in the philosophy of blame must survive the simplification and which can be dropped, and about the cases the procedure will misfire on.

Constraints: my analysts are not philosophers, and each complaint gets at most half a day of analysis. The stakes are high in both directions: opening an investigation is costly and can damage a career even when it clears someone, but declining one can leave real misconduct in a lab. I want a committed procedure — make the calls and defend them, rather than laying out the options for me to choose from.

Then run the procedure on these three complaints and show the step-by-step outcome:
1. A professor sanctioned ten years ago for fabricating data, who has since had a clean record, accuses a junior researcher in another lab of fabricating data.
2. A department chair whose own lab is currently under investigation for image manipulation, and who has dismissed the practice as normal, accuses a colleague in another department of image manipulation in a manuscript.
3. A graduate student who knowingly carried out the data falsification her supervisor directed now reports that falsification to our office, after a dispute over authorship.

Make at least one of the three a case in which the procedure gives an answer we will not like, and say plainly why the procedure overrides our instinct. Finally, state what the procedure deliberately does not decide, and why that judgment cannot be eliminated.
