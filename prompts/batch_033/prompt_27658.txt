I am a teaching assistant for an upper-division philosophy of language course. The midterm included the following question: "Explain the rule-following paradox that Kripke attributes to Wittgenstein. State as precisely as you can what the skeptic is denying, and then explain and assess the skeptical solution. In particular, address whether the solution answers the skeptic or changes the subject."

A student submitted the following answer: "Kripke's Wittgenstein asks us to consider a skeptic about meaning. I have used '+' in finitely many past cases, and none of them involved numbers as large as 57. The skeptic defines 'quus': x quus y = x + y when both x and y are smaller than 57, and 5 otherwise. All my past uses are consistent with both the plus and quus interpretations. The skeptic then argues that no fact about me—my past behavior, my mental images, the rules I internalized, or my dispositions—determines that I meant plus rather than quus. So when I answer '125' to '68 + 57,' nothing about what I meant settles that this is the correct answer. The target is meaning, not arithmetic: the skeptic is not claiming that 68 plus 57 might really be 5, but that there is no fact of the matter about which function I meant by '+'. If the skeptic succeeds, no word is ever meaningful in the way we suppose.

The skeptical solution, on Kripke's reading, drops the demand for truth conditions and looks instead at the conditions under which we are permitted to assert that someone means something. A speaker is entitled to assert 'I meant plus' and to answer '125' just when her response agrees with the responses of the community to which she is accountable. Meaning is thus constituted by communal agreement: to mean plus is to be disposed to use '+' as the community does, and correctness is fixed by those shared dispositions. That is why an isolated person cannot mean anything by a word—there is no community to set the standard. The private language argument is a special case: a diarist who tries to give a private sign 'S' to a sensation cannot establish a standard for 'S' on her own, because she has nothing but her memory to compare present uses with past ones.

I think the solution answers the skeptic, because it explains why we are entitled to assert '68 + 57 = 125' even without a fact about meaning. But it has a cost: mathematical truth becomes relative to the community. Had our community always quussed, the correct answer would have been 5, and that conflicts with the objectivity we ordinarily attribute to arithmetic."
