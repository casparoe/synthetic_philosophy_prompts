For a formal epistemology paper I'm working on, I need a thought experiment built to order. Here's the brief:

Design a realistic case where someone learns a conditional claim — something of the form "if A then B" — and two different rules for updating on conditionals give different practical advice about what to do next. The two rules are: (1) the standard minimum-relative-entropy (min-RE) approach, which leaves P(A) unchanged and keeps P(B | ¬A) fixed, and (2) an approach that treats the conditional as evidence that bears on A itself — so P(A) shifts, because the conditional is more or less surprising depending on whether A holds. 

Constraints: the scenario has to be mundane — a medical diagnosis, a job interview, a weather forecast, a mechanic's assessment, whatever. No paratroopers, no sci-fi. The conditional information must be the kind of thing a real person could actually receive from a real source. And the divergence between the two rules has to matter for a concrete decision, not just for abstract credences.

After the scenario, walk through what each rule recommends and why they diverge. Then tell me: what structural feature of the conditional information (or of the prior) is doing the work to pry them apart? If you altered that feature, would the two rules converge again? And finally: a defender of the second approach would say the min-RE approach is missing something important here — what's their best argument against it?
