As a doctoral student in philosophy of language, I keep encountering the slogan 'assert only what you know' deployed as though it settled arguments about whether a particular utterance was permissible. Most recently, in a graduate seminar on testimony, I defended a speaker who asserted a proposition that was true and supported by strong evidence, but whose evidence was in fact misleading in the familiar way in which a justified true belief can fail to amount to knowledge. A colleague dismissed the speaker's assertion as improper on the grounds that she did not know the proposition; when I pressed him, he simply repeated the slogan.

I want to be able to answer him precisely, rather than either conceding or pushing back vaguely. Please lay out the distinct principles that 'assert only what you know' could be used to express, as a menu of readings rather than a single reconstruction. For each reading, specify the content of the norm being proposed (truth, belief, justification, knowledge, certainty, or something else), its force (a necessary condition on permissible assertion, a sufficient condition, or both), its status (constitutive of assertion, merely conversational, or ethical), and its plausibility. Then indicate which of these readings would actually license the conclusion that the true, justified, but non-knowledgeable assertion in my example was improper, and which would not. I am especially interested in where the slogan turns out to be near-trivial, where it is substantive and defensible, and where it is false or unsupported. Please write your answer in British English.
