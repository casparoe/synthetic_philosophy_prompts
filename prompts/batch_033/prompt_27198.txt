I am a doctoral student working on formal models of rational agency, and I have been unable to escape an argument that seems to show that no consistent reasoner can trust its own reasoning. The conclusion strikes me as absurd, but each step looks unavoidable. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Let T be a recursively axiomatisable theory extending Peano arithmetic, and suppose an idealised agent's judgements are exactly the theorems of T. Write Prov_T(⌜φ⌝) for the formalised claim that φ is provable in T.

(1) An agent trusts its own reasoning only if, for arbitrary sentences φ of its language, it accepts the reflection principle: if Prov_T(⌜φ⌝), then φ. A system that would not move from the claim that it has proved φ to φ would not be reasoning; it would merely be producing strings.

(2) Since the agent's judgements are the theorems of T, whatever the agent accepts is a theorem of T. So if the agent accepts the reflection principle across the board, then T proves each instance: T ⊢ Prov_T(⌜φ⌝) → φ.

(3) Löb's theorem states that for any such T and any φ, if T ⊢ Prov_T(⌜φ⌝) → φ, then T ⊢ φ.

(4) Take φ to be a fixed contradiction, ⊥ (say, 0 = 1). The instance of the reflection principle for ⊥ is Prov_T(⌜⊥⌝) → ⊥, which is equivalent to the claim that no contradiction is provable in T — that is, to the claim that T is consistent. By (3), if T proves this instance, then T proves ⊥.

(5) So if the agent accepts the unrestricted reflection principle for its own proof predicate, T proves its own consistency, and by Löb's theorem T proves ⊥. A consistent agent therefore cannot accept the unrestricted reflection principle for its own proof predicate.

(6) Yet a reasoner that will not trust its own reasoning cannot rationally endorse any conclusion it reaches. Rationality seems to require self-trust.

(7) Therefore rationality either requires inconsistency or is impossible.

I find (7) unacceptable, and I would most like to preserve the thought that a rational agent can trust its own reasoning without thereby proving its own consistency. Please diagnose the argument: identify the step that fails, or, if it is valid, say which premise is least costly to give up. Explain why the offending step looked compulsory, and price the main ways out — for example, distinguishing senses of accepting the reflection principle, weakening the principle, or rejecting the initial formalisation of the agent. What exactly would an agent be trusting on each option, and what does the option lose?
