ok so i'm a junior and i somehow ended up as the student rep on my university's animal research ethics board (my neuroethics professor put my name forward, honestly i think just because nobody else wanted to do it). we've got a protocol to decide on next tuesday and i'm supposed to circulate a short opinion memo beforehand.

the study is a classic intracranial self-stimulation setup: implant electrodes in rats' medial forebrain bundle, give them a lever that triggers the stimulation, and then measure how hard they'll work for it — like, will they keep pressing when they haven't eaten in 24 hours, will they cross an electrified grid to get to the lever, that kind of thing. the PIs say it's about understanding the neuroscience of motivation and has implications for addiction.

here's where it gets wierd. half the committee thinks this is totally routine — olds and milner did this in the 50s, it's a standard paradigm, nothing to see here. the other half is saying: look, if the rats really will starve to death pressing a lever for direct brain stimulation, aren't we basically creating a situation where we've hacked their reward system and then watching them destroy themselves? and even if the protocol technically checks all the IACUC boxes, maybe there's something ethically distinct about an experiment whose whole point is demonstrating that a reward shortcut can override normal behavior so completely.

our chair specifically asked me to write something that engages the philosophical side of this — she knows i'm taking neuroethics — rather than just listing regs. so here's what i need from you: act like an expert in the philosophy of neuroscience and animal research ethics, maybe start by brainstorming the different ethical frameworks that could apply here before you settle on a recommendation, and then draft me a memo (like 800–1000 words) that recommends a concrete decision — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and that uses the concept of wireheading to frame what's ethically at stake. i want you to genuinely engage with the strongest argument from whichever side loses, not just swat it away. and don't just tell me "it's a model of addiction so it's justified" — i want you to think about whether there's something specially troubling about experiments that create what is basically a reward tampering scenario in a living creature, and whether an ethics board should treat that differently from other kinds of suffering in animal research.
