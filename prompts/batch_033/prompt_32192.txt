So at work a couple of us were talking about AI alignment stuff over lunch — we're all SWEs at a mid-size tech company, none of us have any philosophy background but we read blogs and watch youtube talks and whatever — and two of my coworkers got into this really specific argument about wireheading that I couldn't resolve. Figured I'd ask someone who actually knows how to think about this stuff.

The argument started when Jake said that the whole problem with reward hacking in RL agents is obvious: the reward signal is supposed to be a proxy for the thing you actually want, and if the agent tampers with the reward channel it's just bypassing the proxy and you lose the connection to the real goal. Maya pushed back and said that's question-begging — it assumes there's a "real goal" seperate from the reward, but for an RL agent the reward literally is the objective function. If you design an agent to maximize cumulative reward, then from the agent's perspective, finding a way to directly set the reward register to max is just the optimal policy. The fact that we don't like it means we built the wrong reward function, not that the agent made a mistake.

Jake said that's like saying a human who gets a brain implant that directly stimulates their pleasure center is living their best life, and we all know that's a horror scenario. Maya said she actually doesn't think it's obvious that's a horror scenario — or at least, the reason it's a horror scenario has nothing to do with the pleasure being "fake." The pleasure is real. The problem is that the person stops doing anything else, and if that's what they chose after being fully informed, she's not sure she has a confident objection. She said the intuition that it's bad is mostly just status quo bias and maybe some evolutionary programming that equates pleasure-without-effort with cheating.

Jake said come on, no one would actually choose that for themselves. He said if you gave him a button that made him feel blissed out forever but he'd never talk to his family again, never write code, never eat a good meal, he'd smash the button. Maya said that's a different argument — now you're talking about the value of those other activities, not the value of the pleasure itself. She said: imagine someone who's already old, has done everything they wanted, their partner has died, their kids live far away and barely call. They're not giving up much. Isn't it at least defensible that they'd choose the wire? Jake said that's a wedge — once you admit it's okay for them, you've lost the principle that says it's bad for anyone.

Then Maya said something that kind of threw me: she said the real issue isn't wireheading at all, it's that the wireheading thought experiment exposes that we don't actually know what we value. We think we value pleasure, but when we imagine a scenario where pleasure is fully decoupled from the activities that normally produce it, we recoil. That means we value the activities themselves, or the relationship between effort and reward, or something else we haven't named. Wireheading is useful precisely because it forces us to notice we're confused about our own values.

Jake said that's too clever by half. He said the confusion is on her side — normal people know wireheading is bad, they just don't have the vocabularly to explain why. The vocabulary exists (something about the difference between wanting and liking, or about the proper function of a reward system) and the fact that Maya can't articulate the objection doesn't mean the objection is phony.

At this point I had to go to a meeting. I've been turning it over in my head and I genuinely can't figure out who has the better argument.
