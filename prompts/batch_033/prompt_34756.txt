"Lewis's triviality results (1976) show that if we assume that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent — that is, P(A → C) = P(C|A) for all A, C with P(A) > 0 — then the probability function P must be trivial. Specifically, Lewis proved that under this assumption, the probability function can take at most two values (0 and 1), and the indicative conditional collapses into the material conditional. The key assumptions are: (1) the conditional A → C expresses a proposition, i.e., it has a classical truth value at each possible world; (2) probability satisfies the standard Kolmogorov axioms; and (3) Adams's Thesis, which is the equation P(A → C) = P(C|A). Lewis's proof proceeds by showing that the assumption forces P(C|A) = P(C) for all A and C, which means that all propositions are probabilistically independent — which in a classical setting forces the probability function to be two-valued. The implication is that indicative conditionals cannot be analyzed as propositions that obey the standard probability calculus while also satisfying Adams's Thesis. In other words, if you want to maintain that conditionals have truth conditions, you must give up the idea that their probability is the conditional probability; and if you want to keep Adams's Thesis, you must deny that conditionals express propositions. This result is often taken as a decisive argument against truth-conditional accounts of the indicative conditional, and it motivates non-propositional approaches like Adams's own theory, where conditionals are not assertions but expressions of conditional probability, or the suppositional theory due to Edgington, where conditionals are not truth-evaluable. Lewis's result also applies to counterfactual conditionals, since the same probabilistic equation is plausible for them, and analagous triviality results can be derived for subjunctive conditionals."
