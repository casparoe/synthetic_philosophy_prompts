I've been going back and forth with a friend about hedonism — the old view that pleasure is the only thing good in itself and pain the only thing bad in itself — and we kept tripping over the heterogeneity problem. "Pleasure" seems to name a wildly mixed bag: the relief of finding a bathroom after holding it for an hour, the feeling of finally seeing why a proof works, the little pang of schadenfreude when an awful coworker gets humiliated, the warm blur of a second beer, the satisfaction of landing a well-timed sarcastic comeback. These don't feel alike, and they don't obviously share a brain state or a functional role either. The hedonist seem's to owe us some one thing they all have in common; otherwise the theory is stuck saying "they're all good because they're pleasures, and they're all pleasures because they're good in the same way," which is just a circle.

So here's what I want: a thought experiment built to order, designed to put maximum pressure on the idea that there's a single common something. Construct a pair of experiences that any plausible hedonism is forced to count as pleasures — ideally as roughly equal amounts of pleasure — but that share as little as possible at every level you can name: how they feel, what they do functionally, what's going on in the brain, what causes them. Constraints: keep it mundane and realistic (no sci-fi, no electrodes, nothing weirder than caffeine or a beer), and make both experiences ones a hedonist would cheerfully own as genuine pleasures rather than edge cases like masochistic pain or relief that's really just the end of pain. Give me the scenario in one paragraph. Then do the check: run the case through the main hedonist responses — a felt-quality view, an attitudinal view, a functional account, whatever else you think is live — and say where each succeeds or breaks. Finish by telling me which feature of the scenario is doing the real work, how the case would collapse if you removed it, and how a committed hedonist would attack the case to make it look rigged.
