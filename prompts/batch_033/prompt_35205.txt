I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar that pairs moral psychology with nineteenth-century fiction, and this week we're reading Mary Shelley's Frankenstein alongside the experimental philosophy literature on the Knobe effect. I'd like you to give me a reading of Frankenstein through the lens of the Knobe effect — the finding that people asymmetrically attribute intentionality to foreseen side effects depending on their moral valence: a chairman who says he doesn't care about harming the environment is judged to have harmed it intentionally, but a chairman who says he doesn't care about helping the environment is not judged to have helped it intentionally.

Victor Frankenstein foresees, at multiple points, that his creation may bring about harm. He proceeds anyway, and then abandons the creature. The deaths that follow — William, Justine, Clerval, Elizabeth — are not desired by Victor, but they are arguably foreseen or foreseeable consequences of his actions. The question I want to explore with my students is this: does the Knobe effect predict that readers will hold Victor responsible for those deaths in a way that goes beyond what he explicitly intended, and does that prediction illuminate how the novel actually operates on its audience?

But I want the reading to go beyond simply noting that the novel illustrates the asymmetry. I'd like you to identify specific scenes or narrative strategies where Shelley's treatment of foresight and responsibility either aligns with, complicates, or resists what the experimental literature would predict. For instance, Victor's own first-person narration frames his culpability in a particular way — does telling the story from inside his perspective suppress or activate the asymmetry? Does the nested frame structure (Walton, Victor, the creature) multiply or diffuse judgments of intentionality across narrators? And does the fact that the deaths are mediated by another intentional agent — the creature itself — break the analogy with the standard Knobe-effect vignettes, where the harm follows more or less directly from the agent's decision?

I'd also like you to consider what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the experimental literature, with its stripped-down vignettes, cannot capture. The Knobe effect is typically studied using short paragraphs about corporate decisions; Frankenstein gives us a sustained, emotionally rich narrative in which foresight, regret, and responsibility develop over time. What does the temporal and emotional texture of the novel reveal that the vignettes obscure? And conversely, are there ways in which the experimental results should make us suspicious of how the novel manipulates our judgments — for instance, does Shelley stack the deck by making the consequences so horrific that the asymmetry is overdetermined?

Finally, I'd like an honest assessment of where the reading breaks down. Are there moments in the novel where the Knobe effect would predict a judgment that no attentive reader would actually make? Where does the novel resist the theory, and what might that resistance tell us about the limits of transporting an effect from the lab to a literary narrative?
