Hey, so I'm a backend engineer and I've been going down this AI safety rabbit hole for a few months now (mostly reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, I don't have any formal philosophy training). Was arguing with a friend of mine who's also in tech, and he was going on about how goal misgeneralization is this huge problem — you know, when a model picks up some behavior that's fine on the training distribution but then does something totally different and maybe catastrophic out of distribution. And obviously, yeah, that's bad. But the more I thought about it the more I felt like the standard framing is missing something. Isn't this just... how generalization works? Like, the entire point of ML is that the model extrapolates beyond its training data. If you successfully prevent a system from ever doing anything that wasn't implicitly blessed by the training distribution, haven't you just built a lookup table? There's a fundamentaly tension here that I don't see people grappling with head-on. I'm trying to write up a blog post and I want to nail the core argument: that goal misgeneralization is actually a necessary consquence of the kind of generalization we want from capable systems, and that the real safety problem isn't misgeneralization as such but something more specific about which kinds of misgeneralization we get and why.

Please argue that goal misgeneralization is not a bug to be eliminated but a necessary feature of generalization in capable learning systems, and that the AI safety community should refocus on distinguishing benign from catastrophic misgeneralization rather than trying to prevent misgeneralization altogether.
