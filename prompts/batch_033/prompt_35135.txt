I'm a junior in high school, and for my extended essay I've been reading about the idea that consciousness is a kind of user illusion — the thought that what we experience as consciousness is like the simplified desktop interface a computer shows us, not the actual code and circuitry underneath. I've arrived at a set of views I find myself drawn to, but before I write the paper I want to check whether they actually hang together or whether I'm holding beliefs that pull in different directions. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Conscious experience is a user illusion. The brain constructs a streamlined, icon-based model of its own activity, and what we call "consciousness" is our experience of that model, not of the neural processing itself.

(2) Because we only ever access the interface, not the underlying machinery, introspection is systematically unreliable about the causes and structure of our own mental states. We can't just look inward and see why we made a choice or how a thought arose.

(3) There is still something it is like to be me — the felt quality of experience is real, not fake. The user-illusion view isn't eliminativist; it's a claim about what kind of thing consciousness is, not a denial that it exists.

(4) The hard problem of consciousness — why there is something it is like at all, given that brains are physical — is largely dissolved once you accept the user-illusion picture. It becomes like asking why the trash can icon on your desktop looks the way it does: the question misfires because it assumes the interface is a window onto something deeper, when it's actually a tool for navigating the system.

(5) At least some non-human animals — dogs, crows, octopuses — are conscious, and their consciousness is a user illusion of their own kind, suited to their own ecological needs.

(6) I have free will in the sense required for moral responsibility. The fact that my conscious experience of deciding doesn't give me transparent access to the causal origins of my decisions doesn't mean my decisions aren't mine in the way that matters.

(7) Philosophical reflection on consciousness — including the very arguments that led me to (1)–(6) — can yield genuine knowledge about the mind. I'm not stuck in skepticism about my own conclusions.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like they are. I'd especially like to know: does (2) undermine the whole project of arriving at (1)–(7) through philosophical reflection? And is there a tension between (1) and (3) that I'm not seeing, or does (3) just restate what (1) already says? Rank the tensions by severity and tell me what the cheapest revision would be to make the set consistent. If some of my commitments have to go, I'd rather know now than have my teacher point it out later.
