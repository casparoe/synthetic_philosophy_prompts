I am a postdoctoral researcher preparing a paper for a workshop on consequentialism and decision-making under severe uncertainty. The paper defends a qualified act-consequentialism against the cluelessness objection. Its thesis is that cluelessness does not refute act-consequentialism as a criterion of rightness, but it does force a substantial revision of the theory's decision procedure. In particular, I argue that agents should not simply maximise expected value over their own inevitably incomplete probability distributions, because the priors such calculations require are, in the relevant long-run and identity-affecting cases, substantially arbitrary. Instead, I defend a robustness-based rule: an action is permissible only if it would be choice-worthy across a suitably wide range of reasonable empirical models of downstream consequences and reasonable evaluative theories, and where no available action meets that standard the agent should default to a conservative baseline of non-intervention. I try to show that the rule is not ad hoc by deriving it from a modest epistemic principle about suspending judgement under deep uncertainty rather than from risk-aversion.

The audience will be roughly twenty-five people: several act-consequentialists sympathetic to effective altruism, who will hear any departure from expected-value maximisation as status quo bias; one Kantian, who thinks the real lesson of cluelessness is that consequentialism cannot be action-guiding at all; two formal epistemologists working on imprecise probabilities and higher-order evidence, who will press on how the set of reasonable models is specified and updated; a virtue ethicist sceptical of decision rules altogether; and a political philosopher who will ask whether the conservative baseline merely entrenches existing distributions of harm. Please draft a Q&A preparation sheet for this talk: the eight or so objections I am most likely to receive from this specific audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased as the relevant audience member would actually put it. For each objection, include a reply that could be delivered in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me what I should concede up front in the talk so that I do not have to concede it under questioning.

Throughout, please distinguish what is genuinely settled in the philosophical literature on cluelessness from what remains contested, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. Please write in British English.
