In the course of covering economics, climate science, and epidemiology over the past decade, I have noticed a recurring argumentative move that I cannot find a precise label for. A model is built on assumptions that everyone involved concedes are false — sometimes vividly so: perfectly rational consumers, frictionless planes, well-mixed populations, spherical cows. When a critic objects that an assumption is unrealistic, the modeler replies, in effect, that what matters is predictive accuracy, not the realism of the premises. Friedman's 1953 essay on positive economics, or some descendant of it, is typically invoked as a blanket warrant. So far, familiar ground.

The pattern I want to isolate is what happens next. The same modeler, when the model's predictions later fail, will sometimes turn around and blame the failure on one of those very assumptions — the same assumptions whose unreality they earlier dismissed as irrelevant to the model's soundness. The assumption, in other words, enjoys a kind of dual citizenship: when the model works, it is shielded from criticism by instrumentalist logic; when the model breaks, it is retroactively offered as the culprit. Its falsehood is neither here nor there until it is needed to absorb a failure, at which point it suddenly matters a great deal.

I am not asking about the general debate between instrumentalism and realism in modeling, nor am I after the standard F-twist itself. What I want to identify is this narrower configuration: an assumption is treated as immune to empirical challenge when the model succeeds, but is selectively reclassified as an empirically significant defect when the model fails — producing what looks like a systematic asymmetry that protects the modeling framework from ever being genuinely at risk. Is there an established name for this two-step? If not, how would you characterize it in a way that distinguishes it from ordinary instrumentalism, from ad hoc rescue, and from the uncontroversial fact that an assumption can be harmless for some predictions and fatal for others? And is there a principled criterion for telling when the move is legitimate — because the assumption genuinely operates differently across the relevant domains — and when it is merely a rhetorical device that renders a modeling program unfalsifiable?
