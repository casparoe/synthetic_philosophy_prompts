I run a small philosophy discussion group at my local library, and next month we're doing something a little unusual: we're going to role-play an interview with Gregory Kavka, the philosopher who came up with the toxin puzzle. One of us will play Kavka and the rest of us will take turns asking questions. I've been put in charge of drafting the questions, and I want them to be good — not just "what's the puzzle?" but questions that probe the thing from multiple angles and force the person playing Kavka to actually think on his feet.

For anyone who doesn't know it: the toxin puzzle runs like this. Someone offers you a million dollars if you intend, at midnight tonight, to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you violently ill for a day but won't do any lasting damage. The catch is that you get the money based on whether you actually have the intention at midnight — they have a perfect brain scanner that can tell — and the money is deposited in your account before you have to drink the toxin. So by the time tomorrow afternoon arrives, the money is already yours, and you have no reason to drink the toxin. But you knew that all along. So how can you form the intention tonight to do something you know you'll have no reason to do tomorrow? The puzzle is about what intentions are, and whether you can genuinely intend to do something you know you won't follow through on.

I've been turning this over for weeks, and here's what I want the questions to cover. Please organize the response into these sections:

**Section 1: Groundwork (roughly 3 questions, about a paragraph each).** Questions that make sure we really understand what Kavka is claiming. For example: is the claim that you literally cannot form the intention, or just that it's irrational to do so? If the scanner detects your intention at midnight, what exactly is it detecting — a mental state, a disposition, something else? And does it matter whether the reward is large enough to make you wish you could intend it, or is the puzzle the same whether it's a million dollars or a hundred?

**Section 2: The nature of intention (roughly 4 questions, each with a follow-up, about a paragraph each).** These should press on what an intention actually is. For instance: if I believe I won't do something, can I still intend to do it? If intentions are a kind of commitment, can I make a commitment I know I'll break? If I try to intend it and fail, what exactly is the difference between trying to intend and actually intending — is it just that I'm not genuinely resolved? For each question, include a follow-up that anticipates the answer Kavka is most likely to give and pushes one step further.

**Section 3: Ruses and workarounds (roughly 3 questions, each with a follow-up, about a paragraph each).** What if I hire someone to force me to drink the toxin tomorrow, or make a side bet that I'll lose if I don't drink it? Does that count as genuinely intending, or is it just changing the situation so that I'll have a reason tomorrow? What if I try to forget that the money is already in my account? And here's one I keep coming back to: what if I just decide to be the kind of person who follows through on things, and form the intention on that basis — is that a genuine intention, or am I just hoping to become the kind of person who would have the intention? Anticipate Kavka's likely replies and include a follow-up for each.

**Section 4: What it means for the rest of us (roughly 2 questions, about a paragraph each).** This is where we step back. Does the puzzle show something about all intentions, or is it a weird edge case that doesn't tell us much about ordinary decision-making? And if Kavka is right that you can't form the intention, does that mean there's something incoherent about the whole idea of binding yourself to a future action — the way people do with Ulysses contracts, or promises, or New Year's resolutions?
