I'm a PhD student in philosophy of medicine and I've been roped into teaching a one-off session to first-year med students on the naturalism vs. normativism debate about health and disease. It's a 90-minute slot and there are about 30 of them. They've had zero philosophy. Their default position, if you can call it that, is that disease is just whatever the textbooks say it is — they're in the thick of learning pathology and they're not asking ontological questions about it.

I don't want you to explain the debate to me. I know the Boorse stuff, the Wakefield harmful dysfunction thing, the general lay of the land. What I need is a plan for how to teach it. Specifically:

- What order do the ideas have to go in? What has to be in place before they can even see why "is disease a value-laden concept?" is a live question? I'm worried if I lead with the distinction itself they'll just shrug and say "obviously it's natural, look at the DSM" or whatever.

- What's the one carrying example that should anchor the whole session? I need a case that makes the normativist intuition feel unavoidable but also lets the naturalist have a fair shot. I was thinking of using hypertension or maybe obesity but those come with a million distracting associations for med students. What's a case that's clean enough to do the work but not so weird that they dismiss it?

- What misconceptions should I expect them to form, and at what point in the sequence do I head each off? I'm guessing the big one is conflating "disease concepts are value-laden" with "diseases aren't real" or "it's all just social construction." There's probably also a slide from "values are involved in deciding what counts as a disease" to "therefore medicine is arbitrary." I need to know where in the arc these pop up and how to preempt them without just lecturing at them.

- What's one question I can ask near the end that would tell me whether they actually got the distinction rather than just being able to parrot it back?

- And what do I explicitly leave out? I'm not going to cover the metaethical underpinnings of the normativist position or the details of Boorse's statistical machinery. But I want to name what I'm skipping so they know there's more there.

Oh and the answer should be in American English, the med school is in the US. Also dont make it a reading list — they wont do the reading and I just need the teaching plan.
