I am a novelist, and I am currently researching a book in which one of the central characters is a natural-language AI. In the course of that research I keep running into the exchange between Searle and Dennett over whether a program could literally understand language, and I would like an accuracy audit of the way it has been presented to me. A popular science book I was reading summarizes the debate as follows:

"Searle's Chinese Room shows that a machine running a program could pass the Turing test while understanding nothing, because the symbols it shuffles have no meaning to it. Searle allows that the man in the room, the room as a whole, and ordinary computers all have 'derived' intentionality: their states mean something only relative to the interpretations of outside observers. Human brains, by contrast, have 'original' intentionality — their thoughts are intrinsically about the world. Dennett answers with his 'two-bitser,' a vending machine that accepts only quarters. The machine, Dennett says, is just as much 'about' quarters as any brain state is about anything, because intentionality is always a matter of interpreting a system from the outside. Dennett thus shows that the Chinese Room argument fails, since there is no fact of the matter about whether any system 'really' understands anything."

I can tell this is simplifying, but I cannot tell where simplification becomes distortion, and my novel will be shaped by whichever reading I trust. How fair is this summary? Please go through it claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of both Searle and Dennett would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. Then tell me whether the closing inference — that the two-bitser shows the Chinese Room argument fails — follows from the accurate parts once the inaccurate parts are corrected. I have a vague suspicion that the summary slides between two different senses of a system's states "meaning something," but I am not sure whether that matters or what to make of it. Finally, please say how confident you are in your assessment and single out the point you are least sure of.
