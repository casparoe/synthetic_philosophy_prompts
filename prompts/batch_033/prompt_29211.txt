I've been going down this rabbit hole about "acausal trade" lately — you know, the idea that two agents who can't ever causally interact (different universes, outside each other's light cones, whatever) could still cooperate if they can each predict the other's decision process well enough. It's like a Prisoner's Dilemma against a copy of yourself but weirder because the other agent isn't you, just someone whose reasoning you can simulate. I keep thinking this would make an incredible short story but I can't quite figure out how to make it feel real and not just like a philosophy paper with characters.

Here's the setup I'm imagining: sometime around 2040, a physicist running a high-energy experiment accidentally discovers that her apparatus can send exactly one bit of information to a decohered branch of the wavefunction — not another universe per se, more like another version of reality that split off during the experiment itself. The catch is that sending the bit costs so much energy that the equipment is destroyed in the process, and the same constraint applies on the other side. So she and her counterpart each get one shot. They can't negotiate in advance because they didn't know the other existed until the moment the split happened, and after that the channel is one-way and one-use. The question is what, if anything, they can do for each other — and whether they can reason their way into a mutually beneficial "trade" with nothing but a single bit each and a model of the other's mind.

Could you write that story? I want it to really sit with the logic of how they'd think about each other — like, "I know she's reasoning about me reasoning about her reasoning about me..." — but I also want it to have emotional weight. What does it feel like to trust someone you can never meet, can never verify, and can only send one bit to? Maybe 1500 to 2500 words. And please don't just have characters explain the concept in dialogue; make it a real story with actual stakes. I'm thinking the physicist has something concrete she wants from her counterpart — maybe she's trying to save someone's life, or there's some crucial piece of knowledge that only exists on one side — and the whole thing turns on whether acausal cooperation can actually work when it's not just a thought experiment anymore.
