Ok so I'm taking this seminar on AI alignment and there's this debate that's been going on for weeks and I'm supposed to write up an adversarial collaboration document for it. The two positions are basically:

Position A (narrow / intent alignment): The alignment problem is about getting AI systems to do what their operators actually intend. You tell the system to do X, you mean X in the normal way, and the system should figure out what you meant and do that — not some literal misinterpretation, not some weird edge case, not something that technically satisfies the instruction but violates the spirit. This is already insanely hard (Goodhart, specification gaming, etc.) and we should keep the problem focused. Expanding "alignment" to mean "make the AI a good person" is a different project, and confusing the two makes both harder.

Position B (broad / value alignment): Intent alignment is not enough and might be actively dangerous. The operator could have terrible intentions. Or multiple operators could have conflicting intentions. Or the intention could be fine but the downstream effects of faithfully executing it could be awful because the operator didn't think it through. The real problem is getting AI to act in accordance with human values — where "values" means something like what we'd want upon reflection, or what's actually good for humans, not just whatever someone happens to want in the moment. They say the narrow camp is solving the wrong problem, and systems that are "intent aligned" to bad actors are not aligned in any meaningful sense.

The seminar has been going in circles and the prof finally said: write an adversarial collaboration document. Both sides should be able to sign it. It should have:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides agree on (these need to be substantive, not just "we agree AI is important").
2. A disagreements table: each row is a specific point of disagreement, with Position A's view in wording they'd accept, Position B's view in wording they'd accept, and a column saying whether you think the disagreement is substantive or verbal.
3. A cruxes section: what would actually change someone's mind? For each crux, describe a test, case, or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, and state what each side predicts about it.
4. At the very end, a one-sentence summary of the deepest remaining disagreement, in \answer{...} format.

The hard part is getting the wording right. I've noticed that when I try to summarize Position B, I make them sound like they think intent alignment is useless, which they don't. And when I summarize Position A, I make them sound like they don't care about values at all, which they also don't. So the wording has to be precise enough that each side would say "yes, that's actually what I think."

I also have a suspicion that some of this fight is about what the word "alignment" should mean as a term of art, and that underneath the defintional fight they might actually agree on more than they think. If you think that's true, please say so explicitly in the document and separate the terminological disputes from the substantive ones.
