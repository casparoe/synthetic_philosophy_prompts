I spent most of my career in the physical sciences, where a theoretical framework earns its keep by generating precise predictions and, ideally, by being derivable from a compact set of axioms. Since retiring I have been reading around in the philosophy of decision theory, and I find myself puzzled by the fact that the standard axioms of preference seem to be supported by two rather different kinds of argument. On the one hand, there are Dutch book arguments, sometimes called money pump arguments, which purport to show that an agent whose preferences violate the axioms could be induced to accept a series of bets, each of which looks acceptable given those preferences, but whose combined outcome is a sure loss. On the other hand, there are representation theorems, which show that any preference ordering satisfying the axioms can be represented as maximizing the expectation of some utility function relative to some probability measure. These two arguments appear to be aimed at the same target, but they strike me as having very different logical shapes: the first is dynamic and pragmatic, turning on the possibility of being exploited by a clever bookmaker, while the second is static and formal, turning on the existence of a certain mathematical representation.

I would like a careful comparison of what these two families of argument actually establish. Do they justify the same normative conclusion, or do they answer subtly different questions? Does either argument, taken by itself, give a non-circular reason to obey the axioms, or does each presuppose something that a critic of expected utility theory might reasonably reject? And if a skeptic asked why rational preferences must have an expected utility representation at all, would it be better to direct them to the Dutch book argument, to the representation theorems, or to some division of labor between the two?
