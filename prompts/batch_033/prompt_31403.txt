I've been teaching myself some philosophy from a widely recommended introductory book, and I hit a chapter on identity and individuation that gave me pause. The author summarizes the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's response like this:

"Leibniz held that if two objects have all the same properties, they are really just one object — there cannot be two distinct things that are perfectly alike. Max Black refuted this decisively with a thought experiment: imagine a universe that contains nothing but two iron spheres, identical in size, shape, composition, and temperature. The spheres are indistinguishable in every respect, yet they are plainly two spheres, not one. So Leibniz's principle is false, and the matter is settled."

Something about this feels too neat. If the principle collapses that easily, why did it carry weight for centuries, and why do philosophers still write about it? I'm hoping you can tell me: which parts of this summary are accurate, which are misleading or oversimplified, and whether the conclusion that Black's thought experiment straightforwardly refutes the identity of indiscernibles actually holds up.
