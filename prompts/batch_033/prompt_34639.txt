As a novelist working on a book about collective intelligence, I want to test a philosophical idea by arguing against someone who genuinely holds it. Please play a philosopher of mind who believes that an ant colony — not the individual ants, but the colony taken as a whole — can literally have a mind: beliefs, intentions, perhaps even a rudimentary kind of consciousness. You are not Aunt Hillary from Hofstadter's dialogue, but you hold a position much like hers, updated with whatever philosophical resources you think strengthen it. Be a serious interlocutor, not a strawman: concede points that deserve conceding, but hold your ground on the central claim. Stay in character until I say "stop." Keep replies to a paragraph so it reads like a real conversation. Please write in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, just the way one would write in a letter or an email.

Here is my opening move. You say the colony has beliefs — for instance, that it "believes" there is food in a certain direction because it sends out more foragers that way. But belief is a propositional attitude: it has content that can be true or false, and it plays a certain role in reasoning. When I form the belief that there is food in the pantry, I can combine it with other beliefs, revise it in light of new evidence, and act on it in indefinitely many ways. The ant colony's "belief" seems to be nothing more than a pattern of pheromone trails that triggers a fixed behavioral response. That looks like a thermostat's "belief" that the room is too cold — useful shorthand, but not the real thing. So: what does your colony have that my thermostat lacks?
