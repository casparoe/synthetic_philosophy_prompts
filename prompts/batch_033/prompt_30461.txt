I recently joined my county's board of supervisors, and the question that keeps coming up in my district is whether local officials may cite their religious convictions when they justify votes on contested policies — a zoning change that would let a church operate a homeless shelter, a curriculum dispute, a resolution on physician-assisted suicide. A friend who reads this literature more than I do sent me the following summary of "public reason" to help me think it through:

"Public reason is the liberal idea that religion has to be kept out of democratic politics. Citizens in a properly secular state should not justify laws by appealing to their faith; they have to translate their religious convictions into neutral, secular arguments that everyone can accept. So if a Catholic legislator opposes a law permitting assisted suicide because her church teaches that life is sacred, she may vote that way only if she can offer a nonreligious reason that would convince a nonbeliever; if she cannot find one, she ought to abstain. That is why public reason is, in effect, a gag rule for religious citizens: it treats their deepest commitments as illegitimate in public, and it extends the separation of church and state all the way down to individual political speech."

This strikes me as a caricature, but I don't know the material well enough to say where it goes wrong, and my friend is usually careful. Treat me as a reader, not a grader: I want an accuracy audit. Please act as a political philosopher who works on public reason and the place of religious argument in liberal politics, and think carefully rather than giving a canned reply. Start by separating the summary's distinct claims — about the scope of public reason, the kind of reasons it demands, the standing of religious reasons, and the consequences for religious citizens — then tell me for each whether it is accurate, a common oversimplification, or something a public reason liberal in the tradition of Rawls would explicitly deny. Finally, say whether the "gag rule" conclusion follows from the accurate parts of the summary, and what a defender of public reason would say in response to that charge.
