I'm writing an essay on Shusaku Endo's novel Silence and its engagement with the problem of divine hiddenness, and I'd like a philosophical reading that does justice to both. The core of J.L. Schellenberg's argument from divine hiddenness is that a perfectly loving God would ensure that anyone capable of a personal relationship with God and not culpably resisting it would believe God exists; since there are people who genuinely seek God yet remain unbelievers, such a God does not exist. Endo's novel centers on Rodrigues, a seventeenth-century Jesuit missionary in Japan who watches believers be tortured and killed while God remains silent, and who eventually tramples the fumi-e himself. I want to understand where the novel's depiction of God's silence aligns with Schellenberg's argument, where it pushes back, and whether Rodrigues's trajectory — from fervent faith through anguished silence to apostasy and a strange, hidden faith thereafter — exposes something the philosophical debate misses. In particular, does Rodrigues exemplify nonresistant nonbelief, or does the novel suggest that what looks like nonresistance from the outside can be shot through with forms of resistance the philosopher's taxonomy cannot capture? And what should a philosopher of religion make of the novel's final claim that even in apostasy and silence, Christ is present — not speaking, not intervening, but suffering alongside?
