I teach a senior seminar in political philosophy and the history of economic thought, and the next unit is the socialist calculation debate. The class has sixteen advanced undergraduates, mostly philosophy and economics majors, and I have one ninety-minute session. I would like to open the unit with a structured game rather than a lecture: something that makes the students feel, before they read Mises or Hayek, why the debate was never merely about whether a planning board could solve a very large system of simultaneous equations. I want a simulation in which some students act as central planners or enterprise managers in a small economy without market prices for producer goods, while others hold dispersed, concrete information about local conditions, production possibilities, and input requirements; the centralized allocation should fail for reasons that come out of the rules and incentives themselves, not from me telling them that planning is hard. A later round should introduce exchange and price formation, and the students should notice that information that was previously unavailable, or available only in a form no one could use, now moves through the system. The debrief should map what happened onto the three positions precisely: Mises's claim that without a market for the means of production there are no prices for capital goods and therefore no rational comparison of alternative uses; Hayek's claim that the knowledge a planner would need is dispersed, concrete, and often not articulable in advance, and that prices in actual exchange elicit or reveal that knowledge rather than merely transmit an already-existing summary; and Lange's Walrasian trial-and-error answer, in which a central board adjusts prices in response to shortages and surpluses and thereby gets decentralized managers to implement the plan. By the end, the students should see why Hayek thought Lange had answered an easier problem than the one Hayek was pressing, and also what Lange could fairly say in reply. Please give me the rules, roles, and materials — nothing beyond paper, cards, markers, or a whiteboard — a payoff structure that gives students real but small stakes without using money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, a debrief script, and an honest note on which features of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. The names and the theoretical vocabulary should not appear until the debrief.
