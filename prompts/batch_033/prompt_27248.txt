Charles Peirce and William James are often treated as sharing a single pragmatist account of truth, but their views pull in different directions. Peirce ties truth to the long run: a true belief is the opinion that a community of competent inquirers would ultimately settle on if inquiry were carried far enough, and he treats the real as whatever is independent of what any particular person or group happens to think at present. James ties truth to what belief does for a believer: an idea becomes true, on his account, through the way it is verified and made good in experience, and he treats truth as a kind of good, so that the practical consequences of believing are internal to truth rather than mere evidence for it.

Compare these two accounts in detail. In particular:
- Is James's view a rival theory of truth, or is it better read as a claim about the meaning and function of calling something true, with Peirce supplying the logic of inquiry?
- How does each account handle the relationship between truth and verification, including beliefs that no finite amount of evidence could settle or that would converge only in the limit?
- How does each account handle domains where convergence seems absent or where satisfaction varies from person to person, such as ethics, aesthetics, or religion?
- What is the strongest objection to each view, and what is the best reply available to each pragmatist?

Then give a definite, argued verdict: which of the two gives the more defensible account of truth, and why. Finally, state what would change your mind: identify the specific argument, counterexample, or reinterpretation of one of the views that would most plausibly reverse your verdict, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
