I'm a graduate student in a seminar on Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling*, and my study group has been wrestling with a passage from Problema I that we can't seem to crack. The passage—we're working from the Hong translation, and I'll paraphrase since I don't have it in front of me—runs roughly as follows. Johannes de Silentio distinguishes between the tragic hero and Abraham. The tragic hero, like Agamemnon sacrificing Iphigenia, remains within the ethical: he sacrifices a private relationship for the sake of a higher ethical claim, the good of the community. The tragic hero's act is a higher expression of the ethical; the universal is still the telos. Abraham, by contrast, does not act for the sake of the universal. His telos lies outside the ethical entirely—he suspends the ethical for a higher purpose, which is his absolute relation to God. The ethical is not discarded but becomes relative. And then Johannes says something that stops us cold: the tragic hero's act can be understood and wept over, but Abraham's cannot be understood, because his act is a purely personal venture, a trial, and faith is precisely the paradox that the single individual is higher than the universal.

Here's where we're stuck. Johannes seems to be saying that the difference between the tragic hero and Abraham is that the tragic hero's sacrifice is intelligible in universal terms, while Abraham's is not. But we can't tell whether this is supposed to be a description of how faith looks from the outside—to the non-believer, Abraham's act is simply unintelligible—or whether it's a substantive claim about the metaphysical structure of the ethical itself: that in faith, the ethical is genuinely suspended, not just reinterpreted. If it's the latter, then Johannes appears to be endorsing a kind of divine command theory where God's command can override morality. But that reading seems too flat and doesn't square with the rest of the text's insistence on paradox and the absurd. If it's the former, then the whole problemata is just a phenomenological description of the believer's inner life, and the claim that "the single individual is higher than the universal" is a report of how faith feels, not a normative thesis. Which reading is closer to the text, and what turns on the difference? And if neither reading is quite right, what's the third option we're missing?
