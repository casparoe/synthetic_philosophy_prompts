I recently had to make a medical decision between two treatment paths: one whose success rate is well documented, and an experimental one whose evidence base is still too thin to assign a probability. In working through it, I caught myself in the classic Ellsberg pattern: in each of two related comparisons, I preferred whichever option had known odds, even though the two preferences together cannot be reconciled with any single assignment of probabilities. The experience has stayed with me, and I want a poem that lets me sit with it rather than explain it away.

Please write a villanelle set inside the three-color urn: ninety balls, thirty red, and sixty black or yellow in unknown proportion. The two refrains should be the two bets this chooser prefers — the bet on red (a known one-in-three) over black (an unknown chance between zero and two-thirds), and the bet on black-or-yellow (a known two-thirds) over red-or-yellow (an unknown chance between one-third and one). Each refrain should sound, in its own stanza, like the obviously reasonable choice; their alternation should enact the way ambiguity aversion reasserts itself under each framing; and when the two refrains finally meet in the closing couplet, they should expose the contradiction: no single probability for black and yellow can make both preferences consistent with expected utility, so what has to go is the assumption that the unknown proportions can be represented by a single probability distribution. Do not name the paradox in the poem; let the refrains and the urn do the work. Someone who knows the paradox should recognize the two choices and why the form turns them against each other; someone who does not should still feel the pull of each refrain before the ending shows that they cannot both be held.
