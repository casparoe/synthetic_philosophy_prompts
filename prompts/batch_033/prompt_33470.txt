Throughout my career as a molecular biologist, I spoke unreflectively of DNA as containing a "genetic code," of transcription and translation as "reading" and "interpreting" the code, and of the genome as storing "information" about the organism. These metaphors were so embedded in laboratory practice that I never examined them. Now, in retirement, I have been reading philosophy of biology and have encountered serious challenges: arguments that the genetic code is not a code in any semantically interesting sense, that the "information" in a genome is at best a causal notion with no intrinsic aboutness, and that the whole coding metaphor systematically misrepresents what is really going on.

I want to audit the central analogy: DNA sequence as a code carrying information. Please lay out, as precisely as you can, what corresponds to what — what plays the role of the symbols, the syntax, the semantic content, the encoder, the decoder, the message, the channel, the noise. Then walk me through where the mapping breaks down. In particular, I want to know which features of true codes the analogy quietly imports that genomes lack — for instance, the distinction between a code that requires an interpreter with representational capacities and a mere causal mapping that operates without anything resembling reading or understanding. Finally, tell me whether the conclusions that biologists routinely draw from this language — that genes "carry information" about phenotypes, that the genome is a "program" directing development, that there is a meaningful distinction between genetic and environmental "information" — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether we should retire the metaphor, and if so, what a more accurate picture would look like.
