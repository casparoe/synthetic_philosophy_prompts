So I've been going down an Aumann's agreement theorem rabbit hole after a long argument on a rationalist Discord about whether persistent disagreement is evidence of irrationality. Someone linked a blog post that summarized the theorem like this: 'Aumann's agreement theorem is the formal proof that rational people can't agree to disagree. If two people both update by Bayes' rule and share the same prior, then once each of them learns the other's posterior probability for some claim, their beliefs are forced to converge — they can't keep disagreeing while knowing each other's views. That's why stubborn disagreement about politics or religion is a sign that at least one side is being irrational or refusing to update. The theorem basically shows that if we all reasoned correctly we'd all end up agreeing, and any remaining disagreement can only come from different information or from bias.'

This gloss sounds plausible given how much the rationalist sphere leans on 'disagreement is evidence someone's wrong,' and I kind of want it to be true, but I also remember reading that the theorem has finicky conditions that this summary maybe papers over — something about common knowledge of posteriors being stronger than just each person knowing the other's estimate, and the common prior assumption doing alot of work and being way less innocent than the summary makes it sound. I can't tell whether I'm being appropriately suspicious or just rationalizing the fact that I like arguing with people. Can you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on epistemic game theory would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? In particular I want to know whether the jump from 'the theorem is true' to 'persistent real-world disagreement implies someone is irrational' actually survives the finicky conditions, or whether the common-prior assumption quietly does more than the summary admits. Also, is 'different information' really the only out, or is that itself a loaded way of putting it?
