A philosopher of religion teaching a graduate seminar on the divine attributes has, over the term, offered the following verdicts on what an omnipotent being can and cannot do:

- Create a round square: cannot.
- Make it the case that 2+2=5: cannot.
- Create a stone so heavy that the being itself cannot lift it: cannot.
- Bring it about that a past event did not occur: cannot.
- Create a free creature and causally determine that creature's free choices: cannot.
- Create a being more powerful than itself: cannot.
- Bring about an ordinary contingent state of affairs, such as causing a star to form at a particular place and time: can.

At the final meeting, the seminar turns to a question that has not yet been tested against these verdicts: Can an omnipotent being command a creature to perform an intrinsically evil act — for instance, to torture an innocent person for no further end?

The students agree that the professor's track record rules out a Cartesian view on which omnipotence extends even to logical impossibilities. But they disagree about what positive principle the professor is implicitly applying. Some argue the rule is simply that an omnipotent being can do anything logically possible. Others maintain that the professor is running a more restrictive principle: an omnipotent being can do anything that is both logically possible and consistent with perfect goodness — and the cases settled so far happen not to have called the goodness constraint into play.

Predict the professor's verdict on the new case. State clearly which principle, or which refinement of a principle, best captures the pattern of the settled verdicts, and say whether any other principle fits that pattern equally well. If the settled cases genuinely underdetermine the answer to the new question, explain what turns on each way of resolving it, offer your best prediction with reasons, and identify a further case — one not yet among those listed — that would cleanly decide between the competing principles.
