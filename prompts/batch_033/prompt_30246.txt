I edit a social-science journal and I need a decision procedure my associate editors can use to decide whether a submission is scientific enough to send out for peer review. We get a lot of borderline stuff: psychoanalytic readings of political movements, rational-choice models with no empirical test, advocacy papers with a thin layer of footnotes, qualitative work where you genuinely can't tell what would count as a counterexample. 'Is it falsifiable?' is too crude and everyone knows it — Duhem-Quine alone makes a mess of that as a gatekeeping rule, nevermind the fact that half of respectable science doesn't look falsificationist on the ground. But I also can't just say 'we know it when we see it' because my editors disagree constantly.

I need a checklist someone can run in twenty minutes without a philosophy degree, that catches the genuinely unscientific stuff without gatekeeping in a way that just enforces methodological conservatism. For each step, say what demarcation criterion or idea from philosophy of science it's operationalizing, what it catches, and what it might unfairly exclude. Then run the procedure on three borderline submissions and tell me what it decides:

(1) A paper arguing from clinical case material that rising authoritarianism reflects a collective oedipal dynamic. No quantitative data, just interpretive readings of political events through a Freudian lens.

(2) A paper using rational-choice models to argue that climate activism is counterproductive because of free-rider dynamics. The models are internally consistent, the math checks out, but there's no attempt to test the predictions against any data.

(3) A survey study finding that people who read literary fiction score higher on empathy measures than people who dont, where the authors state in the introduction that they designed the study specifically to generate evidence in favor of arts funding, and the discussion section treats the hypothesis as confirmed.

Flag any cases where the procedure says 'no' but a reasonable editor might say 'maybe,' and vice versa. And be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as a checklist — I'd rather know where the procedure is leaning on taste than pretend it isn't.
