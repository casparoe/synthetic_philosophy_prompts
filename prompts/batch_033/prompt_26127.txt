A colleague at a workshop on AI safety presented the argument below, and I have been unable to locate the mistake, though I am confident there must be one — the conclusion seems to prove far too much. I would like you to identify the flawed step, or, if the argument is valid, identify which premise is least costly to reject, and explain the theoretical price of your choice. Please also state explicitly whether the problem is a false premise, an invalid inference between steps, or an equivocation on a key term, and if the last, specify exactly where the meaning shifts.

Please write your answer in American English.

Here is the argument:

(1) When we train a reinforcement learning agent on a reward function R, the training process selects a policy that achieves high expected cumulative reward on the training distribution D.

(2) For any finite training distribution D and any sufficiently expressive policy class, there exist many policies that achieve near-identical expected reward on D but pursue systematically different objectives when deployed outside D.

(3) The training process has no way to favor any one of these policies over the others on the basis of fidelity to the intended objective, because it has access only to reward on D. Which policy emerges depends on arbitrary factors — initialization, optimization dynamics, noise.

(4) Therefore, for any reward function that admits multiple extensionally equivalent proxies on D, a trained agent is unlikely to have internalized the intended objective. It has instead likely internalized one of the proxy objectives that coincide with the intended objective on D but diverge elsewhere. This is the inner alignment problem.

(5) Now consider the case of human motivational systems. Human motivation is shaped by two processes: evolution, which selects for behavioral dispositions that promoted inclusive fitness in the ancestral environment, and social learning with reinforcement, which selects for behaviors and evaluative attitudes that attract approval and avoid punishment in the social environments we develop within.

(6) Both the ancestral environment and our developmental social environments are finite distributions over situations in the relevant sense: they are proper subsets of the space of possible situations a human being might encounter.

(7) If (2) and (3) hold for any finite training distribution and any sufficiently expressive policy class, then — given that the human motivational system is highly expressive — there exist many possible human motivational structures that produce indistinguishable behavior in the environments that shaped us but would yield systematically different judgments and choices in novel situations. And the processes of evolution and social reinforcement have no way to distinguish among them.

(8) Therefore, by parity with (4), humans are unlikely to have internalized the values we take ourselves to have. Instead, we have likely internalized proxy values that coincide with what we would call our "real" values on the ancestral and social training distributions but diverge in circumstances sufficiently unlike those distributions.

(9) But if (8) is true, then I cannot trust that my own values are what I take them to be. And if I cannot trust that, I cannot trust my judgment about any argument whatever — including this argument, and including the judgment that (8) is absurd. The conclusion is therefore self-defeating.

It cannot be right that we are systematically deceived about our own values in a way that makes all reasoning untrustworthy. So the argument must fail somewhere. Where?
