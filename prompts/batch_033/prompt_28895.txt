I supervise doctoral trainees at a university psychology clinic, and one pattern I see repeatedly is a trainee taking a client's self-report at face value when there are strong behavioral indicators that the client is self-deceived about their own motives — the husband who insists he drinks to unwind, not to avoid his marriage, while all the circumstantial evidence points the other way; the student who says she dropped the course because the professor was unfair, not because she was failing. My trainees know the textbook definition of self-deception, but they have no procedure for deciding, in a concrete case, whether that label fits — as opposed to ordinary lying, wishful thinking, akrasia, or simply being mistaken about a difficult-to-know fact about oneself. And when I push them, they discover they are not even sure what they are claiming: the philosophical literature raises the sharp worry that self-deception is strictly impossible, since the deceiver and the deceived are the same person and you cannot simultaneously hold a belief and its negation.

I want to give my trainees a structured interview protocol or decision tree they can work through — something they can use in supervision to assess a specific self-report — that is honest about the conceptual difficulty rather than papering over it. The procedure should draw on whatever philosophy and cognitive science have actually established about self-deception: the conditions that must be met, the mechanisms that have been proposed (motivated reasoning, partitioned beliefs, selective attention to evidence), and the observable markers that distinguish self-deception from neighboring phenomena. It should flag the points where the procedure itself relies on a controversial theoretical commitment — for instance, whether self-deception requires that the person once knew the truth, or merely that the evidence was available — and say what the procedure cannot detect (fully unconscious self-deception, for example). Please write the procedure as a sequence of questions with decision rules, and then apply it to two cases: the husband and the alcohol, and the student and the course. Be explicit about where the procedure gives a determinate answer and where it leaves the supervisor having to exercise judgment.
