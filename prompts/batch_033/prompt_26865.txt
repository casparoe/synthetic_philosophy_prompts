I've been reading an old translation of Luis de Molina's *Concordia* (1588), the part where he introduces the idea of middle knowledge, and there's a passage I can't get my head around. I'll try to give the gist of it here, without quoting directly, because I don't have the original Latin and the translation I'm working from is a photocopy a friend gave me.

Molina is arguing that God's knowledge of future contingent events—specifically, what a free creature would do if placed in some set of circumstances—cannot be classified under either of the two kinds of divine knowledge that the tradition already recognized. It isn't natural knowledge, he says, because natural knowledge covers only necessary truths and the whole range of bare possibilities, and there is nothing in the nature of a free will that necessitates that it would choose one way rather than another under a given set of conditions. But it also isn't free knowledge, because free knowledge depends on God's own decree to create and to act; it is knowledge of what God has willed to bring about. Yet God knew, prior to any decision to create, what any possible free creature would do in any possible situation—and He knew this with certainty, even though the creature's choice is free and not determined by God. This knowledge, Molina says, must therefore be a third kind, standing between the other two, and he calls it *scientia media*.

The part I get stuck on is this: Molina seems to be saying that God's middle knowledge is both pre-volitional (it doesn't depend on what God wills to do) and yet its content is contingent—it could have been otherwise. But if the truth of these counterfactuals about free creatures doesn't depend on God's will, and it doesn't follow from the necessities of the divine nature, then what does it depend on? What makes it the case that Peter, if placed in situation S, would freely deny Christ, rather than freely confess him? Molina keeps insisting that the truth of such a counterfactual is prior to any divine decree, but I don't understand what could ground it, or where the truth of it comes from, if not from God's will and not from mere logical necessity.

I'm not a philosopher or a theologian—I just got interested in these questions on my own. Can you walk me through this part of Molina's argument slowly, and explain what is supposed to be doing the work of making these counterfactuals true?
