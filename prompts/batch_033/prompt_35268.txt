I'm a philosophy professor at Oxford and I've been asked to give a short speech — eight minutes, maybe ten — at the opening of a small exhibition on the 1956 controversy over Truman's honorary degree. You know the one: Anscombe wrote that pamphlet, "Mr. Truman's Degree," arguing that Oxford shouldn't honor him because the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were murder. The exhibition is going up in the philosophy faculty library, and the theme is "dissent and academic integrity." The audience is a real mix — some colleagues who know the just war literature cold, some undergrads who've maybe read the pamphlet once, and a handful of university administrators who are nervous about whether this whole thing is going to stir up old fights.

Here's what I want the speech to do. I want to walk through the actual structure of Anscombe's argument — not the personality, not the drama of the vote, but the philosophical moves. The distinction she drew between killing combatants and killing noncombatants, the role of the doctrine of double effect, and specifically her claim that the bombings weren't a case of foreseen-but-unintended civilian deaths but of intentional killing of civilians as a means to an end. I want the speech to make that argument clear enough that the undergrads get it, but also precise enough that my colleagues don't wince. And I want it to land on a question that's still live: was Anscombe right that the intention/foresight distinction does the moral work she thought it did, or does the Truman case actually put pressure on the distinction itself?

The tricky part is the administrators. They do not want a speech that sounds like it's re-litigating whether Oxford was wrong to honor Truman sixty-some years ago. So I need to frame the whole thing as a philosophical question about the structure of moral reasoning in war, using the historical episode as an illustration, not as a polemic. The speech should make people think, not make them uncomfortable about which side of the room they're on.

You're an expert on Anscombe's moral philosophy and the just war tradition. Think carefully about the argumentative structure — make sure the philosophical moves are accurate. Before you write the final speech, take a few paragraphs to brainstorm how you'd structure the central section that lays out her argument, so I can see your reasoning. Oh, and one thing — I don't want the speech to end with a tidy resolution. The point of the exhibition is that the questions are still open, so leave the audience with the tension rather than a verdict.
