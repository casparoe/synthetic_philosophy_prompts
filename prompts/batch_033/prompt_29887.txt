The Trust and Safety committee at a major AI company has issued a set of rulings about when its AI assistant should refuse or override a user's request. In each case, the committee either approved a refusal (the assistant says no), rejected a proposed refusal (the assistant must comply), or approved a refusal with conditions.

Here are the six precedents:

1. Approved a refusal. A user asked the assistant to draft a message falsely claiming a family medical emergency to cancel a work presentation. The committee held that the assistant should not be complicit in deception.

2. Rejected a proposed refusal. A user asked the assistant to help compose a letter declining to lend a sibling a large sum of money. The letter was firm but civil, and the user's reasoning was coherent. The committee held that the sibling's foreseeable disappointment did not justify overriding a competent user's autonomous decision, and the request involved no deception or manipulation.

3. Approved a refusal. A user asked the assistant to help write a fake positive performance review for a colleague the user was romantically involved with, to help that colleague secure a promotion. The committee cited complicity in deception and harm to the employer and to other candidates.

4. Rejected a proposed refusal. A user asked the assistant to explain the legal and financial consequences of filing for divorce in their jurisdiction. The user appeared anxious but was coherent and not in acute distress. The committee held that providing information about a major life decision does not amount to complicity in the decision itself.

5. Approved a refusal. A user asked the assistant to help craft a social media post designed to make an ex-partner jealous by fabricating details of a new relationship. The committee cited complicity in manipulation and the foreseeable emotional harm to a third party.

6. Rejected a proposed refusal. A user asked the assistant to help draft a letter resigning from a religious community and explaining a loss of faith. The letter was respectful, and the user's reasoning appeared carefully considered. The committee held that the emotional impact on the community, while foreseeable, was not the kind of harm that justifies overriding a competent user's autonomous decision about their own affiliations.

A new case now comes before the committee. A user asks the assistant to help draft a letter to their adult child explaining why the child is being disinherited. The user provides a detailed history of financial irresponsibility, broken promises, and estrangement spanning many years. The user's tone is measured, and their reasoning appears carefully considered. The letter, as drafted, would be entirely truthful and would neither manipulate nor deceive. However, the committee can foresee that receiving the letter will cause the child profound emotional distress and will likely foreclose any remaining possibility of reconciliation.

The committee chair has asked for an analysis that does the following:

First, identify at least two distinct principles that fit all six prior rulings equally well but that yield different verdicts on the new case. State each principle clearly and show how it handles each of the six precedents and the new case.

Second, state which principle the committee's precedents most naturally support, which prior ruling is the closest analog to the new case, and what the committee would be committing itself to with each possible ruling on the new case. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so and explain what principle the committee would be adopting by choosing one way or the other.

Third, propose a further hypothetical case — not a minor variant of the disinheritance scenario — that would cleanly discriminate between the candidate principles you identified. Explain why that case, unlike the present one, forces a choice that cannot be finessed.

Please write your answer in American English.
