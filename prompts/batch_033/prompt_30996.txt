My younger sister was born with dense congenital cataracts and, until three months ago at age nineteen, had only rudimentary light perception. She underwent lensectomy and intraocular lens implantation, and her visual acuity is now approaching normal. In the weeks since her bandages came off, I have been watching her learn to see, and the experience has upended a lot of what I thought I knew about perception. I mentioned some of this to two colleagues — one in psychology, one in philosophy — and they promptly launched into an argument about Molyneux's problem that I have been turning over ever since. I would like you to adjudicate it, move by move.

Here is what happened:

(1) The psychologist said that my sister's case is essentially a modern confirmation of Molyneux's original answer. On the day her bandages were removed, she could not visually identify a cube and a sphere that she had handled moments before while blindfolded. She had to touch them to know which was which. So Molyneux was right: the answer is no.

(2) The philosopher replied that this is too quick. Within a week my sister was identifying those same shapes by sight alone, and within three weeks she could visually recognize objects she had only ever known by touch — her favorite mug, the textured handle of her cane. If the cross-modal transfer happens that fast, he said, then framing the question around the very first instant of vision makes the answer depend on an arbitrary temporal boundary. The interesting question is whether the sensory modalities share an underlying representational format, and the rapidity of her learning suggests they do, at least partially.

(3) The psychologist pushed back: the question Molyneux actually asked — and that Locke and Berkeley debated — was precisely about the first moment. The man in the thought experiment has just gained his sight and is presented with the cube and sphere "before he touched them." My sister's experience answers that question squarely in the negative. The fact that she learned quickly is a separate empirical finding about perceptual learning, not a rebuttal to Molyneux's point, which was about innateness.

(4) The philosopher said that this treats Molyneux's question as a mere empirical prediction rather than as a probe into the architecture of the mind. If the question is "are touch and sight natively linked?" then a few days of rapid cross-modal mapping is nearly as philosophically significant as instantaneous recognition would have been. He added that my sister reported something interesting: on day one, she could not name the shapes, but she said the cube looked "sharp" and the sphere looked "smooth" — visual impressions that correspond to tactile properties. That, he argued, is cross-modal transfer of a kind, and it blurs the clean "no" the psychologist wants.

(5) The psychologist responded that "sharp" and "smooth" are not shape recognition. Molyneux's question was whether the newly sighted person could "distinguish and tell apart" the cube and sphere by sight — that is, correctly identify which is which. My sister could not. The fact that she had some vague cross-modal associations does not overturn the core finding.

(6) The philosopher's final move was to say that if we take Molyneux's question in its strictest form, the answer is indeed no, but the strictest form is a poor question. It is like asking whether someone can ride a bicycle the very first second they sit on one, and when they cannot, declaring that bicycle-riding is not innate — while ignoring that they figure it out in an afternoon. The interesting philosophical work, he said, is in explaining what enables that rapid learning, and the answer to that question may well be that the senses share an amodal spatial representation that is not strictly innate but is developmentally canalized.

Neither of them has budged since. I am less interested in who "wins" than in understanding which moves are sound and which are not. Please go through the six steps and evaluate each on its own terms: where is the reasoning valid, where does it overreach, and what is the strongest version of each position that survives the exchange?
