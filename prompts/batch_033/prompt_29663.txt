I serve on my company's data-center energy committee. A vendor has proposed a twelve-month pilot of reversible-computing hardware in one rack, claiming that conventional servers pay an avoidable thermodynamic cost because they destroy information at every gate operation. The vendor's case is conceptual: Maxwell's demon seems able to reduce entropy without expending work until you account for the cost of erasing the demon's records; by the same reasoning, a processor can approach zero energy per operation only if it is logically reversible and never erases bits, so our standard irreversible logic is paying a penalty that better engineering could remove. The committee is split. Our engineering lead says the proposal is straightforwardly testable and we should run the pilot and measure power draw. Two other members say the vendor has misapplied the Landauer bound on the energy needed to erase one bit, which sits orders of magnitude below the leakage, refresh, and interconnect losses that actually dominate our servers. I keep noticing that the disagreement turns on a deeper question: whether the physics-of-information argument establishes a real floor on the cost of computation, or is a formal bookkeeping point with no practical force. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so please make the reasoning self-contained.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame—approve the pilot, reject it, or approve it with conditions—and it should engage the strongest version of the case on the losing side rather than dismissing it with a one-line objection.
