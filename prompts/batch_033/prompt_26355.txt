I'm running an upper-level undergrad seminar on virtue ethics and we're spending two weeks on the situationist challenge. I want to hand out a decision-tree taxonomy of the main positions people have taken in response to the empirical evidence. I want the tree to sort students by what they actually think about the evidence and about what virtue ethics requires — not by which label sounds nicer.

Here's roughly what I'm picturing. Start with a question like: does the situationist evidence (Milgram, the Good Samaritan study, the dime-in-the-phone-booth stuff, etc.) actually show that behavior varies across situations in ways incompatible with robust character traits? Then branch. The leaves should include at minimum: the empirical-denial response, the rarity claim (virtues are real but rare), the local-traits view (Doris), the cognitive-affective framework response (the idea that the experiments don't test for virtues because they target the wrong psychological constructs), the normative-insulation move (virtue ethics doesn't make the kind of empirical claims the situationists think it does), and the capitulation (virtue ethics as traditionally understood needs to be abandoned). If there are other important positions that deserve leaves, add them.

Build from the logical space first: work out which diagnostic questions carve the positions at the joints, then locate the actual views in that space. Flag unoccupied regions and say whether they're empty because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just haven't been explored. Also flag any pairs of positions your tree can't cleanly separate.

Keep it to something like 7–10 leaves. The branching questions should be concrete enough that a student who's done the reading can answer them without knowing which position they're heading toward.

Finally: at each branch, add a note saying whether the claim that splits it is largely settled or genuinely contested among people who work on this. I don't want students walking away thinking the debate is more open (or more closed) than it actually is.
