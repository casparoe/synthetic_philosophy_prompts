I am trying to work out what principle governs when I should trust aggregated versus disaggregated statistics, and I would like to know whether my verdicts on the following cases add up to a coherent position.

(1) UC Berkeley graduate admissions (1973): overall, men were admitted at a higher rate than women. Broken down by department, women were admitted at the same or higher rate in every department. I think the right conclusion is that there was no bias against women in admissions; the aggregate disparity was an artifact of women applying disproportionately to more competitive departments.

(2) Kidney stone treatments: Treatment A has a higher overall success rate than Treatment B. When you split by stone size, Treatment B is better for small stones and better for large stones. The reversal occurs because doctors assigned the harder cases to Treatment B. I think Treatment B is the genuinely better treatment; the disaggregated data is what matters.

(3) A drug trial: the drug improves recovery rates overall. Among patients who complete the full course, there is no benefit. The drug causes side effects that lead some patients to drop out, and those who drop out are precisely those who would not have recovered anyway. I think the aggregate result is correct: the drug works, and the subgroup analysis is misleading because it conditions on something the treatment affects.

(4) A public health survey: among people who visited a doctor in the past year, those who take daily vitamins report better health. In the full population, vitamin-takers and non-takers are equally healthy. The explanation is that people who visit doctors are a selected group: you are more likely to visit a doctor if you are either very health-conscious (and take vitamins) or sick (and do not). I think the aggregate result is correct; the doctor-visitor subgroup is misleading.

(5) A minimum wage study: overall, raising the minimum wage has no detectable effect on employment. In urban counties, employment rises; in rural counties, employment falls. I think the overall null result is misleading; the policy has genuinely different effects in different contexts, and the disaggregated result is the right one.

(6) A scholarship program: overall, recipients have higher graduation rates. Within each band of incoming SAT score, there is no difference. SAT was measured before the scholarship was awarded. I think the scholarship does not actually cause higher graduation; the aggregate result is confounded by prior academic ability, and the disaggregated result is correct.

I can see that my verdicts are not simply "always trust the disaggregated data" or "always trust the aggregate." But I cannot articulate the principle that distinguishes cases (1), (2), (5), and (6) — where I trust the disaggregated data — from cases (3) and (4), where I trust the aggregate. Is there a single principle that captures all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I revise, and what would the repaired principle be?
