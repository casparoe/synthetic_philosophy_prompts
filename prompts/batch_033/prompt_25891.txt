I'm the director of client services at a municipal rental-assistance program. We have funds to prevent about forty percent of the evictions we see; the rest we have to turn away. Our current process is inconsistent — different caseworkers make different calls on similar cases, and we have no shared rationale for why we help one household and not another. I want to move to a structured decision procedure grounded in luck egalitarianism: roughly, the view that inequalities traceable to brute luck (circumstances beyond a person's control) are unjust and call for redress, while inequalities traceable to option luck (the outcomes of genuine, informed choices) do not, or at least not to the same degree.

Please write a step-by-step decision procedure my caseworkers can follow when evaluating an application. The procedure should take the form of a sequence of questions with decision rules — where the answer to one question sends you to the next, stops the process with a denial, or flags the case for supervisor review. It needs to handle the following difficulties:

- Most real cases involve a mix of brute luck and option luck. A tenant may have lost their job through no fault of their own (brute luck) but then spent down their savings on non-essentials rather than rent (possible option luck). The procedure needs a way to weigh these factors rather than treating cases as all-or-nothing.
- Some choices are made under such severe constraints that treating them as exercises of option luck seems perverse. The procedure needs a test for when a choice is too constrained to count as the kind of option luck that should disqualify someone from assistance.
- Caseworkers have about forty-five minutes per file and no background in philosophy. The procedure must be usable with the information actually in an application — pay stubs, eviction notices, a brief intake interview — not information that would require an omniscient observer.

After laying out the procedure, please apply it to three cases:

1. A single mother who lost her restaurant job when the business closed during a recession. She has no family nearby. She used her last paycheck to pay rent for two months but then fell behind. Her eviction notice gives her three weeks.

2. A man in his thirties who quit a stable job to launch a business importing ceramics. The business failed within a year. He had savings that would have covered six months of rent but chose to invest them in inventory instead. He now faces eviction and argues that his entrepreneurial risk-taking should not disqualify him, since society benefits from people starting businesses.

3. A woman with a chronic pain condition that makes full-time work impossible. She works part-time and receives some disability benefits, but they do not cover her rent. She has lived in the same apartment for twelve years. Her landlord is evicting her after a rent increase she could not absorb.

For at least one of these cases, the procedure should yield an answer that I — as someone who wants to help vulnerable people — will find difficult to accept. Please flag that result and explain why the procedure forces it.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will systematically mishandle, which value judgments it cannot eliminate and merely relocates, and where caseworkers will still need to exercise discretion that the procedure cannot replace.
