I'm the fiction editor at a mid-size literary magazine — we publish maybe six short stories and a few essays a year, print and online. Every year or so we get a submission from someone whose off-page conduct is a problem: credible accusations of sexual misconduct, a conviction for fraud, a history of racist or transphobic public statements, that kind of thing. Our current policy is basically that the work stands or falls on its own merit, until one of our editors objects, at which point we have no process and it becomes a three-week email fight. I want a procedure we can actually run on each case, in maybe an afternoon, with five people in the room, that we could defend to our board and to the public.

Please write me a decision procedure for whether to publish a piece by a person whose conduct or public statements are seriously objectionable. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of this actually has to say — the debates about whether a moral flaw in a work can be an aesthetic flaw or even an aesthetic virtue, whether you can separate the work from the artist, and when knowing something about the artist changes what the work means — but don't give me a survey; give me the operational version. I want: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a person because its a values call rather than a factual one. I have a suspicion there are at least three different questions tangled together — whether the work is good, whether the person is bad, and whether publishing is an endorsement — and I want them pulled apart, not collapsed. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire or just launder a gut feeling.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A genuinely excellent short story submitted by a writer who was credibly accused — but never charged — of sexual assault about a decade ago. The accusations were reported in a major outlet, the writer denies them, and the story has nothing to do with sex or violence.

2. A mediocre-but-publishable personal essay about forgiveness, submitted by a writer who spent years harassing a trans critic online, has since apologized, and the apology reads to half our staff as genuine and to the other half as damage control. Two of our editors say publishing the essay would make them feel the magazine is not a place for them.

3. A formally stunning story that several of us read as morally repugnant — it is written from inside the head of a perpetrator and, to some readers, glamorizes him — but the author has, as far as anyone knows, done nothing wrong.

For each, tell me what the procedure says, where it stops being mechanical, and what a defensible public explanation would sound like. I especially want at least one case where the procedure forces us to do something the room does not want to do.
