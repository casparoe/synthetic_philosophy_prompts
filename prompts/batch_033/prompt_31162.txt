A colleague in political science walked me through an argument that starts as the familiar claim that voting is irrational and ends by indicting every form of mass collective action. I cannot find the exit, and I suspect something goes wrong early.

(1) An action is rational only if its expected benefits exceed its expected costs.
(2) In deciding whether to vote, the only benefit that counts is instrumental: the probability that my own vote is decisive, multiplied by the value of my preferred candidate winning instead of the alternative.
(3) In any large electorate, the probability that a single vote is decisive is vanishingly small — far smaller than the cost of voting divided by any plausible value one could assign to the difference between the two candidates.
(4) So, for an ordinary voter, the expected instrumental benefit of voting is smaller than the cost of getting to the polls.
(5) Therefore ordinary citizens in large elections should not vote.

Then the trouble:

(6) Nothing in (1)–(4) turns on voting. The same premises apply to any individual contribution to a good that is produced only by the aggregation of many small actions: donating to a large humanitarian charity, attending a protest, cutting one's own emissions, recycling.
(7) So, by the same reasoning, no one should do any of those things either.
(8) But a theory of rational action that rules out all individual contributions to large collective goods is not a modest result about the costs of voting; it is a reductio of the theory.
(9) So at least one of (1)–(7) must go, and the so-called paradox of voting turns out to be a paradox about collective action in general.

Which step should I reject, and what exactly does each candidate rejection cost? I would rather keep (1) and (2) than abandon the idea that individuals can rationally contribute to collective goods, but I want to know what I am paying either way.
