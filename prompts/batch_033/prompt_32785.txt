I've been working on a piece about population ethics and I've managed to argue myself into a corner. The repugnant conclusion — that for any world of happy people, there's some much larger world of people with lives barely worth living that's better — seems inescapable to me now, and I want someone to tell me where the argument actually breaks, or whether it doesn't. Here's the chain:

(1) Take a world with a million people leading genuinely wonderful lives. Call it A.

(2) Now consider B: the same million people, each with their welfare reduced by one unit (they're still wonderful off), plus a million brand-new people at that same slightly-lower-but-still-wonderful level. B looks better than A. The loss to each existing person is trivially small, and we've gained a million flourishing lives.

(3) If you accept that B is better than A, you've accepted the principle that a big enough population gain can compensate for an arbitrarily small per-person welfare loss.

(4) Apply that principle repeatedly: reduce welfare by one unit, double the population. After finitely many steps you land at World Z — an astronomically huge population whose lives are just barely worth living.

(5) Since each step is an improvement and "better than" is transitive, Z is better than A. That's the repugnant conclusion.

(6) But the repugnant conclusion is false. A world of a million people with wonderful lives is not worse than a world of trillions of people eking out lives barely worth living.

So something has to give. The standard exits, as I understand them: deny (2) and insist no population gain compensates for any welfare loss, no matter how tiny (which means you'd reject a world with a trillion extra happy people to spare one person a pinprick). Deny transitivity (Temkin's move), which means you can't chain "better than" judgments — goodbye to practical reasoning as we know it. Or posit a lexical threshold where welfare above some cutoff is infinitely more valuable than welfare below it, which feels like it needs God to anchor it.

I can't tell which of these is the least bad, or whether I'm missing a fourth door. And if the argument really is sound and the repugnant conclusion is just true, I want to know what that actually means — for policy, for whether to have kids, for how we think about the long-term future. Is the conclusion really so repugnant, or have we just been describing it in the most viscerally off-putting way possible?
