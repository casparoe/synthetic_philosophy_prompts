I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about Voltaire's Candide for a general-interest magazine. I want to read the novel against Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds, but I keep going back and forth on whether the satire is actually fair to him. Obviously Pangloss is a caricature, and the book piles on the horrors — the Lisbon earthquake, the auto-da-fé, the old woman's story, the mutilated slave in Surinam — to make the everything-is-for-the-best refrain sound grotesque. But I've got a nagging feeling that Voltaire is beating up a dummy. Leibniz, as I understand him, was not saying that everything is going great or that we should just sit back; he was making a claim about constraints on divine choice — that God chooses among possible worlds, and that the best world may have to include evils that are tied to greater goods or to the simplest general laws, so the optimum isn't the same thing as a world with no suffering at all. So here's what I want: read Candide as a philosophical rebuttal and tell me whether it actually lands a blow on Leibniz's theodicy or only on a straw version of it. Where is the satire fair, and where does it trade on a confusion? And does the novel have its own alternative stance at the end — the turn to cultivating the garden — or is that just quietism rather than a rival metaphysics? I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the theodicy seriously, dosn't reduce one to an illustration of the other, and ends by saying what Candide adds to the debate that a straight philosophical essay doesn't. No need for citations or quotes; I can check those myself — I'm after the argument.
