I run the animal welfare grantmaking program at a mid-sized foundation, and I'm seeing more and more proposals that lean on the scale argument: because insects are so absurdly numerous, even a low probability that they're sentient can produce an expected welfare impact that swamps everything else in our portfolio. I need something I can actually use, not a seminar. Could you act as a philosopher who works on moral uncertainty and animal ethics, with a practical streak, and turn the scale argument into a decision procedure my team can run? We review maybe fifteen proposals a cycle, just me and one analyst, usually under an hour per proposal before we have to make a preliminary call, and then the full board (mostly farm-animal people already skeptical of insect work) signs off. So the stakes are real money and our credibility with the board. The procedure should be a checklist or a sequence of tests in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no or a not-yet, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than arithmetic. I want it to actually respect what the scale argument says and what it doesn't — in particular the difference between a genuine expected-value argument with bounded, defensible parameters and a Pascal's-mugging-style trick where the numbers just swamp the probablities. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire, too.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) Funding the rollout of electrical stunning before processing at a large cricket farm, affecting on the order of a billion crickets a year; the evidence for cricket sentience is real but thin (nociceptor-like structures, some avoidance learning), and our best outside estimate is maybe a low single-digit percent chance of sentience. (2) A campaign to stop a planned broadcast pesticide spraying in a national forest, on the grounds that it would affect trillions of larval insects; the campaign's actual causal effect is uncertain, and even if it succeeds the welfare sign is unclear, since the spraying might kill quickly while the larvae that "win" may starve or be parasitized anyway. (3) A requirement that a black soldier fly larvae facility switch from grinding (near-instant) to freezing (slower, possibly less painful, but nobody is sure) at substantial cost, affecting a few hundred million larvae a year. I'd like at least one of these to come out as a no or a not-yet if that's what the procedure honestly gives — my team is insect-curious and I want the procedure to be able to disappoint us.

Before you write the final procedure, do a quick brainstorm of the ways the scale argument can go wrong in practice; I'd like the procedure built to catch those failure modes, and I want it to flag which steps are judgment calls wearing a spreadsheet costume rather than real measurements.
