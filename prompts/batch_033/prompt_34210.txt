I am to be best man at my brother's wedding next month, and I am stuck on the speech. The difficulty is that I am a philosopher of mind, and for the past fifteen years I have been convinced by eliminative materialism: the view that our ordinary folk-psychological categories — beliefs, desires, intentions, hopes, and the rest — do not carve nature at its joints, and that a mature neuroscience will simply dispense with them, much as chemistry dispensed with phlogiston. I do not think there are beliefs, literally speaking. I do not think there are desires. And I am meant to stand up in front of a hundred people and say something about my brother and his new wife that is true, and warm, and worthy of the occasion, without relying on any of that apparatus.

The room will be mixed. My brother knows my views and has been good-humoured about them for years; his wife is a historian who has heard me go on about this at dinners; a handful of colleagues from the department will be there and will notice every dodge and paraphrase. But most of the guests are family and friends who have no idea I hold any such view, and I do not want the speech to become a lecture or to make the day about me. I need it to land as a proper wedding toast — affectionate, funny in places, genuine — while saying nothing I believe to be false.

Please write the toast. It should be about five minutes when spoken. The philosophical constraint must do real structural work: it should shape what the speech can and cannot say, and the speech should find its way to warmth and sincerity through the constraint, not by ignoring it or winking at it. But the speech itself should not name the view or explain it; the philosophers in the room will see what is going on, and everyone else should simply hear a good toast.
