At a lab meeting last week two people in my group got into an argument about whether viruses are alive, and I ended up trying to referee and couldn't. It went roughly like this. (1) Lena said viruses aren't alive: outside a cell they're inert, they don't metabolize or maintain themselves, they're just a genome in a protien shell that gets copied by someone else's machinery. (2) Dev said that makes them like a lot of obligate intracellular bacteria—Chlamydia can't make its own ATP either, and nobody calls it nonliving—and viruses do the one thing that matters most, which is evolve. (3) Lena said Chlamydia is still a cell with ribosomes and thier own ongoing biochemistry; a virus doesn't do any of the work of its own replication, and pointing out that it evolves just says it's subject to natural selection, not that it's alive. (4) Dev said giant viruses blur exactly that line: they carry genes for parts of the translation machinery, so the difference between virus and cell is one of degree, not kind. (5) Lena said fine, but that just shows 'alive' is a cluster concept and we should ask which capacities a thing has instead of insisting on a yes/no. (6) Dev replied that the usual working definition of life has two parts, self-sustaining chemistry and Darwinian evolution, and viruses pass the second but fail the first; so any verdict has to say which clause matters more, and Lena hasn't. Can you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it—say who's right about which specific point, whether the two-part definition is doing real work or just restating the disagreement, and what both of them are missing?
