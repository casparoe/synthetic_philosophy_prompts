I am working through the value-of-knowledge literature and have reached the point where I need the criteria before I go any further. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief must explain, the cases it must handle correctly, the distinctions it may not collapse, and the results from elsewhere in epistemology with which it must be consistent. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or whether it reflects the commitments of a particular camp (reliabilist, virtue-theoretic, knowledge-first, and so on).

I particularly want to know whether any subset of the non-negotiable desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. If the swamping problem shows, for instance, that any theory which analyses knowledge into true belief plus a further condition whose value is merely instrumental with respect to truth will necessarily leave an unexplained value residual, then the desiderata themselves might rule out a whole family of candidate theories — and that is something I want flagged before I start evaluating individual views. More generally, if the landscape contains a tension between, say, explaining why knowledge is more valuable than any proper subset of its constituents and accommodating the fact that some knowledge seems trivial, I want to see that tension made explicit and assessed: can a theory honour both, or must one of those requirements be weakened?

A brief scorecard against the list for virtue epistemology, process reliabilism, and the knowledge-first programme would be helpful, but the weight should stay on the desiderata and their internal tensions. If there is a single requirement that, taken seriously, knocks out the largest number of otherwise plausible theories, identify it and say why.
