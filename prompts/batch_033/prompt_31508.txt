I am trying to work out what I actually think about hard choices and about whether values that cannot be ranked can still be compared, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts. Here they are, as honestly as I can report them.

When I choose between two careers that pull in different directions—say, a corporate job that pays very well and a policy job at a nonprofit that pays modestly but does genuine good—I do not think one is better than the other, but I also do not think the choice is arbitrary. Someone who flips a coin is, I think, missing something, even though neither option is clearly better.

When I choose between two breakfast cereals and I have no preference between them, the choice is arbitrary, and flipping a coin seems exactly right.

When one public health program saves a hundred lives and another saves a hundred and one, the second is simply better, other things equal; I do not think the presence of lives at stake makes the two options incommensurable.

When a friend asks me to betray another friend's confidence for a modest sum of money, I do not need to weigh anything: the friendship matters more, and the money is not a reason at all. Yet I also could not say how much more it matters, and I resist the idea that friendship and money sit on a single scale of value.

When I compare two very different great paintings, or two very different worthwhile lives—say, a life devoted to music and a life devoted to medicine—I do not think there is a common measure, but I also do not think every such comparison is a tie or a wash. I want to say that a person can have reasons to choose one over the other that do not amount to its being better.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this. A person is deciding whether to spend the next year writing a novel or working in a refugee camp. I do not think one is better than the other; I do not think the choice is arbitrary; and I do think either choice can be the right one for them. But I cannot state the principle that makes that so.

Is there one coherent account of value and choice that delivers all of these verdicts, or close? If not, which verdicts should I overturn, and what does the best account say about the novelist-versus-refugee-camp case?
