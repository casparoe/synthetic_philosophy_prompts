For some time now I have been reading the philosophical literature on vagueness — the sorites paradox, the major theories of how vague predicates work — as background for a novel I am writing in which a legal outcome turns on the interpretation of a vague statutory term. What has struck me, as an outsider to the discipline, is that the debate appears to have been running in place for decades. Epistemicists maintain that vague predicates have sharp but unknowable boundaries; supervaluationists treat vagueness as semantic incompleteness, with truth relative to admissible precisifications; degree theorists assign truth values on a continuum; contextualists hold that the extension shifts with conversational context. Each side has sophisticated replies to the others' objections, and yet nobody moves. The same arguments, refined but not resolved, circulate through the journals.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement. Is it, at bottom, a verbal dispute about the word "true"? Is it a clash of temperaments about what an explanation of vagueness must do — one camp demanding that classical logic be preserved, another demanding that the theory respect the phenomenology of borderline cases, a third demanding that it track speakers' actual usage? Are the different theories answering different questions under the single heading "the problem of vagueness" — one about the metaphysics of properties, one about the semantics of natural language, one about the epistemology of classification? Or is there a genuine factual question in play, perhaps about the cognitive architecture underlying categorization, that empirical work could eventually settle?

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a psychological finding, a change of question — and whether we should expect it, or whether the impasse is a permanent feature of the terrain.
