I teach an advanced undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and I want to assign a dialogue that forces students to work through a clinical case rather than recite positions. The case is pain asymbolia: patients can classify a stimulus as painful—locating it, rating its intensity, and describing its quality—yet they do not withdraw, grimace, or complain, and they report that the sensation does not bother them. The underlying dispute is whether the badness of pain, its unpleasantness and its mattering to the subject, is essential to pain itself, or whether it is a separable affective component that can come apart from pain's sensory core.

Write a dialogue between two speakers at a case conference on such a patient. The first speaker defends the view that pain is essentially bad: a state that does not hurt in the relevant sense is not pain proper, so the asymbolic patient's reports should be reinterpreted, or the condition reveals a deficit in the patient's access to their own pain rather than pain without badness. The second speaker defends a dissociative view: pain has distinguishable sensory and affective components, asymbolia shows that the badness belongs to the affective component, and therefore the sensory core of pain can occur without the component that makes pain bad. The dialogue should be a genuine exchange rather than two monologues: each speaker should address the other's strongest arguments. Have them press on what the patient's verbal reports can and cannot establish, whether the word "pain" is being used univocally or equivocally, whether a sensory representation of tissue damage that leaves the subject indifferent still deserves the name "pain," and whether anything clinical or ethical—diagnosis, treatment targets, the management of suffering—turns on the answer. Both speakers should be competent and serious, and neither should win by default.
