The St. Petersburg paradox has been chewed over since the 18th century, and it's still not clear what it's supposed to show. The arithmetic isn't in dispute: if a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads and the payoff is 2^n on the nth flip, the expected value is infinite, but almost nobody would pay more than pocket change to play. What people disagree about is what follows. Some take it to refute maximizing expected value outright; some say it only shows utility has to be bounded; some say it shows a lottery's fair price isn't its expected value; some say it's just an artifact of pretending a casino could have an infinite bankroll. I've started to suspect the fight persists because these camps are answering different questions under one name — descriptive psychology, normative rationality, and formal constraints on preference — and the math is a bit of a red herring. But I'm not sure. Diagnose this: what kind of disagreement is it really — verbal, substantive, about standards of evidence, about bedrock intuitions, or about which cases count as data? Dont just lay out the options; pick a diagnosis, give your reasons, and say what would count as progress given that diagnosis, or whether we should stop expecting any.
