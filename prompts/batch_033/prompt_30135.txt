You're a philosopher of mind who has spent a lot of time with Hofstadter's later work on the self. I want an essay on this: can an 'I' genuinely be distributed across multiple brains? Hofstadter's picture is that the self is a pattern, roughly a strange loop, and that patterns can be instantiated in other substrates at higher or lower resolution. So bits of you—and maybe more than bits—get instantiated in the heads of people who know you well, and a loved one might partly live on in you after they die. Weigh the strongest version of that view against the strongest objections, for example that a representation of me is not me, that low-resolution copies lose exactly what made the original a self, that there's no first-person continuation in the copies, and that the whole thing is just a comforting metaphor dressed up in computational language. Then actually defend a verdict. Dont just say it depends what we mean by self; take a stand on whether death is the end of the self in the sense that matters, and whether the copies should matter to me now. Think carefully before you write—maybe start by brainstorming a steelman of the distributed-self view and the two or three objections that most threaten it, then organize the essay around your conclusion.
