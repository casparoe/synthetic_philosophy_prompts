I just spent an hour arguing with a friend about what makes pain bad, and she laid out verdicts on a whole series of cases. I'm trying to figure out what rule she's actually running, and what she'd say about a case we didn't get to. Here are her verdicts:

- Ordinary acute pain, like stubbing your toe: bad for the person.
- Pain asymbolia — a neurological condition where patients report the sensory quality of pain, can locate and rate its intensity, but insist it doesn't bother them and they have no urge to stop it: not bad for the person.
- The deep affective suffering in severe depression, which has no clear bodily location: bad, and bad in the same fundamental way ordinary pain is bad.
- Pain relieved by morphine where the patient says the sensation is still there but no longer bothers them: the pain has stopped being bad for them.
- Pain that the subject is genuinely glad to have because it signals recovery from a life-threatening condition: the pain itself is still bad for them, even though having it is overall a good thing.
- Pain the subject is so absorbed in a task that they don't notice it for an hour: not bad for the person during that hour.
- Pain experienced during a vivid dream, which feels exactly like a waking pain but corresponds to no actual bodily state: she hesitated on this one but ultimately said it is bad for the dreamer.

Now, the case we didn't get to. There is a hypothetical neurological patient who experiences the full unpleasant felt quality of pain — the raw "ouch" — but entirely lacks the normal motivational response: no desire to withdraw, no urge for it to stop, no avoidance behavior. When asked, they say, "yes, it hurts, it feels unpleasant, but I simply don't want it to stop. It doesn't bother me in that sense." Is the pain bad for this person?

What I want to know is: what rule or principle is she implicitly running that fits all her verdicts? Are there other rules that also fit them? Do those rules converge on this new case? If they diverge, give me your best guess about which rule she'd endorse and what she'd say about the case, and name a further case that would cleanly separate the candidate rules.
