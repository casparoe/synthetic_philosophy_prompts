I've been following the debate between advocates of the "inside view" and the "outside view" in forecasting and decision-making, and I'm increasingly puzzled by the fact that the conversation seems to have stalled despite widespread agreement on the empirical picture. The basic distinction originates with Kahneman and Tversky's work on the planning fallacy: the inside view reasons about a project or problem by attending to its specific features — building a mental model of the particular case, its obstacles, its resources, its timeline — while the outside view treats the case as an instance of a reference class and anchors predictions to the base rates of similar cases. The empirical track record is not really contested. The outside view reliably outperforms the inside view across a wide range of domains, from construction projects and mergers to the kinds of geopolitical forecasting studied by Tetlock and his collaborators. So far, both sides nod along.

The impasse emerges when the conversation turns from the descriptive to the normative. One camp — call them the strong outside-view position — argues that the inside view is a standing cognitive hazard, a systematic source of overconfidence and error that we should regard with suspicion wherever possible and systematically replace with outside-view reasoning. The other camp — call them the contextualists — concedes every empirical result but insists that the inside view remains indispensable in domains where reference classes are unavailable, poorly defined, or contested, and that in such domains the insistence on an outside view amounts to pretending to have base rates when you do not. The contextualists also raise a reflexive worry: if the strong outside-view position is itself a claim about how we ought to reason, what is the reference class that licenses it, and how was that reference class constructed without inside-view judgments about which cases are relevantly similar? The strong camp tends to dismiss this as a rhetorical move rather than a genuine objection, pointing to the brute predictive track record as sufficient to establish a default presumption in favor of the outside view.

I have a working hypothesis that the disagreement is not about the facts — both sides accept the same data — but about the norms of epistemic justification themselves. The strong camp appears to treat the outside view as the default rational stance, putting the burden of proof on anyone who wants to use the inside view. The contextualists treat the two views as tools whose appropriateness depends on the epistemic context, with no global default and no burden of proof that runs in one direction only. If that diagnosis is right, the impasse is about what it means to justify a reasoning method, and neither side can argue for its preferred norm without presupposing it. But I am not fully confident. Could the disagreement instead be a hidden empirical one about the actual availability and quality of reference classes across different domains of reasoning? Or is it a disagreement about the scope of the evidence — about whether the forecasting-tournament results really generalize to reasoning in general, or only to the specific kinds of judgment under uncertainty that those tournaments test? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate forward? Is there a distinction, an experiment, or a formal result that could resolve it, or is this the kind of disagreement about epistemic norms that just has to be lived with?
