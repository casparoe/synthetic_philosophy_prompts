I run a monthly discussion group at my local library for adults who like thinking about science and philosophy but mostly haven't studied either formally. Lately we've been stuck on a single question: why does time have a direction? We can all list the evidence — coffee and milk mix but never unmix, ice melts in a warm room, memories reach into the past but not the future, we age — yet I've gathered from popular science that the fundamental laws of motion don't actually distinguish past from future. The usual story is that the arrow comes from the universe having started in an extremely orderly state, and from the way disorder tends to increase thereafter. I can repeat that sentence, but it doesn't land, and I don't want to spend the evening lecturing.

I once tried to make this vivid by having everyone flip coins and tally heads and tails, expecting them to see some kind of trend, but it just felt like random arithmetic and nobody connected it to time. I'd like a properly designed 75-minute activity for around 14 people, no formal physics background, that makes them run into the arrow of time themselves. The game or simulation should use simple materials — coins, dice, checkers, cards, beads, graph paper — and its underlying rules should not care which way time runs, so that the one-way behavior emerges entirely from the statistics of many pieces and from how we set things up. Include the exact setup, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened in the activity onto the idea that the arrow is statistical rather than built into the laws. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be candid about which part of the real story the activity can't reproduce — for instance, the special initial condition of the universe — and will have to be supplied by me afterwards.
