I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in axiology and practical rationality, and I have just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether "better than" has to be transitive. The argument kept sliding between different claims, and I want my final exam to force students to be more precise than we were.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in axiology, formal value theory, and decision theory. Please think carefully before writing. Begin by brainstorming the distinct positions a strong student might take, and reject any answer that could be produced by merely reciting a summary of spectrum arguments.

Write an exam question on spectrum arguments and the transitivity of "better than" (and the associated relation "not worse than"). The question should be designed for a 75-minute final exam in an upper-level undergraduate seminar. It must be impossible to answer well by summarizing the topic: it should require students to make the transitivity assumption explicit, reconstruct at least two competing responses, confront an objection to their preferred response, and defend a verdict.

Then provide:

1. The exam question, worded as instructions to the student.
2. A model answer of about 700 words. The model answer should (a) lay out a concrete spectrum with adjacent steps that are each intuitively acceptable and endpoints that intuitively differ in value, (b) state the transitivity principle that the argument relies on, (c) develop the equivocation response and the nontransitivity response as live alternatives, (d) address the objection that a nontransitive "better than" relation undermines rational choice or exposes an agent to money pumps, and (e) reach and defend a definite conclusion.
3. A marking scheme worth 25 points. It must specify the moves a top answer must contain, the points for each move, and the difference between an A and a B answer. In particular, it should penalize students who confuse nontransitivity with incomparability or incommensurability, who treat the adjacent-step judgments and the endpoint judgment as obviously using the same standard, or who merely summarize the arguments without taking and defending a position.

Do not ask the student for any bibliography, citations, or reading lists. The exam question should be self-contained: either define its technical terms or make defining them part of the task.
