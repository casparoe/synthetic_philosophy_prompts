I've been working through Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* alongside some introductory material on Buddhist philosophy, and I'm trying to force my intuitions about personal identity to actually earn their keep — rather than just coasting on whichever thought experiment I read most recently. I've written down my honest, pre-theoretic verdicts on a set of standard cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern. Here they are:

1. **Teletransportation (destructive).** I step into a scanner that records the exact state of every particle in my body, destroys me, and transmits the blueprint to a receiver on Mars, where a qualitatively identical copy is assembled. I think I survive this. The person who steps out on Mars is me — not a replica with my memories, but me.

2. **Forking (non-destructive).** Same setup, except the machine malfunctions and fails to destroy the original. Now there's me on Earth *and* a copy on Mars, both psychologically continuous with the pre-scan person. I think I'm the one on Earth, and the copy is a new person who merely happens to be psychologically identical to me at the moment of branching. The copy is not me.

3. **Gradual neural replacement.** Over the course of a year, each of my neurons is replaced one at a time with a silicon functional equivalent. I remain conscious throughout, and the replacements are gradual enough that my stream of thought is never interrupted. I think I survive the whole process. There is no moment where I wink out and an impostor takes over.

4. **Split-brain (callosotomy).** After the corpus callosum is severed, the two hemispheres begin operating with a surprising degree of independence — each seems to have its own perceptual field, its own volitional chain, and in some experimental setups its own beliefs. I think there are now two subjects of experience where there was one, and neither is straightforwardly identical with the pre-surgery me, though both are psychologically continuous with me.

5. **Hemispheric fission.** My brain is divided and each hemisphere is transplanted into a new body; both resulting persons are fully psychologically continuous with me. I don't think I die, but I also don't think either resulting person is me. I'm tempted to say that the concept of numerical identity simply breaks down in this case, and that asking "which one is me?" is like asking whether the amoeba is the same amoeba after it divides.

6. **Dreamless sleep.** I fall asleep, lose consciousness entirely for several hours, and wake up. Obviously I survive. I mention this only because it seems to function as a constraint: whatever principle underwrites the other verdicts had better not entail that I die every night and a new person wakes up in my bed.

The tension I keep circling is between (1) and (2). If psychological continuity is what makes the Mars copy me in the destructive case, then why isn't the Mars copy also me in the forking case? The only difference is whether the original body is destroyed, and that seems like it should be irrelevant to whether the copy is me — it should only matter to whether there are now *two* of me. I can feel the pull of the view that identity is a matter of psychological continuity alone, and that in the forking case I survive as both (or that "me" splits, or that identity simply ceases to be a one-to-one relation), but I can't bring myself to endorse it. Meanwhile, (3) pushes toward a psychological-continuity criterion, but (4) and (5) push against psychological continuity being *sufficient* for identity, since both hemispheres are psychologically continuous with the original and yet neither seems to be me.

Is there a single principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures all or most of these verdicts? If some verdicts have to go, which should I overturn, and why? What does the resulting view predict about cases I haven't listed, such as destructive uploading, or waking up after a century of cryonic suspension? And as a further question: does the principle that best fits my verdicts end up being compatible with the Buddhist doctrine of anatta — the claim that there is no permanent, substantial self — or does it commit me to a kind of self that the Buddhist arguments would challenge?
