I teach an upper-level philosophy of psychiatry seminar, and I've noticed something frustrating year after year. Students can all tell me that Ian Hacking's looping effects are about how classifications "interact with the people classified" and that multiple personality disorder is the classic example. But when I push them even a little — like asking what distinguishes a looping effect from a boring self-fulfilling prophecy, or whether the concept implies psychiatric categories are somehow less real — they freeze up. The machinery isn't there. So I want to write a final essay question that makes them do the work of tracing the mechanism rather than just naming it.

My idea: I'd give them a short, invented scenario about a new diagnostic category (something plausible but made up, so they can't just Google it) where the classification seems to loop in Hacking's sense, and ask them to walk through exactly what's looping and why. The question should also force them to engage with a specific objection — something like: if psychiatric kinds are interactive in this way, doesn't that mean they're socially constructed in a sense that undermines their medical reality? And does Hacking's own view actually support that conclusion, or does he want to resist it? Ideally the question is structured enough that a student who only remembers the slogan can't bluff through it, but open enough that a strong student has room to show real understanding.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 points. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B from a C answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each grade band must contain. Also flag one or two common student errors that cost marks even in otherwise solid answers. For the format: at the very end, give a one-sentence summary of the question as the last line.
