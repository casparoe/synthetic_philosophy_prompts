Im teaching a module on work and technology and want to use Chaplin's Modern Times as one of the texts. Ive always read the film as a straight-up Luddite attack on machines -- the assembly line, the feeding machine, the Tramp literally getting caught in the gears -- but I keep thinking about Marx's point that the machine is not the enemy; its specifically the capitalist use of the machine that turns a labor-saving device into something that lengthens and intensifies the working day and turns the worker into a living attachment to the machine. Im trying to work out how far that distinction can be pushed through the film. Where does Modern Times actually dramatize the Marxian story (machinery as capital's instrument, the worker deskilled and dispensable, speed-up and unemployment as two sides of one process) and where does the film fall back into a more romantic, anti-machine nostalgia that Marx would have diagnosed rather than endorsed? The ending with the Tramp and the gamin walking down the open road has always struck me as weirdly utopian and kind of un-Marxian, but maybe Im being unfair to it. Can you give me a reading that takes both the film and the theory seriously, and say what the film shows about automation that the theory by itself misses?
