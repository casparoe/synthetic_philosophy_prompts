I'm trying to get my views on moral responsibility into a defensible shape before I submit a dissertation chapter, and I'd like a consistency audit. Below are six commitments I find myself drawn to. I want to know whether they form a jointly tenable set, and if not, exactly where the tensions are, how severe they are, and what the least costly revision would be.

Here are the commitments:

(1) I accept Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. As I understand it, the argument runs: you act as you do because of the way you are — your character, preferences, cognitive dispositions, and so on. To be ultimately morally responsible for your actions, you would have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are. But you cannot be responsible for the way you are, because that would require you to have intentionally chosen your own character, which would in turn require a prior character in light of which to choose it, and so on. The regress is inescapable. Ultimate moral responsibility is therefore impossible.

(2) I am a compatibilist about free will, in roughly the tradition of Frankfurt and Watson. I think there is a genuine and important difference between acting on a desire you wholeheartedly identify with and acting on a desire you are alienated from, and that this difference — structural features of the will, not origination — is what freedom consists in. Nothing about determinism threatens this distinction.

(3) I hold that the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, guilt — are not merely useful social instruments. When someone deliberately and unjustifiably harms me, my resentment is not just a strategic move in a social game; it is a fitting response to the wrong. The character of the act itself makes resentment appropriate, not just advantageous.

(4) I endorse a forward-looking, consequentialist justification for the practice of punishment. We should punish only when doing so produces better outcomes — through deterrence, incapacitation, or rehabilitation — than the available alternatives. Retributivist justifications that appeal to desert alone, without any forward-looking rationale, are insufficient.

(5) I hold that the concept of basic desert — the idea that someone can deserve to suffer, or to be the target of blame, in a sense not reducible to forward-looking considerations like deterrence or moral education — is ultimately unintelligible. I do not merely think it fails to refer; I think the notion itself cannot be made coherent.

(6) I believe that a just criminal justice system must impose punishments that are roughly proportional to the seriousness of the offense. A system that punished shoplifting more harshly than aggravated assault would be unjust, and not merely inefficient or counterproductive.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in tension, which only appear to be in tension, and how severe each genuine tension is. I am especially interested in whether (3) and (6) can be sustained without tacitly relying on the very idea of basic desert that (5) rejects. If there are tensions I have not noticed, I want to know about those too. Finally, tell me what the least costly single revision would be and what it would cost.
