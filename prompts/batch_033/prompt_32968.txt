I run a small landscaping business and buy and sell used compact equipment — skid steers, mini excavators, that kind of thing — maybe four or five machines a year, each one eight to thirty grand, so a bad buy hurts for a whole season. I've been reading about markets on my own, and the idea that stuck with me is the no-trade stuff: if a seller is willing to sell me a machine at a price I think is good, their willingness to sell at that price is itself information, and I should treat it as evidence about the machine, not just background noise. I get the logic in the abstract but I don't know how to run it on a Tuesday morning when a contractor texts me about a Bobcat he's letting go cheap.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for whether to go through with a used-equipment purchase, grounded in that no-trade idea and whatever else about how markets do and don't aggregate information actually bears on it. It should be something I can run in about an hour, without an economist, and my wife signs off on anything over ten thousand so the reasoning has to be legible to her too. Don't spend the whole thing re-explaining the no-trade theorem, I've read enough of that; I need the checklist, with the hard cases. Include: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficent answer to each, where the procedure says walk away, and where it stops being a fact question and becomes a judgment call about risk or need. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a contractor selling a 2018 skid steer because he says he's downsizing, (2) a machine at an auction where I can't talk to the seller at all, and (3) a mechanic friend offering me his personal truck at what looks like a great price. At least one of the worked examples should give me an answer I won't like, and I want you to be straight about where this procedure would make me pass up good deals or where it just can't settle the question.
