At a conference on modal epistemology I was covering last month, I found myself seated at dinner between two philosophers who spent most of the meal arguing about whether conceivability is any kind of guide to possibility. I took notes, and I'd like someone to go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move.

The first, Callan, opened with what he called the master argument: if you can conceive of a scenario in full detail without uncovering any contradiction, that is at least defeasible evidence that the scenario is genuinely possible. He said this principle underwrites most of our modal knowledge — we know unicorns are possible because we can imagine them without contradiction, and we know round squares are impossible because we cannot. Without something like it, he said, we would have no access to modal facts at all.

The second, Naomi, replied that the principle has been dead since Kripke. We can conceive of water that isn't H2O, she said, or of Hesperus turning out not to be Phosphorus, or of gold having atomic number 78 instead of 79. In each case we seem to conceive a scenario without contradiction, but the scenario is metaphysically impossible. So conceivability is not even a defeasible guide; it is a guide to our ignorance, not to modal reality.

Callan answered that these are not cases of genuine conceivability. When someone claims to conceive of water that isn't H2O, they are actually conceiving of a different substance — a watery stuff with a different chemical composition — and mistaking it for water. Genuine conceivability requires conceiving of the thing itself under a semantically transparent mode of presentation, not under a contingent description. He said Naomi's counterexamples only work by trading on an ambiguity about what is being conceived.

Naomi said this reply makes the principle useless. If we can only know we have achieved genuine conceivability once we already know all the relevant essential facts about the things we are conceiving of, then conceivability is not doing any independent epistemic work. It is just recapitulating what we already know. She added that the history of science is littered with things that were genuinely conceived by the best minds of their time and turned out to be impossible — Maxwell's demon, for instance, or a deterministic hidden-variable completion of quantum mechanics.

Callan said that is precisely why he called it defeasible evidence, not a guarantee. He said Naomi was holding conceivability to a standard no other source of evidence could meet. Perception is defeasible — we see mirages — but we do not conclude that perception is no guide to reality. He said the interesting question is whether conceivability provides any rational push at all, not whether it is infallible.

Naomi replied that the analogy fails because perception has independent calibration — we can check whether we are seeing a mirage by walking over to it. With conceivability, there is no independent access to modal facts against which to calibrate our conceivings. The whole enterprise is circular: we decide what is possible by conceiving it, and we decide whether our conceiving was genuine by checking whether it matches what we already think is possible.

At this point a third person at the table — I will call him Theo — jumped in and said both of them were missing the real issue. He said the question is not whether conceivability is a perfect guide or no guide at all, but what kind of possibility it tracks. He suggested that conceivability is a guide to conceptual or epistemic possibility, not metaphysical possibility, and that both Callan and Naomi were running these together. Callan said that just relabels the problem: if conceptual possibility does not constrain metaphysical possibility, we are back to having no access to the latter. Naomi said Theo's move vindicated her side, because it concedes that conceivability does not reach the kind of possibility the original principle was supposed to illuminate.

I would like someone to go through this exchange and adjudicate each move. Who scores points where? Does Callan's reply to the Kripke cases work? Is Naomi right that the principle collapses into uselessness, or does Callan's defeasibility reply handle that? And is Theo's intervention a genuine resolution, or does it merely reframe a problem neither side had solved? I am less interested in who wins overall than in which specific arguments land and which do not.
