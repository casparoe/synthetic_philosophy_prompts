I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether there's a moral difference between lying and merely misleading someone, and I came away with my intuitions more tangled than when we started. I'm hoping you can help me sort them out. Here are the cases and how I react to each; I'd like to know if there's a principle that captures most or all of these verdicts, and if not, which ones I should reconsider.

Case 1: A would-be murderer asks where your friend is hiding. You say "He's not here; he left an hour ago." That's a lie. I think it's clearly fine.

Case 2: Same situation, but instead you say "I saw him heading toward the train station about an hour ago," which happens to be true — he did head that way before doubling back and hiding here. This is misleading rather than a lie. I also think it's fine, but oddly I feel slightly worse about it than about the lie, because it feels more deliberately crafted.

Case 3: You're selling your car and a buyer asks if it burns oil. You say "I haven't had to top it up between oil changes," which is strictly true, but you know the previous owner had to add oil constantly — you just haven't driven it long enough to see the problem yourself. I think this is wrong, and I think it's about as wrong as it would be if you'd lied and said "no, it doesn't burn oil at all."

Case 4: A colleague asks whether you think their presentation went well. It was mediocre. Saying "it was great" feels like a clear wrong. Saying "you're clearly passionate about the topic and the slides looked professional" feels less wrong — even though both are meant to spare their feelings and neither gives them the honest feedback they asked for. Here the misleading seems better than the lie.

Case 5: A patient with a serious illness asks their doctor point-blank: "Am I going to die from this?" The doctor believes the patient likely has six months. The doctor says "we're going to do everything we can." This is misleading — it exploits the patient's hope to avoid the question. To my own surprise, I find this worse than if the doctor had just said "you have a good chance," even though that would be a lie. The misleading feels like a deeper violation of the patient's trust in their physician.

Case 6: A spouse having an affair is asked "where were you tonight?" Saying "at the office" is a lie — and a serious betrayal. Saying "I was downtown — I needed some time alone" is true in a sense, though it hides the affair. I can't settle whether the misleading version is better, worse, or the same. It avoids the outright falsehood but it uses a partial truth as camouflage, which feels corrupt in its own way.

Case 7: A politician is asked whether their policy will raise taxes on the middle class. They know it will. They say "we've been clear that the wealthiest will pay their fair share," which is true but irrelevant to the question. If they had instead said "no, it won't raise taxes on the middle class," that would be a lie and I'd condemn it. But I find the misleading answer more infuriating, because it treats the public as people who don't deserve a straight answer.

What I'm after is a principle that distinguishes when misleading is worse than lying, when lying is worse than misleading, and when they're morally on a par — or else an argument that I should collapse some of these distinctions. My friend kept pushing me toward the view that the difference is purely pragmatic: lying is easier to catch and therefore riskier, so we've trained ourselves to feel worse about it, but the underlying wrong is the same deception either way. That doesn't capture why the doctor's misleading feels worse than a lie, or why the politician's evasion does. Is there a principled account here, and which of my verdicts can it hold onto and which ones does it force me to give up?
