I cover philosophy for a general-interest magazine and am working on a piece about a dispute that seems to have been running in place for more than half a century: whether there are genuine moral dilemmas. By genuine I mean cases in which an agent has all-things-considered obligations to do each of two incompatible things, so that whatever she does she acts wrongly—not merely a hard choice between a clearly right option and a tempting wrong one, or a conflict between a real duty and a merely apparent one.

The anti-dilemma side, which tends to come out of formal deontic logic, argues that the idea is incoherent. If an agent ought to do A and ought to do B, then she ought to do A and B together; but if A and B cannot both be done, she would be obligated to perform an impossible conjunctive act, contrary to the principle that no one can be obligated to do the impossible. On this view, apparent dilemmas are conflicts among pro tanto considerations, exactly one of which wins, and the residual unease people report is distress or sadness rather than guilt for an unmet obligation.

The pro-dilemma side replies that this explains away the phenomenon most in need of explanation. In forced choices between grave commitments—two promises that cannot both be kept, or a choice between saving one person and saving another—the retrospective response is not mere grief but something with the structure of remorse, as if the agent really did fail to do something she ought to have done. They add that the formal argument is only as strong as its principles: one can reject the agglomeration of obligations, or qualify the principle that ought implies can, so no contradiction is forced.

Both sides have known the other's moves for years, and from the literature and from interviewing philosophers, neither has shifted. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not about any moral fact but about the evidential standing of a feeling: the anti-dilemma side treats retrospective moral emotions as data to be reinterpreted or explained away, while the pro-dilemma side treats them as among the primary data a theory of obligation must answer to. Assess that hypothesis, and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is—substantive, verbal, or two different questions (one about the formal logic of 'ought,' one about the moral psychology of regret) running under a single heading. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding about how robustly people distinguish remorse from distress, a change of question—or a reason to expect none.
