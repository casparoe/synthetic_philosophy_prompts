I'm a software engineer on a platform team at a mid-size company. Our team uses a structured process for making architectural decisions: we identify the sub-questions a decision depends on, each team member records their judgment on each sub-question, and we aggregate by majority vote. The final decision is supposed to follow logically from the aggregated sub-judgments.

We've started noticing that this sometimes produces results nobody on the team actually endorses. A recent example: we were deciding whether to adopt a new database. We agreed the decision rule was: adopt if (the performance gain is significant) AND (the migration cost is acceptable) AND (at least one of: it simplifies our stack OR it improves our hiring story). We voted. Majority said the performance gain was significant. Majority said the migration cost was acceptable. Majority said it would NOT simplify our stack. Majority said it would NOT improve our hiring story. So the aggregated premises say: significant gain, acceptable cost, but neither simplifying nor hiring-improving — which implies we should NOT adopt. But when we separately voted on the overall decision, the majority said we SHOULD adopt. The aggregated premises and the aggregated conclusion contradicted each other, and we didn't notice until someone pointed it out afterward.

I've since learned that this is a known problem called the discursive dilemma or judgment aggregation paradox. But everything I've found about it is academic — impossibility theorems, formal frameworks, comparisons of aggregation methods. I need something I can actually use.

Please write me a decision procedure my team can follow that avoids this problem. It should be a step-by-step process — a checklist or decision tree in words — that we can run during a one-hour meeting with five to eight engineers. The procedure should tell us:

- When we should aggregate judgments on sub-questions and when we should instead vote directly on the overall decision.
- If we do aggregate on sub-questions, how to structure the vote so the result stays consistent (or how to detect and resolve inconsistency when it arises).
- What to do when team members disagree about the logical structure of the decision itself — that is, what the sub-questions are and how they combine.
- Where the procedure has to hand off to human judgment because there is no mechanical fix.

Then apply the procedure to two worked examples:

1. Our database-adoption case above, showing how the procedure would have handled it and what decision it would have produced.
2. A different case: the team is deciding whether to deprecate an internal service. The decision rule is: deprecate if (all client teams have migrated off it OR the maintenance burden is unsustainable) AND (there is a documented replacement path for every use case). Show how the procedure handles this one, and include a scenario where the procedure gives an answer the team will not like.

I want a committed response — one that picks a specific procedure and defends its choices, rather than surveying the options. I'm aware there are impossibility results in this area; tell me where my procedure will break and what kinds of cases it cannot handle, but do not let that stop you from giving me something usable for the cases it can.
