A couple of months ago I was at a dinner where two friends, Leo and Sara, got into an argument I've been turning over in my head ever since. It started when someone mentioned a news story about a city installing benches with armrests running down the middle, supposedly to keep homeless people from sleeping on them. Leo shrugged and said people were reading politics into a piece of furniture. Sara said that was exactly backwards: the politics was built into the bench. Here is roughly how the exchange went, as best as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Leo: A bench is made of wood and metal. It doesn't have a political party. Saying a bench is political is a category mistake — you're confusing the object itself with how people choose to use it.

(2) Sara: You're confusing "political" with "partisan." The bench was designed with a specific purpose: to allow sitting but not lying down. That design choice reflects a decision about who public space is for and who it excludes. The politics is in the design, not in some after-the-fact use.

(3) Leo: That's still just the designer's intention, not the bench itself. If I use a hammer to commit a crime, the hammer isn't criminal. The bench doesn't have politics — the people who commissioned it do.

(4) Sara: That analogy doesn't hold. A hammer is indifferent to how it's used — it can pound nails or break windows. But a bench with a center armrest is not indifferent. It's designed to block a particular use. The exclusion is built into the physical form of the object. You can't separate the artifact from the politics.

(5) Leo: Okay, but that's a special case — a bench designed to deter a specific behavior. Most technologies are general-purpose. A power grid, a smartphone, a highway — these don't have politics built into them.

(6) Sara: I think many of them do. Take a highway overpass that's too low for buses. Whether or not it was designed that way on purpose, the physical fact of the overpass means that people who rely on buses can't use that route. That's a political effect that lives in the concrete and steel, not just in anyone's head.

(7) Leo: That's just a constraint of the physical world, not politics. A mountain pass also blocks buses. The mountain isn't political.

(8) Sara: But a mountain wasn't built by humans making choices. An overpass was. At some point someone decided — or failed to decide — what the thing would allow and disallow. Those choices encode values, and the artifact carries those values forward whether or not anyone remembers them.

(9) Leo: I'll grant that artifacts can reflect the values of their designers. But that's a descriptive claim about the designers, not the artifact. The artifact just has features. The politics is in the interpretation.

(10) Sara: I think you're drawing a line between "features" and "politics" that doesn't hold up. If a feature systematically advantages some people over others, and that feature is a permanent part of the artifact, then the artifact has a political valence. You can't talk about the artifact without talking about that valence.

(11) Leo: But systematic advantage isn't inherent to the artifact — it depends on the social context. A staircase advantages people who can walk, but that's only an advantage relative to a society where staircases are the norm. The staircase didn't create that society.

(12) Sara: That's fair, but the staircase also reinforces that society. And when you build a new staircase instead of a ramp, you're making a choice that perpetuates the exclusion. The artifact and the social context are in a loop — you can't cleanly separate them.
