So I've been covering philosophy of mind and epistemology for a few years now and there's this one debate that I keep coming back to because it seems totally stuck: the reductionism vs. anti-reductionism fight about testimony. Quick refresher: reductionists (the Humean line) say that believing what someone tells you is only justified if you've got independant reasons to think they're reliable — you're essentially running an inductive inference based on their track record, or on general facts about the kind of claim they're making. Anti-reductionists (the Reidian line) say no, testimony is a basic source of justification, on par with perception and memory. You're entitled to believe what people tell you by default, and you only need reasons when something specific raises a red flag — same way you don't need reasons to trust your eyes unless the lighting is weird or you've been drinking.

This debate has been going in its modern form for decades. Both sides have their stock examples, both sides have their stock replies to the other side's stock examples, and honestly it feels like watching two armies that have dug in so deep that nobody even expects to take new ground anymore.

What I want is a diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute — are they just using "justification" to mean different things? Is it two different projects that got confused — one about the descriptive psychology of how we actually form testimonial beliefs, one about the normative epistemology of when we *ought* to? Is there a hidden empirical question about how human beings actually process testimony that, if answered, would settle the thing, and neither camp has bothered to go look? Or is it a clash of bedrock intuitions where no argument is gonna move anyone because the intuitions are what the arguments are built on top of in the first place?

And then the practical question: given whatever diagnosis you land on, is there anything that would actually move this debate forward? A distinction people haven't drawn clearly enough? An empirical result that would matter? Or should we just accept that this is one of those philosophy problems where reasonable people start from different places and no amount of argument is going to converge them?
