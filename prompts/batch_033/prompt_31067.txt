I'm a software engineer, and I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about mind uploading. The scenario that's been keeping me up: suppose we develop a procedure that replaces individual neurons one at a time with functionally identical artificial neurons — same input-output mapping, same connection patterns, same timing properties. After each replacement, you're awake and can report whether you still feel like yourself. At the end, every neuron has been swapped out, and the biological brain is gone. The question: at the end of this process, is the person who wakes up still you?

I've seen people take extremely confident positions on both sides — some say obviously yes (it's just a hardware swap, like replacing a failing hard drive sector by sector while the OS keeps running), others say obviously no (you'd be dead and a copy would be walking around). I want to get past the intuitions and actually put a number on it. I know this isn't the kind of thing you can settle by running an experiment, but I think you can still assign a credence and show your work — the way you'd estimate the probability that a complex distributed system will fail under a novel load pattern when you can't just spin up a test cluster.

Please give me your credence that personal identity is preserved through a complete gradual-replacement procedure as described above — that is, the person at the end is numerically identical to the person who started, not merely a copy or successor. I don't want a survey of what philosophers have said; I want your actual probability estimate and the reasoning behind it, decomposed into the sub-questions your credence depends on.

Structure your response in these sections:

2. **Decomposition** (roughly 400–600 words): Identify the main sub-questions your credence hinges on — for example, which theory of personal identity is correct (psychological continuity, biological continuity, narrative, something else), whether gradual functional replacement preserves the relation picked out by that theory, whether there's a hidden discontinuity somewhere in the process, whether "feeling like yourself" reports during the procedure are reliable evidence, and any other factors you think matter. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question and explain in one or two sentences why you chose that number.

4. **Sensitivity analysis** (roughly 150–250 words): Which single sub-question is your final credence most sensitive to — that is, if you changed your mind about that one factor, your overall number would move the most?
