I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I've been banging my head against the higher-order evidence literature for weeks now. I figured I'd step back from the papers and just write down my raw verdicts on cases, then see if there's a principle that actually fits them. The problem is that when I try to extract a principle, it either ends up being a gerrymandered mess or it forces me to give up a verdict I'm not willing to give up.

Here are the cases and what I think about them:

1. I've just finished a tricky proof and I'm pretty confident it's sound. Then I'm reliably informed that I was given a drug that subtly impairs deductive reasoning, without my noticing any impairment. I haven't gone back to check the proof. I should now be much less confident. This one feels clear.

2. A colleague and I have shared all our evidence about some philosophical question. We're equally competent, equally well-rested, we've both read the same papers. I conclude P, she concludes not-P. I should reduce my confidence. I know some people resist this, but to me it's obvious.

3. I read a solid study showing that people with my educational background are systematically overconfident about economic questions. I hold a belief about the effects of minimum wage laws. I should reduce my confidence a bit, but nowhere near as much as in the peer disagreement case. The evidence is statistical and I don't know whether I'm one of the overconfident ones.

4. I've been reasoning about a hard problem while severely sleep-deprived. I reach a conclusion and feel pretty confident in it. But I know from experience that sleep deprivation makes me error-prone in ways I can't detect from the inside. I think I should reduce my confidence, but here's the thing — it feels like the right response is to re-check the reasoning when I'm rested, not to revise my belief *right now*. I'm not sure what to make of that.

5. I believe P, and I also believe, on the basis of a reliable test I trust, that my total evidence actually supports not-P. That's just irrational. I can't stably hold both those attitudes. Something has to give, and the answer isn't "just keep both."

6. This is the one that's tying me in knots. I've reasoned carefully about a question and formed a belief. Then I get strong evidence that my reasoning was subtly biased — I learn that the order in which I encountered the arguments is known to skew judgment in this particular domain. I can't find the bias in my own reasoning, but I trust the evidence about the bias. So now I believe P, and I also believe that the process that produced my belief in P was unreliable. Is this epistemically akratic? My gut says it's not the same as case 5, because in case 5 I believe my evidence *supports not-P*, whereas here I just believe my *reasoning was unreliable*. But when I try to make that distinction precise, it starts to collapse — if I've taken the higher-order evidence into account, doesn't "my reasoning was unreliable" amount to "my evidence doesn't support P as strongly as I think it does," which is halfway to case 5?

So I guess my question is: is there a principle that captures 1-4 and 5, and then tells me what to say about 6? I'm worried that if the principle is something like "always defer to your higher-order evidence," then case 5 starts looking rational after all — the akratic subject has higher-order evidence (from the test) that their total evidence supports not-P, and deferring to that would mean abandoning P, at which point they're not akratic anymore, they're just mid-update. And if the principle is "never be epistemically akratic, full stop," then case 6 might force me to either ignore the bias evidence or abandon my belief entirely — and I don't want to do either. Is there a way to split the difference, or do I have to go back on one of my verdicts?
