I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and I am drafting the final exam. I want one essay question on functionalism and multiple realizability that cannot be answered adequately by reciting the standard story — that functionalism defines mental states by their causal roles, so they can be realized in different physical substrates, and this blocks type-identity theory and reductionism. I want students to confront a specific tension: the claim that mental states are multiply realizable is often presented as a pre-theoretic discovery that motivates functionalism, yet once functionalism is adopted, multiple realizability seems to follow almost trivially from the theory's definitions. If this is right, then multiple realizability cannot do the argumentative work it was originally recruited to do — namely, serving as independent evidence against the type-identity theory. The question should ask students to evaluate whether this circularity is real and, if so, whether it is vicious or benign, and what the consequences are for functionalism's anti-reductionist ambitions.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. The marking scheme should also flag one or two characteristic mistakes that weaker answers tend to make, with a brief explanation of why each is a mistake.
