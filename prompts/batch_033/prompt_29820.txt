I've been reading about costly signaling theory — Zahavi's handicap principle, Grafen's formal models, the whole thing about how signals have to be costly to be honest. And I keep coming back to the potlatch ceremonies of the Pacific Northwest, where chiefs would give away or destroy huge amounts of blankets, canoes, and copper shields to gain status. The Canadian government eventually banned the practice in the late 19th century. I know the standard story that its a classic example of costly signaling, but I want to go deeper than that.

Here's what I want: a careful mapping of the potlatch onto signaling theory. What exactly was the signal — generosity, wealth, something about fitness to lead? Who was the audience, and what were they infering? What made the signal hard to fake — was it just the sheer expense, or was there something about the social structure that meant only genuinely wealthy and competent chiefs could pull it off?

Then I want you to push on the theory. Specifically: is the potlatch better understood as a handicap (where the cost is the very thing that guarantees honesty) or as an index (where the signal is unfakeable for structural reasons and the cost is incidental)? The distinction matters because if its an index, then the cost isn't doing the theoretical work Zahavi thought it was.

Also: potlatches weren't one-off displays. They were part of an iterated system with reciprical obligations — if I give you a potlatch, you owe me one, and it has to be bigger. Does costly signaling theory handle iterated, escalating signals well? Most of the classic models (Grafen, Johnstone) seem to focus on one-shot or repeated-but-stationary interactions. What breaks or changes when the signal has to keep getting more expensive?

And the ban: if the potlatch was a stable signaling equilibrium, what does the theory predict when an outside power suppresses it? Do we get a different signal evolving to do the same work, or does the whole system collapse? What actually happened after the ban might tell us something about whether the theory's equilibrium concept is too brittle.

Finally: Zahavi developed this idea watching babblers in the Negev. If the potlatch fits the theory, does that mean the theory is genuinely general — it explains signaling in birds and humans with the same machinery — or are we doing the thing where we take a biological theory and stretch it metaphorically over human culture? I want an honest answer about whether this case is a win for the theory's scope or whether it reveals that we're using "costly signaling" in a looser, more analogical way than Zahavi intended.
