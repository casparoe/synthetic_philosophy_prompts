I retired from a career in experimental physics a few years ago and have since been attending a philosophy reading group at my local university. At last week's meeting, two members — both considerably more advanced in philosophy than I am — got into a dispute that I've been turning over in my mind ever since. I took notes on the exchange and would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

The topic was peer disagreement: what you should do when someone you regard as your intellectual equal holds a different view. Marcus opened by saying he'd recently revised his position on a philosophical question after discovering that a colleague he respects disagreed with him. He said this seemed obviously right: if you think someone is as smart and well-informed as you are, and they've considered the same evidence, then their reaching a different conclusion is itself a reason to doubt your own conclusion.

Elena pushed back. She said that respecting someone's general competence doesn't require deferring to them on every particular judgment. When you've thought through an issue carefully, you have direct access to your own reasoning — the steps, the considerations, the weight you gave to each factor — in a way you simply don't have for theirs. So it can be perfectly reasonable to think: they're my equal, but on this occasion they've made a mistake, just as they presumably think about me.

Marcus replied that this move is available to both parties symmetrically, which is precisely the problem. Each person thinks the other made a mistake. Each has equal credentials. So from a neutral standpoint, the situation is symmetric — and if you can't identify any specific flaw in the other's reasoning beyond the bare fact that they reached a different conclusion, then sticking with your own view amounts to an unjustified privileging of your own perspective.

Elena answered that the symmetry argument, if taken seriously, would require us to suspend judgment on virtually every contested issue in philosophy, politics, and elsewhere — since for any non-trivial question, there exists some equally competent person who disagrees. She said this is an unacceptable result, and that any principle with such sweeping skeptical consequences must itself be flawed.

I see force in both positions and cannot resolve the matter myself. Please go through their exchange move by move, assessing the strength of each claim. Then offer an overall verdict: who has the better case, and is there a way to do justice to the insights on both sides?
