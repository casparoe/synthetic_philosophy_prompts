The student wrote:

"Self-deception is puzzling because it seems to combine two roles that normally can't be combined. When one person deceives another, the deceiver must believe or know that p, and must deliberately get the other person to believe not-p. If the deceiver and the deceived are the same person, then the self-deceiver would have to both know that p and get herself to believe not-p, which looks impossible. That is the paradox.

Davidson responds by dividing the mind. On his view, the mind contains semi-autonomous subsystems separated by boundaries, so one subsystem can hold the true belief that p while another holds the false belief that not-p without the two clashing. He is careful to say these subsystems are really two distinct agents in one skull, each with its own beliefs and intentions, which is what makes the deception genuine rather than mere metaphor.

Mele's deflationary account takes a different route. He says self-deception does not require an intention to deceive oneself; it is enough that the person's false belief is produced by motivationally biased treatment of the evidence. For Mele, what separates self-deception from ordinary error is that the person must be aware, at some level, that the evidence she has actually supports the truth, since otherwise the state is just a mistake rather than self-deception.

I find Mele's account more plausible than Davidson's, because it explains how self-deception can happen gradually, without positing a second agent inside the person. The main worry is whether a non-intentional account can still distinguish self-deception from ordinary wishful thinking; Mele would need his extra conditions to do that work, and I'm not sure they succeed."
