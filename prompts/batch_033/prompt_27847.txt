I'm in a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and we've been working through the debate sparked by Taurek's claim that we are not required to save the greater number when we can save only one group. Last week someone pressed the aggregationist side with an analogy I've been turning over ever since. The idea, roughly, is that we have no trouble aggregating harms and benefits within a single life: if I face a choice between enduring one intensely painful surgery now and enduring five moderately painful surgeries spread across the next decade, I rationally aggregate the pains and pick the one surgery. The analogy then says that choosing between saving one person and saving five is structurally the same — you are simply aggregating across the boundaries between lives the way you aggregate across the boundaries between time-slices of one life. The person offering the analogy used it to argue that anyone who accepts intrapersonal aggregation is already committed to interpersonal aggregation, and therefore that numbers do count and Taurek is wrong.

I am not convinced the analogy can carry that conclusion, but I am having trouble articulating exactly where it breaks. I want a careful audit. Lay out what maps onto what in the analogy — what plays the role of a time-slice, what plays the role of the unified self, what the aggregation operation is supposed to be — and then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion. In particular, I want to know whether the separateness of persons is a difference in kind that blocks the inference from intrapersonal to interpersonal aggregation, or merely a difference in degree that the analogy can absorb. And if the analogy fails, tell me whether there is a better analogy that could do the same work for the aggregationist, or whether the failure of this analogy leaves the aggregationist without a good analogical footing. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would in a letter or an essay — no headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold text.
