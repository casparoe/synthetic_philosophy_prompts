During the Saturday Night Massacre of October 1973, Attorney General Elliot Richardson resigned rather than obey President Nixon's order to fire the special prosecutor investigating Watergate. Richardson had promised the Senate during his confirmation hearings that he would protect the special prosecutor's independence. His deputy William Ruckelshaus likewise refused and was dismissed; the order was finally carried out by Robert Bork, who had made no such promise.

Analyze Richardson's resignation through the lens of Bernard Williams's integrity objection and his notion of "one thought too many." A utilitarian might argue that Richardson should have stayed in office: if he resigned, someone else would fire the prosecutor anyway, and by remaining he could constrain Nixon from within and do more good overall. Williams's critique suggests that this very calculation is alienating — a person of integrity does not need to weigh his deepest commitments against an impartial ledger, and doing so is already to have lost them. Is that what is happening here? And if Richardson's integrity was grounded not in a purely personal project but in a public, institutional commitment — a promise made to the Senate — does the episode expose a tension in Williams's account between integrity as personal authenticity and integrity as fidelity to an office?
