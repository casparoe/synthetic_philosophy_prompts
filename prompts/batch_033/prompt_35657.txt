I recently read a piece on a philosophy blog that summarized Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals like this:

"David Lewis proved that if you think the probability of an ordinary indicative conditional—a sentence like 'if the match is struck, it will light'—is just the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, then you are committed to the absurd conclusion that all probabilities are either zero or one. Lewis showed that no proposition can have its probability systematically equal a conditional probability across a range of probability distributions unless the space of possibilities is trivial. The result is widely taken as a decisive refutation of the Ramsey test for conditionals and a vindication of the material conditional analysis, since it demonstrates that treating conditionals as expressing ordinary propositions with classical truth conditions is incompatible with the way we actually reason about them."

I have a rough sense that this summary pulls off some questionable moves, but I am not sure exactly where. Could you go through it carefully and tell me which parts a philosopher of language specializing in conditionals would accept, which parts they would wince at, and what is just mistaken? In particular, I would like to know whether the result really refutes the Ramsey test, whether it vindicates the material conditional, and whether the final sentence about "incompatible with the way we actually reason" follows from anything Lewis actually established.
