Ok so here's what's bothering me. In the US, almost every consumer contract and employment agreement now has a mandatory arbitration clause with a class-action waiver. You can't sue the company, you can't join a class action, you have to go to a private arbitrator — often one the company effectively chooses. The Supreme Court has said this is fine because arbitration is just an alternative forum and people consent to it when they sign the contract. But here's the thing: every company has to include these clauses because if one company didn't, they'd be the only ones facing class actions, which would put them at a massive competitive disadvantage. So the "consent" of the companies is also coerced by market structure — it's a multipolar trap where each firm's individually rational choice creates a world where no one, on either side, has a meaningful alternative. The whole system of consumer and employment dispute resolution has been quietly privatized, and nobody voted on it. What I want to understand is: what theory of consent does the legal system have to be presupposing to look at this situation and say "yep, that's voluntary"? Is there a coherent philsophical position that says consent is valid even when the market has systematically eliminated the alternatives, or is the law just willfully ignoring the trap structure? And if we wanted the legal system to actually recognize multipolar traps as undermining consent, what would have to change — not just in arbitration law but in the underlying theory of contract?
