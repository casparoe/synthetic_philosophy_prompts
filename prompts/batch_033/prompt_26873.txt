I need a thought experiment for a paper on the wrong kind of reasons problem. The standard move in the literature is to say that the WKRP is about desire and admiration, and that for belief it doesnt really arise because you can't believe at will — any 'wrong kind' reason to believe p is really just a reason to want to believe p, or to take steps to bring it about. I want to challenge that.

Construct a case where the WKRP genuinely arises for belief. Specifically: the agent has a reason to believe p that the object-given/state-given distinction classifies as state-given, but the reason is not a reason to bring about the belief (the agent can't do that), and it's not just a reason to wish they believed p. It seems to bear directly on the question 'ought I to believe p?' — not on 'ought I to make myself believe p?'

Constraints: the scenario must be mundane (a medical decision, a workplace judgment, a family matter — no demons, no threats, no Pascal), the belief must be about something empirical and ordinary, and the pragmatic reason must be genuine. No cheating by making the pragmatic reason secretly evidential.

After the scenario, do the following: (a) state clearly what the agent's reasons are, (b) classify each as object-given or state-given, (c) explain why the state-given reason resists the usual 'it's just a reason to bring about the belief' response, (d) identify the structural feature of the case that makes the resistance possible. End with what a defender of the object-given/state-given distinction would say to handle the case, and whether that response generalizes or is ad hoc.
