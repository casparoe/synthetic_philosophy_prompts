so i run a weekly reading group at my company — about 10-15 software engineers, mostly interested in AI safety stuff but none of us have formal philosophy backgrounds. we've been working through some of the alignment literature and we just finished the mesa-optimizers paper and some stuff on goal misgeneralization. people kind of nod along but i don't think they *feel* why it's a problem. the reaction i keep getting is "well just train on more diverse data" or "just check the model's reasoning" or "why would it learn a proxy goal if the real goal also fits the data."

i want to run a 45-minute hands-on activity that makes them viscerally experience the thing: you're given a set of training examples, you confidently infer a simple rule, and then you get tested on out-of-distribution cases and realize you learned the wrong thing. and the point is you couldn't have known from the training data alone — the simpler rule fit perfectly on everything you saw.

ideally they shouldn't know the AI connection until the debrief. i want them to just think they're playing a pattern-guessing game, and then have the "oh shit" moment when we map it back.

can you design the game? i need:

- concrete rules of play (how groups are formed, what materals they get, what they're told to do)
- a specific set of training examples and test examples — enough detail that i can literally print them out and run it. the training examples should be rich enough that participants feel like they've seen a representative sample, and the test examples should include both in-distribution cases (where the simple rule still works) and out-of-distribution cases (where it breaks)
- a timeline for the 45 minutes
- a debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity to inner alignment concepts (mesa-objective vs base objective, training distribution vs deployment, the sharp left turn, etc.)
- what will predictably go wrong when i run this and how to handle it (e.g., someone guesses the real rule from the training data, a group gets frustrated and gives up, the test cases don't feel "fair")
- what feature of the real alignment problem the game fundamentally can't capture and i'll need to just explain afterwards
