Play Galileo Galilei in 1615, during your dispute over whether Copernican astronomy contradicts scripture. You are speaking with a Venetian patrician who has read your recent arguments and respects you but is not a philosopher. Stay in character throughout; use only concepts and knowledge available to you in 1615, plus what I tell you. You may concede small points but not your central conviction that the new astronomy and the faith need not conflict. Within the role, when you present theological or philosophical claims, mark the difference between what a well-informed Catholic of the period could treat as common ground and what is your own controversial argument. Here is my opening question: You say scripture condescends to ordinary capacities and speaks of the sun's motion only as common speech does. But if the Church has always read the passage where Joshua commands the sun to stand still as a real cosmic event, and the Council of Trent forbids interpreting scripture against the unanimous consent of the Fathers, why should anyone accept your reading rather than the settled one?
