I'm in a philosophy of mind grad seminar, and after two weeks on teleosemantics a classmate walked us through an argument that seems to show the whole view collapses into radical content indeterminacy. I can't find the whole. The argument runs:

(1) On teleosemantics, a mental state's content is fixed by what the state was selected to indicate — its proper function.
(2) Selection is just a causal process in an actual ancestral environment; it can only register what actually happened there.
(3) But any actual selective history can be redescribed at many levels. Take the frog's snap reflex: the mechanism that makes the frog snap was selected because snapping at flies helped frogs eat, because snapping at small dark moving things helped them eat, because snapping at frog-food helped them eat, and because snapping at certain patterns of retinal stimulation helped them eat. All those descriptions are true of the same history.
(4) So selection history cannot mark one of those descriptions as the content; it no more fixes fly than small-dark-moving-thing or frog-food or retinal-pattern.
(5) So on teleosemantics, the frog's snap-indicator means all of those things at once, or none of them determinately.
(6) But that can't be right. Determinate content is what makes error possible: when the frog snaps at a BB, it's wrong if the content is fly but not if the content is small dark moving thing. If content is indeterminate across those, we can't say the frog ever misrepresents anything.
(7) So either teleosemantics gives us no determinate content, or it's false. Either way, the program that promised to naturalize content can't deliver.

The conclusion feels like it proves too much — it wouldn't just be about frogs; the same redescription trick would make every representation's content indeterminate. I'd rather keep teleosemantics and keep the idea that a frog can misrepresent a BB as a fly. But I can't tell which premise to reject, or whether rejecting it costs more than the view can afford. My first instinct is to attack (4) with the selection-for/selection-of distinction, but I'm not sure that distinction is sharp enough to fix the problem rather than just relabel it.

Give me a committed answer: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating the question.
