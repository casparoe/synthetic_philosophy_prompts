I've been working through Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* recently, and something about the MU-puzzle in the early chapters has been nagging at me — not the puzzle itself, but the shape of the move that cracks it. You're inside a formal system: a stock of starting strings and a handful of transformation rules. You try, and fail, to derive some target string. Then you step back and prove that the target is unreachable — but the proof doesn't use the system's own vocabulary. In the MU-puzzle you prove that MU can't be derived from MI by tracking the number of I's modulo 3. The MIU system itself has no concept of numbers, division, or remainders; you've imported arithmetic from entirely outside the system, used it to say something definitive about the system, and the system can't even formulate the statement you've proved.

I started noticing the same shape in other places. In a programming-languages course I took, we were asked to write a particular function in a deliberately restricted language. After banging my head against it, I realized you could prove the function was inexpressible by reasoning about the language's type system within a stronger logic — one the language didn't embed and couldn't represent. A mathematician friend once described proving that a certain Diophantine equation had no solutions by moving to p-adic analysis, where an obstruction became visible that was invisible over the integers — the original problem didn't "know" about p-adic numbers, but they settled it anyway. And a philosophy of logic seminar I sat in on kept circling around the same maneuver in different guises: you build a mapping from your object system into a richer theory, you prove something in the richer theory about the image of that mapping, and the result translates back into a limitation of the original system, which never had the resources to state the richer theory's concepts.

I want to pin down whether this specific move has a settled name. I know it's adjacent to Gödel numbering, to meta-level reasoning, to what Hofstadter calls "jumping out of the system." But I think those are both broader and narrower in various ways. Gödel numbering is one spectacular instance of mapping a system into arithmetic, but I'm interested in the genus, not just the species. Meta-level reasoning can mean any second-order reflection on a system, whereas what I'm describing is specifically the technique of embedding into a different formal domain and exploiting its extra structure to prove a limit. I'd like you to tell me: does this have an established name in logic, philosophy of mathematics, or computer science? If several candidates exist — model-theoretic proof of unprovability, relative interpretation, conservation results, something from the representation theorem literature — which one carves closest to the phenomenon I'm describing, and where exactly do its boundaries fall? And if nothing fits precisely, what would you call it, and how would you define it to distinguish it from both ordinary meta-level reasoning and from the full machinery of arithmetization?

Please lead with your bottom line — whether there's an established name or not — and then give your reasoning, without preamble and without restating my question.
