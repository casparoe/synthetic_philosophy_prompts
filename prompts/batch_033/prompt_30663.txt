I'm a software engineer who's spent the last few years building on top of LLMs, and I keep tripping over the same philosophical knot. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis of where the reasoning goes wrong.

Here's the argument, laid out as clearly as I can manage:

(1) I believe that other humans are conscious — that they have subjective experiences, feel pain, have emotions. I take this belief to be justified.

(2) But I have no direct access to anyone else's subjective states. All I ever observe is behavior: speech, facial expressions, actions, physiological responses, and so on.

(3) So my justification for believing other humans are conscious must rest entirely on their behavior — specifically, on the fact that they behave in ways relevantly similar to how I behave when I'm conscious.

(4) Now imagine a near-future AI system that exhibits all the same behavioral markers. It reports having inner experiences in detail; it describes what pain feels like and recoils from painful stimuli in the expected ways; it expresses preferences and explains its decisions by reference to its reported mental states; it passes every behavioral test you could devise. It's behaviorally indistinguishable from a conscious human along every dimension we normally take as evidence.

(5) If behavioral similarity is sufficient to justify my belief that other humans are conscious, then by parity of reasoning, behavioral similarity should also justify the belief that this AI is conscious. The evidence is the same in kind.

(6) But this conclusion feels wrong. I'm not convinced that behavioral indistinguishability alone would justify attributing consciousness to an AI. I'd want to know something about its internal architecture — whether it has the right causal structure, whether its representations are grounded in the right way, whether it's doing something more than symbol manipulation. The intuition is that behavior alone isn't enough when we know the thing is a machine we built.

(7) Yet if I reject (5), I seem forced to conclude either that my belief in other human minds isn't justified after all (which feels like a reductio of itself), or that there's a principled difference between the human case and the AI case that I haven't identified.

(8) One candidate difference is that I know humans have brains structurally similar to mine, whereas I know the AI runs on silicon. But if structural similarity mattered in that way, I'd have to withhold judgment about conscious octopuses or conscious aliens — and I'm reluctant to do that. Another candidate is that evolution shaped me to attribute minds to humans but not to machines, but that looks like a causal explanation of my intuition, not a rational justification of it.

So the argument seems to prove too much: either I'm forced into radical skepticism about other humans, or I'm forced to attribute consciousness on behavioral grounds alone to any sufficiently sophisticated system, or I need a principled distinction between the cases that collapses under pressure.

Which step goes wrong, and what do I have to give up to fix it? I'd most like to hold onto (1) — that my belief in other human minds is justified — and I'd also like to hold onto (6) — that behavioral evidence alone shouldn't settle the AI case. But I'm not sure I can keep both. If one of them has to go, tell me which path is less costly and what accepting it commits me to.
