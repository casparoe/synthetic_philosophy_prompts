I've been following the back-and-forth between effective altruists and their critics for years now, and it feels like the same arguements have just been circulating since earning to give got big in the early 2010s. The EA side says: if you can take a high-paying job and donate a large chunk to cost-effective charities, that does more good than working directly at a nonprofit, and refusing because it feels gross is just letting measurability bias work in reverse. The critics say: that treats existing economic institutions as a neutral machine you can extract money from, ignores how your job props up the very structures that cause the poverty you're donating to fix, and the obsession with measureable outcomes quietly redefines doing good as doing what's easiest to count. Nobody seems to deny the core facts about which charities are cost-effective or what these jobs pay. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden empirical disagreement about how much harm a career causes, a moral disagreement about whether you're obligated to maximize or just do your fair share, or a disagreement about what counts as evidence in the first place? My working theory is that it's mostly about where the burden of proof sits for institutional harm, not about charity effectiveness. Diagnose the structure of the impasse, and tell me what would actually move it, or whether it's the kind of debate where the two sides can't be moved by the same considerations.
