I am a novelist, and I have repeatedly had my own account of what I meant waved away with the slogan "the author is dead." It has been used on me in an interview, when I explained why a character acted as she did; in a review, when I was told that my novel was really about something I never intended; and in a writing workshop, when a student told another student that her explanation of her own ending was beside the point. In each case the slogan was made to do slightly different work: sometimes it denied that my intention fixes the meaning of the text, sometimes it denied that my intention is even good evidence for what the text means, and sometimes it seemed to deny that I have any authority at all over what the book says.

I would like a precise map of what that slogan could actually be claiming. Lay out the distinct theses it might express, from the nearly trivial to the radical, and for each give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or self-undermining. In particular, distinguish the claims that an author's intention is inaccessible, that it is irrelevant to meaning, and that the author as a source of meaning does not exist; and distinguish meaning as a property of the text from meaning as something constituted by readers or by interpretive communities. Then tell me which of these readings actually licenses the move from "the author's stated intention is not decisive" to "the author's stated intention carries no weight," and which readings a working novelist could accept while still revising, taking responsibility for the effects of her choices, and objecting when a critic gets the book wrong. Sort the readings from the one that concedes least to the one that concedes most. For each of the three scenes I described, say which reading the person was most likely invoking and whether it was strong enough to do the job. Where the slogan is doing genuine work, say what would count against its stronger readings; where it is only a gesture, say so.
