Write an essay on the surprise exam paradox. The standard setup: a teacher announces on Friday that there will be exactly one exam the following week, and that it will be a surprise — meaning the students will not be able to know which day the exam falls until it is handed out. The students reason backward: if the exam hasn't come by Thursday night, they would know it must be Friday, so it cannot be Friday; with Friday eliminated, if it hasn't come by Wednesday night, they would know it must be Thursday, so it cannot be Thursday; and so on through Wednesday, Tuesday, and Monday, concluding that no day can host a surprise exam. Yet on Wednesday morning the teacher hands out the exam, and the students are genuinely surprised. Where does the reasoning go wrong?

Your essay should engage with at least three distinct diagnoses from the philosophical literature. These might include: (1) the view that the teacher's announcement is self-referential or generates a blindspot, so the students cannot justifiably know the announcement even if it is true; (2) the view that the backward-induction argument requires the students to know they will retain their knowledge and rationality through the week, and that this assumption fails under the very conditions the announcement creates; (3) the view that the reasoning is formally valid but the announcement is simply unknowable in advance, making the paradox epistemic rather than logical; (4) the view that the first step — eliminating Friday — already smuggles in an illicit interpretation of "surprise" that the later steps inherit; and (5) the view that the paradox dissolves once we distinguish the students' epistemic position at the start of the week from their position mid-week, when some days have passed without an exam. You need not cover every one of these, but your essay should weigh competing accounts, identify the strongest objections each faces, and defend one as the most satisfactory resolution. Maximum 1,000 words.
