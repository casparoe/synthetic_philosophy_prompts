At a recent reading group on well-being, we were discussing hedonism — the view that pleasure is the only thing ultimately good for a person and pain the only thing ultimately bad — when the conversation turned to the heterogeneity problem. The problem, as our instructor framed it, is that the states hedonists class together as pleasures seem to have almost nothing in common: the pleasure of cracking a difficult proof, the pleasure of a warm bath, the pleasure of spiteful gossip, the pleasure of a drug, the pleasure of a headache lifting. If "pleasure" does not name a single kind of state, then the claim that pleasure is the good threatens to become either false or empty. Two members of the group, Dana and Marcus, got into a sustained argument about this, and I came away unable to tell who had the better of it.

Dana argued that the objection is close to fatal. Introspect honestly, she said, and the pleasure of being absorbed in a demanding piece of music shares no felt quality with the pleasure of eating when hungry; so "pleasure" is not a natural kind but a label for a grab-bag, and "pleasure is good" reduces to "whatever I happen to enjoy is good" — trivial if true, and not obviously true either. Marcus replied that she was confusing pleasure with its objects. On his view, pleasure is not a special sensation alongside the music or the taste; it is an attitude — the liking, the welcoming, the being satisfied by — that a person takes toward those experiences. The heterogeneity is all in the objects, while the attitude is the single common thread, and that attitude is what the hedonist identifies with goodness. Dana answered that the attitudes are just as heterogeneous as the sensations: liking a proof is an active, effortful, intellectual stance, while liking a backrub is passive and bodily, so calling both a "pro-attitude" does no real unifying work; it is the same grab-bag moved up one level. Marcus responded that she was demanding the wrong kind of unity. A common element need not be a common felt quality; it can be a common functional role, and these attitudes all involve being drawn to the experience, wanting it to continue, or being satisfied with it. That, he claimed, is enough for a single theory of value. Dana's final move was to ask what "being satisfied with" picks out if not a feeling; if the answer is only a disposition to seek or continue the experience, then hedonism has gone silent — it no longer tells us what is good, only how we tend to behave.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each step of the argument, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or defensible but incomplete, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall about the heterogeneity problem as posed, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
