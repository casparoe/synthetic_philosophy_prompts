I've been a regular on LessWrong and the EA Forum for the better part of a decade, and there's one debate that keeps resurfacing in different guises without ever seeming to make progress. I'm talking about the moral status of the doing/allowing distinction — the claim that there is a deep normative difference between acting to bring about an outcome and merely allowing it to occur, between killing and letting die, between pushing the man off the footbridge and pulling the switch.

You see it in arguments about whether we have stronger obligations to the global poor than we do to our neighbours (the drowning-child thought experiment is meant to collapse the distinction, but does it?). You see it in debates about whether AI alignment should treat an AI that passively allows a catastrophe the same way it treats an AI that actively causes one. You see it in the perennial forum threads about whether the reason we don't donate every spare pound is a genuine moral insight or a failure of will. And you see it in the way the community itself splits between those who think the distinction is a fundamental feature of ethics — call them the Fundamentalists — and those who think it's a contingent feature of human moral psychology that a properly reflective agent would discard — call them the Illusionists.

I've watched smart people on both sides articulate their views clearly, and I've also watched them talk straight past each other. What I've never seen is a proper adversarial collaboration: the kind of document where both sides sit down, work through their disagreements, and produce a joint statement of common ground, residual disagreement, and cruxes. I'd like you to draft that document.

Here's what I want in it:

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered list of substantive claims both sides can endorse — not just meta-level observations about the debate itself, but claims about the psychology, the normative structure, and the practical implications of the distinction that survive scrutiny from both directions. For instance, both sides might agree that human beings do in fact draw the distinction intuitively, that it patterns with certain features of causal cognition, and that it has some pragmatic value; but they might also agree on more specific claims about edge cases or about where the distinction breaks down.

2. **Disagreements table.** A table with three columns: the precise question at issue, the Fundamentalist's answer, and the Illusionist's answer. Each answer must be phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — no straw-manning. The questions should range from the psychological (does the distinction track anything in the structure of moral cognition beyond mere salience?) to the normative (would a fully rational agent with unlimited cognitive resources still draw the distinction?) to the practical (should we design institutions and AI systems that encode the distinction?).

3. **Cruxes.** The sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. For each crux, specify: (a) a concrete thought experiment, empirical test, or argument that both sides agree bears on the crux; (b) the Fundamentalist's prediction about what that test would show; and (c) the Illusionist's prediction. At least one crux should involve a novel trolley variant or a scenario that isolates the doing/allowing distinction from confounds like certainty, proximity, salience, or intention.

4. **Verbal dispute diagnosis.** A brief assessment of whether any part of the disagreement is about words rather than substance. If the two sides use 'fundamental', 'moral', or 'distinction' in different senses, say so, and restate the substantive issue in a way that doesn't depend on the contested terminology.

I'm not asking you to adjudicate or to pick a side. I'm asking you to construct the text that the collaboration itself would have produced — the common ground they'd discover, the residual disagreement made precise, and the tests that both would agree could actually move the needle.
