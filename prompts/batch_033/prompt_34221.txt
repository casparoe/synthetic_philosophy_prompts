I am a retired computational neuroscientist. For the past few years, after leaving the lab, I have been reading philosophy of action—Bratman, Searle, Margaret Gilbert, and the surrounding literature on collective intentionality—and this semester I find myself teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Minds, Groups, and Joint Action." I am writing the final exam and I need your help with one question in particular.

You are an expert philosopher of action who specializes in collective intentionality and joint action. Please think carefully about what I am asking before you write, and produce a polished, rigorous exam question together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme.

I want one essay question that separates the students who have genuinely understood the theoretical architecture of the three main accounts we covered—Bratman's planning theory of shared intention, Searle's we-intention account, and Gilbert's joint-commitment account—from those who have merely memorized the labels and can recite summaries. The question should present a novel vignette: a short, realistic case study of two people engaged in coordinated activity that is ambiguous between genuine joint action and mere parallel individual action with coordination. The vignette should target a theoretical pressure point, such as whether the "shared goal" in joint action can be analyzed in purely individualistic terms, or whether the normative structure of joint commitment is essential to joint action, or what happens when the participants have asymmetric attitudes toward the joint activity. The students should be asked to analyze the vignette through the lens of each account, explain why the accounts yield different verdicts (or the same verdict for different reasons), and argue for which account handles the case best.

Please produce the following three items:

1. **The essay question itself**, including the vignette. The vignette should be specific enough to apply the theoretical conditions to, realistic rather than obviously contrived, and designed to expose a genuine structural difference between the accounts.

2. **A model answer of roughly 800 words.** This should demonstrate the analytical moves that distinguish an A-level answer from a B-level answer. It should not merely summarize the three accounts but should use the vignette to expose the structural differences between them, identify where each account's analysis bottoms out (in planning structures, in biological primitives, in normative relations), and offer a reasoned, non-question-begging argument for which account handles the vignette best.

3. **A marking scheme allocating 25 marks across five criteria.** For each criterion, include a brief note on what distinguishes an A-level answer from a B-level answer. The five criteria should be: (i) accurate reconstruction of each account's conditions for joint action; (ii) correct application of those conditions to the vignette; (iii) identification of the deeper theoretical disagreement the vignette exposes; (iv) quality of argument about which account handles the vignette best and why; and (v) clarity and philosophical precision of writing.

Before you write the final deliverables, I would like you to think through the logical structure of the three accounts—what each requires for an activity to count as genuinely joint or shared, and what each account takes as primitive or irreducible—and then identify what kind of vignette would most sharply expose their differences. The question should reward the students who have done the hardest thinking, not the ones who have done the most reading.
