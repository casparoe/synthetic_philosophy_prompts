I've been thinking about Van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the idea that my current credence in something should equal my expected future credence in it. I'm trying to figure out what I actually think by going through cases, and I'm finding that my gut gives different answers in situations that feel structurally similar. Here are my verdicts:

1. I'm about to look at a reliable weather forecast. Right now, conditional on the forecast saying 80% chance of rain tomorrow, my credence in rain is 80%. That's exactly what Reflection says, and it feels right.

2. I'm about to take a sleeping pill that will knock me out and also make me forget I ever took it. I know that when I wake up tomorrow, I'll have credence near zero that I took a sleeping pill last night. But right now my credence that I took it is 1. It would be insane for me to set my current credence to match my expected future credence. So Reflection fails here.

3. I'm going to a party and I'll get quite drunk. I know from experience that when I'm drunk, I become irrationally confident I can drive home. My current credence that I'm safe to drive is very low, and I've already arranged a ride. Conditional on my future drunk self having credence 0.9 that I'm fine, my current credence is still very low. Reflection fails again.

4. I'm about to have a long conversation with a colleague who I consider my epistemic peer — we're equally smart, equally informed, and we've both thought hard about the issue. I know that after the conversation, my credence will shift, because she always gives me new ways of looking at things. But here's the thing: right now, before the conversation, my credence in the proposition is 0.7. Should my credence conditional on my future credence being (say) 0.4 equal 0.4? My gut says yes, actually — I should defer to my future self here, because my future self will have the benefit of the conversation. But I also feel a bit queasy about it, because I currently have my reasons for 0.7, and I'm not sure I should just abandon them in advance.

5. Here's the one that's really bothering me. I'm about to take an exam that I've studied hard for. I'm confident I passed: credence 0.85. But I know myself, and I know that immediately after the exam, I'll be in a state of anxious self-doubt — I'll have credence maybe 0.5 that I passed. Now, my future self has actually taken the exam and has more information than I do (I've seen the questions, even if I'm second-guessing my answers). But my future self is also clearly irrational — I know in advance that the anxiety distorts my judgment. So should my current credence equal my expected future credence? I genuinely don't know. My gut says no — I shouldn't defer to a panicked version of myself — but my future self does have more evidence, and the whole point of Reflection is that you should defer to someone who has more evidence than you do.

6. Last one: I know I have a genetic predisposition to early-onset dementia. In twenty years, my future self might have credence 0.3 that I have two hands. Right now my credence is basically 1. Obviously I don't set my current credence to match my expected future credence. But notice: the reason is that I know my future self's cognitive faculties will be compromised. In the exam case, I also know my future self's cognitive faculties will be compromised (by anxiety). So why do those two cases feel different? In the dementia case, I don't feel any pull to defer; in the exam case, I feel at least some pull.

So my question is: what principle captures all of this? Is there a single condition on when Reflection holds and when it doesn't, and what does it say about the exam case and about other cases I haven't thought of? Or do I need to give up one of my verdicts?
