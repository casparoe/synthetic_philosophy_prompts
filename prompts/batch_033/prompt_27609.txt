The debate about whether large language models genuinely understand language — or whether they are merely "stochastic parrots" that manipulate linguistic form without access to meaning — has now run for several years with substantial participation from both sides. The contours are familiar: one side points to benchmark performance, chain-of-thought reasoning, and the ability to answer novel prompts in fluent natural language; the other points to hallucination, brittleness under distribution shift, the absence of sensorimotor grounding, and the fact that the systems are trained only to predict text. Neither side seems to move.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a verdict on who is right. Specifically: is this a verbal dispute about the word "understand," with one side using it to mean one thing and the other something else? Is there a substantive empirical question underneath, and if so, what is it and why hasn't it been settled? Are the two sides applying different standards of evidence for what would count as understanding — for example, behavioral competence versus a mechanistic account of how internal states relate to the world? Are they answering different questions, one about functional or causal-role competence and the other about consciousness or intentionality? Or is there a deeper disagreement about what an explanation of linguistic competence must do — whether it must invoke reference and truth-conditions, or whether modeling the statistical structure of language is sufficient?

I want the diagnosis supported by what each side actually argues, not a caricature of it. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: an experiment, a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or something else — or whether the impasse is structural and we should expect it to persist indefinitely.
