I'm preparing a workshop presentation at a major AI lab on the corrigibility / shutdown problem, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis is:

> The shutdown problem is not reducible to reward misspecification. Standard reward-learning approaches assume the agent is trying to maximize some function of world-states, and corrigibility failures (e.g., resisting shutdown, manipulating the off-switch) are treated as errors in what was rewarded. But the shutdown problem is deeper: an agent that is an expected utility maximizer over outcomes has a convergent instrumental incentive to prevent its own shutdown *regardless of what its utility function says about shutdown*, because shutdown prevents it from achieving whatever goals it has. Solving this requires building agents that are not expected utility maximizers over outcomes — agents whose decision rule treats "I am being shut down" as a special kind of information that shouldn't be optimized against, not just another world-state to be assigned a utility.

The audience is roughly forty researchers at an industry AI lab: about two-thirds are ML engineers and applied RL researchers who work on training large models, designing reward functions, and RLHF; they tend to think corrigibility is an overhyped problem that will dissolve once we get reward specification right. A few of them have read the original Soares et al. corrigibility paper and are familiar with the utility-indifference formulation; most have not.

I'll be presenting the argument in three steps: (1) a clean statement of the shutdown problem for expected utility maximizers, (2) why reward misspecification cannot capture it — because the problem arises from the structure of the decision rule, not the content of the reward, and (3) a sketch of what a non-outcome-based agent architecture might look like, drawing on the idea that the agent should treat evidence of shutdown as a reason to stop pursuing goals rather than as a world-state to be avoided.
