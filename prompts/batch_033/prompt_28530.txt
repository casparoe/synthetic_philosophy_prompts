I've been thinking about the two-envelope paradox for a while now, and I've arrived at a resolution that feels satisfying to me. Here's the setup as I understand it: you're shown two envelopes and told one contains exactly twice as much money as the other. You pick one and open it to find, say, $100. You reason that the other envelope must contain either $50 or $200, and since you had no reason to prefer one envelope over the other when you chose, those two possibilities seem equally likely. So the expected value of switching is $125, which is more than the $100 you're holding, and you should switch. But the same reasoning would have applied no matter what amount you saw — and more troublingly, it seems to apply even if you don't open the envelope at all, which would mean you'd want to switch, and then switch back, and then switch again, forever. That can't be right.

The resolution I've settled on is this: the mistake is in the step that says "each equally likely." That step smuggles in an assumption about how the amounts were chosen — specifically, it assumes that for whatever amount X you find, the probability the organizers put the pair (X, 2X) in the envelopes is equal to the probability they put the pair (X/2, X). That's not something you can assume without knowing how the amounts were generated. Once you put a reasonable prior over the possible pairs — one that assigns finite total probability — the expected gain from switching is no longer positive for every X, and the paradox dissolves. This is essentially the Bayesian resolution, and I find it clean and persuasive.

But I want to know how exposed this resolution actually is. What would count against it? I'm not looking for the standard objections that get repeated in every discussion — I want to know what someone who defends this resolution ought to acknowledge as a genuine challenge. Are there versions of the paradox that survive even when the prior is proper? Are there reformulations that don't rely on probabilistic expectations at all, and that would therefore bypass the prior entirely? What formal results, edge cases, or variations in the setup should make me reconsider whether I've really located the core of the problem? And I'd like an honest assessment of whether the resolution has been qualified to the point where nothing could threaten it — and if so, whether that's a problem for a resolution of this kind. Please rank the counterarguments from fatal to merely awkward, and throughout, separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what remains contested.
