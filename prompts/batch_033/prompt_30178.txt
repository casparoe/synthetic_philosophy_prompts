I've been teaching a graduate seminar on theories of well-being this semester, and we spent the last two sessions on hedonism and its discontents. One objection we discussed at length is the heterogeneity problem: the pleasures of finally grasping a difficult proof, of a warm bath, of hearing a familiar voice after a long absence seem, as experiences, to have almost nothing in common. Yet hedonism needs pleasure to pick out a unified phenomenon — or at least a unified good-making property — if it is to do any theoretical work.

Here is what has been nagging at me since those discussions. The way the heterogeneity problem is usually presented, it looks as though it is really the measurement problem in phenomenological clothing. Suppose we somehow solved all the problems about interpersonal comparison and cardinal measurement of pleasure — suppose we had a perfect hedonometer that assigned a real number to any pleasure anyone experiences. Would the heterogeneity objection not simply vanish? If we can measure it, we must have found the common property. And if we cannot measure it, then that — the absence of a common scale — is the genuine problem, not the phenomenological diversity as such. On this diagnosis, the heterogeneity objection gets its force parasitically from the measurement problem: it is vivid and compelling because diverse pleasures resist being placed on a single scale, not because the bare fact of diversity poses any independent difficulty for the hedonist. I am starting to suspect that the heterogeneity problem is a misdiagnosis — that the real challenge for hedonism is measurement and interpersonal comparison, and the phenomenological diversity is just a rhetorically effective way of illustrating that challenge.

Does this collapse two distinct problems that need to be kept apart, or have I correctly identified what is doing the work in the heterogeneity objection?
