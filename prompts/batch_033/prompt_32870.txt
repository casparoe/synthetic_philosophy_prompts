I serve on the jury of a national award for investigative journalism in science and medicine. We are considering a nominated feature that exposed a pattern of data fabrication in a clinical-trials unit, resulting in retractions, a parliamentary inquiry, and revised consent protocols that now protect trial participants across the country. The piece is, by any measure, outstanding journalism with significant public benefit.

What has divided the jury is not the quality of the work but the motive behind it. Through a deposition in an unrelated proceeding, we have learned that the reporter undertook the investigation not out of concern for scientific integrity or patient welfare, but because the lab director had ended a personal relationship with the reporter's sibling, and the reporter saw the exposé as a means of revenge. Internal correspondence, now part of the court record, includes the reporter describing the story as payback and expressing indifference to the institutional reforms that eventually followed.

The award criteria are silent on motive: they require that the work demonstrate excellence in investigative journalism and yield a demonstrable contribution to public knowledge and welfare. The jury is split. Some members argue that the only relevant considerations are the quality of the reporting and its consequences; the reporter's reasons are a private matter. Others argue that awarding the profession's highest honor to work undertaken as an act of personal vengeance would corrupt the purpose of the award and signal that any motive is acceptable so long as the results are good.

I must draft the written opinion that the jury will review before our final deliberation next week. It should recommend a concrete decision on the nomination — award the prize, withhold it, or award it with a statement — and it should engage seriously with the position you recommend against. The deeper question the opinion must address is this: under what conditions, if any, does the reason for which a piece of journalism was undertaken bear on whether the profession ought to honor it?
