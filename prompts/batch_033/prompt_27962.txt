After thirty-odd years in physical chemistry I've been spending retirement on the philosophy of language, and the sorites paradox has got its hooks in me. My reading group has two members who go in circles on it: one is an epistemicist about vagueness — she thinks 'heap' and 'tall' really do have sharp cutoffs, and our inability to say where they fall is just ignorance — and the other is a supervaluationist — he thinks borderline sentences are neither true nor false, because the meaning of the predicate underdetermines all the admissible precisifications. They've agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I got asked to draft the thing.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. Start with a numbered list of claims both camps can accept: about the sorites series itself, about the tolerance premise (that one grain can't make the difference), about classical logic and bivalence, about the 'definitely' operator and higher-order vagueness, and about whether the sentence "there is a sharp boundary" is one they can both assert, and what exactly it commits them too. Then a dissagreements table: the precise formulations where they part ways, each worded so its own side would sign it, and a note on which parts of their fight are just verbal. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would actually move at least one of them — and for each crux, a case or argument both would accept bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I keep reaching for the image of a phase boundary: sharp in the thermodynamic limit but smeared out in any finite sample. Use that if it helps, ignore it if it doesn't.
