A philosopher of language has given the following verdicts about whether particular assertions are epistemically proper — that is, whether the speaker meets the epistemic standard required for flat-out assertion:

1. Smith asserts "The person who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket," based on strong evidence that Jones will get the job and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Unbeknownst to Smith, he himself will get the job, and he also happens to have ten coins in his pocket. The assertion is true and justified, but the truth is disconnected from Smith's evidence in the usual Gettier way. Verdict: improper.

2. Brown asserts "The bank is open on Saturdays," based on having visited on a Saturday two weeks ago. The bank is open on Saturdays, but unbeknownst to Brown it recently changed its hours and was open on the Saturday he visited only because of a one-time staffing exception. Verdict: improper.

3. Green asserts "That's a zebra" while looking at a zebra in a zoo pen. It is a zebra, and Green can reliably distinguish zebras from other animals. However, the zoo has disguised several mules to look exactly like zebras in nearby pens, and Green would have been fooled had she looked at one of those instead. Verdict: proper.

4. White asserts "The train leaves at 3:15" based on glancing at a posted schedule that he knows is notoriously unreliable — most entries on it are incorrect. He happens to look at one of the few correct entries, and the train does leave at 3:15. Verdict: improper.

5. Black asserts "It will rain tomorrow" based solely on a reliable weather service that reports a 90% chance. Black does not understand meteorology and has no independent evidence. The service's prediction is correct, and it rains. Verdict: proper.

Now consider a new case: Gray is a medical student who has examined a patient presenting with a textbook set of symptoms for disease X. On the basis of this examination, Gray asserts to a surgeon, "The patient has disease X." The evidence is strong — in an ordinary context, it would easily suffice for knowledge. However, the stakes are extremely high: if Gray is wrong, the surgeon will remove a healthy organ. Gray is fully aware of the stakes.

Predict the philosopher's verdict on Gray's assertion. Identify the candidate norms of assertion that are consistent with the first five verdicts, explain which norm you think the philosopher is running and why, and specify a further case — not a mere variant of Gray's — that would cleanly distinguish between the candidate norms.
