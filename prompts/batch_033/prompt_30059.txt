I have been working through Kant’s *Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch* (1795), and I am stuck on a passage in the First Supplement, where Kant introduces the idea that nature itself provides a guarantee of perpetual peace. The passage reads:

> The guarantee of perpetual peace is nothing less than that great artist, nature (natura daedala rerum). In her mechanical course we see that her aim is to produce a harmony among men, against their will and indeed through their discord. As a necessity working according to laws we do not know, we call it destiny. But, considering its design in world history, we call it "providence," inasmuch as we discern in it the profound wisdom of a higher cause which predetermines the course of nature and directs it to the objective final end of the human race.

I understand that in the rest of the section Kant describes mechanisms—war, population dispersal, trade, and especially the selfish and hostile inclinations of human beings—that drive people into lawful arrangements almost against their will, and that this is connected to the famous thought that the problem of a state could be solved even for a people of intelligent devils. But I cannot tell what kind of claim the “guarantee” is supposed to be. Is Kant making a metaphysical claim that history is actually directed by a wise nature or providence? Or is he saying only that we must adopt this teleological way of looking at history for practical purposes, even though we cannot theoretically prove it? And is he really claiming that perpetual peace will come about through nature’s mechanism alone, or only that nature supplies the conditions under which moral action can bring peace about? I would like a committed reading: take a definite position on what function the guarantee of nature plays in Kant’s argument for perpetual peace, and defend that reading against the obvious alternatives.
