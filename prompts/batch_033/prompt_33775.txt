I'm giving a seminar next month to a cognitive neuroscience lab group, and I want a prep sheet for the question period rather than more rehearsal of the talk itself. The thesis I'm defending is Dennett's line on the color-phi phenomenon: that the choice between an Orwellian revision and a Stalinesque revision is not a real empirical alternative. On the Orwellian account, the subject really was conscious of the unedited sequence—red at the first flash, green at the second—and only the memory of that experience was later tampered with to insert a color change at the midpoint of the illusory motion. On the Stalinesque account, consciousness of the sequence was held back until the second flash had been processed, so the subject was never conscious of the unedited sequence at all; the editing happened before experience reached the subject. I argue, with Dennett, that every piece of evidence that seems to favor one account can be redescribed to favor the other at no cost, because any criterion that would tell them apart has to smuggle in a privileged finish line—a moment at which the edited or unedited content becomes conscious. Without a Cartesian theater, the question of when the subject became conscious of the color change has no determinate answer, and the apparent puzzle dissolves rather than gets solved.

My audience: a vision and temporal-perception lab, about fifteen people, mostly psychophysicists who work on postdiction, flash-lag, backward masking, and temporal-order judgments. There are also two or three philosophers of mind who think the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction can be operationalized. This is a group that spends its days designing experiments that claim to measure when a representation reaches awareness, and several of them will take it as a challenge, not a comfort, to be told that the timing question is ill-posed. They will not be moved by appeals to Dennett's authority, and they will be unimpressed by any argument that sounds like a philosopher telling scientists which questions they are allowed to ask. They will want to know, concretely, which experiment I would run and what result would change my mind.

The argument I'll actually give, so the objections can target it, has three moves. First, the two accounts predict exactly the same report—the same retrospective description of a moving dot that changes color midway—so report data cannot separate them. Second, any proposed neural criterion for separating a late memory edit from an early delay needs an independent marker of the moment of consciousness, and that marker is exactly what the debate is about; the candidate markers on offer, such as the end of the feedforward sweep or the onset of recurrent processing, either beg the question or can be absorbed by the other side. Third, the felt difference between the two accounts comes from picturing the subject as an inner witness inspecting a stream of finished experiences, and dropping that picture leaves nothing to distinguish the accounts.

That's the position. Include the objections that have real force, not the ones I can bat away, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. I want this to be candid about my exposure, not a pep talk.
