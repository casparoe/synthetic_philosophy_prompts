I'm on my city's affordable housing task force and we're stuck between two proposals for a city-owned parcel. Proposal A: a mixed-income building with 65 deeply affordable units (for households below 30% AMI) plus 135 market-rate units, where the market-rate revenue cross-subsidizes the affordable ones so we use zero additional public dollars beyond the land. Proposal B: a 100% deeply affordable building on the same parcel, but because there's no cross-subsidy it only pencils out to 50 units — fewer total affordable units than A, but every single one goes to a low-income household.

One of the members keeps saying Rawls's difference principle decides it: we have to maximize the position of the worst-off, and B gives them 100% of the building instead of about a third. I think he's confusing the *share* going to the worst-off with the *absolute position* of the worst-off — 65 affordable units houses more families than 50 does — but I also suspect there's a deeper issue about whether the difference principle even applies to a one-off policy decision rather than the basic structure of society as a whole. I need to make the case clearly because three other members are nodding along with him and we vote in ten days.

Draft me a short memo (2–3 pages) for the task force that: (1) explains what the difference principle actually says, including what Rawls meant by "the basic structure" and why he thought the principle applies to that and not necessarily to every individual policy; (2) applies it honestly to our two proposals and says which one it favors, if either; (3) takes the other guy's argument seriously — don't just dismiss the "share" intuition, say what's right about it and where it goes wrong; and (4) gives a clear recommendation with reasons. Assume no one on the task force has read Rawls, so spell out the concepts as you go.
