I'm writing a paper on the inside/outside view distinction and what it actually demands of us epistemically, and I keep finding that the literature is full of advice that pulls in different directions. Kahneman and Flyvbjerg tell us to trust the outside view — reference class forecasting — and distrust the inside view, especially for planning: the base rate of cost overruns and schedule slips should dominate our estimate. But then Tetlock's superforecasters seem to do something much closer to inside-view work: they dig into case-specific details, and the forecasters who merely lean on base rates don't perform as well as the ones who integrate both. And there's a deeper problem: the outside view is supposed to be a corrective to overconfidence, but selecting a reference class is itself a judgment call that can be done in a motivated or biased way. So the distinction, as it stands, doesn't come with instructions for its own use — it's a tool that can't tell you when you're wielding it wrong.

Before I try to resolve any of this, I want to get clear on what a theory of the inside/outside view distinction — and the kind of epistemic modesty it's supposed to underwrite — actually has to deliver. Write me the desiderata. What must an adequate theory of how to navigate the inside and outside views get right? Which cases must it handle (the planning fallacy, unique one-off projects with no obvious reference class, domains where base rates are unavailable or contested, forecasting tournaments, the expert who knows she's in a reference class of overconfident experts), and what feature of each case makes it a requirement rather than an optional extra? Which distinctions must the theory respect and not collapse — for example, the distinction between the outside view as a prior and the inside view as a likelihood, or between epistemic modesty and blanket deference to base rates, or between the outside view as a debiasing technique and the outside view as a general epistemic stance? Are there results from adjacent areas — the reference class problem in frequentist statistics, Goodhart's law, the conjunction fallacy literature, the "no free lunch" theorems in machine learning — that an adequate theory must be consistent with, and why? And, crucially, flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I'm especially interested in whether the demand that the theory give concrete, action-guiding advice (pick this reference class, weight the inside and outside views this way) conflicts with the demand that it not smuggle in the very biases it's supposed to correct — since any procedure for picking a reference class is itself a judgment made from somewhere on the inside/outside spectrum. If some single desideratum knocks out most of the existing approaches, tell me which one and why.
