A few of us were talking after a meetup, and two people got into a long argument about what Libet's experiments actually show about free will. I took notes because it seemed like the kind of dispute where both sides were making real points and I wanted to think it through later.

The first person (call him Dan) argued that the Libet paradigm straightforwardly undermines free will: the readiness potential fires hundreds of milliseconds before subjects report conscious awareness of deciding, so the brain has already committed to the action before "you" think you chose it. Conscious will is a post-hoc story, not a cause.

The second person (call her Mira) pushed back on several fronts. She said the readiness potential isn't a decision — it's more like the brain warming up, a stochastic accumulation process that sometimes crosses threshold and sometimes doesn't. The conscious experience of deciding might be the threshold-crossing itself, not something that comes after.

Dan replied that this just relocates the problem: if the accumulation process is unconscious and deterministic (or random), then whether you call the threshold-crossing "conscious" or not, it's still not free in any sense that matters for moral responsibility. You're a spectator to a process that was already underway.

Mira said that's too sweeping. First, Libet himself emphasized the conscious veto — subjects could abort the planned action up to about 100ms before movement, and this veto didn't seem to have its own preceding readiness potential. So consciousness might play a blocking or filtering role even if it doesn't initiate. Second, she said the whole paradigm involves arbitrary, contentless decisions — "flex your wrist whenever you feel like it" — and it's a mistake to generalize from that to the kinds of deliberative, reasons-responsive choices that free will debates actually care about.

Dan countered that the veto point is empirically contested — later work has found precursors to "free" choices seconds in advance using fMRI, and the veto itself may have unconscious antecedents we just haven't measured yet. And on the triviality objection: if free will is real, it should show up even in simple choices. If it only appears in complex deliberation, that looks like moving the goalposts.

Mira came back with: no, it's the opposite. If you define free will as the capacity to act on reasons after deliberation, then a paradigm that deliberately strips away all reasons and deliberation is testing something else entirely. It's like testing whether vision exists by putting someone in a dark room.

At this point they were going in circles. I'm hoping you can adjudicate this move by move: for each substantive claim made by each person, say whether it's well-supported, questionable, or just wrong, and explain why. Then give your overall assessment: does the Libet paradigm (and its successors) substantially weaken the case for free will, or are the methodological objections strong enough that we should mostly set it aside?
