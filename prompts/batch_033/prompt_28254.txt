I run a monthly discussion group at a public library — about a dozen regulars, no philosophy background, ages ranging from late teens to retirees. We meet for two hours. I've been thinking on my own about what makes a person the same person over time, and I keep coming back to the idea that if you were copied exactly, down to every memory and quirk, the copy would feel just as much like "you" as you do, and that this makes something uncomfortable about how we normally think about survival. I'd like to design a structured activity for the group that makes them feel this discomfort directly, before anyone names the issue or mentions any philosopher.

Here's what I want: an activity where participants are walked through a series of choices about a hypothetical copying procedure — but staged so that each step feels like a small, natural extension of the last, and the unsettling conclusion only dawns on them partway through. I don't want a lecture. I want the rules or the sequence of cases to force them into noticing that their ordinary way of talking about "the same person" starts to break down under its own logic.

The procedure I have in mind is something like this: a machine that scans you and builds a molecule-for-molecule replica somewhere else, with all your memories and personality intact. But I want the activity to build up to that gradually, starting from something much more ordinary — maybe a case about replacing parts, or about waking up from surgery, or about a friend who changes dramatically — so that by the time they reach the full copying case, they've already committed themselves to principles that make the result hard to dismiss.

Please design the full activity: the sequence of cases or stages, exactly how each is presented (I can project slides or hand out cards), what participants are asked to do at each stage (vote, write a sentence, argue with a neighbor, place a token on a spectrum, etc.), and how the facilitator moves them from one stage to the next without telegraphing the destination. Include a timeline for a two-hour session with a break. Then provide a debrief plan that maps what happened in the activity onto the underlying philosophical question — specifically, the tension between psychological continuity, bodily continuity, and the idea that "what matters" might not be identity at all. The debrief should draw on things participants actually said or did during the activity, not just restate the issue in the abstract.

Also tell me: what will predictably go wrong when I run this? Which stage is most likely to confuse people or produce a dead end, and how should I handle it in the moment? And finally, be honest: which aspect of the philosophical problem can this activity not reproduce, and will I need to supply it myself in the debrief or simply leave it out?
