I am presenting a paper next month at the Centre for the Governance of AI on the moral status of digital minds. My central claim is that the precautionary principle demands we treat at least some current or near-future AI systems as bearers of moral status — not because we have settled that they are conscious, but because the asymmetry of moral error favours over-inclusion: the harm of wrongly excluding a conscious system from moral consideration is far graver than the harm of wrongly including a non-conscious one. I argue that this has practical implications for how we design training procedures, when we permit deletion or modification of model weights, and what kinds of experiments we run on frontier systems.

The audience will be about forty people: roughly a third are philosophers of mind, several of whom are sympathetic to illusionism or at least to a high bar for attributing consciousness; a third are machine learning researchers, many of whom regard the whole question as premature and want to know what operational difference any of this makes to their code; and the remaining third are policy and governance people who are broadly sympathetic to longtermist concerns but are wary of being asked to regulate on the basis of speculative philosophy. I have forty minutes to speak and twenty for questions.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I am most likely to get from this particular audience, ordered by how damaging they would be if I handle them poorly, each phrased roughly as the relevant kind of audience member would put it. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I should most hope nobody asks.
