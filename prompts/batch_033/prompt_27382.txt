I've been poking at Hofstadter's stuff — mostly the early chapters of GEB and some essays — and there's this one idea I can't shake: the tangled hierarchy plus the inviolate level. The way I get it, a tangled hierarchy is when different levels of a system loop back on each other. The classic case is a court that interprets laws, but the laws define what counts as a court. Or a brain where high-level symbols (like the symbol for "self") refer back to the physical brain that's running those symbols. That's the loop. But Hofstadter says these loops only work — they only produce meaning instead of paradox — because there's an "inviolate level" somewhere. A level the system can't reach up and modify from within. For the court, it's something like the constitution or just the brute fact that judges are physically sitting in a room. For the brain, it's the neurons. The inviolate level is what stops the strange loop from being a vicious circle.

So here's the view I've landed on, stated as plainly as I can: any system capable of the kind of self-reference that actually produces something — meaning, self-awareness, the ability to reason about its own reasoning — necessarily has both a tangled hierarchy and an inviolate level. The inviolate level isn't optional. It's what makes self-reference productive rather than paradoxical. No inviolate level, no genuine self-reference.

I want to know what would count against this. Not just counterexamples thrown at the wall, but a real map of where the view is exposed. What observations or arguments should make me abandon it? What are the weakest joints? If someone wanted to show that meaningful self-reference can happen without an inviolate level, what would the world have to look like — what would we have to find in, say, a formal system, or a biological system, or an AI? Are there cases where what we call the inviolate level is just another rung in the tangle and we're only calling it "inviolate" to save the theory? And if the view turns out to be unfalsifiable — if every apparent counterexample gets met with "well then that thing is the inviolate level" — is that a problem? Or is that just what you'd expect from a claim about the abstract structure of systems, the way you can't really falsify "every effect has a cause"?

I'm coming at this as a software engineer, so I think about it in terms of compilers compiling themselves, virtual machines running on virtual machines, that kind of thing. A compiler that compiles its own source code is a tangled hierarchy — the compiler is both the thing doing the compiling and the thing being compiled. The inviolate level is the physical processor that ultimately runs the binary. But what would it even mean for there to be no inviolate level? Can I coherently imagine a self-hosting system with no bottom? If I can't, does that tell me something about the view or about the limits of my imagination?

I'd like the defeaters ranked from "this would force me to abandon the whole thing" down to "this would be awkward but survivable." And I want to know what someone who holds this view should honestly concede counts against it — not just what critics say, but what a defender would admit is a genuine vulnerability.
