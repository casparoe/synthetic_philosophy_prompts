In many legal systems, a signed advance directive refusing life-sustaining treatment will be honored even when the person who signed it later develops severe dementia, no longer recognizes anyone, and now seems content — even cheerful — and occasionally says she wants to go on living. The same systems, however, treat a present, competent refusal of treatment as decisive and would not override it. Reconstruct the theory of personal identity, autonomy, or well-being that this practice presupposes, or show that the practice is philosophically underdetermined and compatible with several rival theories. Does it strictly presuppose that the earlier competent self has authority over the later self because they are one person in a normatively loaded sense, or that a life is better assessed as a whole than moment by moment, or only that a formally valid exercise of capacity occurred? Separate what the practice presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say whether its parts presuppose consistent things, and explain what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by its main rival.
