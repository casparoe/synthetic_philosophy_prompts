I spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether the law of excluded middle is universally valid. I was defending the constructivist position — roughly, that mathematical truth is proof-based, and that for statements about infinite totalities where no decision procedure exists, there is no warrant for asserting P or not-P, so excluded middle is not a logical law we are entitled to. My friend kept pressing me to say what would actually count against this view, and I found I couldn't give a clean answer. I could list what critics say, but I wasn't sure which of those criticisms a committed constructivist should acknowledge as genuine problems, and I wasn't sure whether the position risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualification.

What would count against the constructivist rejection of excluded middle as a universally valid principle? I want the defeaters — arguments, formal results, commitments the view is forced into that turn out unacceptable — sorted by how serious they are, from fatal to merely awkward. I want to know which of these a constructivist would concede as counting against them and which they would dismiss as question-begging or as importing classical assumptions. And I want an honest assessment of whether the position has been rendered unfalsifiable — and if so, whether that is a legitimate feature of a view in the philosophy of logic or a sign that it has stopped being a substantive claim.
