I'm on the ethics review commitee at a mid-size tech company — we handle the cases that aren't clear-cut legal violations but feel wrong, like dark patterns, questionable data practices, or features that are technically opt-in but designed so nobody reads the consent. We've been at this for two years and I'm frustrated that we don't have a shared framework. I just read Theory of Moral Sentiments and I'm convinced Smith's impartial spectator is basically what we need — the idea that you judge an action by imagining how a well-informed but disinterested observer would react to it, and that this spectator is supposed to correct for the fact that you're partial to yourself and your own crowd. But I have no idea how to turn that into something we can actually run a case through on a Tuesday afternoon.

Can you write me an actual procedure? Like a checklist or a series of questions we ask in order, with guidance on what counts as a good enough answer to each before moving on. A few things it needs to handle:

- We're all insiders. We know the people who built the feature, we have stock, we want the company to succeed. Smith talks about how we naturally overvalue our own interests — the procedure should have a step that explicitly corrects for this.
- The impartial spectator is supposed to be well-informed. So we need a step where we make sure we actually have the facts, not just the version the product team gave us.
- Smith distinguishes between whether an action is proper (proportionate, fitting) and whether it's meritorious (deserves reward or punishment). I think our procedure should track that distinction somehow — some things we review are just indecent without being actively harmful, and vice versa.
- There's also the thing where Smith says the impartial spectator's judgments are shaped by general rules that emerge from repeated social experience — so the procedure shouldn't just be "what would a random reasonable person think," it should somehow incorporate the accumulated norms of our industry and society.

Mark clearly which steps are mechanical and which require a judgment call. And flag the cases where the procedure will break down or give ambiguous output.

Then run it on two cases we're actually looking at:

Case A: We want to launch a feature that auto-plays short-form video after any search query, using a recommendation algorithm we know exploits the same reward mechanisms as slot machines. Engagement projections are through the roof. It's not illegal, it's not even unusually aggressive by industry standards, but our own UX researchers say it'll be hardest to disengage for users with ADHD and older users who don't understand recommendation algorithms.

Case B: We're considering entering a market where we'll need to hire local distributors. The standard practice in that market is that distributors pay small facilitation fees to customs officials to avoid multi-week delays. These aren't bribes to get something you're not entitled to — they're bribes to get what you're legally entitled to, on time. We could refuse and just accept the delays, but our competitors won't, and our local team says we'll be at a serious disadvantage. We wouldn't be paying the bribes directly — the distributors would — but we'd know it's happening.

I want to see the procedure give me an answer I don't like for at least one of these. And I want to know what the procedure can't do — what kinds of ethical questions it's just not built for.
