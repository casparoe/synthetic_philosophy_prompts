You are a philosopher of language and a formal poet. Take your time to work through the paradox before composing, and think carefully about how the sonnet's structure can do philosophical work.

Write a Shakespearean sonnet about Moore's paradox — the absurdity of asserting a sentence of the form "p, but I do not believe that p." The poem should be spoken by someone who sincerely asserts: "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." The speaker is not being coy or paradoxical for effect; they are earnestly reporting what they take to be two true things about themselves and the world, and they are genuinely confused about why their listener finds the statement unintelligible.

Use the sonnet's structure to do philosophical work. The octave (first eight lines) should build the speaker's report: the rain, the evidence of it, the speaker's sincere conviction that it is raining — and then, without transition, the speaker's admission that they do not believe it. The volta (the turn, traditionally at line 9) should mark the moment the speaker feels the ground shift beneath them: the realization that something has gone wrong, not logically but in the act of speaking itself. The closing couplet should capture the philosophical puzzle: the sentence is not a contradiction (both parts could be true at once), yet asserting it seems to violate a constitutive norm of assertion — that to assert that p is to present oneself as believing that p. The poem should leave the paradox unresolved, the speaker stranded between what they said and what they meant.

The rhyme scheme should mirror the argument: let the quatrains suggest orderly, deductive movement while the content pulls against it. Avoid merely name-dropping Moore or Wittgenstein; the poem should embody the paradox, not lecture about it. A philosopher of language should recognize the paradox at work; a general reader should find it a good and unsettling poem.
