I've been reading arguments about whether a computer could ever genuinely think or understand anything, and I keep noticing the same move. Someone will say: sure, the machine produces the right outputs, but those outputs only mean something because we interpret them — the symbols don't mean anything to the machine. A red light, a word on a page, a calculator readout: they all mean what they mean because we lend them meaning. But a person's beleif that it's raining means something all by itself, from the inside, even if nobody else ever interprets it. And that difference is supposed to keep the machines out of the club.

Then I read a reply that turned the move around, and it's been bugging me. If you look inside the brain, a neuron firing doesn't mean "it's raining" all by itself either — it's just an electrochemical event, and it only counts as a belief about rain because some other part of the system (or some outside theorist) treats it that way. The reply kept pressing a question: what exactly does the genuine article have that the imitation lacks? Whenever someone tried to answer, it seemed to come out as "more of the same, just fancier" — or it quietly smuggled in an interpreter and called it original.

So I'm after two things. First, is there an established name for the distinction between meaning something on its own and meaning something only because an outside interpreter treats it as meaningful? Second, is there a name for the counter-argument that the supposedly original kind of meaning is just as borrowed as the rest — that the distinction is apperently worth less than people think? I don't just want labels. I want to know what the real difference is supposed to be: whether the distinction collapses entirely, whether it's a difference of degree rather than kind, and where the border cases live — a dog's fear, a baby's first words, a chess program's "intention" to castle. What exactly is the original side supposed to have that the borrowed side lacks, and does anything actually have it?
