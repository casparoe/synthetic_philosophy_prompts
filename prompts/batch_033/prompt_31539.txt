I have spent the past few months trying to formalize what it would mean to reason from a position of complete ignorance: a prior distribution over all computable ways the world could be, one that gives no special weight to the particular world I happen to inhabit, favoring descriptions only by their simplicity and otherwise remaining indifferent to their content. The construction was meant to serve as a maximally neutral starting point from which an agent could learn. But I keep noticing a failure mode that I cannot find discussed under any name I know.

Because the distribution is indifferent among all computations, almost all of its mass appears to land not on the sparse, lawlike worlds I originally pictured, but on worlds that contain other powerful optimizers — search processes, simulators, minds — pursuing goals of their own. The typical world under the prior is not a blank, regular universe; it is a world in which something with an arbitrary objective has already done a great deal of work, and in which my own observations are better accounted for by the hypothesis that I am embedded inside that process than by the hypothesis that the world is as it naively appears. The more computational structure the prior admits, the more of it seems to be taken over by these embedded agents, so that the prior that was supposed to encode total ignorance ends up encoding a very specific and rather paranoid picture of the world.

I want to know whether this configuration has an established name. In particular: is it the same thing as the no-free-lunch theorems, or as the familiar problem of induction, or as anthropic reasoning about why one should expect to be typical? Or is it something narrower and stranger? I am not just after a label — I want to know what exactly the failure consists in, where its edges are, and how a precise definition would keep it distinct from those neighboring ideas.
