I'm a PhD student in metaethics, and I'm trying to think through the wrong kind of reasons (WKR) problem as it bears on buck-passing accounts of value. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters who disagree about whether the WKR problem can be resolved without abandoning the buck-passer's reductive ambitions. One character defends the view that the distinction between object-given and state-given reasons adequately handles the problem; the other argues that this distinction either cannot be drawn without circularity or, once properly drawn, undermines the point of analyzing value in terms of reasons in the first place.

The dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth in which each character engages with the other's strongest arguments. Neither side should be a strawman. I'd like the exchange to work through at least the following pressure points, though the characters can raise others as well:

1. Whether the object-given/state-given distinction can be formulated without implicitly relying on the very evaluative notions (good, admirable, desirable) that the buck-passer set out to analyze.
2. The structure of the demon incentive case — whether it differs in kind from cases where a putative reason to φ is really just evidence that the object of φ-ing has some feature that makes φ-ing fitting, and whether this difference can be stated in reasons-theoretic terms alone.
3. Whether the buck-passer can retreat to a primitive, unanalyzed notion of fittingness (or to a "fitting-attitude" account that treats fittingness rather than reasons as fundamental) without thereby conceding that reasons-based analyses of value fail on their own terms.

The dialogue should be roughly 800–1000 words. Both characters should be presented as equally competent philosophers who know the literature well, even if they ultimately disagree.
