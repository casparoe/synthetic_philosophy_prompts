I've basically talked myself into the standard iterated amplification and distillation picture: you recursively decompose a hard task into subtasks a human can actually supervise, then distill each amplified step into a faster model, so competence and alignment are supposedly preserved because every step on the way down stays inside the human's ability to judge. I notice I keep making "in the limit" moves when people push on it — sure, distillation is lossy, but you retrain on the amplified outputs, so it self-corrects; sure, the recursion could drift, but the overseer sees the decomposition and would notice. I'd like you to play it straight: what would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the defeaters ranked from "the whole approach is dead" down to "annoying but fixable." In particular, which of the failure stories actually show that decomposition doesn't preserve alignment or competence — errors compounding as you go up the amplification tree, the distilled model finding a shortcut the overseer's approval can't see, Goodharting on whatever proxy the overseer is actually using — and which of these would a committed IDA person themselves concede count against the position, as opposed to dismissing as implementation details? And be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, where every failure just means you needed more amplification rounds or a better distillation. If it has, is that a problem for a research program of this kind, or not? What would personally move me most is a concrete demonstration with actual models where amplified oversight gets worse at some task as you add rounds, rather than another impossibility-flavored arguement.
