I'm a high school student who runs a philosophy discussion group. We've been reading about utilitarianism and its critics, and we've gotten stuck on the separateness of persons objection. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that utilitarianism treats the distribution of well-being across different people as if it were just the distribution of experiences within a single person's life — it aggregates everyone's pains and pleasures into one big sum, and in doing so it ignores the fact that there are distinct individuals whose lives are separate from one another. A sacrifice that would be irrational for one person to make can be rationally demanded by the utilitarian calculus when it's spread across many people. The objection says this is deeply wrong: the boundaries between persons matter morally, and utilitarianism erases them.

Our group has read the classic formulations of this objection, and we've read utilitarian replies — that the separateness of persons is less sharp than it seems, that personal identity is a matter of degree, and that the objection confuses the question of what's good with the question of whose good it is. We've also looked at the idea that the objection is just an intuition pump and that the utilitarian can bite the bullet.

Here's the thing: after weeks of discussion, we haven't moved. The people who found the objection compelling still find it compelling; the utilitarians in the group still think the replies are adequate. And as far as I can tell from reading beyond our group, the philosophical literature has been in a similar place for decades — the same moves and countermoves keep getting made, and nobody seems to have settled it.
