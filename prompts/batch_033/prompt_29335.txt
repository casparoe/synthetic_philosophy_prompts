I have been working through an objection to Hume's argument about testimony for miracles, and the version I have built seems to prove too much. I need help locating exactly where it breaks. Here is the reasoning.

(1) Belief should be proportioned to evidence, and where the evidence is testimonial, its force depends on the credibility of the witness and on how well the reported event coheres with our past experience.

(2) A miracle is a violation of a law of nature, and a law of nature is an exceptionless regularity established by uniform, unbroken experience.

(3) For any report of a miracle, the evidence against the event is therefore the whole of our uniform experience of how nature behaves, while the evidence for it is only the word of a finite, fallible human being.

(4) Consequently, testimony can never make it reasonable to believe that a miracle has occurred; it could do so only if the falsehood of the testimony would be more miraculous than the event reported, which it never is.

(5) But the same reasoning seems to apply to the first report of any event of a kind never before observed — a black swan, stones falling from the sky, a mammal that lays eggs. There too, uniform experience opposes the report and only fallible testimony supports it.

(6) Yet some such reports turned out to be true, and it was reasonable to accept them. So the argument seems to show either that the principle in (1) is false or that the move from (2) to (3) is illicit, unless Hume's conclusion in (4) is to be abandoned.

I would like to keep something like (4) if that can be done honestly. Which premise or step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a principled difference between a miracle and a merely unprecedented natural event that blocks the slide from (4) to (5), or is the distinction ad hoc? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, and use American English.
