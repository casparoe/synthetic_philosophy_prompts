I am at work on a novel in which one of the central characters is a reclusive nineteenth-century natural philosopher, loosely inspired by James Clerk Maxwell, who becomes obsessed with the idea that the second law of thermodynamics is not a law of nature at all but a limitation of human perception. In researching the period, I have been reading Maxwell's *Theory of Heat* (1871), and I keep returning to the famous passage in which he introduces what would later be called the demon:

> … if we conceive of a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is impossible to us. For we have seen that molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform, though the mean velocity of any great number of them, arbitrarily selected, is almost exactly uniform. Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and only the slower molecules to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics.

What I find puzzling — and what I suspect holds the key to the philosophical richness of the thought experiment — is the phrase "whose attributes are as essentially finite as our own." Maxwell seems to be doing something careful here: he gives the being a perceptual power we lack, yet he insists that the being is not in any fundamental sense superhuman. I am trying to understand what philosophical work this qualification is doing. Why does it matter that the being is finite like us? Is Maxwell already gesturing, even in 1871, toward the idea that the demon's finitude — perhaps the finitude of its memory, or the thermodynamic cost of the information it would need to gather and process — is what would ultimately reconcile the thought experiment with the second law? Or is he making a different kind of point, perhaps about the relationship between the microscopic and macroscopic levels of description, or about what it means to "do" something impossible?

I would be grateful for a close reading of the passage that helps me see what Maxwell meant by insisting on the demon's finitude, and how that insistence connects to the later history of the puzzle — particularly the idea, associated with Szilárd and Brillouin, that information is physical. I am less interested in a bibliography than in your own interpretation of the passage and its implications; I want to understand what Maxwell himself might have been reaching toward, so that I can render my character's intellectual trajectory with some fidelity.
