So I've been going down this rabbit hole about the Rietdijk–Putnam argument and the block universe, and I think I've convinced myself but I'm not sure I should be. The argument, as I understand it: special relativity says there's no absolute simultaneity — whether two events happen "at the same time" depends on your reference frame. So for any event in my future (say, me finishing my next novel), there's some observer moving at the right velocity relative to me for whom that event is happening *right now* in their present. If we don't want to privilege any particular reference frame as the one true "now" (which would go against the whole spirit of relativity), then we have to say that all events — past, present, future — are equaly real. The block universe.

As a novelist this is actually kind of beautiful to me. I spend my days constructing narratives where the ending already exists while I'm still writing chapter two. The reader's present moment in the story is different from the character's present moment. Time as a landscape you can walk around in — that's basically how I already think about narrative time. So the block universe feels like a metaphysics I can work with.

But I worry I'm just falling for something that flatters my priors. So I want to know: what counts against this? I'm not asking for a list of every objection to eternalism since the pre-Socratics. I want to know specifically what would count against the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe. What assumptions does it make that could turn out to be false? What would physics have to discover, or what philosophical moves would have to work, for the argument to collapse even if special relativity holds? Are there ways the argument could be wrong that are actually live possibilities, not just "well logically it's possible that..."? Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly which ones a defender of the argument would concede and which ones they'd dismiss.

And I want the response to turn the same scrutiny on itself: what would change *its* mind about this? Where's the weakest link in its own reasoning? If its giving me a map of the argument's exposure, I want to know how exposed the map itself is.
