I've been going in circles on an argument about counterfactual mugging and I need someone to tell me where the mistake is, because the conclusion is clearly insane but I can't find the faulty step.

Here's the setup. Omega (a perfect predictor) flips a fair coin. If heads, Omega gives you $10,000 — but only if Omega predicted that you would pay $100 in the tails case. If tails, Omega asks you to pay $100 right now. You don't know the coin result. The standard argument says you should pay: if you pay, that means you're almost certainly in a heads world where Omega predicted you'd pay, so you already got $10,000. If you don't pay, you're almost certainly in a tails world where Omega predicted you wouldn't pay, so you missed the $10,000. Paying is evidence that you already got the money, so you should pay.

Now here's the argument that seems to prove too much:

(1) You should pay the $100 in the original counterfactual mugging with a perfect predictor. The expected value of paying is way higher than not paying.

(2) The reasoning in (1) doesn't depend on the predictor being perfect. Even with a predictor that's only 70% accurate, the expected value of paying is still positive. The principle is: if someone has already taken an action that benefits you conditional on a prediction about your behavior, and you don't know whether they made the favorable prediction, your choice gives you evidence about which world you're in, and you should choose the action that correlates with the favorable prediction.

(3) But this principle generalizes wildly. Suppose a stranger walks up to you and says: "I flipped a coin this morning. If heads, I already deposited $10,000 in your bank account, conditional on my predicting that you'd hand me $100 now if tails." By the same reasoning, you should hand them the $100. Your paying is evidence that you're in the heads world where you got $10,000.

(4) And it doesn't stop there. If the stranger is delusional and has zero predictive accuracy, you might still have some nonzero credence that they're telling the truth, and for sufficiently high stakes, paying could still have positive expected value. Worse, you can't verify their accuracy without checking your bank account, but by the time you check, the opportunity to pay (and thus get evidence about the coin) might be gone.

(5) The argument seems to prove that you should pay off random people making bizarre claims about predictions and rewards. That's absurd. So the argument must be wrong somewhere.

Where's the flaw? Is (1) wrong even in the original counterfactual mugging case? Is (2) wrong about the principle generalizing to imperfect predictors? Or is (3) wrong about the principle applying to strangers? And whatever objection you give, does it also rule out one-boxing in Newcomb's problem, or can you keep that?
