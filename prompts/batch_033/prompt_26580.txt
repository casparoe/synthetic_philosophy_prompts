I've been reading the epistemology of disagreement literature for a while now and I'm struck by how little the central debate -- conciliationism versus steadfastness -- has actually moved since the mid-2000s. You've got conciliationists like Christensen and Elga saying that when you find out an epistemic peer disagrees with you, you've got to significantly revise your credence, and you've got steadfasters like Kelly and van Inwagen saying no, you can reasonably stick to your guns. Both sides have responded to each other's arguments, sure, but it feels like the responses are always "here's why your counterexample doesn't land" or "here's a new case that favors my view," and then the other side says the same thing back. Nobody seems to have changed their mind about the fundamental question.

Here's my working hypothesis: I think this debate is actually two different disagreements masquerading as one. One disagreement is about what rationality requires -- a normative question about epistemic oughts. The other is about what we can psychologically do -- a descriptive question about whether we can actually suspend judgment or split the difference when we confront a peer. And I suspect some of the steadfasters are really making the descriptive point (look, nobody actually conciliates in real life, not even conciliationists) while the conciliationists are making the normative point (it doesn't matter what we do, it matters what we should do). If that's right, the debate hasn't stalled because it's hard -- it's stalled because the two sides are answering different questions and keep talking past each other.

But I'm not sure my diagnosis is right. Maybe it's a genuine normative disagreement and the impasse is just because our intuitions about cases really do diverge at a bedrock level. Or maybe there's a hidden factual question -- like, maybe it turns on empirical facts about how often peer disagreement tracks which party is actually right, and we just don't have the data. Or maybe the whole thing is a verbal dispute about what counts as a "peer."

So: diagnose this debate for me. What kind of disagreement is it really? Is my talking-past-each-other hypothesis on the right track, or is something else going on? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate forward -- a new distinction, an empirical study, a formal result, a change of question? Or should we just accept that it's not going anywhere and why?
