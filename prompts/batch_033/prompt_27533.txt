I write about philosophy and the sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I recently read a short piece summarizing Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism. I would like to use it as background for an article, but I am not confident about how much of it to trust. The summary, as I remember it, ran roughly like this:

Williams's integrity objection is usually treated as a decisive refutation of utilitarianism. He asks us to consider cases such as a man offered a job developing chemical weapons who knows that if he refuses, someone more ruthless will take the job and do greater harm, or a traveller told that unless he shoots one innocent person, a squad will kill twenty. Utilitarianism appears committed to saying that in both cases the agent should do the appalling thing, because the consequences would be better. Williams takes this to reveal a deep flaw: the theory treats an agent's deepest commitments, to a spouse, to friends, to a life's work, as just another source of utility to be traded away whenever the ledger says so. In doing so, it demands that we sacrifice our integrity, by which Williams means something like honesty and fidelity to our own principles. The conclusion drawn is that utilitarianism is false. Williams is also said to have extended the point with the one-thought-too-many objection: he imagined a man choosing whether to save his wife or a stranger, and argued that if the man had to check whether saving his wife was permitted by an impartial moral rule, that extra thought would corrupt the motive, which is supposed to show that no impartialist moral theory can account for the reasons of personal relationships.

I would be grateful for an accuracy audit of that summary. Which parts would a careful defender of Williams accept, which are subtle misrepresentations, and does the conclusion it draws survive correction of the inaccurate parts? Please also separate what is settled among philosophers working on this material from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
