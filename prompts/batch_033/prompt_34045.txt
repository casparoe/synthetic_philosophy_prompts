As a retired physicist who has lately turned to philosophy, I have been working through the literature on objective chance and rational credence. Lewis's Principal Principle strikes me as an elegant codification of something intuitive: if you know the objective chance of an event, your degree of belief should track it. The principle says that a rational initial credence function C should satisfy

C(A | H_t & T_w) = Ch_t(A)

where H_t is the complete history up to time t and T_w is the complete theory of objective chance for the world. But I have now encountered the undermining-futures problem and the New Principle that Hall, Thau, and Lewis himself later proposed as a fix:

C(A | H_t & T_w) = Ch_t(A | T_w)

The New Principle evidently avoids the contradiction—the chance of T_w conditional on itself is trivially 1—but it also seems to back away from the original idea. Now you are not setting your credence to the chance simpliciter, but to the chance conditional on the chance theory's holding.

Please compare these two principles in depth. I would like the comparison to address the following:

(1) Under what conditions do the two principles actually diverge? Are the divergences confined to arcane self-referential cases, or do they propagate into ordinary probabilistic reasoning? Given my background, I would appreciate an assessment of whether a scientist interpreting, say, the output of a Monte Carlo simulation or a statistical-mechanical model would ever need to worry about which principle she is using.

(2) Does the New Principle capture the full intuitive content of deference to chance, or does it weaken the principle in a way that matters? If you think it does weaken it, explain what is lost. If you think it does not, explain why conditioning on T_w is a harmless technical adjustment.

(3) If you are dissatisfied with both principles, propose a modification and argue for its superiority. If you endorse one of them, defend it against the strongest objection you can think of.

I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature review; I am interested in a clear, argument-driven comparison that reaches a verdict.
