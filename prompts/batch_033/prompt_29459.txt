I fell down an s-risk rabbit hole a few months ago, right around when my partner and I started seriously talking about having kids, and now every normal decision seems to get swallowed by the same argument. I'll lay it out the way I keep reconstructing it:

(1) If an action has even a small positive chance of causing an astronomical amount of future suffering, its expected disvalue is astronomical, and that swamps ordinary pros and cons.

(2) For almost any action with long-run effects — having a child, taking a job in tech, publishing a blog post — the chance of its eventually making an s-risk worse is small but positive. We can't show it's zero, and the causal chain from anything to everything is too long to rule out.

(3) So by (1), almost any such action is wrong.

(4) But "trying to reduce s-risks" is itself an action with long-run effects and a positive chance of backfiring catastrophically — the safety work could enable the very disaster its meant to prevent.

(5) So the framework forbids the actions it was supposed to recommend, and also their opposites. A principle that forbids everything can't be the right guide.

(6) Therefore at least one step has to go.

I can't find the exit without losing what made s-risks seem important in the first place. If I reject (1), I'm not sure what's left of "take small probabilities of huge suffering seriously." If I reject (2), I'm claiming to know probabilities are zero when I don't. If I reject (4), I'm special-pleading for my own side. And if the problem is that "small" isn't the same as "small enough that the expected value is still huge," then I need a non-arbitrary rule for when a probability is too small to count, and I don't see one.

Where does this argument actually break, and what does whichever fix you pick cost? I'd rather keep taking s-risks seriously than give up on expected-value reasoning entirely, but I want to know what I'm paying.
