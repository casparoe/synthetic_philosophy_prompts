Been a longtime lurker on the rationalist blogs and this is the kind of thing I'd normally just chew on alone, but I'm stuck. I've been going back and forth on dialetheism — not just paraconsistent logic as a tool for reasoning about inconsistent theories without everything exploding, but the stronger claim that some contradictions are true in the territory. And I just reread Borges's "The Garden of Forking Paths," and it feels like it's doing something to that distinction that I haven't seen the usual posts touch.

Quick recap in case it's been a while: the story's labyrinth turns out to be a novel by Ts'ui Pên where, at every choice, all the outcomes happen simultaneously — the book doesn't pick a branch, it keeps every branch, even the mutually incompatable ones. So in the novel-within-the-story, the same man both kills the visitor and doesn't. What I want is a reading of the story through dialetheism and paraconsistent logic that takes both the story and the logic seriously.

Where is Borges actually dramatizing a dialetheia — a contradiction that's true in one world, not just spread across possible worlds — and where is he doing the cheaper many-worlds move where "both outcomes happen" is really just branching, and a dialetheist would say there's no contradiction at all? I keep going back and forth on whether the story is a picture of an inconsistent world or just a picture of an unmanageably huge consistent one.

Also, the story as a story: it's a spy confession, a meditation on a lost novel, and a puzzle about sending a message all at once, and the labyrinth is also a book. Is the text itself supposed to be an inconsistent object — the kind of thing you'd need a paraconsistent logic to even think about — or is that just a metaphor, and the actual logic never has to get its hands dirty? Does Borges know the difference between "the map contains contradictions" and "the territory is contradictory," or does he deliberately blur it? I'd rather get an honest answer that says where the story cheats than a reading that just uses Borges as a pretty illustration of Priest. And it'd be nice to end on what the story adds to the dialetheist's stock examples — the liar, the instant of change, that sort of thing — if anything. Not looking for a reading list or citations, just the actual reading.
