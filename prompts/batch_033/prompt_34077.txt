I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been working through Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950) for a paper I'm trying to write on the history of arguments against machine intelligence. Most of the paper is straightforward — the imitation game, the various objections, the digital computer as a universal machine. But I keep getting stuck on section 6, "Contrary Views on the Main Question," and specifically on subsection (9), the argument from extrasensory perception. I just can't figure out what Turing is doing there.

The passage that throws me is where Turing says he assumes the reader knows about ESP — telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis — and then calls these phenomena "disturbing" because they seem to deny all our usual scientific ideas. He says we'd like to discredit them, but then he claims the statistical evidence for at least telepathy is overwhelming. He goes on to say that if telepathy is real, then the imitation game as he set it up has a problem: the human interrogator might be receiving telepathic cues from the human witness, which would give the human an unfair advantage. His proposed fix is to build a "telepathy-proof room" for the test. And then he just… moves on.

What I can't parse is the tone. Is this a genuine concession? A piece of deadpan humor? A reductio of some kind? Turing seems to be treating ESP as a *real* empirical challenge to the validity of his test — he's not mocking it, he's proposing a physical countermeasure. That's wild for a paper that's otherwise so foundational to computationalism. My advisor says Turing was just being a good empiricist and taking the published parapsychology of his day at face value, but I've also heard people argue it's a subtle joke about the unfalsifiability of certain objections to AI. I need to figure out how to read this section before I can write about it, and honestly, the more I stare at it the less sure I am what the hell Turing meant. Any help?
