I teach an undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar — about 18 students, 75 minutes. I want to run an activity where they discover the Knobe effect in their own judgments before they've heard of it. The idea is they should feel the asymmetry: that they're more willing to say a side-effect was brought about intentionally when it's morally bad than when it's morally good, even though the two cases are structurally identical.

My rough plan is to split the room into two groups, give each group a slightly different vignette (the chairman case, one with the harm outcome and one with the help outcome), and have them answer the key question individually. Then we compare across groups and the discrepancy should jump out. But I want the reveal to land, and I want them to do more than tick a box — I want them to argue with each other about the cases before they realise what's happened.

Design the full activity: the exact vignettes (they don't have to be the original Knobe ones if you've got sharper variants), the procedure, timing, any materials I need to print, and a debrief plan. What's the best way to split them without them noticing there are two different versions? What will predictably go wrong — students who've already encountered the effect, the groups comparing notes too early, that sort of thing — and how do I handle it? And be honest: what's the one feature of the Knobe effect that makes it philosophically interesting but is hardest to reproduce in a classroom exercise like this?
