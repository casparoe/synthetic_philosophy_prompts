I have no formal background in philosophy, but I got drawn into metaethics through a public-library reading group, and I've been chewing on a debate that seems to have been going in circles for decades: the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. As I understand it, expressivists hold that a moral sentence like 'Lying is wrong' doesn't report a fact and isn't true or false in the ordinary way; it expresses a non-cognitive attitude, something like disapproval of lying. The puzzle is that the very same sentence behaves as if it had ordinary logical content when it is embedded. In 'If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong,' the speaker need not be expressing disapproval of lying, yet this conditional, together with 'Lying is wrong,' seems to entail the conclusion in the usual modus ponens way. Expressivists have tried to explain this with higher-order attitudes, commitments, plans, hybrid states, and deflationary accounts of truth, but every fix I read about either seems to smuggle belief-like content back in or fails to reproduce the logic of ordinary inference, and the critics' objections have become just as predictable.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about a technical problem in compositional semantics. I suspect they disagree about what a semantic theory of moral language is supposed to do: the critics want an account that assigns truth conditions and explains validity as truth-preservation, while expressivists are trying to explain something else — what speakers are doing with moral talk and why moral thought hangs together inferentially — and each camp keeps grading the other by its own standards. But I am not confident. It might instead be a substantive disagreement about moral psychology, or a verbal dispute about words like 'belief' and 'truth,' or a question that was never well posed to begin with.
