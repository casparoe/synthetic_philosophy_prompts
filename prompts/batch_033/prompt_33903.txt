**Exam question:**

In the literature on AI alignment, "impact measures" have been proposed as a way to prevent an AI system from causing harmful side effects while pursuing its assigned goal. Some authors have argued that impact measures can substantially reduce our reliance on a full specification of human values. Explain the core idea behind at least one such proposal, and then critically evaluate the claim that impact measures allow us to avoid side effects without needing a complete theory of value. In particular, discuss the role of the so-called inaction baseline and whether it reintroduces normative assumptions through the back door.

**Student's answer:**

Impact measures are a class of techniques designed to constrain an AI agent so that it achieves its objective while minimizing how much it changes the world beyond what is strictly necessary. The central motivation is that if we cannot fully specify human values — which seems likely given their complexity — we might still build safe AI by penalizing the agent for having a large causal influence on its environment.

The most influential approach combines two ideas: an inaction baseline and a penalty term. The inaction baseline is a reference policy that represents what the world would look like if the agent simply did nothing (or took only minimal, default actions). The agent's actual trajectory is then compared against this baseline, and any deviation that would reduce the "attainable utility" of a wide range of possible reward functions is penalized. This is the core of the Attainable Utility Preservation (AUP) framework, which was first proposed by Armstrong and Levinstein. The idea is elegant: by preserving the ability to achieve many different goals in the future, the agent avoids foreclosing options that humans might later care about, without ever needing to know which specific goals those are.

Does this fully sidestep the need to specify human values? I think it does, and here is why. The penalty term is defined over a distribution of reward functions that are, by design, agnostic about what humans actually want. The agent doesn't need to know whether we value biodiversity, economic growth, or artistic flourishing — it just needs to avoid making any of those goals permanently harder to achieve. The inaction baseline plays a crucial role here: it anchors the comparison so that "impact" is measured relative to a world where the agent doesn't intervene. Because the baseline is defined purely in terms of agent inaction, it carries no normative content whatsoever. It is simply a technical convenience, like setting the zero point on a ruler.

One objection is that some goals might be incompatible — preserving the option to industrialize a forest and preserving the option to keep it wild cannot both be maintained. But this is not a problem for impact measures specifically; it's a feature of any multi-objective setting. The agent will simply find a compromise that minimizes total impact across all reward functions, which is exactly what we would want from a safety perspective. A deeper worry is that impact measures might prevent the agent from doing anything useful at all, but this can be tuned by adjusting the penalty weight so that the agent is still allowed to have some effect on the world when its assigned task requires it.

In summary, impact measures like AUP solve the side-effect problem by penalizing deviations from an inaction baseline across many reward functions, and they do so without smuggling in any assumptions about what humans value. The inaction baseline is normatively neutral, and the distribution over reward functions is deliberately uninformative. This is what makes the approach so promising: it gives us a way to build safe AI even in the absence of a complete theory of value.
