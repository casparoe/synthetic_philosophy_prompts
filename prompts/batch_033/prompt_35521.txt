I retired from a career in evolutionary biology and have lately been working through the philosophy of biology, and one methodological dispute keeps drawing me back. A prominent line of argument in evolutionary psychology describes the human mind as a Swiss Army knife: a collection of functionally specialized cognitive tools, each shaped by natural selection to solve a distinct adaptive problem in the ancestral environment. On this picture, the researcher's task is reverse engineering — examine the structure of a cognitive mechanism, infer the adaptive problem it was built to solve, and then test for the predicted design features. The artifact analogy is made to do real work: it is supposed to show that adaptationist hypotheses about the mind can be disciplined and testable, rather than mere just-so stories. Critics reply that the analogy is exactly what makes the stories just-so, because an artifact invites a plausible function for every feature whether or not that function is real.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy. Please set out what corresponds to what: the designer, the knife, the individual blades, the design specifications, the manual, the manufacturing process, and the environment in which the tool is used. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from those that are incidental. For example, a Swiss Army knife has a known maker, a deliberate design, and one canonical function per blade, whereas a cognitive mechanism has a phylogenetic history, can be co-opted for new uses, and produces side effects that were never selected for. I want to know whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that reverse engineering can make evolutionary psychology a genuine science rather than a catalogue of just-so stories — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy would carry the methodological weight better.
