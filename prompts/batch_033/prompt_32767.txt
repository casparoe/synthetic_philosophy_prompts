In my philosophy club, I've ended up defending the position that taxation is theft. The core argument, as I understand it, is that taking someone's property without their genuine consent is theft, the state takes property through taxation, and the standard appeals to consent — voting, hypothetical social contracts, the benefits citizens receive — do not count as genuine consent because they are either non-optional or imposed retroactively. I find this argument compelling, but I worry I am not being critical enough. I want to know what would have to be true for the position to be false. Not just what people typically say in response — I have heard the standard replies — but a structured map of its vulnerabilities. What evidence, arguments, or conceptual distinctions would count against it? I would like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed defender of the view would themselves acknowledge as genuine problems rather than misunderstandings. I am also interested in whether the position has been insulated — whether, for example, "theft" has been defined in a way that makes the conclusion unavoidable regardless of how the state actually operates, and if so, whether that kind of insulation is a problem for a normative claim of this sort. Finally, I would like to know what the position predicts that its rivals do not: what would the world look like if the position were true, and does it match what we see?
