I've been reading about the Doomsday Argument on and off for years now — ever since I first encountered it on LessWrong and then worked through Nick Bostrom's Anthropic Bias. The basic move is simple enough: if you treat your birth rank among all humans who will ever live as a random draw from the set of all such humans, then you should expect to be roughly in the middle, which means there probably won't be a vast future population — and that has obvious implications for existential risk, space colonization, and how optimistic we should be. The argument has been around since the 1980s, and the literature on it is substantial. Yet as far as I can tell, the debate has been essentially frozen for at least fifteen years. The same moves recur: one side says the self-sampling assumption is the only principled way to handle indexical uncertainty, the other replies that the self-indication assumption avoids counterintuitive reference-class gerrymandering, someone points out that the whole thing hangs on what reference class you pick, someone else observes that the choice of reference class looks suspiciously like it's doing all the argumentative work, and then we go around again. No one seems to have changed their mind.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about probability theory at all — both sides seem to agree on the formalisms once you fix a reference class. It looks more like a disagreement about what counts as an acceptable starting point for anthropic reasoning: a bedrock intuition about whether you should reason as if you were randomly selected from the actual population or from the set of all possible observers. If that's right, then the impasse isn't a sign that the question is hard but that it's ill-posed — or at least that it's a question about which priors to adopt, and we don't have a procedure for adjudicating between priors. But I'm not confident in that diagnosis. Maybe there's a hidden empirical question — for instance, about whether we'll ever actually encounter other conscious species or simulation forks, which would give us additional data points and break the symmetry. Or maybe there's a formal result I'm missing that would force one side's hand.

I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement. What kind of disagreement is it, really? Is it a dispute about probability theory that could be settled by a theorem or an empirical discovery, a clash of unarguable starting intuitions, a verbal dispute about what "you" should condition on, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or is this one of those debates where no progress should be expected? Please separate what is settled among people who work on anthropic reasoning from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
