I'm a senior software architect and site reliability engineer. I spend my days thinking about distributed systems, incident response, and postmortem culture. I'm at home with concepts like root cause analysis, blame allocation in microservice architectures, fault domains, blast radius, circuit breakers, graceful degradation, error budgets, and the distinction between accountability and blame that underpins the blameless-postmortem approach.

I keep hearing about a debate in philosophy concerning "responsibility gaps" for autonomous systems. The worry, as I understand it, is roughly this: when an autonomous system — a self-driving car, a military drone, an algorithmic trading bot — causes harm, it can turn out that no human being is morally responsible for that harm. The designers could not have foreseen the specific decision the system made; the operator was not in control in the relevant sense; the system itself is not a moral agent. Responsibility seems to fall through the cracks, and we are left with a gap.

To me, this sounds like a familiar kind of problem. In a large distributed system, a production outage often has no single owner — service A was within its SLO, service B handled errors gracefully, the network behaved within spec, and yet the combination produced a cascading failure. We have a whole vocabulary for this: we talk about emergent failures, about the difference between a root cause and a contributing factor, about designing systems so that no single component's failure can take down the whole, and about the cultural shift from "who caused this?" to "what in the system made this possible?"

Restate the philosophical debate about responsibility gaps for autonomous systems in the vocabulary of distributed systems engineering as faithfully as you can. Use the concepts I actually work with: fault domains, blame versus accountability, incident postmortems, error budgets, defense in depth, and so on. Do not smuggle in the engineering framework's assumptions and call them a translation; represent the philosophers' claims as they are, and let the translation reveal what it reveals.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate translate cleanly into my vocabulary? Which parts translate only with distortion, and what gets lost in the distortion? Which parts have no counterpart at all in how we think about system failures — and for those, tell me whether the absence is a sign that my engineering vocabulary is missing something important, or whether it suggests that the philosophical worry is less clear than it first appears. I particularly want to know whether the blameless-postmortem culture I practice every day already has the answer to the responsibility gap, or whether the philosophers are gesturing at something that blamelessness cannot dissolve.
