I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in contemporary political philosophy, and I am grading the final exam. One of the essay questions was:

"Explain the distinction between option luck and brute luck and the work it is meant to do in luck egalitarianism. Does a luck egalitarian have to say that a person who suffers a serious loss through bad option luck deserves to bear that loss? In answering, discuss at least one major objection to luck egalitarianism and say whether it succeeds."

A student submitted the following answer:

"Luck egalitarianism rests on the distinction between option luck and brute luck, which G. A. Cohen develops in his account of equal access to advantage. Option luck is a matter of deliberate gambles: the person who buys a lottery ticket or starts a speculative business takes a risk and must live with how it turns out. Brute luck, by contrast, is a matter of how unchosen risks fall out: being struck by lightning or being born with a serious disability are standard examples. The luck egalitarian holds that inequalities flowing from brute luck are unjust and call for compensation, whereas inequalities flowing from option luck are ones the agent can reasonably be asked to bear. On this view, the gambler who loses a fair bet deserves the resulting loss, and society has no obligation to make it good.

Dworkin's hypothetical insurance market is the mechanism he proposes for fixing how much compensation is owed for brute bad luck such as handicaps. We imagine people facing an equal risk of developing a disability before they know whether they will be disabled, and ask what insurance they would buy at fair premiums; the compensation owed to the disabled is then set by the payouts those policies would have produced. This lets the view soften many apparent harshnesses, because prudent people would insure against at least catastrophic losses.

The most serious objection is Anderson's harshness objection. She argues that luck egalitarianism would abandon the imprudent to their fate: the uninsured motorist who crashes, or the worker who turns down training and later loses his job, would be told that his bad option luck is his own doing and that he has no claim to help. Anderson says this is not only callous but incompatible with equal respect, because it requires the state to classify some citizens as irresponsible before it assists them. I find the objection powerful against a crude luck egalitarianism, but not decisive against the view as a whole. A sophisticated luck egalitarian can reply that Dworkin's insurance device already cushions many cases of bad option luck, because a reasonable person would have insured against catastrophe. The real lesson of the objection is that luck egalitarians must not infer, from the fact that a loss was chosen, that the person is owed nothing. So the objection narrows the view but does not refute it."
