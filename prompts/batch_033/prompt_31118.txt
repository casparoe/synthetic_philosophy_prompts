I spent much of last evening arguing with a friend about whether Berkeley's master argument really establishes its conclusion or merely trades on a confusion between the act of conceiving and the thing conceived. I now have to grade a set of exam answers on this material, and I want the marking to be disciplined: no student should be rewarded or penalized merely for taking one side of that debate. Below is the question the students were set, followed by one student's answer. Please grade the answer for me.

Question. Explain Berkeley's master argument for the conclusion that sensible objects cannot exist unperceived by any mind. Your answer should distinguish the master argument from the other considerations Berkeley uses in support of idealism, and it should say how much of the conclusion the argument can establish on its own.

Student answer. Berkeley's master argument is his decisive proof that objects cannot exist outside any mind. If you try to imagine a tree standing in a park with no one there to perceive it, you will find that you cannot: the very act of imagining the tree brings it before your mind, so the tree you are imagining is a perceived tree. An unperceived tree is therefore inconceivable, in the same way that a square circle is inconceivable. Since whatever is inconceivable is impossible, the notion of an object existing wholly unperceived is self-contradictory. This is the same point Berkeley makes with the example of the water that feels warm to one hand and cold to the other, where sensible qualities turn out to exist only as they are perceived. To be clear, Berkeley is not denying that tables and chairs are real; he is denying that they are mind-independent material substances. A tree continues to exist when no human being perceives it only because God perceives it, and this is what his principle that the being of an idea consists in its being perceived comes to. Some critics object that Berkeley confuses the act of conceiving with the object conceived, but that objection is merely verbal: if I cannot form any idea of an unperceived object, then an unperceived object is not possible for me, and that is all the argument needs to establish.

Please assign a grade — a mark out of 20 or a letter grade, with whatever rubric you think appropriate — and give the point-by-point comments that justify it. I am more interested in the specific comments than in the final mark. In the comments, please:
- say what the answer gets right and where it goes wrong;
- separate errors that any competent reader of Berkeley would count as settled mistakes from points on which the answer merely takes one side of a genuinely contested question;
- wherever a point is contested, state the main rival view rather than presenting either the student's position or the standard objection as the consensus.
