I work on the discovery and ranking layer of a mobile app marketplace. When a user runs a search, the default sort is by total installs; the home screen opens with a trending carousel; every listing displays a download count and an aggregate star rating; and apps that cross certain thresholds receive a Top Charts badge. Internally, these features are justified by two claims that seem to me to pull in opposite directions. The product principles state that the marketplace should help users make informed, independent decisions, and the design team maintains that the features merely show users factual information about what other people have done, without taking any position on whether they should follow it. At the same time, our own A/B tests show that making popularity salient changes behavior: the same app shown with a higher install count gets more installs, and the ranking model is trained on engagement data that was itself partly produced by earlier rankings, so popularity feeds on itself. Our experimentation platform, in the meantime, assumes that users are effectively independent of one another, which is precisely the assumption these features seem designed to exploit or undermine.

I would like to understand what view of human decision-making this set of practices actually presupposes. Does it presuppose that the fact that many other people chose an app is evidence that the app is good, so that a rational user should weigh that fact alongside their own assessment? Or does it presuppose that users are liable to imitate others even when the crowd carries no information, or some mixture of the two? Can one coherent theory of choice underwrite both the popularity features and the stated commitment to independent decision-making, or is the team quietly committed to two inconsistent pictures of the user? Is the defense that we are only showing data and letting users decide sustainable, or does the decision to make popularity the default ranking already take a side? Reconstruct the implicit theory that the practice commits us to, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and say what would have to change in the product, the ranking defaults, and the way we design experiments if we replaced that theory with its opposite: for example, if we concluded that popularity carries no information about quality, or, at the other extreme, that users are pure conformists whose choices reveal nothing about the product.
