We have spent several weeks on backward induction and the puzzles it generates — the centipede game, the unexpected hanging, and so on — and I am trying to work out whether the logic actually applies to a situation I am in.

A coursemate and I have been developing a board game together over the past term. We have settled into an arrangement where we take turns designing components: I draft a set of mechanics, then he designs the card deck around them, then I work on the board layout, then he handles the rulebook, and so on. Each round of work makes the game more polished and more likely to attract a publisher's interest. We have both put in substantial time already, and there are perhaps four or five more rounds of work before we would have something ready to pitch.

Here is the difficulty. At any point, either of us could take the existing materials, develop the remainder alone, and approach publishers solo — cutting the other out entirely. We have never discussed this possibility openly, but we both know it is there. My turn is coming up next. I am genuinely torn: if I do my part, I increase the project's value, but I also make it more tempting for him to walk away with everything on his next turn. And I can see that he must be thinking the same thing about me. If I follow the backward-induction reasoning we covered in lectures, it seems to say I should take the materials now — but that feels wrong, and it would also guarantee we end up with a weaker game than if we both kept cooperating.

I would like a committed answer: should I cooperate on my next turn, or should I take the materials and go solo? I want you to take a definite position and argue for it, drawing on the theoretical resources from rational choice theory and game theory — but I also want you to say whether you think backward induction actually dictates the answer here, or whether there is something about this situation that resists that kind of reasoning.
