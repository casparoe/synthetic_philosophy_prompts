I've been working through the decision-theoretic literature on money pump arguments, and I find myself unable to settle on a bottom-line assessment. The basic structure is familiar enough: an agent with preferences that violate some putatively rational requirement — intransitivity over lotteries, say, or a pattern of preferences that violates Independence and thereby generates dynamic inconsistency — can be led through a sequence of trades, each of which looks favorable at the moment it is made, but whose combined effect leaves the agent with an outcome they disprefer relative to their starting position. The argument then claims that vulnerability to such a pump reveals irrationality in the preference structure itself, not merely an unfortunate practical predicament.

But the dialectic gets tangled fast. The sophisticated agent foresees the whole sequence and refuses the first trade, apparently blocking the pump — yet critics reply that the sophisticated agent's refusal pattern itself exhibits a kind of irrationality (for instance, they end up choosing a dominated option in a sequential choice problem, or they violate principles of backward induction). Meanwhile, some theorists argue that the real work is done not by the diachronic story at all but by a synchronic consistency condition that the money pump merely dramatizes; others think the diachronic setting is essential and that the argument's force evaporates once we allow agents to be resolute or to bind themselves. I have a rough sense that money pumps are pointing at something genuine, but I'm not sure how much of that "something" survives scrutiny.
