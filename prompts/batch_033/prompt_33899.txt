I did forty years in experimental physics — mostly scattering and spectroscopy, the kind of work where you're trying to infer the structure of something you can't poke directly from signals that are messy and incomplete. So I'm comfortable with observables, latent variables, model selection, identifiability, the whole apparatus. A few years into retirement I started reading philosophy, and the problem of other minds immediately grabbed me because it looked like an inference problem. We can't observe consciousness directly; we observe behavior, speech, maybe brain activity. We infer minds. Classic latent-variable problem, right?

But the more I read the more I feel like something buckles when I try to actually do the translation. The philosophers don't seem to be arguing about likelihood functions or priors. They're arguing about whether the inference is analogical or abductive, whether we perceive minds directly or infer them, whether the whole framing is a Cartesian mistake, etc. And I can't tell how much of this is genuine structure I'm missing because my vocabulary is too coarse, and how much is philosophers talking past each other in ways that would dissolve if you forced them to state their claims in terms of observables and models.

So here's the assignment. Restate the problem of other minds — the skeptical worry, the argument from analogy, the inference-to-the-best-explanation move, and any of the contemporary alternatives you think matter (I keep seeing "direct perception" and "phenomenological" approaches waved at) — in the vocabulary of statistical inference: observables, latent variables, likelihoods, priors, model identifiability, underdetermination, model comparison. Do it term by term, claim by claim, as rigorously as you can. Don't just wave your hands and say "it's like Bayesian inference." I want to see the mapping.

Then the hard part: the residue report. Which bits of the philosophical debate translated cleanly? Which bits you had to stretch or fudge? Which bits have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? And then give me your honest verdict: is the untranslatable residue pointing to something real that my framework can't handle — something about other minds that is genuinely outside the inference paradigm — or is it pointing to philosophical claims that were never precise enough to survive translation, and the excercise exposes them as, well, not much?

I'm not looking for the right answer. I'm looking for an honest map of what carries and what doesn't, and what that tells us.
