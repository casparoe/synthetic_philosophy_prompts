I host a small podcast about decision theory and the places where expected-value reasoning gets weird, and in two weeks Im recording with a philosopher who's known for arguing that the two-envelope paradox is a prior problem rather than a genuine paradox about expected value: the switching argument secretly assumes a uniform distribution over all possible amounts of money, and since no such distribution exists, the 1.25x calculation never gets off the ground. I can state the standard setup fine (two envelopes, one holds twice the other, you pick one, and then you reason that the other is equally likely to be half or double, so switching has expected value 1.25 times whatever you hold), and I can repeat his resolution back to him, but it still feels like a bit of a magic trick, and a lot of my listeners have said the same thing.

Write me a set of interview questions, as many as you think a tight hour needs, that pressure-test this resolution instead of having him restate it. The sequence should dig into at least these spots: (1) why seeing, say, $20 in your envelope doesn't make $10 and $40 equally likely, and what would actually have to be true about the setup for that 50/50 thought to be licensed; (2) the apparent symmetry between the envelopes before you open one, and what exactly he thinks breaks that symmetry; (3) the finite-bankroll version where the maximum possible prize is capped, so all the relevant expectations are finite and the usual diagnosis can't be invoked — does the paradox just come back for some amounts; (4) whether his prior-based resolution is secretly the old risk-aversion point that log utility makes switching a wash, or whether those are two genuinely different cures, and which one he takes to be doing the real work. For each of these, include the follow-up you'd ask after the answer you expect him to give — especially the follow-up that pushes on the weakest step in that answer. End with one question about what a legitimate two-envelope-style argument would look like, if any, so the interview doesn't leave the impression that expected-value reasoning only fails when it's convenient.
