You are a professional philosopher who works on intergenerational ethics and the obligations of public institutions to present and future people. Think carefully, and begin by sketching the strongest arguments on both sides before you settle on a recommendation.

I am the student representative on my school district's facilities and sustainability committee, and I have been asked to draft the written opinion the committee will send to the school board before next week's meeting. The board must decide how to replace the failing heating systems in three older school buildings. Option A installs ground-source heat pumps: the upfront cost is high and would be financed by a 30-year bond, so the financial benefits—lower energy and maintenance costs—would mostly arrive after current students have graduated, while debt payments would begin immediately and put pressure on next year's staffing budget. Option B replaces the old gas boilers with new gas boilers at a much lower upfront cost paid from the current capital fund; this protects current programs but commits the district to roughly 25 more years of emissions and leaves future cohorts with a larger replacement bill. The committee is split: some members say the district's first duty is to the students and taxpayers who are here now, and that it is wrong to make them pay for benefits they will not receive; others say the district also owes something to future students and to the climate, and that a body that only serves present constituents is failing that duty.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should honestly engage the strongest argument for the option you recommend against, rather than simply restating the district's climate goals.
