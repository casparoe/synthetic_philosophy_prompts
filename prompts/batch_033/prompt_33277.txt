I have come to accept a dialetheist treatment of the semantic paradoxes: the Liar sentence is both true and false, at least some contradictions are true, and the correct response is to adopt a paraconsistent logic that blocks the principle of explosion rather than to revise classical semantics in a consistent direction. I want you to tell me what would count against this view. Please do not restate the view or give me a general introduction; I want a structured map of its exposure. Which arguments, formal results, and cases should genuinely move a dialetheist, and which should be set aside as question-begging or merely unwelcome? In particular, address the following, but do not limit yourself to them: Curry's paradox and the question of whether the standard dialetheist replies require weakening the conditional or structural principles far beyond what the paradoxes themselves force; the strengthened Liar and whether the dialetheist can express the claim that a sentence is false only, or true and not false; whether dialetheism can give a stable account of rejection, denial, and rational belief if some contradictions are true; the risk that the view collapses into trivialism or into a merely verbal variant of a consistent paraconsistent approach; and whether the minimal-mutilation argument really favors dialetheism over paracomplete alternatives. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that the view's defenders would themselves concede as counting against it from those they would dismiss, and give an honest assessment of whether dialetheism has been insulated by qualifications—and, if it has, whether that insulation is a problem or a reasonable cost of the view.
