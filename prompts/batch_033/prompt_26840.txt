I've been working through the antinatalist literature lately, and I find myself increasingly convinced by Benatar's asymmetry — the claim that there is a fundamental axiological difference between the presence and absence of pain and pleasure when we evaluate possible lives. Specifically: the presence of pain in a life is bad, and the presence of pleasure is good; but the absence of pain in a life never lived is good, even though there is no one there to enjoy that good, while the absence of pleasure in a life never lived is not bad, because there is no subject who is deprived of it. This asymmetry is what does the work in the argument that coming into existence is always a harm — the ledger of existence has a bad entry (pain) and a good entry (pleasure), while the ledger of nonexistence has a good entry (absent pain) and an entry that is merely not-bad (absent pleasure), so nonexistence wins.

What worries me is that the asymmetry has a kind of suspicious immunity. Every time I see someone try to attack it — by offering a symmetrical case, by denying that absent pain can be good without a beneficiary, by arguing that the asymmetry collapses into a simpler view about person-affecting harm — the defender's reply is almost always that the objector has failed to hold the relevant contrasts steady, or has smuggled in an intuition that already assumes the asymmetry is false. I want to know whether this is just what a correct axiological insight looks like when it's genuinely basic, or whether the position has been quietly insulated from falsification. So I'm asking for a map of the asymmetry's genuine exposure. What would count against it? I want the strongest arguments and thought experiments arrayed against it, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. I want to know which of those a committed defender of the asymmetry would actually concede as counting against it — not just what critics say, but what the view's own proponents would admit is a cost. I want to know what other commitments the asymmetry drags along with it: about the badness of death, about whether we can weigh absent goods and bads at all, about the coherence of impersonal value, about procreative ethics in edge cases. And I want an honest answer to the meta-question: has the asymmetry been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, is that a defect in this case, or is it just what one should expect of a claim at the foundations of population ethics, where nearly everything is underdetermined by intuition and the only real test is whether a view generates conclusions we can live with?
