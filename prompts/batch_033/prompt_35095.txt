I'm a retired physicist. For four decades I built and tested mathematical models of systems I couldn't directly observe — scattering amplitudes, effective field theories, renormalization-group flows. I'm comfortable with the idea that a model can contain terms that are predictively indispensable without being literally true, and with the question of when we should upgrade a modeling convenience to the status of something real.

I've recently been reading the interpretability literature in deep learning, and I keep running into the same dispute: do neural networks possess genuine internal representations — features, circuits, or concepts that are really there in the trained network — or are the structures that interpretability researchers extract merely artifacts of their probing methods, akin to overfitting a lens to see patterns that the network itself does not use? The realist says that when we find, say, a neuron or a direction in activation space that tracks a coherent feature across inputs, we are discovering something about the network's actual computational economy. The anti-realist says we are projecting our own categories onto a high-dimensional object and finding what we went looking for.

I want to know whether this is genuinely the same philosophical problem in a new domain, or whether the neural network case introduces something — perhaps about the relation between the investigator and the investigated system, or about the status of artifacts generated by a measurement process that is itself a learned model — that the philosophy of science has not yet digested.
