so me and my friend got into this huge argument last night about aristotle and functionalism and its been bugging me all day. we were reading de anima for this philosophy of mind course and my friend is convinced that aristotle is basically a proto-functionalist — he points to the whole thing where aristotle says the soul is to the body what sight is to the eye, and the bit about how if an axe were a living thing its soul would be its capacity to cut. the idea being that what matters is the function or the form, not the specific material. so my friend's line is that if you transported aristotle to the 20th century and explained turing machines and computationalism to him, he'd be like "yes, obviously, the mind is the program and the brain is the hardware, i've been saying this for 2300 years."

but i'm not convinced and we went back and forth on it for hours. the part i kept coming back to is that aristotle's hylomorphism seems way more entangled with the actual living body than functionalists usually admit. like, the soul is the form of a natural organized body that potentially has life — it's not just any form in any matter. the matter has to be the right kind of matter, and the form and matter are internally related in a way that doesn't map neatly onto the software/hardware distinction. plus aristotle is pretty clear that the soul isn't separable from the body the way a computational description might be — the whole point of hylomorphism is that you can't just peel off the form and stick it in something else. and then there's his teleology, the idea that the body's organs are for the sake of the soul's activities, not just accidently correlated with them. that seems like a much thicker relationship than functionalism's "whatever realizes the causal role."

so here's my question. imagine you could actually sit aristotle down and walk him through the whole contemporary functionalist picture: turing's formalism, putnam's argument that pain isn't identical to c-fiber firing because an octopus or a silicon-based alien could be in the same functional state with different wetware, the idea that mental states are individuated by their causal roles — the whole package. given what he actually wrote and believed, not just the de anima greatest hits but the metaphysics and the physics too, what would he say? would he recognize functionalism as a natural extension of his own framework, or would he see it as a fundamental distortion of what he was trying to do? and if he'd reject it, what specific piece of his philosophy would be the sticking point — the teleology, the account of substance, the role of the living body, something else? i want a real prediction with reasoning, not just "well it depends how you interpret him." my friend and i already did that part for two hours and neither of us budged.
