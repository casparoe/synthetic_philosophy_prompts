So at a meetup two friends of mine got into it over the centipede game. We'd actually played a few rounds (with real money, small stakes, six rounds) and almost nobody took on the first round — people passed and the pot grew and both players ended up with more than if someone had grabbed early.

Nate is the kind of guy who reads decision theory papers for fun, and he was insisting that the only rational play is to take on round one. His argument was the standard backward induction one: at the final round, taking dominates passing. So at the second-to-last round, you know a rational partner will take at the final round, so you should take now. And this unravels all the way back to round one. Rational players defect immediately and the game is boring.

Lena (who does stats for a living) said this is a case where the theory is giving you the wrong answer for the actual situation. She said backward induction assumes that if you pass, your partner continues to believe you're rational and plans to take at the next opportunity — but in a real game, passing is a move that should make your partner update. If you pass, you're signaling that you might pass again, and a rational partner should incorporate that signal. She said the backward induction argument only works if common knowledge of rationality is preserved no matter what moves are played, which is exactly what's in question.

Nate came back with: that's just describing how to exploit irrational players. The fact that you can make money off people who don't reason through the game correctly doesn't mean the correct reasoning is wrong. The solution concept is about what rational players do against rational players. If you're playing against someone who makes mistakes, sure, exploit that, but the game-theoretic solution isn't claiming to be a money-making strategy against humans.

Lena said he was begging the question. She said: imagine you actually find yourself at round four. You now have to decide whether to take or pass. Nate's theory says you should take, because your partner will take at round five. But if your partner is rational, how did they let the game get to round four in the first place? If you can't answer that, then your model of "rational partner at round four" is incoherent, and you can't use it to decide what to do. She said this is the standard critique — the one about how backward induction falls apart when you hit nodes off the equilibrium path and your beliefs about the other player's rationality have to be revised.

Nate said that's exactly backwards: the fact that you can't give a coherent story about how rational players reach round four is precisely *why* rational players don't reach round four. The whole point of the equilibrium analysis is that those off-path nodes don't happen. You don't need a theory of what happens there because you never get there.

Lena said: okay, but then the equilibrium analysis isn't actually giving you a decision procedure. It's telling you where an idealized model ends up, but if you're sitting across from another human being with cash on the table and that human just passed to you, you're *in* an off-path node whether the theory likes it or not. The question is what to do now, and a theory that says "this situation is impossible" isn't helpful — you need a theory that says "this situation is improbable for the following reason, and here's how you should update." She added that in the actual games we played, the strategy of "take immediately" looses to "pass a couple times and see what happens" against real opponents, and if your theory of rationality consistently underperforms against actual humans, maybe it's answering a different question than the one you think it's answering.

I'm especially interested in whether the off-equilibrium-path objection Lena raised is fatal to backward induction as a normative theory, or whether she and Nate are just talking past each other about what equilibrium concepts are even supposed to do.
