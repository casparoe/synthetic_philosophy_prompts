So I'm a philosophy professor and I've gotten myself into an interesting situation. The county vector control district reached out to the university looking for someone to help them think through the ethics of their mosquito control program, and somehow I'm the person they found. I've been meeting with them for a few months now — I sit in on their planning meetings, I've gone out with the field crews, I've read a ton of their technical reports. They're good people; they're not just looking for ethical cover for what they already want to do. But they're also not philosophers, and they need something they can actually use.

The literature on the ethics of insecticides and invertebrate suffering is actually pretty rich — you've got the animal ethics people (Singer, McMahan, the wild animal suffering crowd), you've got the environmental ethics tradition that thinks about ecosystems rather than individuals, and you've got the public health ethics framework that's mostly about balancing risks to human populations. The problem is that none of these translate directly into a decision procedure for the kinds of calls they actually make: should we fog this neighborhood for adult mosquitoes after a West Nile positive? Should we treat these catch basins with larvicide? Is aerial spraying over the wetlands ever justified? They make these decisions weekly during mosquito season, often with a 48-hour turnaround, and the stakes are real — people can get sick, and the treatments kill a lot of non-target insects including bees and butterflies that people in the community care about.

What I want to build for them is a step-by-step decision procedure — something like a flowchart in words — that walks them through the relevant ethical considerations in order. It should start with the human health risk (what's the actual threat, how well is it established), then move through the question of necessity (is this about preventing a real harm or just about convenience and comfort), then the expected suffering inflicted on target and non-target insects, then the availability of less harmful alternatives. At each step, the procedure should say what counts as a sufficient answer to move forward, where it stops with a "no, don't treat," and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is fundamentally one of values rather than facts. I want the procedure to be honest about its own normative commitments — for instance, it's going to have to take some stance on whether insect suffering matters at all, and if so how much relative to human welfare, and I'd rather that be explicit than smuggled in.

Then I'd like you to run the procedure on two real-ish cases: (1) a request to fog a residential neighborhood for adult mosquitoes where there's been a confirmed West Nile positive in a trap, using a pyrethroid that the district's own data shows will cause significant collateral kill of pollinators; and (2) a proposal to apply Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis larvicide in storm drains — very targeted, very low non-target effects, but it requires monthly reapplication all summer and the disease risk is lower (it's preventative, not responsive to a positive test). And I'd like at least one case where the procedure says "don't treat" even though the political pressure to spray is enormous — because that's the test of whether the procedure is doing any real work or just rubber-stamping whatever they were going to do anyway.
