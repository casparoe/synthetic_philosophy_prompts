I have noticed a recurring pattern across several domains of inquiry, and I am trying to determine whether it has been identified and analyzed as a unified phenomenon, or whether the resemblance I am seeing is merely superficial.

The pattern is this. When someone is uncertain about what to believe or what to do, they sometimes try to resolve the uncertainty not by examining the evidence or arguments more carefully in the moment, but by asking what they themselves would conclude under better conditions. "Better conditions" can mean many things: if they were more intelligent, if they had more time to deliberate, if they knew all the relevant facts, if they were free of the particular biases or fatigue they are currently experiencing, or if they had undergone some process designed to improve their judgment.

I have encountered this move in several distinct settings:

1. In moral philosophy, there is a long tradition of appealing to what an impartial, fully informed spectator would approve of, or what would be chosen behind a veil of ignorance, or what norms would be accepted under idealized discourse conditions. The common thread seems to be: instead of asking directly whether an act is right, you ask what judgment would survive a certain kind of idealized scrutiny.

2. In everyday practical reasoning, I have heard people say things like "I do not know what to think about this right now, but I trust what I would conclude if I were not so tired," or "I am going to defer to what I will want after I have had a proper night's sleep and thought it through." The present self treats the judgment of a better-positioned future or counterfactual self as authoritative.

3. In epistemology and decision theory, there is discussion of what an ideally rational agent would believe or choose, and this is sometimes treated as normative for actual agents — as though the way to determine what you ought to believe is to ask what a perfectly rational version of you would believe in your evidential situation.

4. In some discussions about the long-term future of artificial intelligence, I have seen the suggestion that we should not try to specify human values directly, but should instead build systems that work out what we would want if we were more informed, more reflective, and less subject to the distortions of the moment.

What strikes me is that in all these cases the underlying move is structurally similar: you defer to a counterfactual, improved version of yourself (or of a generic agent) rather than tackling the question head-on with the cognitive resources you actually have. But I can also see differences. Sometimes the idealized version is defined by substantive properties (impartiality, full information, internal consistency), and sometimes by procedural ones (more time, less bias, better conditions for reflection). Sometimes the appeal is to a single idealized judge whose verdict you try to approximate; sometimes it is to a process of improvement you would undergo, whose endpoint you try to anticipate.

My question is this: is there an established name for the general pattern — the move of resolving uncertainty by deferring to a more idealized version of your own deliberative process or its output? And has anyone treated this as a unified phenomenon that cuts across ethics, epistemology, and decision theory, rather than as a set of separate moves that merely happen to share a shape? I am less interested in receiving a bare label than in understanding whether the similarity I am noticing is deep or shallow. If the cases are genuinely instances of the same thing, what exactly is that thing, and where are its boundaries? If they are only superficially similar, what is the most principled way to carve them apart?

Finally, I would like the response to indicate where its classification is most vulnerable: which alternative way of drawing the boundaries would be most plausible if the central cases turn out to differ in some important but non-obvious respect, and what consideration would be most likely to shift the analysis.
