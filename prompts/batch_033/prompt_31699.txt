Friedrich Nietzsche wrote at length about the connection between physiology and philosophy, insisting that a thinker's diet, climate, and bodily habits are not incidental to his ideas but belong to their very substance. He was withering about the pursuit of health as an end in itself, tracing in the ascetic ideal a will to self-denial that he thought had outlasted its theological scaffolding. At the same time, he was himself minutely attentive to his own digestion, his sleep, the altitude at which he lived, and the effects of particular foods on his capacity to think — and he was not shy about asserting that some diets conduce to greatness while others produce only resentment and mediocrity.

Now consider the landscape of contemporary health optimisation. We have the quantified-self movement, in which people track sleep stages, heart-rate variability, continuous glucose, and dozens of other biomarkers, using the data to fine-tune their performance. We have biohackers who treat the body as a system to be reverse-engineered and upgraded through nootropics, cold exposure, fasting protocols, and experimental supplements. We have wearable devices that nudge their users toward meeting daily targets, turning health into a gamified project of self-management. And we have a broader cultural sensibility in which maximising one's longevity and functional capacity is treated as an obviously rational goal, almost a duty.

What would Nietzsche have made of this? On one reading, the quantified self is a textbook case of the ascetic ideal in modern dress: a regime of self-surveillance and self-discipline that locates meaning in the suppression of impulse and the pursuit of a purified, risk-minimised body — a body that, in its anxious optimisation, expresses not strength but a fear of decay. On another reading, the biohacker who seeks to push past ordinary human limits, who regards the body as raw material for the will, could be seen as an instance of the will to power expressing itself through techno-scientific mastery — something closer to the Übermensch than to the ascetic priest.

I would like you to work through this tension. What resources does Nietzsche's thought provide for distinguishing between these two interpretations, and can the distinction be made to hold? Would he see a difference in kind between the biohacker who wants to transcend human limits and the wellness enthusiast who wants to eliminate risk, or would he regard both as expressions of the same underlying impulse, differently styled? How should we weigh his own fastidious dietary self-regulation against his critique of those who make health an ultimate value? And are there features of contemporary health culture — the role of algorithmic recommendation, the social-comparison dynamics of shared metrics, the commercial interests that shape what is tracked and optimised — that resist his categories altogether, because they belong to a form of social control he did not anticipate? I am not after a one-sentence verdict; I want you to apply his concepts to cases he never encountered and say where they illuminate, where they strain, and where they break.
