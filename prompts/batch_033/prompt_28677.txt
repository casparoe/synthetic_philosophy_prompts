I am a professor of metaphysics, and I have spent several months in correspondence with a colleague who designs programming languages and database schemas for a living. He works fluently with a precise vocabulary — types and terms, classes and instances, nominal and structural typing, compile-time and run-time existence, type erasure and reified types, inheritance and composition, schemas and the data that satisfy them — but he has never read academic philosophy. In our exchange he has repeatedly claimed that the problem of universals is nothing more than a disagreement about whether types should be reified at run time, and he tends to describe every nominalist position as a form of type erasure. I want to know how much of the standing metaphysical dispute can be represented in that formal, artefact-oriented vocabulary before it quietly changes subject.

Please restate the principal positions in the problem of universals — realism in both a strong (ante rem) and a moderate (in re) form, class nominalism, resemblance nominalism, trope theory, conceptualism, and the deflationary view that the problem is a pseudo-problem — in my colleague's vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. For each position, distinguish what translates cleanly, what can be translated only by being distorted, and what has no expression at all. Then provide the residue report: set out which commitments of the original positions fail to survive the translation, and offer a judgement on whether the residue exposes a gap in the software vocabulary (something it cannot say because it lacks a notion of mind-independent existence, grounding, or truthmaking, for example) or whether it reveals that some of the metaphysical positions are themselves less precise than they appear. I am less interested in clever analogies than in an honest account of where the translation breaks down, so please flag every place where you have had to fudge.

Please write your answer in British English.
