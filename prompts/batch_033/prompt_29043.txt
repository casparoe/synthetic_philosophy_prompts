I work as a software engineer on product analytics, and I keep running into the same situation: an overall comparison points one way, but when I split the data by some variable, the comparison flips. My gut reaction about which result to trust differs from case to case, and I cannot articulate the rule behind my reactions. I know this kind of reversal has a name, but the name alone does not tell me which direction to trust in a given case. I would like you to extract the rule I seem to be applying, state it precisely, and tell me where it breaks.

Here are the cases.

1. Referrer and retention. We compared two referrers, A and B, on 30-day retention. Overall, A-referred users retain better: 42% versus 36%. But A sends us mostly iOS users, and iOS users retain better regardless of referrer. Split by platform, B is better within both platforms: on iOS, A 50% versus B 52%; on Android, A 30% versus B 34%. My verdict: I trust the platform-split result. B is the better referrer, and the overall comparison is misleading.

2. Signup page and activation. We tested an old signup page against a new one. Overall, the new page produces more activated accounts: 45% versus 40%. But when we split users by time spent on the signup page, the old page produces more activations within every time-on-page bucket. My verdict: I trust the overall result here. Time on page is something the page version itself changes, so splitting on it is misleading.

3. Banner exposure and satisfaction among purchasers. We looked only at users who made a purchase and found that users shown a discount banner at checkout reported lower post-purchase satisfaction than users not shown it. But across all users, seeing the banner and satisfaction appear to be unrelated. My verdict: I do not trust the purchasers-only comparison. Restricting the analysis to purchasers creates an association that I doubt reflects the banner's actual effect.

4. Plan type and revenue. We compared two subscription plans, Basic and Pro, on revenue per user. Overall, Pro users generate more revenue. But when we split users by how many support tickets they filed in their first month, Basic generates more revenue within every ticket-count bucket. My verdict: I trust the overall result. Support-ticket count is downstream of plan choice, so splitting on it feels like controlling away part of what Pro does.

5. Tutorial completion and retention. We compared users who completed an onboarding tutorial with those who skipped it on long-term retention. Overall, completers retain much better. But among users who were active in their first week, completers and skippers retain at about the same rate. My verdict: I am genuinely torn. On one hand, the tutorial seems to cause first-week activity, which would make this like case 2 or case 4. On the other hand, the week-one-active comparison feels informative in a way that the time-on-page comparison does not, and I cannot say why.

Cases 1 and 2 are the pair that bothers me most. In both, an overall result and a within-bucket result point in opposite directions, but I trust the split in case 1 and the overall in case 2. The only difference I can gesture at is that platform is true before the user ever encountered the referrer, whereas time on page happens after the user saw the page version. My working hypothesis is therefore something like: condition on variables that were true before the treatment, and never on variables that happen after it.

Please test that hypothesis against all five cases, repair it if necessary, and state the resulting principle precisely. In particular, tell me what the principle says about case 5, and about a case I have not listed: two email subject lines are compared on open rate, subject line assignment is based on signup cohort, and the overall result conflicts with the result split by signup cohort. If no single principle gets all five verdicts, say which verdict it forces me to drop and why.
