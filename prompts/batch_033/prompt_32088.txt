I have recently retired from a career in the physical sciences and have begun to read philosophy seriously, particularly the literature on rational choice. An incident from last autumn continues to trouble me, and I would be grateful for a considered judgement.

A former student of mine, now a self-employed inventor, wrote to me requesting a gift of £1,000. His letter explained that he has devised a small piece of apparatus which, if he throws a certain switch, will bring about the suffering of a truly enormous number of sentient beings—he spoke of billions of simulated minds in torment for a duration comparable to the remaining life of the universe. He conceded that the chance his apparatus works as described is minuscule; he put it at one in a trillion, and he invited me to regard that estimate as generous. Nevertheless, he argued, the expected harm of my declining to pay is astronomical, since a one-in-a-trillion chance of such an enormity still dwarfs the certain loss of £1,000. He assured me that if I pay, he will dismantle the device and provide a notarised statement to that effect.

I find myself unable to fault his arithmetic, yet I am strongly disinclined to pay, and I suspect that most reasonable people would share my reluctance. Is this reluctance merely a failure of nerve—an inability to live with the implications of expected-utility reasoning—or is there a defensible principle that would justify refusing him? I would especially value an account of where such reasoning goes wrong, if in fact it does.
