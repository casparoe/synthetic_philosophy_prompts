"Kripke's central insight in Naming and Necessity is that the traditional philosophical picture which identified the necessary with the a priori and the contingent with the a posteriori is mistaken. According to Kripke, these are two separate distinctions: the necessary/contingent distinction is metaphysical (about how things could have been), while the a priori/a posteriori distinction is epistemological (about how we can know something). The traditional view goes back to Kant, who held that all necessary truths are analytic and thus knowable a priori, while all synthetic truths are contingent and knowable only a posteriori.

Kripke introduces the notion of a rigid designator to show how the two distinctions come apart. A rigid designator is a term that designates the same object in every possible world where that object exists. Proper names, Kripke argues, are rigid designators: 'Aristotle' refers to Aristotle in every world where Aristotle exists. Definite descriptions, by contrast, are typically non-rigid: 'the teacher of Alexander' picks out Aristotle in the actual world, but in a possible world where someone else taught Alexander, it would pick out that other person.

This is key to understanding the necessary a posteriori. Consider 'Hesperus is Phosphorus.' We can imagine a possible world where the evening star and the morning star are different celestial bodies — say, a world where the bright object in the evening is Venus but the bright object in the morning is Mars. This is why the identity is a posteriori: we need empirical astronomy to rule out the hypothesis that we live in such a world. However, in the actual world, 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' both rigidly designate the planet Venus. Since Venus is self-identical in every possible world, the statement 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is necessarily true — it could not have been false, even though we had to discover it empirically.

The same structure applies to natural kind terms. 'Water is H2O' is necessary because 'water' and 'H2O' both rigidly designate the same substance. But it took empirical science to discover water's chemical composition, so it's a posteriori. Similarly, 'gold has atomic number 79' is necessary a posteriori.

This severs the traditional link between necessity and apriority. Kant was wrong to think that all necessary truths are analytic and therefore a priori; we now have clear cases of necessary truths known only through experience. Kripke also argues the other direction, that there are contingent a priori truths — for example, 'the standard meter bar in Paris is one meter long' is knowable a priori (by fixing the reference of 'meter') but contingent (the bar might have been longer or shorter). So the two distinctions crosscut each other: all four combinations are populated."
