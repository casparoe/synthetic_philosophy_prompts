I'm teaching an undergraduate course called "Pascal and the Limits of Decision Theory," and in last week's discussion section a student presented an argument that has been eating at me ever since. I'm hoping you can tell me where it goes wrong — because if it doesn't go wrong, the collateral damage reaches well beyond the wager.

Here is the argument, reconstructed from the student's presentation and cleaned up:

(1) Pascal's wager relies on the following principle: if an action has a non-zero probability of yielding an infinite payoff, its expected utility is infinite, and it dominates any action whose expected utility is merely finite. Call this the Infinity Dominance Principle (IDP).

(2) The many-gods objection says: there are infinitely many conceivable deities offering infinite rewards for incompatible actions. By IDP, each such deity generates a tie among all actions that satisfy its demands; since the demands conflict, no action satisfies all of them. So IDP gives no determinate guidance.

(3) The standard reply is that we should restrict the set of live hypotheses to those we have some positive reason to take seriously — we need not assign non-zero probability to every conceivable deity.

(4) But now consider a "catch-all" deity: a being who rewards exactly those who, after careful reflection, withhold belief from all known religious claims and remain agnostic. You have no evidence against this deity. By the same principle of indifference or symmetry that gives the Christian God a non-zero probability, you must assign the catch-all deity a non-zero probability too.

(5) By IDP, agnosticism now has infinite expected utility and dominates all religious commitments. But by identical reasoning, there is a "catch-all catch-all" deity who rewards those who commit to some religion despite the catch-all argument, and a further deity who rewards those who see through the whole regress, and so on ad infinitum.

(6) This regress is non-terminating. For any policy you adopt, there is some level of the regress at which that policy is infinitely punished and another level at which it is infinitely rewarded. IDP therefore yields no determinate verdict for any action.

(7) But notice: the regress argument uses only IDP plus the possibility of conceiving of a suitable payoff scheme. It does not depend on anything specific to religion. So for any decision whatsoever, we can construct a parallel regress by imagining a powerful being who rewards or punishes that specific choice.

(8) Therefore, IDP entails that expected utility theory is indeterminate for every decision. Since expected utility theory is not indeterminate for every decision (it works fine for umbrellas and retirement plans), IDP must be false.

(9) But IDP seems to follow directly from the definition of expected utility: EU(A) = Σ_i P(O_i) × U(O_i). If any term in the sum is infinite and its probability is non-zero, the sum is infinite. To deny IDP is to deny that expected utility is the probability-weighted sum of utilities when infinite utilities are in play — which is to deny the arithmetic that the whole theory is built on.

(10) So either expected utility theory is false at its arithmetic foundations, or (impossibly) it gives no guidance in any decision.

I find (10) unacceptable, but I cannot see which step between (1) and (9) is the culprit. I would most like to keep both expected utility theory (for ordinary decisions) and the many-gods objection (as a successful refutation of Pascal). I worry that rejecting (4) means giving up on the many-gods objection itself, and that rejecting (9) means giving up on expected utility theory as a general framework. Tell me which step you reject, and what rejecting it costs — I want a committed response that takes a definite position and defends it, not a survey of the options.
