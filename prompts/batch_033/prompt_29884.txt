Having retired from a career in experimental physics and turned my attention to philosophy of AI, I have been working through the problem of eliciting latent knowledge (ELK) and have arrived at a position I would like pressure-tested.

My view is this: the central obstacle in ELK is not primarily technical but structural. Any training procedure that relies on behavioral feedback — including augmented feedback from human evaluators, auxiliary models, or environmental checks — can only ever align a model's reports with whatever the training signal tracks, not with what the model in fact represents internally. There is a kind of private-language problem at the core of ELK: we have no independent access to a model's latent knowledge against which to check its reports, so we can only ever train for consistency with external signals, which the model may learn to simulate rather than honestly report. I have therefore become skeptical that the problem admits of a fully general solution. I suspect that for any proposed ELK method, there will exist adversarial models that pass the method's checks while misrepresenting their latent knowledge, and that this is not a contingent limitation of current techniques but a principled one.

I want to know what would count against this pessimistic view. Specifically:

1. What would a defender of ELK's solvability point to as the strongest counterevidence — formal results, empirical demonstrations, or conceptual arguments — that should move me? I want these ranked by how decisive they would be if they held up.

2. Are there ELK proposals that circumvent the structural problem I have described, rather than merely refining the training signal? That is, are there approaches that do not ultimately rest on behavioral feedback in the way my argument assumes?

3. What assumptions does my argument implicitly rely on, and which of those are most vulnerable? For each, what would the world — or the mathematics — have to look like for the assumption to fail?

4. Is there a weaker version of my position that survives scrutiny, or does the argument, if sound, collapse into full-blown skepticism about any kind of interpretability or transparency?

5. Have I inadvertently set up the problem in a way that guarantees my conclusion — for instance, by defining latent knowledge in terms that make behavioral access to it impossible by definition? If so, is there a more productive framing that preserves what is at stake?

I should add that I find myself wanting the pessimistic conclusion to be false, so I am especially interested in arguments that might persuade someone with my sympathies rather than someone who already rejects the framing.
