I overhead this argument between two friends at a conference and I've been turning it over in my head. They were walking past a poster session and noticed that almost everyone was crowded around one particular poster — like 40 people clustered at one board while the other twenty posters had maybe two or three people each.

A: "We should go to that one. All those people can't be wrong — the poster must be the most interesting one here."

B: "That doesn't follow. What if the first three people stopped there by chance, and then everyone else just assumed those three knew something? The crowd could be completely uninformative."

A: "But that's still rational on my part. If I see a crowd, the rational inference is that there's something worth seeing. You'd have to be wildly overconfident in your own judgment to ignore that much social evidence."

B: "You're missing the structure of the problem. If everyone is reasoning the same way — 'I'll go where others are going' — then the crowd can form around something totally arbitrary. The first few people's choices get amplified regardless of quality. So the crowd doesn't give you new information beyond what the first couple of people knew, and you don't know what they knew."

A: "Okay but we're not the first couple of people — we're latecomers looking at a crowd of 40. Even if the first three stopped randomly, by the time you get to 40, surely at least some of them actually read the poster title and made an independent judgment. The crowd has had time to correct itself."

B: "But how would it correct itself? Everyone arriving after the first few sees the same crowd you're seeing, and makes the same inference you're making. The crowd is self-reinforcing. It doesn't self-correct unless people start leaving, and nobody's leaving because the crowd keeps growing."

A: "That assumes literally nobody exercises independent judgment. In reality, some fraction of people will break away and check other posters. If the crowded poster were actually mediocre, word would get around and the crowd would dissipate. The fact that it hasn't suggests it really is good."

B: "Maybe. But here's the deeper problem — even the people who do exercise independent judgment are doing so in light of the crowd. They see 40 people and think 'wow, this must be important, let me read carefully.' That's not independent. You're not getting 40 independent evaluations; you're getting maybe three or four semi-independent ones and 36 people who are mostly following."

A: "So what's your alternative? We ignore the crowd and pick posters at random? That seems strictly worse — at least the crowd is some evidence, even if it's noisy."

B: "I'm not saying ignore it entirely. I'm saying the crowd gives you much less information than its size suggests, and you should discount it heavily. We should probably just walk around, read the titles ourselves, and decide."

A: "But that's exactly what I mean — you're treating your own two-minute title-scan as more reliable than the aggregated behavior of 40 people, many of whom presumably did the same scan. That seems like the kind of arrogance that gets people into trouble in markets."

At that point they had to go to their session so I didn't hear the resolution. I keep thinking about who had the better argument. Can you go through this exchange and adjudicate it — who's right about which specific points, where does each person go wrong if at all, and what's the overall balance?
