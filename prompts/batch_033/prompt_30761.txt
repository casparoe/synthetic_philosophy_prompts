I'm a PhD student in metaphysics, mostly working on modality, and a friend in my seminar keeps pressing this argument on me and I can't shake it. He presents it as a reductio of transworld identity, with counterpart theory as the only way out; but the conclusion it forces is absurd, and I want to know exactly wich step fails, if any, and what rejecting it costs.

(1) I could have been a plumber.
(2) If transworld identity is true, then "I could have been a plumber" is true only if there is a possible world w in which I — this very person — am a plumber.
(3) So, assuming transworld identity, there is a world w in which I am a plumber.
(4) In w, the person who is a plumber is identical to me; that's just what transworld identity says.
(5) By the indiscernibility of identicals, whatever is true of that person is true of me. Being a plumber is true of that person in w, so being a plumber is true of me.
(6) But being a plumber is not true of me — I'm not a plumber and never have been.
(7) Contradiction. So if transworld identity is true, (1) must go: I couldn't have been a plumber. And the same moves work for any contingent feature, so nothing could have been otherwise than it actually is. Since that's absurd, transworld identity is false; we should be counterpart theorists.

I'd much rather keep (1) and the indiscernibility of identicals than give either of them up. I'm less attached to the claim that I myself exist in other worlds, but I worry that denying it just is counterpart theory, with all the Humphrey-objection costs that brings, and I'm not commited to the idea that those are the only two options. So: which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what do the main views — counterpart theory, world-indexed-property views, maybe strict actualism — each have to pay to avoid the absurd conclusion? You're an expert on the metaphysics of modality and the logic of possible worlds. Take your time, maybe start by canvassing the candidate diagnoses before you commit to one. And if you think I've misdescribed the dialectic or that the argument doesn't have a clean failure point, tell me that plainly instead of humoring me.
