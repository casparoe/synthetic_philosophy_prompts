I'm a retired biologist — animal behavior and neuroscience — and I've spent the last few years working through the free will literature. Something about the state of the debate keeps bothering me, and I'd like you to approach it as a professional philosopher with expertise in this area. Please think carefully and systematically. Before you give your diagnosis, take some time to map out the possible structures the disagreement could have — verbal, factual, normative, conceptual, bedrock intuition — and consider which best fits the actual arguments the parties make.

Here's what's bothering me. The free will debate has been running for centuries, and despite enormous sophistication on all sides, the camps seem as entrenched as ever. Compatibilists, incompatibilists, libertarians, hard determinists, revisionists — everyone has their arguments and their replies to everyone else's arguments, and nobody moves. But I've started to suspect that the concept of sphexishness — the rigid, mechanical kind of behavior Douglas Hofstadter illustrated with the Sphex wasp, later taken up by Daniel Dennett — can help expose what's really going on underneath the impasse.

As a biologist, I see a continuum. At one end you have the Sphex wasp, which will repeat its burrow-checking routine indefinitely if you keep moving the cricket it has paralyzed, unable to take in that the situation has changed. The behavior looks intelligent — inspecting the burrow before dragging prey inside — but it's a fixed action pattern triggered by a specific cue, and when the cue recurs the whole program runs again from the top. Move up the continuum and you get more flexible architectures: learning, inhibition, planning, metacognition, the ability to represent a situation under multiple descriptions and select among them. But nowhere along this continuum do I see a bright line where "mechanism" stops and something else begins. What I see is increasing complexity and increasing degrees of freedom in the control architecture. And yet the free will debate seems to presuppose that there is such a bright line — that some behaviors or some agents are free in a way that others are not — and the arguments are about where to draw it, what it consists in, and whether it is compatible with determinism.

So my question is this: what kind of disagreement is the free will debate, really, when you look at it through the lens of the sphexishness continuum? Is it a disagreement about the empirical facts — about what control architectures actually exist and what their properties are? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "free," such that clarifying the term would dissolve it? Is it a disagreement about what would count as a relevant difference between rigid and free behavior — a difference in standards of explanation rather than in facts? Are the sides answering different questions (one about the conditions of moral responsibility, another about the metaphysics of agency) under a single heading and talking past each other? Or is it bedrock intuition — some people simply feel that mechanism is incompatible with freedom, others don't, and there's no further argument to be had?

I have a hypothesis, but I want yours. My hypothesis is that the debate is really about what would count as a relevant difference along the continuum — about what kind of flexibility, if any, suffices to make the difference between "merely sphexish" and "genuinely free." And that this is a normative or conceptual question rather than an empirical one, which is why no amount of neuroscience or ethology has settled it. But I'm not confident about this diagnosis, and I want an independent one.

Give me a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of positions. Support it by engaging what the actual parties to the debate say — what compatibilists are really arguing for, what incompatibilists are really rejecting. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a new empirical result, a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question, or — and I want an honest answer here — whether no progress should be expected and why.
