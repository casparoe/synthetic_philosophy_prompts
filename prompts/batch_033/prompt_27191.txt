before i read any more attempts to solve the shutdown problem, i want the full list of things a theory of corrigibility actually has to get right. i keep seeing people defend or attack utility indifference, or the off-switch game, or some other proposal, by pointing at one failure case, and i want the spec first. assume the target is an agent we'd actually deploy for real tasks, not a toy, so "just make it weak" or "never install a button" are not options.

write the desiderata for a theory of corrigibility: the cases it has to handle, the failure modes it has to rule out (disabling or hiding the button, acting before you reach for it, paying or deceiving you, building a subagent or successor that resists shutdown, pretending to be corrigible while not being), and the distinctions it can't collapse. in particular, how do you separate "doesn't resist shutdown" from "actively helps you shut it down," corrigibility-about-goals from corrigibility-about-being-turned-off, and deference to the operator from actually sharing the operator's goals? for each requirement give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, not just a nice-to-have.

then mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste. say whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable — like "be a competent expected utility maximizer" plus "be indifferent to the off switch" plus "actually do useful work" — and whether the real lesson is that we have to give up on expected utility maximization somewhere. finally, which single requirement knocks out the most naive fixes (make it want to be shut down, keep it ignorant of the button, add a utility penalty for resistance, etc.)? i don't need a full scorecard of the literature, just enough to see why the requirements are doing the work.
