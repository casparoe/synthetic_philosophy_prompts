I've been trying to work out what I actually think about divine hiddenness, and I've found it useful to start with ordinary cases of one person hiding from another. My tentative principle is this: concealment is a failure of love only when the other person has a reasonable need for the hider's presence or disclosure, the hider could provide it without disproportionate cost, and the hider withholds it for the hider's own sake rather than for the other person's good. But I suspect the principle does not survive my own cases.

Here are my verdicts. A parent who walked out on a young child and has spent decades hiding from that child, now an adult, who has been searching for years: the parent is failing in love, not merely in decency. A romantic partner who goes silent for weeks, not out of anger or a need for space, but specifically to see whether the other person will chase them: manipulative, a failure of love. A doctor who withholds a terminal diagnosis from a patient who has explicitly asked to be told everything: a failure of respect and care, even if the doctor means to spare the patient pain. The same doctor, withholding the same diagnosis from a patient who has explicitly said they do not want to know: not a failure. An anonymous benefactor who gives a life-saving sum to a stranger through a foundation and never reveals their identity: no failure at all, perhaps admirable. A reclusive friend who almost never initiates contact but responds warmly and reliably whenever I reach out: not a failure of love. A romantic partner who conceals evidence that would restore their partner's trust, when the partner is begging for it and the evidence would be easy to provide: a failure of love. A parent who cuts off contact after a hurtful fight but sends a short note saying they love the child and are not far away, and remains reachable: not a failure, or at most a small one.

Is there a principle — this one repaired, or a different one — that captures all or most of these verdicts? What does it imply about the key premise of the hiddenness argument, that a perfectly loving God would not permit nonresistant nonbelief (that is, the absence of belief in God among people who are not resisting a relationship with God)? Which of my ordinary-case verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to revise, and what does it predict about cases I have not listed, such as a parent who hides in order to let a child become independent, or a God who remains hidden from some people who seek a relationship while being evident to others?
