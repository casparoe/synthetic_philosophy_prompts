I'm writing a paper on the moral difference—or the lack of one—between lying and misleading, and I want to use Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* as an extended case study. The philosophical terrain, in broad strokes, is this: some philosophers argue that there is a morally significant difference between lying (asserting what one believes to be false) and merely misleading (saying something true but in a way calculated to produce a false belief), such that lying is pro tanto worse, or violates a distinct norm, or requires a different kind of justification. Others hold that this distinction is morally shallow—that what matters is the deceptive aim and the harm done, not whether the deceptive move happened to take the form of a literal falsehood. The debate turns partly on whether there is something special about the act of assertion itself, and partly on whether the person deceived has been wronged in a distinctive way when the deceiver has lied rather than merely misled.

I'd like you to read the novel through this lens. Stevens, the narrator-butler, is famously meticulous with his words: he withholds, deflects, deploys strategic understatement, and retreats into professional formulae at moments of emotional pressure. But does he ever outright lie? And more importantly, does the novel itself take a position on whether his characteristic mode of deception—if we should call it that—is morally different from lying? There is a case to be made that the novel's entire tragic structure depends on the idea that misleading-without-lying is its own distinct kind of moral failure, one that allows Stevens to preserve a self-image of dignity and professional integrity even as he systematically deceives others and, crucially, himself. On this reading, the distinction between lying and misleading is precisely what enables his moral evasion. But there is also a case that the novel ultimately collapses the distinction: that Stevens's scrupulous "honesty" about his deceptions is just one more layer of self-deception, and that the book shows the lying/misleading distinction to be a comforting fiction that serves the powerful and the complicit.

I want a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously, and that is faithful in both directions—neither reducing the novel to an illustration of a philosophical position, nor distorting the philosophical categories to fit the novel. Where does the novel dramatize something the philosophical literature captures well? Where does it complicate or resist the philosophical categories? Are there specific scenes—Stevens's handling of Lord Darlington's guests, his conversations with Miss Kenton (especially the ones he later re-narrates), his account of his father's death and his own behaviour during it—where the lying/misleading distinction does real moral work, or where it breaks down under scrutiny? And what, if anything, does the novel add to the philosophical debate that argument alone would miss—something about the temporality of self-deception, perhaps, or about the way a life built on misleading-without-lying corrodes the capacity for genuine assertion at all?
