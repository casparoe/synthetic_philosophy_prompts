I'm drafting a paper on the separateness of persons and its downstream implications for distributive ethics and population axiology, and I've ended up with the following package of views. I'd like a consistency audit before I build an argument around them. Here they are:

(1) The separateness of persons is a decisive objection to classical utilitarianism. The problem is not merely that utilitarianism licenses objectionable trade-offs between individuals; it is that utilitarianism treats the boundaries between lives as having no intrinsic moral significance — as if distinct persons were merely distinct locations where utility happens, and the only thing that matters is the sum across locations. The separateness of persons is a fact about what persons are, and any moral theory that fails to register it is getting something deeply wrong.

(2) Prioritarianism is a defensible alternative to utilitarianism that avoids the separateness objection. By giving extra weight to benefits that go to the worse off, prioritarianism captures the distributive concern that egalitarianism gestures at, without treating equality itself as a non-instrumental good. And because the weighting is tied to each person's absolute level of welfare rather than to comparisons between persons, prioritarianism does not simply re-introduce the objectionable kind of interpersonal aggregation.

(3) The levelling down objection is fatal to telic egalitarianism. A world where everyone is equally badly off is not in any respect better than a world where everyone is equally well off except some are even better off. Equality is not an intrinsic good — it matters only because of what it does for people, not because a more equal distribution of welfare has value in itself.

(4) The repugnant conclusion is genuinely repugnant, and any adequate population axiology must avoid it. A world of very many lives that are barely worth living is not better than a world of fewer lives of very high quality, even if the total sum of welfare is larger in the first world. This is not a bullet I am willing to bite.

(5) The person-affecting intuition — roughly, that one outcome can be better than another only if it is better for particular people — is broadly correct. Outcomes are not better or worse simpliciter; they are better or worse for the individuals who exist in them. This intuition is what makes the non-identity problem so puzzling rather than trivial, and I think we should try to preserve it rather than abandon it.

I can see that some of these might be in tension with others, but I'm not confident I've identified all of them, and I'm also not sure which are merely apparent. Please go through the set and audit it for consistency. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what would have to give to resolve it, and identify the cheapest revision. Also flag any apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection — I want to know which objections to the package I can safely set aside.
