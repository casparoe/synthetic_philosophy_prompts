Heres something I keep seeing in my department meetings and I'd like to know whether it has an established name—and, more than that, how to carve it off from the neighboring things. A proposal gets floated, say a new grading rubric or hiring someone outside the advertised area. The first person to speak is lukewarm but ends up endorsing it. A second colleague, who I know privately thought it was a bad idea, watches that and then speaks in favor too. By the fourth or fifth comment everyone's on board, and afterward two people admit they had serious doubts all along. Nobody was lying and nobody was trying to ingratiate themselves; each later person seems to have reasoned that several smart people before them went along, so their own misgivings were probably the outlier. Each step looks sensible by itself, but the group lands somewhere almost no one privately believed. It's not groupthink—the doubt isn't suppressed, it's overridden by what earlier people did. It's not plain conformity either—they're not matching others to be liked. What confuses me is that in these meetings people do give reasons, but the reasons are usually hedged and post-hoc, and later speakers seem to weigh the mere fact that others endorsed the thing more heavily than the content of the reasons. Is there a term for this exact mechanism? And if the term covers my case, what are its necessary ingredients—does it require people to act one at a time, does it require actions to hide the strength of each person's private judgment, and does it fall apart once some actual reasoning is also visible? I'm after the carving, not just a label.
