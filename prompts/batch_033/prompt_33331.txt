I am working through a set of deontological commitments and would like to know whether they are jointly tenable. Here is what I currently hold:

(1) There are agent-relative deontological constraints — paradigmatically, a constraint against killing an innocent person — that apply even when violating them would prevent more violations of the same constraint by others.

(2) The ground of these constraints is the inviolability of persons: each person has a moral status that forbids using them as a mere means, and this is what explains why the constraint has the force it does.

(3) The constraint against killing is not absolute in the sense of admitting no exceptions whatsoever, but it is resistant: it cannot be outweighed merely by the consideration that violating it would prevent additional violations of the same constraint by others.

(4) The constraint is not about keeping one's own hands clean or maintaining one's own virtue. It is genuinely about the victim — about what is owed to the person who would be killed.

(5) The doing/allowing distinction is morally significant: there is a genuine normative difference between bringing about a harm and merely failing to prevent it, and this distinction does real work in deontological theory rather than being a rationalization for self-regarding scruples.

(6) In a case where you must choose between killing one innocent person yourself and killing five innocent persons yourself, with all other options blocked, the constraint still forbids killing the one — even though you will end up killing five if you refuse.

Go through this set and identify which commitments are in genuine tension with one another and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, rank its severity, explain what generates it, and say what would have to give to resolve it. For apparent tensions, explain why they dissolve on inspection. Finally, tell me what the least costly revision to the set would be, and what it would cost.
