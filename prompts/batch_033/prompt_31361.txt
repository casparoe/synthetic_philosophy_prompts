I'm in a philosophy of art seminar and we've been circling the perfect-fake problem: whether a forgery that is perceptually indistinguishable from an original would be aesthetically just as good, and whether any leftover difference in value has to come from history, provenance, or the act of creation rather than from anything visible. I keep feeling the debate gets to clean, with its idealizing talk of perfect fakes and idealized experts, so I want to drag it through an actual case and see what breaks.

Take Han van Meegeren's Vermeer forgeries from the 1930s and 40s. The broad facts as I have them: his Supper at Emmaus was bought by the Boijmans museum in Rotterdam and hailed by Abraham Bredius, the leading Vermeer expert of the day, as a major discovery; he later sold a fake Vermeer to Göring; after the war he was arrested for selling Dutch cultural property to the Nazis, and his defense turned on proving he had painted the things himself and swindled Göring — he was eventually convicted and sentenced over the forgeries. The paintings were later exposed on technical grounds, and to most modern eyes they now look obviously not-Vermeer.

What I'd like is a careful case analysis through the lens of the forgery/originalism debate. Who or what plays the role of 'the work' here — is Supper at Emmaus a bad Vermeer, a good van Meegeren, or something whose identity flipped when the attribution did? Walk through what the main positions would say about the case: the view that aesthetic value lives entirely in perceptible properties, the view that the original's history and the artist's achievement are part of its aesthetic value, and any other position you think earns its keep. Then tell me where the case fits the theory badly — in particular these weren't perfect fakes; they fooled sophisticated viewers for historically contingent reasons and then stopped fooling people, which seems to cut against the clean thought experiment. Does the episode show that seeing is theory-laden enough that 'perceptually indistinguishable' is an unstable notion, or does that overread it? And what should I make of the fact that the same canvases are now exhibited and valued as van Meegerens rather than destroyed as worthless copies? I don't want a bibliography; I want you to think through the dialectic. And if the whole framing is off — if you think van Meegeren can't tell us anything about the perfect-fake problem, or that the perfect-fake problem itself is confused — say so plainly and make that case instead of humoring me.
