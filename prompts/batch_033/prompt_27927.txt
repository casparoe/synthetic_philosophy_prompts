Take the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission and analyse it through the lens of the philosophical literature on standing to blame and hypocrisy. I'm not after the general moral case for or against the TRC — I know that debate. What I want is specifically about standing. Did the commissioners have standing to grant amnesty when they weren't the ones who'd been tortured or had family members killed? If a perpetrator confesses, gets amnesty, and then later criticises someone else's moral conduct, have they forfeited their standing to blame? And here's the really tricky bit: the ANC itself had blood on its hands — the TRC wasn't a case of pure victims judging pure perpetrators. Does that hypocrisy undermine the whole enterprise, or is there a way of thinking about standing that lets a partly-compromised institution still do legitimate moral work? I want the analysis to run both ways: use the theory to illuminate the TRC, and use the TRC to stress-test the theory. Where does the standing literature handle this well, where does it go quiet, and does the episode ultimately support or trouble the idea that standing matters for blame?
