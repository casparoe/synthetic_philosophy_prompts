You are a professional philosopher of science who specializes in the epistemology of thought experiments. You have published on the topic and taught graduate seminars on it. Think carefully, reason step by step, and do not rush to a conclusion before working through the strongest version of each position.

Write an essay on the following controversy: How do thought experiments in science yield knowledge? Scientists from Galileo to Einstein to contemporary physics have used thought experiments to arrive at new conclusions about the world — yet on their face these are exercises of pure imagination, conducted from the armchair, with no new empirical input. How can merely thinking through a scenario produce genuine knowledge about nature? This is not a fringe puzzle: if thought experiments can yield knowledge, then any adequate epistemology of science must explain how.

The essay should weigh the three main competing accounts in the philosophical literature. Address each in turn:

1. The argument view: Thought experiments are really just logical arguments dressed in narrative clothing. The story is dispensable; what does the epistemic work is a chain of deductive or inductive inference from premises we already accept. On this view, a thought experiment yields knowledge in exactly the way any good argument does — and nothing more.

2. The mental model view: Thought experiments work by prompting us to construct and manipulate mental models built from tacit empirical knowledge. We run a simulation in our heads — a kind of cognitive analog to a laboratory experiment — and read off the result. The knowledge is genuinely empirical, but the empiricism is buried in the model rather than gathered from a new observation.

3. The Platonist view: At least some thought experiments give us a priori access to abstract reality, much like mathematical intuition. They are not disguised arguments or tacit simulations but a kind of intellectual perception of the laws of nature themselves. On this view, certain thought experiments yield knowledge that is neither empirical nor merely logical.

For each view, explain what it gets right about one or two canonical thought experiments — such as Galileo's falling bodies, Newton's rotating bucket, Einstein's chasing-a-light-beam, Maxwell's demon, or the EPR thought experiment. Then identify what each view struggles to explain: the cases that resist its treatment, the epistemic steps it cannot account for, or the commitments it incurs that look implausible.

Finally, reach and defend a verdict. You may defend one of the three views, combine them into a hybrid, or argue that the whole debate is misframed — but you must take a definite position and justify it. A nuanced view is welcome; a non-committal survey is not.

Structure the essay as follows:

- Introduction (~200 words): State the puzzle, explain why it matters, and preview the essay's structure and conclusion.
- The argument view (~400 words): Exposition, illustration via a canonical case, strengths, and weaknesses.
- The mental model view (~400 words): Same.
- The Platonist view (~400 words): Same.
- My assessment and verdict (~500 words): Weigh the views against each other and against the hardest cases. Identify where each view earns its keep and where it fails. Then state and defend your own position.
- Conclusion (~200 words): Summarize the argument and draw out one broader implication for how we should think about scientific knowledge.

Before writing the essay itself, spend time brainstorming: list the canonical thought experiments you plan to draw on, sketch the core claim of each view in your own words, and write down the single hardest objection each view must answer. Then proceed to the essay.
