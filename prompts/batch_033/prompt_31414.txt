Two friends of mine, both in a decision-theory reading group, got into a long argument about money pumps and I havent been able to sort out who had the better of it. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Dan started by saying money-pump arguments are the cleanest case for why rational preferences have to be transitive. If you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone can start you with C, offer you B for a penny, then A for a penny, then C again for a penny, and you're back where you started minus three cents. Since you'd accept each trade individually, you get drained. So intransitive preferences are irrational — they're exploitable.

(2) Mira pushed back: that version only works if you can't see the whole sequence coming. If you know the setup in advance, you'd refuse the very first trade because you can see it leads back to C minus cash. So the real problem isn't intransitivity — it's myopia or being bad at backwards induction. A farsighted agent with intransitive preferences shouldn't get pumped.

(3) Dan said fine, but there are diachronic versions that don't rely on myopia. Suppose your preferences shift over time: on Monday you prefer A to B, but on Tuesday you prefer B to A. On Monday, someone trades you A for your B plus a penny (you accept, since Monday-you prefers A to B). On Tuesday, they trade you B for your A plus a penny (you accept, since Tuesday-you prefers B to A). Now you hold A again and you're down two cents. You haven't been myopic at all — each choice was rational given your preferences at the time. But the sequence is a disaster. So diachronic rationality requires stable preferences, not just transitive ones.

(4) Mira answered: that only works if Monday-you can't bind Tuesday-you. If on Monday you can sign a contract or set up a commitment device that prevents Tuesday's trade, the pump collapses. So the lesson isn't that preferences must be stable — it's that if your preferences are going to shift, you'd better have the power to commit. And if commitment devices exist, shifting preferences are fine.

(5) Dan said commitment devices aren't always available, and anyway the deeper point is that Monday-you would prefer, ex ante, not to be the kind of agent whose preferences flip like that. So you have a second-order reason to reshape your preferences — which is still a rationality requirement, just one level up.

(6) Mira said that's a big retreat. The original claim was that money pumps show you must have certain preferences (transitive, stable). Now you're saying they show you must want to have certain preferences, or that you must avoid being in situations where you can't commit. That's a lot weaker, and it makes money pumps into a practical problem about institutional design rather than a deep constraint on rationality itself.

They went in circles after that and never resolved it. Could you go through the six moves and say who's right about what at each step? I'm especially interested in whether Mira's point (6) is fair — did Dan actually retreat, or is the second-order-preferences point just the natural continuation of the same argument? And please say what would change your mind about the overall verdict: which consideration, if it turned out differently, would flip your assessment of who's more right?
