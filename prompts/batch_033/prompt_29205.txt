I am a PhD student in cognitive science, and I have been trying to get clear on exactly what the symbol grounding problem establishes and what it leaves open. A handbook chapter I was assigned summarizes it roughly as follows:

"The symbol grounding problem, introduced by Stevan Harnad, is the problem of how the symbols in a computational system acquire meaning. Harnad observed that a computer manipulating symbols according to syntactic rules has no intrinsic connection between those symbols and the things they are supposed to stand for, so its symbols are meaningless to the system itself and only meaningful to an outside interpreter. This is essentially Searle's Chinese Room argument restated in computational terms, and it shows that no purely computational system could ever possess genuine understanding. Harnad's proposed remedy was to connect the system to the world through sensory transducers, so that its most elementary symbols refer to objects it can perceive; once those elementary symbols are grounded, the rest of the system's symbols inherit their meanings by being defined in terms of them."

The gloss sounds mostly plausible to me, but I suspect it runs the symbol grounding problem and the Chinese Room together in a way that might distort one or both, and I do not know enough to say exactly where. Could you give me an accuracy audit of this passage: which parts are faithful to the problem as standardly formulated in cognitive science and philosophy of mind, which parts are common simplifications, and which parts would Harnad or Searle push back on? In particular, I would like to know whether the symbol grounding problem, as standardly formulated, is best read as a proof that computational systems cannot have semantics, or as a more conditional and constructive challenge; and whether grounding the elementary perceptual symbols is generally taken to solve the problem for the rest of the vocabulary, or whether the problem re-emerges for abstract and theoretical terms.
