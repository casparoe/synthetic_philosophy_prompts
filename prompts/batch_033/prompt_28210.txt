Two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into it at a party over whether we ever actually see the world directly. It started because Dana mentioned that a straw in a glass of water looks bent even though it isn't, and Marcus wouldn't let it go. The way I remember it, there were four moves.

(1) Marcus said: if the straw looks bent and there is no bent thing in front of you, then what you are actually aware of is a bent appearance inside your own mind, so you aren't directly seeing the straw. Dana said that's too fast: the straight straw looks bent, sure, but it's the straw itself that looks that way, and "looking bent" doesn't require a bent thing to exist any more than a person far away looking tiny requires a tiny person.

(2) Marcus then moved to a stronger case. He said: imagine I'm having a vivid hallucination of a dagger while you're seeing a real dagger, and the two are indistinguishable from the inside. Then our experiences have to be the same kind of thing. But in my case there's no dagger, so the experience can't be a relation to a dagger. So in your case too, what you're directly aware of isn't the dagger — it's a mental image. Dana said no: those are two different kinds of state that just happen to be indistinguishable; not being able to tell them apart from the inside doesn't make them the same kind of thing. Marcus said: but if they're different kinds, you owe me an explanation of why they feel exactly the same.

(3) Dana answered: they don't have to feel exactly the same — indiscriminable isn't the same as identical, any more than two paint chips you can't tell apart are the same color — and anyway the similarity is explained by what I can't rule out from the inside, not by some shared mental picture. Marcus said that's a dodge: if there's no common element, then the perfect match between the real case and the hallucination is a coincidence, and that seems like magic.

(4) Dana fired back that Marcus's picture has the worse problem: it puts a curtain of appearances between us and the world and then has to explain how we ever get past it, which is the very thing the argument was supposed to settle. Marcus said that's not fair: indirect access isn't no access. We can know about the world through appearances the way we know about a fire from its smoke, and the question was never whether we know things, just what we know directly. Dana said that analogy gives up the game, because seeing a dagger isn't like inferring a fire from smoke — when I see the dagger, the dagger is right there, not behind a sign.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldnt. Could you go through this exchange and say who's right about what at each step — including any place where one of them is sliding between cases or talking past the other — and who is more right overall?
