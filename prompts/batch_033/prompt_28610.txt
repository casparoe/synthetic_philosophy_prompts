I read Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov a few years ago, and I keep coming back to the Grand Inquisitor chapter. The way I understand it, the Grand Inquisitor tells Christ that the church has been correcting his work: Christ gave people freedom, but freedom is a burden most people cannot bear. What they really want is someone to take the burden off their shoulders — to tell them what to do, to give them security and bread, and to let them hand over their freedom in exchange for happiness. The Inquisitor argues that people will gladly lay their freedom at the feet of anyone who gives them certainty and material comfort, and they will curse those who try to give them freedom instead.

I have been trying to figure out whether he is right. On one hand, history seems to back him up — people have repeatedly chosen strong leaders, rigid ideologies, and comfortable conformity over the hard work of freedom. On the other hand, there is something deeply unsettling about the idea that freedom and happiness are in such direct conflict, and I am not sure the Inquisitor is being fair to what people actually want.

I would like an essay that weighs the Grand Inquisitor's case: is he right that most people, if given the choice, would trade freedom for happiness and security? The essay should engage seriously with the strongest points on both sides and then reach a clear conclusion — not a non-committal "it is complicated." If you think he is right, say so and defend it. If you think he is wrong, say why and defend that.
