I spent most of my working life as a research chemist and retired a few years ago; since then I have been reading moral philosophy with more seriousness than I ever could while employed, and one problem has gradually crowded out the others: the standing to blame. The core idea is that whether someone may blame a wrongdoer is not settled by whether the wrongdoer is blameworthy, because facts about the blamer can deprive her of the entitlement to do the blaming — that she is a hypocrite, that she was complicit in the wrong, or that she is meddling in business that is not hers. What unsettles me is a distinction that recurs throughout the literature between the content of a piece of blame and the blamer's standing to issue it: many writers hold that a hypocrite's blame can hit its target — the person blamed really is blameworthy — while nevertheless being something the hypocrite is in no position to offer. I find that separation compelling but obscure, and I suspect that whether it can be made precise is the hinge on which much of the surrounding debate turns.

The explanations I have encountered are numerous and mutually incompatible. Some trace the loss of standing to the hypocrite's failure to endorse the norm she invokes; some to the absence of the right kind of relationship between blamer and wrongdoer; some to a broadly epistemic defect, as if her blame purported to teach a lesson she has not herself learned; some to the thought that blame is a public performance and the hypocrite's performance is somehow self-undermining. I cannot tell whether these are rival accounts of a single phenomenon or descriptions of several different phenomena that the word "standing" has been stretched to cover, and that uncertainty is itself part of what I would like to resolve.

I have roughly three years of unscheduled time, a close reader's habits, and some leftover comfort with careful argument, but no graduate training in philosophy and no reliable access to a research library. My aim is not to produce another survey but to make at least one genuine contribution to this debate. If you were setting my agenda, which five open questions about standing and hypocrisy would you have me work on, and in what order? For each, I want the reasoning rather than just the label: why it is more worth my limited time than the alternatives, whether it is upstream of other questions, how tractable it is for someone with my background, and what evidence in the first year would lead you to demote it and promote something else. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
