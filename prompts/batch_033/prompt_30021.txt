I read history of science for pleasure, and one pattern keeps jumping out at me: a theory is accepted by the most careful people of its day, makes impressively accurate predictions, and then gets discarded anyway. The caloric theory of heat explained and predicted a great deal before thermodynamics replaced it; the phlogiston theory of combustion did real explanatory work before the oxygen theory of burning took over; the ether theory of light was widely relied on before relativity made the ether unnecessary. From this pattern I arrived at a skeptical conclusion that now bothers me, because the argument seems to swallow itself.

The reasoning goes like this. (1) Over and over, theories that were accepted and predictively successful turned out to be false, at least in what they claimed about the hidden parts of the world they posited. (2) So the best guess is that our current accepted, predictively successful theories are also probably false in their claims about the hidden parts of the world. (3) But my evidence for (1) depends on our current theories and the instruments they underwrite: I say phlogiston was wrong because current chemistry tells me combustion is oxidation, and I trust the historical record in part because I trust the chemistry and physics behind how we interpret old experiments and texts. (4) If (2) is right, then that evidence is unreliable in just the way the induction says current theories are unreliable. (5) So the induction undercuts its own premises. And yet I cannot bring myself to reject (1), because the historical pattern looks too consistent to be a coincidence, and (2) feels like the natural conclusion to draw from it.

Where does this argument go wrong? If I have to reject one step, which is the least costly to reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the original skeptical conclusion? I am not asking which historical examples I have gotten wrong or which current theory is true; I want to know where the reasoning breaks, and what has to be given up to stop it from proving too much.
