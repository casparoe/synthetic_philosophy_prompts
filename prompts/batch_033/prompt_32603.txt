I spent my career in the natural sciences, and like many of my generation, I was trained on null hypothesis significance testing as if it were the only game in town. The replication crisis has made it clear that something is wrong with how we use statistics, and the Bayesian/frequentist divide seems to be at the heart of many diagnoses. But I have now read enough philosophy of statistics to know that the debate is vast — it ranges from the foundations of probability to the mechanics of multilevel modeling — and I want to know where to direct my remaining attention.

Suppose a small research group with both philosophical and statistical competence could spend five years on questions at the Bayesian/frequentist interface that are meant to change how working scientists actually behave — how they design studies, analyze data, and interpret results. Which open questions should they prioritize, and in what order? For each entry in your ranking, I want the reasoning: how exactly does progress on this question translate into different practices at the bench or in the field? What makes it tractable — are the conceptual pieces already in place, or would it require new formal work? And what would have to happen over the next few years for you to move it up or down the list? I am not looking for a survey of the debate; I am looking for a defended agenda, one that a former experimentalist could judge against decades of watching scientists misuse statistics in practice.
