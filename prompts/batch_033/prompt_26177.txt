I spent forty years in molecular biology before retiring, and I've recently got into philosophy of mind. The thing that's been keeping me up is epiphenomenalism — the view that conscious experiences are caused by brain processes but don't themselves cause anything. I'm trying to work out what I think by going through cases where someone makes a judgment about their own conscious state, and asking: does the conscious state itself seem to be doing causal work in producing that judgment, or is it all the underlying physics?

Here's where I've landed on particular cases:

Stubbed toe. Someone stubs their toe and says "god that hurts." The pain is why they said it. If the pain weren't there they wouldn't have said it. Clear case of consciousness causing the report.

Blindsight. Patient correctly guesses the location of a light they swear they can't see. Their "I don't see anything" report seems caused by the absence of visual experience — the physical processing is there (that's how they guess) but consciousness isn't, and the lack of consciousness drives the denial. So here the absence of a conscious state seems causal.

Anton's syndrome. Cortically blind patient confidently describes the room, gets it all wrong. No visual experience at all. The brain damage causes both the blindness and the confabulated report. Consciousness doesn't enter the causal chain. This one feels epiphenomenal.

Waking from anaesthesia. Patient says "I was completely out, no experiences." I want to say the absence of experience causes the report, same as blindsight. But then I realise: if consciousness is epiphenomenal, its absence can't cause anything either. So this case seems to cause trouble for both views.

Change blindness. Subject fails to notice the engine vanishing from a photo of a car, reports "nothing changed." The physical failure of the brain to represent the change causes both the lack of conscious experience and the report. Another one that feels epiphenomenal.

Lucid dream. Dreamer says "I know I'm dreaming right now." This feels just like the stubbed toe — the conscious realisation itself drives the report. If they hadn't consciously realised they were dreaming, they wouldn't have said it.

So I've got cases pulling me both ways, and the anaesthesia case that seems to break both accounts. Is there a principle that captures most of these verdicts? What does it say about a case I haven't considered — someone hypnotised to feel no pain, who then reports no pain when pricked with a pin? And which of my verdicts does a clean principle force me to abandon?
