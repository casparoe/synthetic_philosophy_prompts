I'm working on a paper about the relationship between rational credence and objective chance, and I want to get the lay of the land before I commit to any particular view. Could you lay out the desiderata for an adequate theory of how credence and chance relate? I'm thinking of the territory around Lewis's Principal Principle, but I want the requirements stated in a way that doesn't presuppose his formulation is the final word.

The kinds of things I have in mind: the account must explain why and when a rational agent should align her credences with known chances, and it must specify the exact form of that alignment — is it a conditional probability statement, and if so, conditional on what? It must handle the problem of undermining futures that Lewis himself identified, where evidence about the future seems to undercut the very chance one is supposed to defer to. It must give a principled account of what counts as inadmissible evidence and why some kinds of information (about the future, about chance itself) are ruled out while others are not. It must be compatible with whatever the correct metaphysics of chance turns out to be — Humean or non-Humean — or else must argue persuasively that only one of those can work, and the argument for that claim must itself be part of the package. It must not generate contradictions when combined with Bayesian conditionalization or with principles like van Fraassen's Reflection. It must make sense of the fact that chances are time-indexed and evolve as time passes. It must connect properly to causal decision theory, since chances are the quantities that are supposed to guide action in Newcomb-style problems. And it must address the question of whether the principal constraint is a constraint on what chance is (a conceptual role for chance) or a constraint on what rational credence is (a norm of rationality) — because the whole shape of the theory changes depending on which way you read it.

For each requirement, I want to know what case or problem forces it onto the list, and whether it is a genuine non-negotiable constraint or more of a desideratum that one could reasonably drop. I am especially interested in whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. If there is a known impossibility result — a no-go theorem that shows you cannot simultaneously satisfy, say, deference to chance, Bayesian conditionalization, and something else — I want to know exactly which requirements conflict and why. Please just write in continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold text.
