I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I've been asked to lead a two-hour seminar session on the Doomsday Argument for a reading group of about fifteen people — mostly graduate students from other departments (economics, physics, computer science) who are philosophically curious but have no formal training in philosophy. Most of them have heard of Bayesian reasoning but haven't applied it to self-locating uncertainty. I want to walk them through the argument so that by the end they can state it precisely, see why it has force, and identify where it might go wrong — without me simply telling them where it goes wrong.

Don't explain the argument to me; plan how I should explain it to them. I need the dependency structure: what idea has to be in place before the next one can land, and in what order. I need one carrying example — a concrete scenario, perhaps involving urns or numbered rooms — that can bear the weight of the whole argument and that I can return to at each stage. I need a map of the misconceptions they'll form: for instance, confusing the argument with the gambler's fallacy, or thinking it assumes a fixed future human population, or thinking it's a prediction rather than a shift in credence, or failing to see why one's birth rank is treated as evidence at all. For each misconception, tell me where in the sequence it is most likely to arise and how I should head it off — whether by preempting it before it forms, or by letting it surface and then addressing it. I also need to know what to leave out: which technical issues (the reference class problem, the choice of prior, the SSA vs. SIA dispute) I should mention only in passing or not at all, and which ones I must include for the argument to be honest. Finally, give me one question I can pose at the end that will tell me whether they've understood the argument's structure, not just its conclusion.
