I've recently joined the operations analytics team at a regional health system, and I've been put in charge of recommending a metric that will sit on a dashboard and partly determine whether our patient-engagement program is judged a success. The metric will not just be observed; managers will be rewarded for moving it. I know the standard Goodhart's-law worry — that people will game whatever number they are evaluated on — but I've become convinced that gaming is only one failure mode, and not the one that keeps me up at night. What worries me is a quieter one: a metric that is genuinely predictive of the outcome we care about in the historical data, that nobody manipulates, but that stops tracking the outcome as soon as we intervene to improve it, because the original correlation was produced by a common cause rather than by any direct causal path from the metric to the outcome. The stakes feel concrete, because I can already imagine the meeting where I have to explain why the number went up and the underlying outcome did not.

Please construct a thought experiment to these specifications. The scenario should be one paragraph and set in a realistic hospital or workplace. No one in it should game, cheat, or misreport the metric. The proxy should be genuinely correlated with the true target in the historical data, so that a reasonable person could have chosen it in good faith. When a well-intentioned manager intervenes to raise the proxy, the target should fail to improve, or should worsen, and the failure must be produced by the causal structure: a common cause that generated the historical proxy–target correlation, with the result that intervening on the proxy transmits no change to the target. The case should be clearly distinguishable both from specification gaming and from the failure that occurs when a proxy is pushed outside the range where the correlation held.

After the scenario, walk through the demonstration. State the proxy, the target, and the common cause; lay out the causal structure explicitly, a simple directed graph is fine; explain why the historical correlation was real; show step by step why the intervention on the proxy leaves the target unchanged or worsens it; and identify which feature of the case is doing the work. Then say what would happen if that feature were removed — would the case collapse into gaming, into a merely bad metric, or into something else? Finish with a short note on how someone defending the choice of this metric would attack the case. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the causal structure you have built, and single out the specific point in the demonstration you are least sure about.
