Two friends of mine got into it the other night about a weird corner of Turing's 1950 paper, the one where he sets out the imitation game. The corner is near the end, where Turing answers objections and eventually gets to extrasensory perception. I went back and reread the section. As far as I can tell, he says the statisical evidence for telepathy is overwhelming and that he'd like to discredit these phenomena but can't, calls the objection a strong one, and then worries that a telepathic contestant could answer questions the machine couldn't, which would let the interrogator identify the human. His proposed fix is to put the contestants in a room that blocks telepathy so the test can still be run.

Here's how the argument went. Mike said this is the most intellectually honest moment in the paper: Turing faces a fringe objection, grants its premise for the sake of argument, and shows the test survives with a small repair — that's just good experimental design, control for the confound and move on. Dana said it's the opposite of honest: if telepathy is real enough to need a special room, then Turing's whole behavioral criterion for thinking is in trouble, because mental content could pass between people without showing up in typed answers; quarentining it with a room doesn't answer the objection, it just changes the subject. Mike said that's confused — the test is about whether a machine can hold up its end of a conversation, and telepathy is only a confound for the human contestant's answers, so blocking it is exactly right. Dana came back with: either Turing believes the evidence is overwhelming, in which case he should say what telepathy means for minds, or he doesn't, in which case the objection didn't need a room in the first place; you can't have it both ways. Mike said granting an uncomfortable premise and showing your conclusion still follows is stronger than denying the premise, and that's just what Turing is doing. Dana said Turing doesn't read as granting it for the sake of argument — he reads as actually convinced — and that makes the room a piece of special pleading, a way to keep his test clean without taking the phenomenon seriously. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what, and what either of them is missing?
