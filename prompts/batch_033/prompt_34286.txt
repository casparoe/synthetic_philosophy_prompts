I retired two years ago from a career in experimental physics, and I have been spending my leisure on philosophy — lately I have been immersed in the literature on Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment (Elster, Schelling, Holmes). I now run a monthly discussion group of about twelve people, mostly retired scientists and engineers who share this late-blooming interest and who enjoy structured, hands-on activities. We meet for two hours.

I would like you to design a game — with rules, roles, a simple payoff scheme using tokens rather than real money, and a debrief — that makes the participants feel the force of precommitment from the inside. The goal is for them to discover, through their own choices, why a rational agent might bind themselves against their own future preferences, and then to discover what goes wrong when the binding is absent, too weak, or too rigid. There should be no lecture upfront; they should not hear the term "Ulysses contract" until the debrief.

The game should be playable in small groups of three or four, with the whole room playing simultaneously. I want the payoff structure to create a genuine tension between a long-term goal and a series of short-term temptations, such that participants who are not permitted to precommit predictably do worse than those who are — but the precommitment should also carry a cost, so that the choice is not trivial. The activity should run for about ninety minutes, leaving thirty minutes for the debrief.

In the debrief, please map the moments of the game onto the philosophical structure: the distinction between first-order and second-order preferences, the paradox of voluntarily surrendering future choice, and the constitutional analogue — why a polity might entrench certain rights or procedures against future majorities. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the activity and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of Ulysses contracts the game cannot capture and I will need to supply myself.

About half the group has read Elster; the other half has not. The activity should work for both, but the debrief should give the Elster readers something to chew on without losing the newcomers.
