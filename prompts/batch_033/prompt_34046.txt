I retired from physics a few years ago and have been reading my way through philosophy of probability—Lewis, Hájek, that crowd. The Principal Principle seemed straightforward when I first read it: roughly, your credence in a proposition, conditional on its objective chance being x, ought to be x, provided you don't have "inadmissible" evidence. But I've been testing my own intuitions against a bunch of cases and I can't find a clean statement of what makes evidence inadmissible that gets all of them right. I'd like you to take my verdicts and tell me what principle—what account of admissibility—captures them, or which ones I have to give up.

Here are the cases. Case one: I know a coin is fair, I'm about to flip it, the objective chance of heads is one half, and my credence should be one half. Fine. Case two: the coin was flipped an hour ago in another room, I haven't seen the result, the chance was one half at the time of flipping, and my credence should still be one half. The fact that the outcome is already settled doesn't seem to matter. Case three: same setup, but I've been given a reliable crystal ball glimpse of the coin showing heads. The chance is one half, but my credence should be near one. The crystal ball information is inadmissible, and that feels right. Case four: I'm about to flip the coin, but I've just watched it land heads on the last hundred flips. The chance according to the setup is still one half—the coin is fair by construction, let's say I built it myself and tested it exhaustively. But having seen a hundred heads in a row, I'd put my credence above one half. I'm not sure whether this means I think the chance isn't really one half (maybe I've been deceived about the setup), or whether I think the streak is inadmissible evidence, or whether I'm just violating the principle. Case five: I know the chance of a radiactive atom decaying in the next hour is one half. I set up a detector and leave the room. While I'm out of the room, before the hour is up, my credence that it will decay is one half. After the hour is up but before I check the detector, my credence is still one half—even though the decay either happened or didn't. This seems right, and it seems like the same thing as case two. But now case six: I'm told by a perfectly reliable oracle that the atom will not decay. The chance is one half, but my credence drops to zero. The oracle is like the crystal ball: inadmissible. But what's the difference between the oracle (inadmissible) and the mere fact that the hour has passed (admissible)? Both give me information about what actually happens, but only one seems to undermine the chance. Case seven: I'm told that the objective chance of some event E is 0.7, but I'm also told that if the chance is 0.7, then the laws of nature plus the present state of the world entail that E does not happen. This is the undermining future case. I honestly don't know what to say. If the laws entail not-E, can the chance really be 0.7? But if I accept the chance claim, must my credence be 0.7? I feel like something has to break here.

So those are my verdicts—or confusions, in the last case. I want a principle that tells me, for any body of evidence E, whether E is admissible relative to a chance proposition. State the principle clearly, show how it handles each case, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate (and whether I should revise them), and apply it to a case I haven't listed: I know the chance of heads is one half, but I also know that the coin is one of ten that were all flipped simultaneously, and nine of them have already been revealed as heads. What's my credence about the tenth, and why?

And please don't format it like a textbook—just continuous prose, like you're writing me a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no tables.
