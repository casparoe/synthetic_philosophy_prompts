So I've been going down this rabbit hole about whether technology is neutral or not, sparked by some debate at work about the recommendation system we're building. I started keeping a list of cases and my gut reactions to them, and now I'm trying to figure out if there's actually a coherent principle behind my judgments or if I'm just all over the place.

Here's what I've got. Cases where I think the artifact clearly *does* have politics built into it: (1) Those low-hanging overpasses on Long Island parkways that Robert Moses designed to keep buses—and therefore poor people and people of color—from reaching certain beaches. (2) Facebook's newsfeed algorithm tuned to maximize engagement, which ends up amplifying outrage and polarizing content. (3) Those park benches with armrests down the middle so homeless people can't sleep on them. (4) An AI resume-screening tool that learns from historical hiring data and ends up systematically downgrading women applicants, even though nobody explicitly told it to discriminate. Cases where I think the artifact is basically neutral: (5) A kitchen knife—it's just a tool, you can cook dinner or stab someone, the knife dosent care. (6) The TCP/IP protocol—it routes packets, it doesn't know or care what's in them. (7) Git for version control—it's just a tool for managing code history.

Now here's where I start getting tangled up. Case (4) bothers me because the discrimination wasn't intentional—the engineers didn't *mean* to build a sexist hiring filter—but I still want to say the artifact has politics. But then why don't I say the same about TCP/IP? After all, TCP/IP embodies values like decentralization and openness; it was funded by DARPA during the Cold War and designed to survive nuclear attack by routing around damage. You could argue that's a political vision baked right into the architecture. And yet my gut says TCP/IP is neutral in a way the resume screener isn't. Is that just me being inconsistent?

And then there's a case I genuinely can't settle: a city's bike lane network. Suppose it was designed through a public process that, in practice, was dominated by affluent cyclists, so the resulting network overwhelmingly serves wealthy neighborhoods while skipping lower-income areas where people also bike to work. Nobody sat down and said "let's exclude poor neighborhoods." But the outcome is systematically unequal. Does the bike lane network have politics? My gut says... maybe? But I can't articulate why this would be different from, say, a road network that also connects wealthy suburbs better than poor neighborhoods because that's where the tax base is. So I guess my question is: is there a principle that captures all (or most) of my verdicts here? What does it say about the bike lane case? And which of my verdicts, if any, do I need to give up to be consistent?
