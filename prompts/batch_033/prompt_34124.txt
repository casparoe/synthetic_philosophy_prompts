So I've been thinking about this thing called the Ellsberg paradox, which I came across while reading about how people make decisions under uncertainty. Basically the setup is: you've got two urns. Urn A has 50 red balls and 50 black balls. Urn B has 100 balls total, some red and some black, but you have no idea what the ratio is — could be all red, could be all black, could be anything. You get to bet on a color and then draw a ball, and if it matches you win $100.

First they ask: do you want to bet on red from Urn A, or red from Urn B? Most people pick Urn A. Then they ask: black from Urn A, or black from Urn B? Most people pick Urn A again. But this is weird, right? If you prefer Urn A for red, that means you think red is more likely in A than B. If you prefer Urn A for black, that means you think black is more likely in A than B. But in Urn A, red and black have to sum to 100% — so if both are more likely in A, you're basically saying the probabilities in B sum to less than 100%, which doesn't make sense.

What's going on is that people don't just dislike bad odds, they dislike *not knowing* the odds. They're ambiguity averse. And this seems to violate the standard expected utility model where you should have some subjective probability for everything and act accordingly.

I've been going back and forth on wether this is actually irrational or not. On one hand, if you literally can't assign a probability, what else are you supposed to do? On the other hand, the math clearly shows you're being inconsistent — you're acting like there's a probability that doesn't add up. But maybe the math is just a map and the territory is that true uncertainty (where you don't even know the distribution) is a real thing that expected utility theory is just papering over.
