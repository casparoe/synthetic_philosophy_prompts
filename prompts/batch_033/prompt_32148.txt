I'm teaching a fourth-year undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mathematics, and in three weeks we reach Skolem's paradox. The students have done a first course in logic — they have worked through the completeness and compactness theorems and can follow a Henkin construction — and they have seen the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem proved, but they have no real fluency with model theory and their exposure to set theory is limited to the ZFC axioms presented informally in the first two weeks of the seminar. The paradox, that ZFC proves the existence of uncountable sets yet has a countable model if it has any model at all, reliably produces a productive vertigo in students at this level, but it also reliably produces a tangle of confusions that I would prefer to head off in the design of the session rather than sort through during the discussion. I am not asking for an explanation of the paradox for my own benefit; I need a teaching plan for a single seventy-five-minute meeting.

What I would like from you is the following: the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the paradox lands as a genuine puzzle rather than as a trick or a triviality; the one worked example or concrete case that should carry the session and why it is better than the obvious alternatives, such as working directly with the reals or with the power set of the naturals; the misconceptions students predictably form — I already expect 'but then the reals are really countable after all,' 'the theorem must be wrong,' and 'this is just a verbal trick about what "countable" means' — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off, whether by framing, by example, or by direct address; and a question I can pose in the final five minutes to check whether they have grasped the distinction between the internal and external viewpoint rather than merely learned to parrot it. Please also indicate what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and why. The answer should be in British English.
