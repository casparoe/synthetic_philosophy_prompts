In a set theory reading group, someone raised a question about why algebraists standardly reach for Zorn's lemma when they need a maximal element, rather than invoking the well-ordering theorem, given that the two are equivalent over ZF. A senior participant deflected the question by quoting the well-known line: "The Axiom of Choice is obviously true, the well-ordering principle obviously false, and who can tell about Zorn's lemma?" It got a laugh and the conversation moved on, but I think the joke is doing real argumentative work under cover of wit, and I am not sure it survives scrutiny. I want to know exactly what that one-liner could be claiming. Lay out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing — about the epistemic status of the three equivalent statements, about the reliability of mathematical intuition when it comes to non-constructive principles, about proof-theoretic or constructive differences among them, about what makes a principle psychologically obvious or natural, about the pedagogical order in which the three are introduced, or about the criteria by which mathematicians actually choose which equivalent to invoke. For each reading, say whether it is true, false, or too vague to judge, and whether it is a claim that a competent mathematician should endorse or merely a witticism that does not rise to the level of an assertion. Then say which reading, if any, actually supports using the quip to settle the original question about why Zorn's lemma is the standard tool in algebra.
