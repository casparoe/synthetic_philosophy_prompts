I have been working through the accuracy-first epistemology program and have constructed an argument that seems to show the framework cannot simultaneously hold two claims its proponents endorse: that probabilistic credences are immodest and that conditionalization is the rational update rule. I am unable to locate the flaw, and I would like a diagnosis of which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs.

The argument:

(1) The accuracy-first framework measures epistemic disvalue by a strictly proper scoring rule. A scoring rule is strictly proper just in case for any probabilistic credence function c and any distinct credence function c', the expected inaccuracy of c according to c itself is strictly less than the expected inaccuracy of c' according to c. Thus every probabilistic credence function is immodest: it uniquely expects itself to be the most accurate credence function.

(2) Suppose an agent has a probabilistic credence function c over a finite set of worlds W, and the agent is about to learn, with certainty, which member of a partition E = {E₁, …, Eₙ} of W is true. The agent considers two update policies. Stick: retain c regardless of what is learned. Conditionalize: upon learning Eᵢ, adopt the conditionalized credence function c(·|Eᵢ).

(3) The expected inaccuracy of Stick, from the perspective of c, is simply the expected inaccuracy of c: Σᵢ c(Eᵢ) · S(c, wᵢ), where wᵢ is a representative world in Eᵢ. The expected inaccuracy of Conditionalize, from the perspective of c, is Σᵢ c(Eᵢ) · S(c(·|Eᵢ), wᵢ).

(4) A standard accuracy-first result shows that the expected inaccuracy of Conditionalize is strictly less than the expected inaccuracy of Stick. That is, from c's own perspective, the policy of conditionalizing yields lower expected inaccuracy than the policy of keeping c unchanged.

(5) If Conditionalize has lower expected inaccuracy than Stick, then from the perspective of c, the credence functions that result from conditionalizing are, in expectation, more accurate than c. So there is a genuine sense in which c expects that there exist credence functions—namely, the various c(·|Eᵢ)—that will be more accurate than c.

(6) But premise (1) says that c is immodest: c expects itself to be more accurate than any other single credence function. In particular, for each i, c expects itself to be more accurate than c(·|Eᵢ).

(7) Premises (5) and (6) appear to contradict one another. According to (5), c expects the set of conditionalized credence functions to outperform c; according to (6), c expects each of those same credence functions to be outperformed by c. If both expectations are of the same kind, we have a contradiction.

(8) Therefore, the accuracy-first framework cannot consistently maintain both that probabilistic credences are immodest and that conditionalization is the rational update policy. At least one of the premises must be rejected.

I am most reluctant to abandon the rationality of conditionalization, but I also find the immodesty claim well-motivated, and the mathematical result supporting it is compelling. Where exactly does this argument go wrong, and what is the cost of the necessary repair?
