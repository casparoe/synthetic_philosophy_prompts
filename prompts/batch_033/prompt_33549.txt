I've just joined my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and last month we spent two hours on a request from a family whose short-but-healthy fourteen-year-old wants growth hormone, then the very next week we had a request for a cochlear implant for a small child, and somehow half the room thought one of these was clearly "treatment" and the other clearly "enhancement" while the other half thought the exact opposite, and neither side could say what rule they were actually using. So I want something I can bring to the next meeting: a decision tree or taxonomy of the positions on the therapy/enhancement distinction, built from the questions that actually split people, not from the labels.

Please make the instrument sort people by how they answer concrete cases rather than by which school they already like. The branch nodes should be questions like (but not only) whether the baseline is species-typical functioning, whether the goal is restoring a capacity the person once had or is expected to have, whether the intervention is invasive or reversible, whether it's a drug, a device, or surgery, whether the condition causes present or future suffering, and whether there's a social or disability-rights framing that matters (the Deaf community stuff around cochlear implants, for instance). The leaves should include at least: a strong bioconservative position (natural functioning has intrinsic value), a normal-function or disease-based position (therapy restores normal functioning, enhancement goes beyond it), a welfarist position (what matters is whether it reduces suffering or increases wellbeing), a transhumanist or permissive-enhancement position, and a deflationary position that says the distinction is doing no real work and we should drop it. If any combination of the branching questions has no defenders, say so and say whether you think that's because the position is incoherent, just unattractive, or unexplored; and flag any two leaves the tree can't genuinely seperate. Aim for something a group of non-philosophers (doctors, nurses, a chaplain, a lawyer) could work through in about twenty minutes, with a few concrete cases to anchor it — the growth hormone kid, the cochlear implant, and a student wanting modafinil in exam season would do. I'd rather have a tree with some empty boxes than a neat one that papers over the mess. Oh, and could you write the whole thing in British English? The committee's paperwork uses UK spellings and I keep tripping over it.
