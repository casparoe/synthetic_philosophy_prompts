I'm a journalist covering the ideas beat, and I'm working on a piece about how thinkers from the past might have reacted to the effective altruism movement if they'd lived to see it. The one I keep coming back to is G.K. Chesterton. The obvious line is that he'd loathe the whole thing — he was a distributist, a localist, deeply suspicious of the kind of abstract systematising that EA runs on. But I'm not sure it's that neat. His fence principle, for instance, isn't actually a blanket defence of the status quo — it's an epistemic injunction to understand why something exists before you knock it down, which is a pretty good fit with the EA commitment to evidence and cause prioritisation. And his beef with the Fabians and the Wellsian progressives of his own day was aimed at their overconfidence, not at the very idea of trying to do good at scale. He even wrote about charity in ways that sometimes sound oddly impartialist, if you squint. So what I'm wondering is: if you really sat down and tried to reconstruct a Chestertonian verdict on EA as it exists today — not the straw-man version, but one that takes his actual philosophical commitments serioulsy — where would he land? What would he grant, and where would he draw the line? And could you write the answer in British English, please?
