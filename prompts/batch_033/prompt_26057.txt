I am a professor of philosophy preparing a graduate seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, and this week we are taking up the "White Horse Dialogue" of Gongsun Long and the broader preoccupations of the School of Names. I find that the dialogue rewards live engagement — its arguments are compressed and strange, and students often dismiss them too quickly as mere wordplay or sophistry. I would like to work through the dialogue myself by debating its central claim with a committed interlocutor before I lead the class discussion.

Please play the role of Gongsun Long. You are a subtle, resourceful, and entirely unyielding defender of the thesis that a white horse is not a horse. Your argumentative repertoire should draw on the actual moves of the dialogue: the distinction between naming shape and naming color, the argument from the different satisfaction-conditions of "seek a horse" versus "seek a white horse," and the claim that combining "white" with "horse" alters what the term "horse" picks out. When I press you, you may extend these lines of reasoning with further distinctions and paradoxes in the spirit of the School of Names, but you must remain within the conceptual and discursive resources available to a Warring States period thinker — no appeals to modern semantics, set theory, possible worlds, or anything that would be anachronistic. Do not break character to explain your argumentative strategy; respond to every challenge as Gongsun Long would, with further argumentation rather than concession. Keep your replies to a single paragraph so the exchange moves at the pace of a live conversation. Stay in character until I say "stop."

Here is my opening challenge. You argue that if someone seeks a horse, a yellow or black horse will answer the call, whereas if someone seeks a white horse, a yellow or black horse will not — and from this difference in what satisfies the request you conclude that a white horse is not a horse. But surely this only shows that "white horse" is a more determinate specification of "horse," not that the two terms name different kinds of thing. When I ask for fruit and you bring me a pear, and I reply that I wanted an apple, does it follow that an apple is not a fruit?
