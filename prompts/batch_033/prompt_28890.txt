I've been reading about how psychology experiments get approved and something's bugging me. My uni's research ethics committee makes researchers get informed consent before a study: participants are told what they're signing up for and sign a form. But the same committee also approves studies that involve deception — researchers lie about the purpose of the study, give fake feedback, or plant a confederate who pretends to be another participant. The participant signs a form saying they agree to be deceived, but they're obviously not told what the deception will actually be, becuase that would ruin the study. At most they're told in vague terms that they might be misled at some point. What theory of valid consent does this practice presuppose? Does it treat consent as a one-off thing at the start rather than something ongoing, and is it coherent to consent to being deceived when you don't know what you're agreeing to? And what about the fact that most participants are students earning course credit — does the committee's practice quietly presuppose that mild inducements don't undermine voluntariness? I want to know what this institutional practice implies about how deception, coercion, and competence each bear on consent, whether the practice is internally consistent, and what would have to change if the committee instead held that any deception at all invalidates consent. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question. And write in British English please.
