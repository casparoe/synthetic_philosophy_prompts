I am giving a talk next month to a reading group in philosophy of time, philosophical logic, and the philosophy of language, defending a Peircean account of future contingents. My thesis is that a present assertion of "It will be the case that p," where p concerns a contingent future event, is true only if p occurs in every causally possible continuation of the present, and is otherwise false. Thus, for a genuinely contingent future event, both "It will happen" and "It will not happen" are false, while "It might happen" and "It might not happen" are true; the disjunction "It will happen or it will not happen" remains true when read as a whole, though neither disjunct is. The future is open, and the semantics of "will" and "might" reflect that openness. My argument rests on three claims: a branching-time semantics in which truth at a moment is defined over the set of histories through that moment; the claim that natural-language "will," as opposed to "might," carries a commitment beyond mere possibility; and the claim that this is the only way to deny fatalism without giving up a stable truth for the past.

The audience is mostly philosophers of language, time, and modality. Two members are committed Ockhamists who accept a thin red line: they think there is already a fact of the matter about which branch is the actual future, so future contingents have determinate truth values now. One is a supervaluationist who holds that such utterances are neither true nor false at the time of use, with the disjunction super-true. Two are B-theorists who are suspicious of any deep asymmetry between past and future. The rest are agnostics with strong logical instincts and no fixed position. Nobody in the room is a fatalist; everyone grants that future contingency is compatible with the absence of causal necessitation, so the fight is over truth and semantics, not over freedom.
