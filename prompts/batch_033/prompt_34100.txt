I'm preparing for a seminar on the limits of impartiality in ethics, and I've been trying to get my own commitments straight on Williams's integrity objection before I present. I keep finding that when I state them all together, I'm not sure they all fit. Here is where I currently stand:

(1) The correct moral theory must be impartial at the foundational level. I don't think there are irreducible agent-relative reasons — any legitimate partiality has to be justified by appeal to impartial considerations (e.g., that allowing people to favour their loved ones produces better outcomes, or that it's what impartial agents would agree to behind a veil of ignorance).

(2) But I also think Williams's "one thought too many" objection is a genuine problem for impartial theories, not just a complaint about how it feels to do the calculation. The objection, as I understand it, is that the attitudes constitutive of intimate relationships — love, loyalty, the sense that someone is irreplaceable to you — are incompatible with the kind of deliberative stance that impartial theories require. If you have to be prepared to justify saving your wife by appeal to the greater good, you are not fully in the relationship.

(3) The right response to this, I've long thought, is some kind of two-level or self-effacing view: impartial principles justify the practice of partial deliberation, but the agent on the ground should not invoke those principles while acting. The justification sits in the background; the agent acts from the partial motives directly.

(4) However, I also hold that transparency about one's reasons is a regulative ideal. If a moral practice requires the agent not to think about its justification while engaged in it — if the practice would be undermined by the agent's full awareness of why they are doing it — that strikes me as a serious cost. It looks like a kind of bad faith, or at least a departure from the ideal of self-understanding.

(5) Finally, I think that in Williams's rescue case, the husband who saves his wife and answers "because she's my wife" has given a perfectly adequate moral answer. He hasn't failed to think morally; he has thought morally in exactly the right way. The fact that he didn't run a utilitarian calculation is not a defect.

I'd like an audit of this set. Which commitments are in tension with which others, and how severe are those tensions? Are there any that only look like they're in tension but dissolve on closer inspection? For each genuine tension, what would have to give to resolve it, and what is the cost? And if I had to revise one commitment to make the whole set consistent, which revision would be least costly?
