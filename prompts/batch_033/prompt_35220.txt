I'm writing an essay for my philosophy class on the idea of moral trade — roughly, whether doing a good thing can ever cancel out doing a wrong thing, or whether some moral considerations cannot be traded off against others. I've been trying to work out what I actually think by going through cases and recording my verdicts. Here they are:

1. A doctor kills a healthy patient to harvest their organs for five dying patients. Verdict: wrong, no matter how many are saved.
2. A company pollutes a river, harming a village, but donates a very large sum to a children's hospital. Verdict: wrong. The donation doesn't cancel the harm.
3. A government demolishes one family's home without consent to build a hospital for thousands, but compensates them at market value. Verdict: reluctantly acceptable.
4. Breaking a promise to meet a friend because you encounter a stranger having a medical emergency. Verdict: acceptable.
5. Breaking a promise to meet a friend because a better invitation came along, even though the friend says they don't mind. Verdict: wrong.
6. A student cheats on a university entrance exam to secure a scholarship their family depends on. Verdict: I keep going back and forth on this one.
7. A politician takes a bribe from a developer to approve a building project, but uses the money to fund a homeless shelter that saves lives. Verdict: wrong. The bribe remains wrong regardless of what the money does.
8. A parent steals bread to feed their starving child. Verdict: acceptable, so long as the harm to the baker is not severe.

Is there a single principle that captures these verdicts? What does it say about the cheating case? And if no principle fits all of them, which of my verdicts should I reconsider, and why?
