I've been following this back-and-forth about whether a self can be distributed across multiple brains, and honestly it feels like it's been going in circles for years. On one side you've got the Hofstadter camp — the idea from *I Am a Strange Loop* that the self is a pattern, and patterns can be copied or partially instantiated elsewhere. If your spouse has a rich enough model of you running in their head, then in some sense a low-resolution version of "you" exists in their brain. On the other side you've got people who find this obviously wrong: a self is the subject of experience, and there's no "what it's like" to be the copy of me in someone else's head, so calling that a distributed self is just a metaphor that got out of hand.

What's weird is that both sides seem to agree on the underlying facts. Nobody denies that we build detailed models of other people. Nobody denies that those models can influence behavior in ways that resemble the original person. And nobody (I think) is claiming that the model in your spouse's head has its own phenomenology. So what are they actually disagreeing about?

My hunch is that this is really a disagreement about what the word "self" picks out — whether it refers to the phenomenal subject (the thing that it's like to be) or to the psychological pattern (the personality, memories, dispositions). If that's right, it's a verbal dispute and there's nothing to resolve exept to notice the ambiguity. But I'm not sure, because I've seen people on both sides insist they're not just talking past each other — that there's a substantive question about whether the pattern *is* the thing that matters, or whether the subject-of-experience is. And Hofstadter himself seems to want to say something stronger than "let's use 'self' to mean pattern" — he seems to think the pattern view reveals something deep about what we actually are, which suggests he thinks it's not just a terminological choice.

So diagnose this: what kind of disagreement is it really? Is it verbal, is it about different things mattering to different people, is it a disagreement about where the burden of proof sits, or is there a hidden empirical question about how brains work that would settle it if we had the answer?
