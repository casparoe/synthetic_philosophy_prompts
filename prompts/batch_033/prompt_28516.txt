I'm a third-year PhD student in aesthetics, and I'm trying to settle on a dissertation topic within the paradox of tragedy — roughly, the puzzle of why we willingly seek out artworks that make us feel sadness, fear, or grief, emotions we ordinarily go to great lengths to avoid. I've read enough to know the major families of response: catharsis theories that treat the experience as purgative or cleansing; conversion theories according to which painful emotions are somehow transformed into pleasurable ones by the aesthetic frame; compensation or "rich experience" views that say the overall value of the experience, including its cognitive or artistic rewards, outweighs the negative affect; and metaresponse accounts, where what we enjoy is not the sadness itself but our higher-order reaction to it — for instance, satisfaction at our own capacity to be moved, or at the fact that we care about the right things. I also know there are deflationary lines, such as the make-believe theory, which denies that we feel genuine fear or sadness at fictions at all, dissolving the paradox rather than solving it.

What I don't have a grip on is which of these directions is actually worth years of my life. I want to make progress, not just recombine existing moves. So I'm asking for a prioritization of the open questions and lines of work in this area, defended in terms of: (a) how much turns on the question — does resolving it settle or reframe other debates in aesthetics, philosophy of emotion, or value theory? (b) tractability for one person with roughly three years of dissertation work and no special empirical training; (c) whether the question is currently underappreciated, meaning there are promising angles that philosophers have mostly overlooked or dismissed too quickly. I'd also like to hear what you would deprioritize: which approaches or questions in this area seem to lead in circles, produce answers that wouldn't change much downstream even if they were right, or have already been worked over thoroughly enough that a newcomer is unlikely to break new ground. The deliverable is a defended research agenda, not a literature review — I want to know where to dig, and why there rather than somewhere else.
