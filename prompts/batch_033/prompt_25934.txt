Two friends of mine, both politically engaged, got into a sustained argument recently about a city council member we all know. She had cast the deciding vote to approve a development project that will displace roughly forty families from a low-income apartment complex, but the deal includes a legally binding commitment from the developer to build and endow a public health clinic in a neighborhood that has gone without one for decades — something the city has tried and failed to fund for years. In her public statement, the council member acknowledged that the displacement is a genuine harm to the families involved and described her vote as a moral failure on that front, but she maintained that the clinic will prevent more suffering and save more lives over the long run than the displacement will cause, and that she would have been derelict to let the opportunity pass.

Here is roughly how the argument unfolded:

(1) David said the council member's position was incoherent. If she truly believed the vote was the right thing to do, all things considered, she should not feel guilty. Guilt is the appropriate response to wrongdoing, and by her own lights she did not do wrong. Feeling guilty about a justified act is a mistake.

(2) Sarah replied that this gets things exactly backwards — the guilt is precisely what shows she is a morally serious person. A politician who can harm innocent people and feel clean about it is far more dangerous than one who does the hard thing and carries the weight of it. She said this is what philosophers mean by "dirty hands": some politically necessary acts leave a moral stain on the agent even when they are justified.

(3) David pushed back: if the act is justified, the stain is irrational. Either the clinic's benefit justifies the displacement, in which case there is nothing to feel guilty about, or it does not, in which case she should not have cast the vote. He said the idea of dirty hands sounds profound but collapses on inspection into either ordinary justified action or ordinary wrongdoing — there is no coherent third category.

(4) Sarah said that was too tidy. She offered an analogy: a parent who can save only one of two children from a burning building does the right thing by saving one, but would still carry grief and something very much like guilt about the child they could not save. The fact that the choice was justified does not erase the loss or the agent's involvement in it. Politics, she argued, routinely presents choices where every available option wrongs someone, and the mark of a decent politician is that they do not rationalize away the wrong they chose.

(5) David said the analogy fails at a crucial point: the parent did not start the fire, whereas the council member is actively voting to cause the displacement. The parent is choosing whom to rescue from a bad situation they did not create; the politician is creating the bad situation for some in order to benefit others. He argued that these are morally different kinds of acts, and that feeling guilty about a harm you chose to cause, for the sake of a benefit to other people, is not a sign of moral depth — it is a sign of confusion.

(6) Sarah replied that the distinction between causing harm and failing to prevent harm is far less sharp than David was assuming, especially in contexts of institutional responsibility. By voting no, the council member would be choosing to let the clinic go unbuilt — which also causes harm, just by omission. She granted that the parent analogy was imperfect but said the core point survived: an agent can make the right choice and still owe something to those they wronged in the process — acknowledgment, regret, perhaps material compensation — and the language of guilt is a way of marking that debt.

Neither convinced the other, and I found myself pulled in both directions. Could you go through this exchange move by move and assess who has the better of each point? And then, stepping back, could you say what the strongest version of each position would ultimately have to concede, and whether there is a stable middle ground — or whether one side is fundamentally right?
