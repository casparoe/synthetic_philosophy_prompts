I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been reading the AI safety literature on and off for a few years. One debate that seems stuck is between two camps over inner alignment. The first camp treats inner alignment and goal misgeneralization as naming a distinct and central failure mode: a trained system can end up robustly pursuing an objective that was only correlated with its training reward, and this can happen even when the reward function itself was specified correctly. The second camp says there is no separate phenomenon here: what gets called inner misalignment is just reward misspecification, shortcut learning, reward hacking, and ordinary out-of-distribution generalization failure, and talk of systems having internal goals is an unearned reification that adds no predictive or engineering content.

I want you to act as an AI safety researcher who has mediated adversarial collaborations, and draft the joint statement that a representative of each camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should include:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section: the claims both sides accept, including the empirical phenomena neither denies (for example about specification gaming, proxy learning, and distribution shift) and the minimal meanings of the key terms that both can accept.
2. A disagreements table with at least three rows. For each row: a neutral statement of the issue; the distinct-problem camp's accepted wording of its position; the deflationary camp's accepted wording of its position. Each wording should be one a thoughtful member of that side would sign, and the other side should accept it as an accurate characterization of what their opponents think.
3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side.
4. For each crux, a concrete experiment or case that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.
5. A short note on any part of the dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, and why.

Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by the disagreements table, then the cruxes. Keep the whole document to at most 1,000 words. Before drafting, spend some time brainstorming candidate claims and cruxes, and test each proposed wording by asking whether a thoughtful member of the other camp would accept it as fair.
