So we were at my place last Thursday — me, Clara, and Dan, maybe a bottle of wine too many — and somehow we got onto climate policy. Clara does environmental consulting and Dan's an economist, so this happens. Clara said something about how we have a moral obligation to spend whatever it takes to stabilize the climate for future generations, and Dan kind of snorted and said that's exactly the kind of thing that sounds noble until you think about it for ten seconds. Future people are going to be much richer than us, he said — like, imagine asking a peasant in 1300 to sacrifice for your 401(k). Clara said that's not the same at all, the peasant couldn't have known about us, whereas we know exactly what we're doing to the atmosphere. Dan said knowing doesn't change the direction of the transfer: you're asking poorer people to sacrifice for richer people, and calling that justice is backwards. Clara came back with: so by that logic we shouldn't care about suffering in Switzerland because the Swiss are richer than Nigerians? Dan said no, that's spatial, this is temporal — you can transfer resources across space right now, you can't send carbon capture tech back to 1850. Clara said the asymmetry goes the other way: we can affect them, they can't affect us, which makes our responsibility greater, not less. Dan said that's just the drowning child argument and it proves too much — by that logic we should reduce our consumption to subsistence level right now to invest for the people of the year 3000. Clara said that's a sorites problem, not a refutation: the fact that you can't draw a bright line doesn't mean there's no obligation. At that point I refilled everyone's wine and changed the subject, but I've been turning it over since. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what — not just who wins overall, but which specific points land and which don't?
