I'm a novelist working on a book set in classical Athens — a character study centered on a metic family that owns slaves — and I've been reading around the intellectual culture of the period to get the texture of contemporary attitudes right rather than projecting modern sensibilities backward. In a recent popular history of Greek philosophy (well-regarded but aimed at a general audience, not specialists), I came across this summary of Aristotle's views on natural slavery:

"Aristotle defended slavery on the grounds that some people are born to be slaves. He argued that just as the soul naturally rules the body, and reason rules the appetites, so too some human beings are naturally fitted to be ruled by others. These natural slaves, he claimed, lack the capacity for reason — they can follow instructions but cannot deliberate or plan for themselves. Because they are incapable of managing their own lives, slavery is actually in their best interest: they are better off being directed by someone with a fully developed rational faculty. Aristotle was aware that many slaves in Athens had been captured in war and might in fact be perfectly rational, but he dismissed this as a contingent injustice that didn't undermine the underlying principle. He even extended the argument to women, claiming that although women possess reason, their reason is defective in a way that places them in a different but related category of natural subordination. In essence, Aristotle took the social arrangements he saw around him and worked backward to a metaphysical justification, making the conventional hierarchies of his own society look like necessities of nature."

Something about this summary strikes me as too tidy — the kind of tidy that makes a subtle thinker sound like a cartoon villain. As a novelist I'm trained to be suspicious of that. But I lack the philosophical training to say exactly where it goes wrong, or even whether it does go wrong, or whether my unease is just discomfort with an unpalatable view. Could you give me a committed audit of this summary? I want to know which parts are accurate renderings of what Aristotle actually says in the Politics, which parts are subtle misrepresentations — conflations, overstatements, anachronistic framings — and what a careful defender of Aristotle's overall project (not of slavery, but of the philosophical seriousness of the argument) would object to. I'm less interested in whether Aristotle's view is morally repellent — I can adjudicate that for myself — than in whether the conceptual architecture described here corresponds to the one Aristotle built.
