**Exam question**

In his 2003 paper, Joshua Knobe presented experimental evidence that folk attributions of intentional action are sensitive to the moral valence of an action's outcome. Explain the experimental design and the asymmetry it revealed. Then evaluate the following claim: the Knobe effect shows that the folk concept of intentional action is fundamentally a moral concept, not a psychological one. What does the effect actually license us to conclude about the relationship between moral evaluation and intentionality judgments?

---

**Student answer**

Knobe's experiment used two vignettes involving a corporate chairman making a business decision. In both vignettes, the chairman is told that a new profit-maximizing program will have a side effect: in one case it will harm the environment, in the other it will help the environment. In both cases, the chairman explicitly says he doesn't care about the side effect — he only wants to make a profit. The program is implemented and the side effect occurs. Participants are then asked whether the chairman intentionally brought about the side effect.

The key finding is a striking asymmetry: participants are far more likely to say the chairman intentionally harmed the environment than to say he intentionally helped it. This is surprising because the chairman's mental states — his beliefs, desires, and expressed attitude — are identical across the two conditions. The only thing that differs is whether the side effect is morally bad (harm) or morally good (help). So the asymmetry seems to be driven purely by moral considerations.

This result has been taken to show that the folk concept of intentional action is fundamentally a moral concept. On this interpretation, when ordinary people decide whether an action was intentional, they are not merely checking off a list of psychological criteria (did the agent believe the outcome would occur? did the agent desire the outcome?). Instead, they are making what is at bottom a moral judgment: if the outcome is bad, the agent is seen as having brought it about intentionally; if the outcome is good, the agent is not. The concept of intentional action, on this view, is not a descriptive psychological concept at all — it is a normative concept we use to assign praise and blame.

I think this interpretation goes too far, but it captures something real. The Knobe effect does not show that the folk concept of intentional action is entirely moral rather than psychological. After all, participants are not asked "is the chairman blameworthy?" — they are asked whether he acted intentionally, and they presumably understand this as a question about his mental states. What the effect does show is that moral considerations play a role in how we apply the concept. The standard belief-desire model of intentional action cannot explain the asymmetry on its own, because belief and desire are held constant. So we need to revise our account of the folk concept to include a moral dimension.

However, there is an alternative explanation that the "moral concept" interpretation overlooks. The asymmetry might reflect not the concept of intentional action itself, but a conversational implicature: when the side effect is harmful, saying the chairman brought it about "intentionally" might serve to emphasize his culpability, whereas when the side effect is helpful, saying he brought it about "intentionally" might misleadingly imply he deserves credit he explicitly disavowed. On this pragmatic account, the asymmetry reveals something about how we use language in morally charged contexts, not something about the structure of the concept itself.

In my view, the Knobe effect licenses a moderate conclusion: moral evaluation influences intentionality judgments, but it does not replace or exhaust the psychological criteria. The folk concept of intentional action has both descriptive and normative dimensions, and the Knobe effect is evidence that these dimensions interact in ways that purely descriptive theories cannot capture.
