I am a novelist, and I am working on a book whose central character lives through a brief interval of experience that is altered by an experimental technology before she can report it; the plot turns on whether she lived through the unedited version first and then had her memory overwritten, or whether what she lived through was already the edited version. To keep the book's metaphysics from being careless, I want to understand the actual state of thinking about the color phi phenomenon and the contrast between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of conscious experience.

Let me set up the question precisely. In the color phi paradigm, a red spot is flashed at one location and then a green spot is flashed at a nearby location, with a brief interval between the two; normal observers report seeing a single spot move from the first location to the second and change color roughly midway along its apparent path. Two families of explanation are standard. On the Stalinesque account, the subject's conscious experience itself is delayed and assembled only after the second stimulus has arrived, so the moving, color-changing spot is what the subject actually experiences; there was never an unedited conscious experience of the earlier stimulus in isolation. On the Orwellian account, the subject consciously experiences the sequence in something closer to real time, and the later report of a single moving, color-changing spot is produced by a post hoc revision of memory rather than a revision of experience itself.

Please give me a number between 0 and 1 for each of three possibilities: the Stalinesque account is correct; the Orwellian account is correct; and the distinction between them is not a real distinction, so there is no fact of the matter about which is correct. The three numbers should sum to 1. But I am more interested in the decomposition than in the headline numbers. For each of the following terms, give a rough probability and a one- or two-sentence justification:

1. That the Orwellian/Stalinesque contrast survives once one abandons the idea of a single place or moment in the brain where experience is brought together; that is, that the contrast marks a real empirical difference rather than an artifact of a picture of consciousness we have independent reason to reject.
2. Conditional on the contrast being real, that the temporal construction of visual experience is postdictive in the strong sense the Stalinesque account requires: the brain waits for later information and then builds an experience that includes the color change before the second stimulus is consciously registered.
3. That the color phi phenomenon itself can discriminate between the two accounts, as opposed to being compatible with both and serving only to make the problem of timing vivid.
4. That independent evidence from related phenomena such as backward masking, apparent motion, and other postdictive effects favors the Stalinesque account over the Orwellian account.
5. Any further term you think the final credence should include.

Then show how the terms combine to yield your three numbers. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, and describe one concrete piece of evidence — an experiment, a neural measurement, or a theoretical argument — that would move your final credence the most, and in which direction.
