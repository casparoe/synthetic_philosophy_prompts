I'm a science journalist workin on a piece about the unity of consciousness and I keep going in circles on the cases. Here's my gut:

Split-brain patient after the corpus callosum is severed: left hand unbuttons a shirt while the right hand buttons it back up. That's not one person having a conflicted moment — that's two streams of experience that don't know about each other. Verdict: two consciousnesses.

Hemispherectomy on a toddler for intractable epilepsy: whole right hemisphere removed. Kid grows up normal, one personality, one set of memories. The idea that we "killed one of two people" feels absurd. Verdict: one consciousness.

Conjoined twins with a shared thalamus: two heads, some neural bridge. They report different visual experiences but can sometimes feel what the other is feeling. Verdict: two consciousnesses, but leaky.

DID with amnesic alters: the host loses time, the alter has its own preferences and memories. Verdict: more than one consciousness, but somehow not in the same way as the split-brain case — maybe because they take turns rather than run in parallel?

Deep anesthesia: no experience at all. Verdict: zero consciousnesses.

Flow state: playing guitar, lose all sense of self, fully absorbed. Clearly conscious though. Verdict: one.

What I want is a principle that draws the line between one and more-than-one consciousness in a way that gets most of these right. And then I want to know what it says about the case that keeps me up: an adult who gets a hemispherectomy — does the answer change from the toddler case, and if so why? I'm also pretty sure my DID verdict and my split-brain verdict can't both survive a clean principle. Which one goes, and what does the principle look like on the other side?
