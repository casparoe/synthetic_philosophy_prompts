Two people in my reading group got into an extended argument about Douglas Hofstadter's claim that creativity is best understood as "knob-twiddling" on existing conceptual structures — the idea that genuine innovation arises not from mysterious leaps but from the gradual, deliberate adjustment of parameters within a richly structured space of possibilities, guided by deep domain expertise.

One of them, Callum, argued that the model is either false or empty. If it means creativity is local hill-climbing in a fixed conceptual space, then it can't explain discontinuities: the move from Newtonian to relativistic mechanics, or the invention of atonal music, or any case where the space of possibilities itself gets restructured. If instead it means that any innovation can be redescribed, after the fact, as a "variation" on some suitably abstract precursor, then the model is unfalsifiable — a just-so story, not a model.

The other, Sasha, replied that this is a strawman. The knob-twiddling model, she said, explicitly rejects a fixed conceptual landscape: as you adjust parameters, the space itself transforms, and some adjustments reveal that what you took to be a wall was actually a door. She argued that even paradigm shifts come from noticing that a parameter everyone treated as fixed can in fact be varied — and that noticing this is itself a higher-order knob-twiddle. She accused Callum of smuggling in a romantic "bolt from the blue" picture of creativity that doesn't survive scrutiny of how discoveries are actually made.

Callum came back with: if the space transforms as you explore it, then "knob-twiddling" is a misleading metaphor — knobs on a device presuppose a stable device. He said Sasha was preserving the term by draining it of content. He also pressed her on whether she could name a single historical innovation that the model rules out as impossible, since a model that permits everything explains nothing.

Sasha answered that the model does rule things out: it says you can't get genuine creativity without deep mastery of a domain, and you can't get it by random mutation alone. She said Callum's demand for a single counterexample missed the point because the model is about mechanisms and necessary conditions, not about drawing a bright line around "creative" versus "uncreative" outputs.

The argument stalled there. I've been turning it over since and genuinely can't tell who had the better of it. Can you adjudicate this dispute move by move — assessing each point, saying who is right about what, and offering an overall verdict on whose position is stronger?
