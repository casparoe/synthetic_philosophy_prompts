I was reading this pop-behavioral-econ book and it had a paragraph on the Allais paradox that I've been turning over. Here's the summary they gave:

'The Allais paradox is a famous experiment where Maurice Allais showed that people's choices violate the independence axiom of expected utility theory. In the first choice, most people take a guaranteed $1 million over a lottery that gives them a slightly higher chance at a much larger prize. In the second choice, where both options are gambles, they pick the one with the bigger potential payoff even though the difference in probabilities is the same. This is a contradiction: if you're an expected utility maximizer, the two choices should line up the same way. Allais's demonstration proved that expected utility theory is descriptively false, and it also showed that the independence axiom isn't a requirement of rationality — people aren't making a mistake when they choose the sure thing, they're revealing a legitimate preference for certainty. Kahneman and Tversky later showed that this preference for certainty is just one instance of loss aversion, and their prospect theory fixes the problem by weighting probabilities nonlinearly.'

Is this summary fair? I'm especially curious about the move from 'people make these choices' to 'the independence axiom isn't a requirement of rationality.' That seems like a bigger jump than the author acknowledges. But maybe I'm missing something about how the paradox is supposed to work.
