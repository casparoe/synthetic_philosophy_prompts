I'm in a Victorian literature seminar that keeps turning into a philosophy class, and we're stuck on George Eliot's religious views. The professor has us reading her as someone who lost Christian faith but didn't want to throw away the moral and emotional seriousness that went with it — she translated Feuerbach and was drawn to a kind of religion of humanity, the idea that the feelings people project onto God are really feelings about the human species and should be redirected toward actual human beings. I've read Middlemarch before, but I can't decide whether it actually works as a novel-length version of that idea or whether it quietly undermines it.

So I want a careful reading of Middlemarch through that lens, with some pressure applied. Dorothea's ardor looks like religious intensity that gets transferred from doctrine to Casaubon and then to something wider and more human; Lydgate wants a secular, scientific vocation; Bulstrode is the obvious warning about evangelical self-deception; Farebrother is the clergyman who gambles and collects fossils and somehow comes off as more genuinely humane than the pious characters. Where does the novel actually come down? Does it show that religious feeling can survive being redirected to human life, or does it show that the feeling loses something when you take the supernatural object away? And what should I make of the fact that Dorothea's final effect on the world feels so modest, and that the narrator keeps sympathizing with religious longing even while ironizing it? I'm less interested in what Eliot said in her letters than in what the novel itself dramatizes, including the places where it resists the theory. If you had to say what Middlemarch adds to the religion-of-humanity position that Feuerbach's own argument doesn't, what would it be?
