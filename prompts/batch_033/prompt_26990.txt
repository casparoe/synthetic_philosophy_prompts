You are a professor of political philosophy with expertise on the problem of dirty hands, writing for an audience of advanced philosophy graduate students. Before you produce your final answer, take a moment to brainstorm and evaluate candidate scenarios; then develop the strongest one in full.

The problem of dirty hands in politics is the idea that a political leader may sometimes have to do something genuinely morally wrong — not merely unpleasant or regrettable, but wrong — in order to bring about a greater good or avert a disaster. The literature offers three main families of response:

1. **Denial / absorption**: The action is not really wrong if it is genuinely necessary for the best outcome. What looks like a dirty action is simply the right action in tragic circumstances, and the agent's discomfort is a psychological residue, not a moral signal.
2. **Tragic necessity**: The action is genuinely wrong, and the agent must do it anyway, bearing the guilt and the moral remainder. The wrongness persists even though the action was, all things considered, what had to be done.
3. **Role-differentiated morality**: The norms governing political office are not the same as the norms governing ordinary interpersonal morality. An action wrong for a private citizen may be permissible — or even required — for a political leader, precisely because of the role.

I would like you to construct a thought experiment in which the dirty hands problem survives all three of these responses — that is, a case where each of the three standard ways of dissolving or accommodating the problem runs into serious difficulty. The specifications are:

- Set the scenario in ordinary municipal or regional governance — no war, no national security, no intelligence agencies, no life-or-death stakes.
- The politician must be a sincere moral agent who has genuinely tried to find alternatives; the dirtiness must not arise from negligence, corruption, or self-dealing.
- The action must be one that would be wrong for a private citizen in an analogous context, so the case cannot be resolved by simply noting that the politician's role changes the moral calculus in an unproblematic way.
- The action must be genuinely necessary for a significant public good, not merely convenient or politically expedient.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following:

1. Walk through what each of the three families of response would say about the case, and explain why each is inadequate or at least deeply strained.
2. Identify which specific feature of the case does the work against each response — that is, what about the case makes the denial response fail, what makes the tragic-necessity response unsatisfying, and what makes the role-differentiated response problematic.
3. If you were to modify the case to rescue one of the three responses (pick whichever you think is most defensible), what would you change, and would the modified case still capture what we care about in the dirty hands debate?
4. Conclude with an honest assessment: does your case show that the dirty hands problem is genuinely insoluble, or does it merely highlight the costs of each theoretical approach without refuting any of them?
