Hey—I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been trying to write up a short paper on the color phi phenomenon and the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction. I'd love comments on the argument I'm sketching, especially on whether it's too quick. Here's the idea in note form.

The setup: in color phi, two dots flash in quick succession, one red then one green at different locations, and subjects report seeing a single dot move and change from red to green roughly halfway along the path. The puzzle is that the green seems to be experienced before the green dot physically appears. The standard split: an Orwellian account says the initial experience is of two stationary flashes and the moving-dot story gets writen into memory after the fact (records altered retroactively); a Stalinesque account says the whole experience is delayed and edited before it ever reaches consciousness (a show trial with the verdict fixed in advance).

My thesis, roughly: the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction only looks like a real empirical difference if you assume a Cartesian theater—a single place and time where experience becomes conscious. Without that assumption there's no fact of the matter about whether the editing happened before or after consciousness, since there's no well-defined boundary for before to be relative to. And any evidence for one account would have to come through report or memory, which is exactly the medium the two accounts disagree about, so they can't in principle be pulled apart.

Questions: is this argument too quick? Is there a version of the Stalinesque view that doesn't need a Cartesian theater but still says something distinct? Can you think of an empirical result—direct neural decoding, maybe—that would actually let us choose between the two, or would any such result just re-describe the same process? Also, am I right that the argument generalizes to backward masking and the cutaneous rabbit, or is it specific to color phi?
