I keep noticing a tension in sequential reasoning about rational choice, and I do not have a name for it. Consider a two-player game in which the players alternate turns. On each turn, the player can either end the game and take the accumulated prize, or pass it to the other player, with the prize growing on each pass and with the payoffs arranged so that each player would rather take at any turn than pass and watch the other take on the next turn. The final turn, if reached, has an obvious rational move. So the player who would move just before that final turn should anticipate it and not pass; the player before that should anticipate that anticipation and not pass; and so on, back to the very first move. The backward chain seems to determine the whole play, and it says the first player should end the game immediately. But the chain rests entirely on a layered assumption: each player is rational, and each believes the others are rational, and each believes the others believe this, and so on without end. Now suppose someone deviates and passes when the chain said they would not. The next player has just observed an event that the layered assumption said could not happen. If they conclude that the other player is not rational, the premise that drove every earlier step of the chain is gone, and the remaining steps lose their force. If they instead keep the premise, they must treat what they saw as somehow compatible with it, but then the chain was not as binding as it seemed. Either way, a single observed deviation seems to unravel the entire structure, not just the step where it occurred. I have seen the same structure elsewhere: a firm deciding whether to fight a series of potential entrants, a state deciding whether to retaliate in a repeated confrontation, any situation in which today's move is supposed to be dictated by what everyone can infer about everyone else's future moves.

Is there an established name for this problem: the way the layered assumption seems to be exactly what the observed deviation calls into question, so that a single event contradicting the predicted path seems to call the whole path into question? I know there are standard labels for the backward chain itself and for the layered assumption taken separately, but I am after the specific difficulty that arises when the two are combined and a deviation occurs. What exactly fails when the deviation is observed: the rationality premise, the belief in others' rationality, or something about how one should revise beliefs after seeing an event to which the theory assigned no probability? I am not satisfied with a bare label; I want the concept carved out precisely, including what it is not, and how it differs from the ordinary backward chain and from the layered assumption on which the chain rests.
