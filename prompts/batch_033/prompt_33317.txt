I've been going back and forth with someone on a forum about the omnipotence paradox and I think I've gotten myself tangled up. The standard version is: can God create a stone so heavy he cannot lift it? If yes, there's a stone he can't lift, so he's not omnipotent. If no, there's a stone he can't create, so he's not omnipotent. The usual reply is that omnipotence only covers logically possible actions, and a stone an omnipotent being can't lift is a logical contradiction — like a square circle — so it's not a real task. Fine.

But here's what's bothering me. The same structure seems to show up in completely secular contexts, and the "logically impossible" reply doesn't obviously work there. Consider a maximally powerful chess engine. You might say: it can win any chess game. But then ask: can it win a chess game that it loses? That's just a game where the engine makes losing moves — there's no logical contradiction in describing such a game. So either the engine can win a game it loses (contradiction) or it can't win that game (so it's not maximally powerful). But "maximally powerful chess engine" is obviously not an incoherent notion — it just means an engine that plays optimally, and optimal play against another optimal engine might mean a draw, not a win. So "can win any chess game" was the wrong way to cash out maximal power. But then the parallel move for the theological case would be: "omnipotence, cashed out as 'can do anything,' was the wrong way to cash out God's power." And that seems like a much bigger concession than most theists want to make.

Or take a maximally effective safety system that prevents all accidents. Can it prevent the accident of its own failure? If it fails, there's an accident it didn't prevent; if it doesn't fail, there's no accident to prevent. But the concept isn't incoherent — we just understand it as preventing all accidents in its domain, and its own failure isn't in the domain. So the secular versions seem easy to dissolve with domain restrictions, but the theological version is supposed to be deep.

Here's the argument that seems to prove too much, laid out:

(1) The stone paradox shows that the specification "can perform any task" is incoherent.
(2) If (1) is true, then structurally parallel specifications like "can win any chess game" or "can prevent any accident" are also incoherent.
(3) But these specifications are not incoherent — they just need domain restrictions.
(4) So the stone paradox doesn't show that "can perform any task" is incoherent; it shows that it needs a domain restriction.
(5) But if we restrict the domain for omnipotence to "all logically possible tasks," we rule out the stone because the stone is a logically impossible task.
(6) And whether the stone is logically impossible is exactly what's at issue — the paradox is supposed to show that it isn't, or that the notion of omnipotence can't get off the ground.

I feel like there's an equivocation somewhere, or a scope error, or something about implicit domain restrictions I'm not seeing clearly. What I want is: where exactly does the stone paradox go wrong? Is it a scope error, an equivocation, a failure to recognize an implicit domain restriction, or something else? And does the same diagnosis handle the secular versions cleanly, or do we need different diagnoses for different cases? If the secular versions are easy and the theological one is hard, what's the relevant difference between them?

Lead with your bottom line — which premise you reject or which step fails — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble.
