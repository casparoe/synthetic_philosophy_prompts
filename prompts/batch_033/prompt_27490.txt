Write an essay on this controversy. The standard impossibility results in fair ML say that a risk score can't generally be both calibrated across groups and satisfy balance for the positive and negative class at once; the only cases where it works are degenerate ones where base rates are equal or the predictor is basically perfect. In plain terms: you can't have a score that means the same thing in every group and also equalizes false positive and false negative rates across groups, except when there's not much to fight about.

One side reads this as showing that algorithmic fairness, as its mostly been formalized, is internally incoherent: the criteria encode incompatible demands, so a risk score that has been certified as fair in pretrial detention or lending is an impossible object, and the honest conclusion is that we shouldn't be building these scores at all, or shouldn't pretend they've been made fair. The other side says the theorems don't show fairness is impossible — they show that fairness can't be reduced to a bundle of statistical properties of a score. On this view the results are a warning about measurement: fairness is a property of decisions and institutions, not error rates, and the right move is to change the object we optimize, e.g. by picking thresholds per group, optimizing decisions directly under constraints, or dropping calibration instead of dropping fairness.

Take the threshold/decision-theoretic reply seriously and weigh it against the first position.
