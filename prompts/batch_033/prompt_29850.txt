I teach high school biology, and I have been asked to respond to a parent's complaint about something I said in class. We were working through the evolution of the vertebrate eye, and at one point I told the students that "the reason the lens bulges the way it does is to focus incoming light onto the retina." The parent — a thoughtful person, and not a creationist, which is why I am taking her seriously — wrote to the principal arguing that I was quietly teaching the students that nature is designed. Her position is that lenses do not have reasons, retinas do not have goals, and natural selection does not intend anything; it simply sorts variants by their downstream effects. If I keep saying "the reason X is the case" about evolved parts, she says, I am training students to hear a designer's voice wherever they look at nature. My department head has asked me to draft a reply to the parent, and to change my classroom language if the objection is sound.

I have two conflicting intuitions here. On the one hand, the parent seems right that there is no agent anywhere in the evolutionary process — no mind that considered the optics and settled on a curvature. On the other hand, the sentence I said in class does not feel like a mere metaphor or a loose shorthand for something else: the lens really does seem to be for focusing light, and "the reason" seems to pick out something real that "the cause" or "the function" would miss. I do not want to teach my students a false picture of the living world, but I also do not want to surrender a way of talking that seems to capture something true about it.

What should I say in my reply to the parent, and how should I think about this myself? Is there a defensible way to speak of reasons in biology when no one designed the system, or is the parent right that all such talk is a category mistake?
