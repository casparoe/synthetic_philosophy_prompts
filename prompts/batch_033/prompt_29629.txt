I'm on my university's IRB and we're stuck on a weird one. A cognitive psychology lab wants to run a study testing whether people can reliably distinguish genuine from faked emotional expressions in short video clips. Here's the thing: they're screening participants by having them do exactly that task — watch a batch of clips, judge genuine vs. fake — and they'll only enroll participants who score in the top quartile. The researchers call this standard screening to reduce noise and say the main task uses different stimuli so there's no contamination.

But one of our reviewers filed a sharp dissent arguing the design is epistemically circular in a way that tracks the bootstrapping problem: if the screening task actually measures what it claims to (ability to detect genuine emotion), then the study's main question is already settled in the affirmative — you've just identified a group of people who can do it. And if the screening doesn't measure that ability, then the whole selection procedure is invalid and the results are uninterpretable either way. The reviewer says you can't use a putative measure of X to select subjects and then use those subjects to test whether the measure tracks X — that's bootstrapping, and the different stimuli don't escape the circle because the ability is supposed to be the same ability.

Half the board thinks this is a genuinely deep objection and we should reject or require a major redesign. The other half thinks it's crankish — "by that logic you could never use any pre-test to screen participants for anything" — and wants to approve as-is. I need to draft the decision memo for the full board: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It needs to take the circularity objection seriously (not just dismiss it) and give reasons the researchers could actually respond to. Keep it under 500 words.
