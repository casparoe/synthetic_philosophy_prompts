"Explain the illusionist position on phenomenal consciousness. What exactly is claimed to be the illusion, and how does illusionism differ from both standard physicalism and eliminativism about consciousness? Then present and evaluate the strongest objection to illusionism."

And here's the student's answer (unedited):

Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, defended most prominently by Keith Frankish, holds that phenomenal consciousness—the kind of consciousness that involves 'what it's like' to have experiences—is an illusion. According to illusionism, when we introspect, we represent ourselves as having private, ineffable, non-physical qualitative properties, but we don't actually have any such properties. The brain produces a kind of introspective model that misrepresents our own cognitive states as having phenomenal qualities.

The key difference from eliminativism is that illusionism doesn't deny that we have conscious experiences. It just denies that those experiences have the special phenomenal properties we take them to have. Standard physicalism, by contrast, typically accepts that phenomenal properties are real but holds that they are identical to or constituted by physical properties. So illusionism splits the difference: it agrees with the physicalist that there are no non-physical properties, but agrees with the eliminativist that the concept of phenomenal consciousness picks out something that doesn't exist.

The most serious objection is that illusionism seems self-refuting. If phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, then there must be an experience of that illusion—and that experience just is phenomenal consciousness. The illusionist responds that the illusion is not itself a phenomenal experience; it's a representational state that misrepresents. But this response seems inadequate. A representational state that misrepresents something as being the case when it isn't is just a false belief. The illusion of phenomenal consciousness feels like something—that feeling is precisely what's in question. So the illusionist seems to be saying that there is no feeling, but there is a feeling of there being a feeling, which is incoherent.

Moreover, if illusionism were true, we would have to say that the entire history of philosophy of mind has been based on a massive error. While this isn't a knockdown argument, the burden of proof on the illusionist is extremely high, and I don't think illusionism meets it.
