I've backed myself into a view about moral testimony and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The view has four parts. (1) There's a real difference between forming a moral belief by taking someone's word for it and forming one by working through the considerations yourself. (2) Taking someone's word can get you a true moral belief, and maybe even moral knowledge, but it can't get you moral understanding — the grasp of why the moral facts are what they are, the ability to handle nearby cases on your own. (3) That understanding is required for your moral beliefs to be fully justified, or at least for them to play their proper role when you actually deliberate. (4) So there's a standing pro tanto defect in moral deference, even to a reliable testifier, that doesn't attach in the same way to deferring about empirical trivia. I keep noticing I lean on all four of these, but I've never stress-tested which ones are doing the real work.

What would count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted from "this kills it" to "this just forces a small amendment." Which of (1)–(4) is the most exposed? What cases should a defender of the view concede are genuinely hard — someone with plainly better moral judgment than you, a friend who knows your blindspots, a society that seems to have moral experts? What does this view rule out that a rival view permits, and which of those predictions should I actually stand behind rather than quietly drop? And be straight with me: is there a live version of the view that actually risks being wrong, or has it been hedged and qualified until nothing could touch it? And if it has, is that a defect here, or just what you'd expect from a view about something as slippery as moral understanding?
