You are a decision theorist working at the intersection of formal epistemology and rational choice. Think carefully, and begin by sketching two or three candidate scenarios before settling on the one that best satisfies the specifications.

Construct a decision problem to the following specifications. The scenario must be an ordinary choice — an insurance purchase, an investment allocation, or a medical decision — with explicit probabilities and finite monetary payoffs. No exotic physics, no perfect predictors, and no utility functions stipulated merely to encode the desired verdict. The case must drive a wedge between expected utility (EU) theory and risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory, in which a risk function is applied to the probability of receiving at least a given payoff before utilities are aggregated. The divergence must not run through a tiny probability of catastrophe; it should concern moderate stakes and intermediate probabilities. Engineer the case so that the REU verdict is intuitively attractive, while the only way for EU to match that verdict is via utility curvature so severe that the same agent would be implausibly risk-averse in a larger-stakes version of the choice. The purpose of the case is to isolate what REU's risk function contributes over and above a concave utility function.

After presenting the scenario: show the EU and REU computations and the verdict each theory delivers; identify the precise structural feature of REU — the behavior of the risk function on intermediate probabilities, not merely risk aversion — that does the work; explain how a defender of EU would attack the case; and say what happens when the risk function is replaced with the identity function.
