I've been reading the recent philosophical literature on moral trade — the idea that when different moral perspectives conflict, agents can sometimes reach mutually beneficial agreements by trading concessions across their respective value systems, rather than fighting to a single winner. The thought is that if you care deeply about animal welfare and I care deeply about global poverty, we might each accept a minor setback to our own preferred cause in exchange for a major gain to the other's, and both walk away with more of what we value. The literature tends to frame this as a problem of rational choice under moral uncertainty: you model your credences over moral theories, assign weights, and bargain.

But I keep thinking about Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." The story describes a utopian city whose prosperity depends on the abject misery of a single child locked in a cellar. Every citizen knows the child is there, and most — after an initial shock — come to accept the arrangement as the price of the general happiness. A few, however, walk away into the darkness and never return. The story does not tell us where they go; it just says they seem to know where they are going.

I want to read Omelas through the lens of moral trade. The citizens of Omelas are, in effect, making a trade: the child's suffering for everyone else's flourishing. And the trade has the structure that moral-trade theorists emphasise — it is between incommensurable considerations (dignity, innocence, suffering on one side; art, love, health, joy on the other), and the exchange rate looks absurdly favourable by the lights of the many. Yet something about the trade feels impermissible in a way that the formal models seem ill-equipped to capture. The walkers-away, in particular, do not argue, do not try to reform the system, and do not seem to be making a calculation at all. They simply refuse.

What does the story reveal about the limits of the moral-trade framework? Does it dramatise a genuine objection — that some values cannot be traded, or that some trades corrupt the trader — or does it merely trade on emotional vividness that a more careful philosophical treatment would expose as a bias? And what should a moral-trade theorist say about the walkers-away: are they irrational, or are they responding to something the model misses? I would like a reading that takes both the philosophy and the story seriously, and that does not reduce either to an illustration of the other.
