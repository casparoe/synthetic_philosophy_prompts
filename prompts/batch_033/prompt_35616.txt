I've been reading about hinge epistemology, and in a seminar discussion someone offered an analogy that has stayed with me. They said that hinge certainties — the kind of bedrock commitments Wittgenstein discusses in On Certainty, like the existence of the external world, the reliability of our senses, or that the earth has existed for more than five minutes — are like the rules of chess. The rules of chess aren't themselves moves in the game; you don't justify them by making moves, and you can't challenge them from within a game without implicitly relying on them. They constitute what it even means to play chess. Similarly, the thought goes, hinge certainties aren't ordinary beliefs justified by evidence; they're the framework that makes evidence-gathering, doubt, and justification possible in the first place. And just as it would be a category mistake to ask "but is it really true that a bishop moves diagonally?" in the way you'd ask "is it really true that this is the best move?", the skeptic who asks "but do you really know you have hands?" is making a category mistake: they're treating a rule of the game as if it were a move within it.

I find the analogy compelling, but I'm uneasy about how far it actually carries. Chess rules are explicit, conventional, and we can step outside them by changing the rules or playing a different game. Our hinge certainties don't seem to be like that: we can't just decide to adopt different ones, and they don't seem to be matters of convention in the way the en passant rule is. The analogy also seems to imply that hinge certainties are entirely beyond rational evaluation, which worries me: if someone's fundamental "hinges" included, say, that their race is inherently superior, we'd want to say that's not just a different game but straightforwardly false — and we'd want to be able to give reasons for saying so. And yet the chess analogy seems to rule that out, or at least to make it very hard to see how such criticism could get a grip.

So I'm trying to work out systematically: what exactly does the chess-rules analogy get right about hinge epistemology, what does it distort, and whether the conclusions people draw from it — about the illegitimacy of skeptical doubt, about the non-propositional or non-epistemic status of hinges — survive once the disanalogies are on the table. If you could give me a mapping of correspondences (what in the target domain plays the role of a rule, a move, a player, stepping outside the game), identify the points where the analogy breaks, and tell me which of those breaks matter for the philosophical work the analogy is being asked to do, I'd be grateful. And if there's a better analogy waiting in the wings — one that captures what the chess analogy gets right while avoiding its distortions — I'd like to hear it.
