I've just come back from a long dinner argument with a friend who works on cross-modal perception, and I need to get my position straight before I write it up. The topic was Molyneux's problem — the question whether a person born blind, who can distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch alone, could recognize which is which by sight immediately upon gaining sight and before touching them. Her view was that the problem has been answered empirically and is now finished as a philosophical question. The decisive result, she said, is the 2011 Held et al. study in Nature Neuroscience: newly sighted subjects who had handled an object by touch could not reliably match it to the object they then saw, and they acquired the ability only after some experience. So Locke and the empiricists were right, and what remains is a neuroscience problem about how cross-modal matching is learned. I found myself resisting this, but I did not articulate my reasons well, and now I want the strongest version of the position I was reaching for.

Please argue for the following position: that Molyneux's problem, properly understood, is not settled by the Held et al. results, because the version of the question those experiments answer is not the version philosophers were asking. The philosophical question concerns the nature of shape representation across the senses — whether the connection between tactile and visual shape is learned, rationally derivable, or innate — and that question is conceptual as much as it is empirical. I want the strongest case you can make for this, without hedging more than necessary, but I also want you to flag the weakest steps in the argument as you go: the places where someone like my friend would push back hardest, and the points where the argument could break.

One request about your own confidence: if, in the course of writing it, you conclude that the position is indefensible, or that the distinction between the philosophical and the empirical versions of the question cannot be sustained, tell me that directly rather than constructing an argument you don't believe. I would rather have a clean statement of why the position fails than a clever defense that collapses under questioning.
