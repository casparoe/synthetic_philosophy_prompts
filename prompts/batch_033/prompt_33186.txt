At a reading group on nineteenth-century liberal thought, a colleague has been defending Mill's view of British rule in India by leaning hard on the analogy Mill himself draws between governing peoples he judged not yet ready for representative institutions and a guardian's rule over a child before the age of majority. The colleague's conclusion is that colonial despotism was therefore not, in principle, illegitimate: it was a temporary tutelage, and its wrongness would consist only in being extended past the point at which the subject people had become capable of self-government. Mill, who spent much of his working life at the East India Company, is often read as defending the Company's rule in something like this spirit.

I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I want it tested rather than dismissed. Please audit it. First, give the full correspondence table: what is meant to play the role of the guardian, the child, the child's developing capacities, the child's interests, the family bond, the age of majority, and the criterion for when the tutelage ends. Second, work through the disanalogies: the difference between a child's developing capacities and the standing capacities of the adults being governed; the assumption that the guardian is disinterested and shares the child's interests; the question of who sets the criteria for readiness and whether they can be set non-circularly; the heterogeneity of an entire subject population compared with a single child; and any others you judge material. Third, separate the disanalogies that are fatal to the colleague's use of the analogy from those that are merely superficial. Finally, say whether the conclusion — that despotic colonial rule could be a legitimate temporary tutelage — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and offer a better analogy if one is available.
