I spent thirty years in geophysics before retiring, and I've been reading philosophy seriously for about two years now — mostly epistemology and philosophy of language. I keep coming back to vagueness because it reminds me of measurement problems: you've got a continuous quantity, you need to draw a line somewhere for practical purposes, and the line always feels arbitrary. But the philosophical question is whether the line really *is* arbitrary or whether there's a fact of the matter we just can't access.

I've found myself pretty convinced by epistemicism — the view, roughly, that vague predicates like "heap" or "tall" have sharp boundaries, we just can't know where they are. The sorites paradox isn't a reason to reject bivalence; it's a reason to think our knowledge is gappy in a particuar way. Williamson's book was what pushed me over, especialy the idea that if you reject epistemicism you end up having to revise classical logic, and that's a bigger bullet to bite than just accepting unknowable sharp boundaries. I know supervaluationism tries to keep classical logic too, but it does it by making truth relative to precisifications in a way that feels like a fudge — you get all the classical theorems but lose the standard semantics that made them make sense in the first place.

But here's my worry: I'm a scientist by training, and I know what it looks like when a theory has been insulated from falsification. So I want to know: what would actually count against epistemicism? Not just the things critics say — I've read those. I want a map of its exposure. What observations, formal results, or conceptual arguments would move a comitted epistemicist? Which of these would be fatal (the view just collapses), which would be seriously awkward but survivable, and which are things epistemicists themselves would concede as genuine costs? I'm especially interested in whether the view has been qualified in ways that make it effectively unfalsifiable — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind, or whether that's just what you'd expect when you're dealing with something as fundamental as the relationship between language and the world.

Give me this as a table. Columns: the defeater or line of objection; what kind of thing it is (empirical finding, formal result, conceptual argument, intuitive cost, etc.); how decisive it would be on a scale from "fatal" to "merely awkward"; whether epistemicists themselves would concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it as question-begging; and a one-sentence summary of the state of play on that objection. Then after the table, give me a paragraph that takes a definite stand: is epistemicism well-insulated, and is that insulation a feature or a bug?
