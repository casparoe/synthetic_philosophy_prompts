I'm a journalist covering the science of decision-making and I'm working on a piece that weaves together the St. Petersburg paradox and Dostoevsky's The Gambler. The angle is: the paradox is the founding scandal of rational choice theory — a coin-flip game where the expected value is infinite but nobody would pay more than a few bucks to play, which launched two centuries of attempts to fix the model (diminishing marginal utility, risk aversion, finite bankrolls, prospect theory, etc.). The novel is about a guy who can't stop gambling even though he knows the odds are against him. They're not the same problem — the roulette in the novel has negative expected value, not infinite — but they're both about the gap between what a mathematical model says a person should do and what actual people do.

I want to read the novel through the paradox. Specifically: the resolutions to the paradox mostly tinker with the utility function — make it concave, truncate the prizes, assume the house has finite resources. But the novel's psychology seems to be about something else entirely: compulsion, the thrill of risk itself, superstition, the way winning and losing reorganize your sense of self. Take the scene where the grandmother shows up at the casino and blows her fortune: she's not confused about the odds, she's possessed by something the utility-function framework doesnt even have a variable for. Does the novel suggest that the whole project of resolving the paradox by adjusting the utility function is missing the point — that the real action isn't in the shape of the curve but in the way risk transforms desire? Or is there a way to read Alexei's swings between wealth and ruin as actually dramatizing diminishing marginal utility, where the money stops meaning purchasing power and starts meaning something else?

I'm also curious whether the novel has anything to say about the "moral expectation" idea that some of the early paradox literature played with — the idea that you shouldn't just maximize expected money but expected something-else. The novel is soaked in moral psychology: Alexei's gambling is tied up with his obsession with Polina, his social humiliation, his sense of being an outsider. Is that just a different problem, or does it point to something the paradox literature has never adequately captured?

What I'm looking for is a reading that takes both the novella and the paradox literature seriously, that says where the novel genuinely complicates the philosophy and where it's just talking about a different thing. And I'd like the response to separate what's actually settled about the paradox (if anything) from what's still contested among decision theorists, and not present a contested resolution as the consensus view.
