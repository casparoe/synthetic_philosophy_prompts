I teach a graduate seminar on the foundations of decision theory, fifteen students, one eighty-minute session. They've already worked through the St. Petersburg paradox and the move to expected utility, so they understand why infinite expected value doesn't straightforwardly dictate infinite willingness to pay. Next session I want to introduce the Pasadena game and the deeper problem it raises: that a gamble can fail to have *any* well-defined expected value, even though the natural expression for its expectation looks like a perfectly respectable convergent series. I want them to feel this, not just hear it from me.

Design an in-class activity that forces groups to price the same lottery in different listing orders and discover that they get different—and irreconcilable—fair prices. The game should be: the Pasadena game in its original form (a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if heads first occurs on toss n, the payoff is $2^n/n if n is odd, and −$2^n/n if n is even). Each group receives a handout that enumerates the outcomes of this lottery in a particular order, along with the probability of each outcome. They are told only that they are looking at the full list of possible outcomes and their probabilities, and they are asked to compute the fair price of the lottery by summing expected-value contributions in the order given. The natural-order group sees outcomes n = 1, 2, 3, 4, … and their partial sums drift toward ln 2. A second group sees a rearrangement—for instance, pattern two positives then one negative, which drives the partial sums toward (3/2) ln 2. A third group sees a rearrangement that drives the partial sums toward +∞. They should not be told that the underlying lottery is the same across groups. After they report their prices, reveal that they all priced the same game, and ask them to reconcile the disagreement.

Give me rules, the exact ordering specifications for each group's handout, a timeline, the debrief script that maps what happened to the formal distinction between conditional and absolute convergence and to why expected utility theory requires Lebesgue integrability, an honest statement of what the activity cannot reproduce about the real mathematical situation (finite partial sums versus the full infinite series, and the distinction between the Riemann rearrangement theorem and the fact that expected value is defined as a Lebesgue integral), and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for example, a student in one group noticing that the terms are the same as another group's terms, or a student objecting that the handout is not the same lottery because the pairing of outcomes with probabilities is supposed to be an unordered set.
