I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea is this: in a lot of informal heuristic reasoning—philosophy, science, policy—people line up several considerations that each point the same way and then treat them as if they're independent pieces of evidence, so the combined case looks much stronger than any single consideration alone. Sometimes that's fine, but other times it's clearly double-counting: the considerations all trace back to the same source, or they only look different because they've been carved up differnetly. People sort of wave at this in arguments by saying those aren't independent or that's just the same point twice, but there doesn't seem to be an account of when you're entitled to a default assumption of independence and when you're just laundering one piece of evidence into three.

Turn that into a proposal where the question is precise enough to have a determinate answer—something like: under what conditions is a presumption of probabilistic independence among informal considerations epistemically justified, and what formal properties would a defensible default rule have? Explain why the obvious existing treatments (Bayesian accounts of independent evidence, maximum-entropy methods, robustness reasoning in philosophy of science) don't already settle it. Then lay out what I'd actually do month by month: probably first taxonomize the ways informal arguments allege independence, then build small formal models of the different roles independence plays, then test candidate default rules against clear cases of double-counting. Say what a positive result would look like and be honest about what would make the project fail—for example, if the only defensible rule turned out to be checking the actual dependence structure case by case, which would make the whole presumption pointless. Keep it scoped to one year and don't bother with citations; I'll add those.
