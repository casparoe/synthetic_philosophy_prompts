I sit on the board of trustees of a private university founded in the 1830s. Recent archival research, commissioned by the university itself, has established two facts: a significant portion of the original endowment came from donors whose wealth derived from the transatlantic slave trade, and enslaved laborers were used in the construction of the university's first buildings, which remain in active use. A joint faculty–student committee has recommended that the university establish a reparative justice fund — an annual commitment, drawn from endowment returns, to full-tuition scholarships for descendants of enslaved people in the surrounding county, plus funding for community-directed historical research and memorialization projects. The proposal has divided the board. Some trustees argue that present-day members of the university community bear no personal moral responsibility for actions taken nearly two centuries ago by people now dead, and that scholarship funds should be allocated on the basis of current financial need, not ancestry. Others reply that the university as an institution persists across generations and carries forward both the benefits and the moral debts of its past, and that need-based aid alone cannot address the distinctive character of the historical wrong. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate ahead of our vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the proposal as written, reject it, or adopt a modified version — and should engage the strongest argument on the losing side without caricaturing it.
