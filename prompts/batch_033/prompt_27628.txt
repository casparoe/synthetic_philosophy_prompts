I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether there is any real argument that rational agents must maximize expected utility, or whether expected utility theory is just a modeling convention economists impose on people. By the end, my friend offered what he called the standard coherence argument, which he summarized for me roughly like this:

"There is a real proof here. If your preferences over gambles satisfy a few axioms — completeness, transitivity, and independence — then a theorem shows they can be represented as maximizing the expected value of some utility function. Conversely, if your preferences violate those axioms, you are vulnerable to a money pump: a sequence of trades, each of which you would accept given your preferences, that leaves you worse off no matter what happens. So a person who does not maximize expected utility is provably irrational, in the sense of being self-defeating by their own lights. And the axioms are just minimal consistency requirements; they are purely formal and do not presuppose any substantive view about what is valuable. The only way to reject the conclusion is to deny that choices reveal preferences, which is a far less plausible position. That is why expected utility maximization is the received view of instrumental rationality."
