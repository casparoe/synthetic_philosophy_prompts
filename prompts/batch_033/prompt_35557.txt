At a dinner party last weekend, I found myself seated between two philosophers of mind who spent the better part of the evening arguing about heterophenomenology. I took notes on my phone — I'm a novelist, and I'm researching a character who works in a consciousness lab — but I'm not a philosopher myself, and I couldn't sort out who had the better of the exchange. I'd like someone to adjudicate it for me.

Here is what I remember, reconstructed from my notes:

(1) Miriam said that heterophenomenology is the only method that takes consciousness seriously as a scientific object without falling into naive introspectionism. You start with the data you actually have — what subjects sincerely report about their experience — and then ask what neurocognitive processes could explain those reports. Everything else, she said, is either credulously treating introspection as infallible or eliminating the phenomenon entirely.

(2) David replied that this is precisely the move that dooms the whole enterprise. By treating the reports as the primary data, he said, you've already given up on studying consciousness itself and are studying the narrative people produce about their consciousness. Those aren't the same thing, and the method has no way to tell them apart.

(3) Miriam answered that no science starts with anything other than the phenomena as they appear. Optics began with how things look, then explained why they look that way. The fact that subjects sometimes misdescribe their own experience isn't a problem for the method, she said — it's a finding the method can deliver.

(4) David came back: in optics, you can check the appearances against the physics. You can measure a wavelength. With consciousness, the appearance is the whole game — there's no independent check. So treating the reports as neutral data already smuggles in the assumption that the reports are all there is to study. And if you're prepared to override the reports when the neuroscience says otherwise, he added, then what exactly is the method adding? It seems like neuroscience with extra steps.

(5) Miriam said that's a misunderstanding of the word "neutral." The method isn't neutral about whether the reports are true — it brackets that question. That's the whole point of the "hetero" prefix. The reports are the things to be explained, not the explanation itself. You can discover that a subject was wrong about their own experience, and that's not a failure of the method — it's a substantive result.

(6) David said: but if the method itself doesn't tell you when to trust the reports and when to override them, then the method is just a ceremonial gesture. The real work is being done by whatever independent theory of consciousness you're using to decide which reports to take seriously. Heterophenomenology is a flag you plant on territory you've already conquered by other means.

They went back and forth a while longer, but these were the main moves. I'd be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication: who is right about what in each of these six moves, and what, if anything, are both of them missing?
