For most of my career I worked as an experimental physicist, and I have only lately begun to read philosophy of science in a systematic way. Lakatos's account of research programmes has been on my desk, and it has made me want to get clear about something I repeatedly observed in the working life of my field but never articulated.

The practice I have in mind, described as neutrally as I can, is this. When a mature experimental field obtains a result that conflicts with a well-established central theory, the community's first response is almost never to treat the central theory as falsified. The standard sequence is to question the experiment, then the analysis, then the auxiliary assumptions: calibration, background conditions, detector models, assumptions about the sample. Only after many independent failures does the central theory itself become a live target. So far this looks like Lakatos's protective belt. But the practice has two further features. First, the field does not allow just any auxiliary adjustment: an adjustment is taken seriously only if it is itself testable and eventually leads to new successful predictions, and ad hoc patches that merely neutralize the anomaly are treated as a mark of decline. Second, in my experience, funding panels and hiring committees often reward proposals that challenge the central theory once a programme has stagnated, even when no single anomaly has been decisive.

What theory of theory appraisal does this ensemble of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and say which parts of it are strictly required by the practice and which parts are merely compatible with it. In particular, does the practice really presuppose something like Lakatos's distinction between a hard core and a protective belt, together with his criterion of progressive versus degenerating problemshifts? Or is the same behaviour equally licensed by a Bayesian view in which the core simply carries a very high prior, or by a Popperian view in which the core is a conjecture tested only at the level of the whole programme? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would produce the same behaviour? And is the ensemble internally consistent? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the day-to-day norms of a field — in peer review, in the treatment of anomalies, in funding decisions — if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforward falsificationism on which every failed prediction counts directly against the core.
