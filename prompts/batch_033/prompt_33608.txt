I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, mostly) and I've gotten into philosophy in the last few years, including some of the AI alignment literature. I now sit on a small safety-review board for a startup — nobody there has philosophy training — and every few weeks an engineering team brings us a proposed requirement saying a system should "avoid side effects" or "minimize impact" or "be low-impact," usually with a metric attached, like keeping some set of state variables close to what they would have been if the agent had never acted. I have about an afternoon before each design review, and the engineering lead has to sign off on whether the metric goes into the spec. My worry, which I keep going back and forth on, is that you cant define a side effect without first deciding what the normal, desired course of events is — and that decision already encodes a bunch of values.

You are an expert on AI alignment and the philosophy of value. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming the distinctions that matter before you commit to anything. Then give me a reusable checklist or decision procedure I can run on any proposed impact measure to decide whether "avoid side effects" has actually been defined well enough to be a requirement, or whether it secretly presupposes a value specification the team hasn't made explicit. For each step say: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what a "no" tells me (ill-defined, unmeasurable, or value-laden). Be explicit about the points where the procedure hands the decision to a human because its a judgment call, not something the checklist can settle. Then run the procedure on three concrete cases: (1) a coding agent that edits a repository and is told to leave the repo "as it would have been" except for the requested change; (2) a home robot told to clean one room while minimizing changes to the rest of the house; and (3) a metric that measures impact by how much the agent's actions change the expected values of a fixed list of sensor readings over the next hour. Make sure at least one of the worked cases is one where your procedure rejects a metric the team would really like to use, and explain what the procedure can and cannot do about the underlying value question.
