My epistemology professor has given these verdicts across the semester:

- Standard Gettier case (Smith, Jones, ten coins): no knowledge.
- Fake barn county (Henry and the one real barn among façades): no knowledge.
- Lottery ticket: a single ticket in a million-ticket fair lottery; the subject believes his ticket will lose based solely on the odds. No knowledge.
- Stopped clock: the subject glances at a clock showing 3:00 and believes it is 3:00; the clock stopped exactly 24 hours ago and shows the right time by sheer accident. No knowledge.
- Ordinary perception: seeing a coffee cup on the desk in good light, no reason for doubt. Knowledge.
- Reliable testimony: a passerby who knows the city gives accurate directions; the subject believes her. Knowledge.
- A true belief formed by consulting a weather app that is normally reliable but malfunctioned when queried, displaying yesterday's forecast — which, by coincidence, matches today's weather exactly. No knowledge.

Next week she will rule on this new case: a subject believes she is not a handless brain in a vat being fed illusory experiences. She bases this belief on ordinary sensory evidence — she seems to see her hands, manipulate objects, and has no special reason to suspect anything is amiss. Does she know she is not a handless brain in a vat?

Identify the candidate theories of knowledge consistent with the professor's track record. For each candidate, predict her verdict on the new case and explain the reasoning. Which candidate gives the most satisfying overall account of her pattern of verdicts, and what verdict on the brain-in-a-vat case does it favor? Finally, propose one further test case — not itself a skeptical hypothesis — whose verdict would most decisively rule between the surviving theories, and say what each theory would predict for it.
