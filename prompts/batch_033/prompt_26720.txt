I'm teaching a course on science and religion, and next week we're doing Brecht's *Life of Galileo* alongside Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina. I want to use the play to get at something specific: Galileo's actual hermeneutic position — that scripture speaks in the language of common people and accommodates itself to their understanding, so it shouldn't be read literally on matters of nature, and that the Book of Nature and the Book of Scripture can't ultimately conflict because they have the same author. Brecht's Galileo is a bit of a sensualist and a materialist (that's Brecht's marxism showing), and the Church in the play comes off as a monolithic obstruction. What I'm trying to figure out: does the play actually dramatize anything like Galileo's real scriptural hermeneutics, or does it sidestep the theological argument entirely and make it about institutional power? Are there scenes where you can see the two-books idea working, or scenes where Brecht's Galileo says something about scripture that the real Galileo wouldn't have? And is the play's treatment of the Church's position a fair representation of the theological case Galileo was actually up against, or is it a strawman? I want to be fair to both the play and the history, and I don't want my students walking away thinking the whole thing was just science vs. anti-science.
