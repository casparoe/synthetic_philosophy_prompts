I have been thinking about the Ship of Theseus puzzle for several years now, largely through discussions in an online forum where a group of us — none of us are professional philosophers — have been trying to make headway. The puzzle, as you will know, is this: if one replaces every plank of a ship one by one over time, is the vessel at the end the same ship as the original? And if one then takes all the original planks, which have been carefully stored, and reassembles them into a ship, which of the two — the continuously maintained ship or the reassembled one — is the Ship of Theseus?

Our discussions have settled into something of a stalemate. Roughly three positions have emerged, and nobody has moved between them in years. The first group holds that the continuously maintained ship is the real one, because identity follows spatiotemporal continuity and functional integrity; a ship is not merely a collection of planks but an ongoing entity that persists through gradual change, much as a person persists through the replacement of their cells. The second group holds that the reassembled ship is the real one, because the original planks are what the Ship of Theseus was made of, and if one puts them back together in the same arrangement, one has restored the very thing itself; the continuously maintained ship, on this view, is merely a copy that happened to be built in the original's place. The third group holds that the question has no determinate answer — that we are simply arguing about which of two equally valid conventions for using the word "same" we ought to prefer, and that there is no fact of the matter beyond our linguistic choices.

What troubles me is not that we disagree, but that the disagreement seems immune to argument. Every thought experiment meant to favour one side is met by the other side with a counter-thought-experiment that pulls intuition the opposite way. Every appeal to real-world cases — organisms, artefacts, institutions — cuts both ways. We have all the same information, we understand one another's arguments perfectly well, and yet nobody budges.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about the world at all, but a clash of bedrock intuitions about what identity consists in — intuitions that are not themselves responsive to further argument because there is nothing more basic to appeal to. But I am not confident in this diagnosis, and I would like your view.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about the meaning of "same," a substantive metaphysical disagreement about the nature of identity, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a new way of framing the question, or perhaps an acknowledgement that the question as posed is ill-formed and that we should be asking something else instead? Please write your answer in British English.
