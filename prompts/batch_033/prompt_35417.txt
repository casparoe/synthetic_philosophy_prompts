The debate over how to recover the Born rule in the many-worlds interpretation has been running for more than half a century without visible convergence. The formalism is not in dispute: everyone works with the same Schrödinger dynamics, the same decoherence story, and the same picture of an emergent branching structure. And yet the central question — why should a rational observer in a branching universe expect to find themselves on a branch whose relative frequency matches the squared amplitude — remains unresolved, with each major approach accused by its critics of circularity.

Here are the rough camps as I understand them. The decision-theoretic program argues that a rational agent who cares about the welfare of their successors across branches, and who faces quantum games with payoffs weighted by branch amplitude, is compelled by standard decision-theoretic principles to act as if the Born rule holds — so probability enters not as a fundamental postulate but as a constraint on rational action. Critics reply that the decision-theoretic premises already smuggle in the Born rule, for instance by assuming that a rational agent should care about branches in proportion to their amplitude rather than, say, their number. The envariance approach tries to derive Born-rule probabilities from the symmetry properties of entangled states, arguing that an agent who knows they are part of an entangled system must assign equal probabilities to Schmidt-basis outcomes that can be swapped by a unitary transformation on the environment. Critics reply that the move from symmetry to probability requires an additional principle — something like indifference — that is no less in need of justification than the Born rule itself. A third camp holds that the Born rule simply has to be postulated as a primitive bridge principle connecting the quantum state to experience, and that attempts to derive it are misguided because probability is not the kind of thing that can be deduced from a deterministic dynamics. A fourth camp argues that the whole notion of probability is incoherent in a theory where every outcome occurs, and that the Born rule problem exposes a genuine crisis for the interpretation rather than a puzzle to be solved within it.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a survey or an adjudication. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is ultimately methodological: the camps disagree about what would count as an acceptable explanation. For the derivers, postulating the Born rule is a failure of the reductionist ambition that made many-worlds attractive in the first place — the whole point was to get a theory with nothing but the wavefunction and the Schrödinger equation. For the postulators, demanding a derivation is asking for a free lunch — probability is an additional ingredient that any interpretation needs, and many-worlds at least restricts it to a single rule rather than a collapse postulate plus a rule. If I am right, the debate is not empirical or formal but a clash of explanatory norms. But I am not confident. Maybe there is a hidden formal question — whether the decision-theoretic derivations are or are not circular in a way that can be made mathematically precise — that would settle the matter if answered. Or maybe the disagreement is verbal, turning on different uses of "probability" (objective chance versus subjective credence versus long-run relative frequency) that the branching structure renders ambiguous.
