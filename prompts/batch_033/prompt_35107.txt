I chair the Governance Procedures Review Committee for my university's faculty senate. We are charged with recommending a voting method for the senate's upcoming election of its president and for standing committee chairs — elections that routinely involve three or more candidates. The senate currently uses plurality voting, but a well-organized reform faction proposes switching to ranked-choice voting (instant runoff). Their case rests on the familiar arguments: RCV ensures majority support, eliminates the spoiler effect, and reduces the pressure on voters to vote strategically rather than sincerely.

Two members of my committee, however, are social choice theorists, and they have raised the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem as an objection. Their argument, as I understand it, runs as follows: the theorem proves that any non-dictatorial voting rule accommodating at least three candidates is vulnerable to strategic voting; therefore RCV is no improvement in principle over plurality voting, because it merely replaces one form of manipulability with another. Some on the committee find this argument decisive; others find it opaque and suspect it proves too much — if the theorem rules out every possible voting method, then it cannot guide us in choosing among them, and we should fall back on the practical considerations the reformers emphasize.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. It should recommend a specific voting method for the senate to adopt, with a rationale that engages the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem seriously. In particular, the memo should clarify: what the theorem actually establishes and what it does not establish; whether the theorem's result applies with equal force to all voting methods that fall under it, or whether some methods handle strategic voting more robustly than others in practice; and, most importantly, what practical weight an impossibility theorem should carry when a committee must choose a real voting procedure rather than a theoretically ideal one. The strongest consideration on the losing side of the recommendation should be stated fairly and addressed, not dismissed.

The audience is the full faculty senate — academics across disciplines, most with no training in social choice theory. The memo must be self-contained enough that a historian or a biochemist can follow the reasoning and see why the recommendation follows from it. It should conclude with an actionable resolution the senate can vote on.
