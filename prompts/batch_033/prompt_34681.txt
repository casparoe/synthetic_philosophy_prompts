Two colleagues of mine in a policy-research unit got into a long argument about a proposal to run part of a city's budgeting through prediction markets, and neither would budge. I was the only other person in the room, and I could not referee it. I would like you to act as an expert in institutional design and the economics of prediction markets, and to adjudicate the exchange move by move, thinking carefully rather than answering from a memorized summary.

The proposal on the whiteboard was this. The city council would first vote on a small set of measurable welfare targets — say, median household income, self-reported life satisfaction, and preventable mortality — combined into a single index with council-chosen weights. For any spending proposal, standing markets would then estimate the expected value of the index at some horizon, conditional on the proposal's being adopted and conditional on the status quo's being kept. The policy whose market implied the higher expected index would automatically become policy. Each market would settle against the realized value of the index only if its policy were adopted.

The argument went roughly as follows.

Eli opened by defending the proposal. Most political arguments, he said, are really two arguments tangled together: what we want, and what will get us there. Voters and their representatives have a claim on the first question but no systematic incentive to be right about the second. Prediction markets do have that incentive, because traders put money behind their beliefs and get paid only if they are right. So the proposal splits the two questions cleanly: the council votes on the ends, and the market bets on the means.

Dana said the split is not clean at all. Choosing the index is itself loaded with causal beliefs. Whether you weight income over life satisfaction over preventable mortality presupposes a view about what makes lives go well and about which measures track it. The council would be doing exactly what Eli says voters are bad at — forecasting which policy will improve welfare — except now disguised as a choice of metric. The proposal has not separated values from beliefs; it has moved the beliefs into a place where they no longer face a market test.

Eli replied that even if the metric is not perfectly neutral, it is far easier to get a council to agree on a small set of metrics than on every spending proposal. Disagreement about the metric can be handled by revising it over time, by putting rival weights to a vote, and by publishing the index's track record. He added that Dana's objection proves too much: every governance procedure has to start from some objective, and the market at least makes the means–ends reasoning public and testable.

Dana came back with what she called the deeper problem. The markets can be settled only for the policy actually adopted. If the market says a new transit plan would push the index down and the status quo therefore wins, the market's claim about the transit plan is never observed. The losing policy's market never pays out against reality, so traders can say whatever they like about roads not taken. The mechanism is supposed to be disciplined by settlement, but the discipline applies only to the winner. Over time the city would be choosing among alternatives whose predicted scores are, for the most part, undisciplined speculation.

Eli said that was too pessimistic. The chosen policy's market is settled and gives real information. The unchosen markets are still disciplined by the possibility of future settlement — policies get reconsidered, and other cities try similar things — and by competition among informed traders, who can profit if a price is wrong and the policy is eventually tried. Even one-sided settlement, he said, is often enough to rank options.

Dana then raised the manipulation point. In an ordinary prediction market, a manipulator who pushes the price away from the truth loses money to arbitrageurs, because the price eventually settles at the truth. But here the payoff that matters is not the settlement value; it is which policy the market selects. A contractor who wants the transit plan built has a direct stake in the market's ranking, not just in its price. If he can buy the ranking he wants, his trading losses are simply the price of the contract. The usual arbitrage defense assumes traders care about being right; in this market, they may care about being decisive. Deep pockets can buy policy.

Eli answered that the manipulator's purchases are public, and the resulting price distortion is a profit opportunity for informed traders; the more the manipulator distorts, the more capital he must put at risk against traders who know the price is wrong. Dana said that reply smuggles in the very thing in question: a pool of informed traders willing to bet real money against a price that will settle only if the policy is adopted. If the policy is never adopted, the correcting trade never pays out. The manipulator and the would-be corrector are not on symmetric footing.

That was where they left it.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the main moves — Eli's opening, Dana's metric objection, Eli's reply, Dana's settlement objection, Eli's reply, Dana's manipulation objection, Eli's reply, and Dana's rejoinder — state whether the move is clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and briefly justify each rating. Before judging, reconstruct each speaker's strongest version of their point. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the most important consideration that both of them missed or underweighted.
