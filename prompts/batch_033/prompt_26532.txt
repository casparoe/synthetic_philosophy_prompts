In most jurisdictions, a competent adult can execute an advance directive — a legal document specifying what medical treatments she wants or refuses if she later loses decision-making capacity. If she later becomes incapacitated and her physicians determine she would have wanted treatment withheld, they are legally bound to follow the directive, even if the incapacitated patient now visibly struggles, cries out, or otherwise seems to express a contrary preference. The earlier self's wishes override any apparent wishes of the later self.

At the same time, the law is generally reluctant to let a person bind her future self in other domains. You cannot sell yourself into slavery, no matter how sincerely you consent. You cannot sign an irrevocable employment contract lasting decades. Marriage is dissoluble. Even ordinary contracts are subject to doctrines of unconscionability and changed circumstances. The legal system treats the future self as a distinct bearer of rights whom the present self cannot fully alienate.

What theory of personal identity and autonomy does the practice of advance directives presuppose? Does it commit the law to the view that the earlier and later selves are, in some deep sense, the same person — and that the later self's apparent preferences are not truly hers because they arise from a compromised state? Or does it presuppose something closer to a narrative or whole-life view of autonomy, where a life goes well when it unfolds according to a plan endorsed from a position of full competence? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, address the apparent inconsistency: can the law's treatment of advance directives be reconciled with its reluctance to enforce other forms of self-binding, or does the practice rest on premises that, if taken seriously, would require revising those other doctrines?
