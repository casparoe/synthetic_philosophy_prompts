I was arguing with my roommate about whether our university should abolish its ancient language requirement (you have to take two semesters of Latin or Greek, which nobody uses, and it eats up electives). He pulled out this thing he called Chesterton's fence and Burkean conservatism, which he summarized like this:

"The idea is that you should never tear down a social institution unless you can prove it's actively harmful. Chesterton's point with the fence is that if you come across a fence in a field and can't see what it's for, you shouldn't take it down — because the fact that you personally don't see its purpose doesn't mean it doesn't have one. Burke made the same point about society: traditions and institutions encode the accumulated wisdom of generations, and since no single generation is smart enough to redesign society from scratch, we should preserve existing institutions unless there's overwhelming evidence they're causing harm. The burden of proof is always on the reformer, never on the tradition. This is basically a conservative argument that all change is presumption and you should default to keeping things as they are."

I pushed back and said that sounds like it would have ruled out abolishing slavery, women's suffrage, basically every moral advance. He said those were the rare cases where the harm was obvious enough to meet the burden of proof. But something about his framing seems off to me, like it's turned a principle about epistemic humility into a blanket ban on change. I don't know the original texts well enough to say where the distortion is. Can you go through his summary and tell me what Chesterton and Burke actually said, what parts of his version are fair, what's subtly wrong, and whether his conclusion that "the burden of proof is always on the reformer" actually follows from their views?
