I am writing a paper for a high school course that touches on decision theory, and I have been reading the back-and-forth over Herbert Simon's notion of bounded rationality and his claim that real agents "satisfice" — set an aspiration level and accept the first option that meets it — rather than maximize expected utility. What puzzles me is that the two sides of this debate do not seem to be converging. Defenders of expected utility maximization argue that satisficing is not a rival standard of rationality at all: once the costs of searching for and processing further information are built into the decision problem, choosing the first adequate option can itself be the utility-maximizing policy, so satisficing is simply maximizing under a more complete description. Defenders of satisficing reply that this re-description assumes exactly what a bounded agent lacks, namely the ability to know the value of continued search without already having done it, and that the aspiration level, not the global optimum, should be treated as the fundamental notion.

I am not asking which side is right, and I do not need a restatement of the two positions. I am asking why the debate has the shape it does, because it seems to me that each side can absorb the other's central claim into its own framework, and no amount of new examples will change that. Is this a merely verbal disagreement about the word "rational," with one side meaning an ideal standard and the other an executable procedure for a limited agent? Is there a hidden empirical question underneath it — for example, how well simple aspiration-level stopping rules approximate optimal search in realistic tasks — such that an answer would move one side? Or are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading, one about the norms for an idealized agent and one about what a cognitively limited agent ought actually to do? I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, and then an account of what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a specific empirical finding, a formal result, or an argument that the impasse should be expected and perhaps dissolved rather than resolved.
