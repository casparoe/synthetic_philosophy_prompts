I serve on the Constitutional Review Commission of a parliamentary democracy. We are considering a proposal to entrench a provision requiring that the government's structural budget deficit not exceed 0.5% of GDP in any fiscal year, with the provision amendable only by a two-thirds supermajority of the legislature. The proposal's advocates argue that ordinary political processes systematically favor deficit spending — future generations bear the costs while current voters reap the benefits — and that only a constitutional precommitment can correct this intergenerational externality. Opponents argue that the proposal amounts to an illegitimate dead hand: it would allow today's majority to bind future democratic majorities on a matter of ordinary fiscal policy, stripping them of the capacity to respond to economic circumstances we cannot foresee.

The Commission is evenly divided. I have been asked to draft the majority opinion recommending either adoption or rejection of the entrenchment proposal. The opinion should state a clear recommendation, engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side, and address the broader question of what principled criterion distinguishes legitimate constitutional precommitments from illegitimate ones — since whatever we say here will shape how the Commission handles future entrenchment proposals.
