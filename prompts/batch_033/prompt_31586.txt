I've come to accept the Frankfurt verdict: moral responsibility doesn't require the ability to do otherwise, and Frankfurt-style cases are what convinced me. Partly it's because I served on a jury earlier this year, and the question of whether the defendant could have done otherwise kept coming up in a way that felt much more concrete than it ever does in seminars. Before I write this up for a reading group, I want to know what would actually count against it. Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: the flicker-of-freedom reply, the dilemma defence (the prior sign is either causally sufficient — in which case the case presupposes determinism and begs the question — or it isn't, in which case an alternative possibility is left open), the charge that the cases just build in compatibilist intuitions so an incompatibilist can withhold the judgement of responsibility without contradiction, and the source-incompatibilist move that even if PAP is false, determinism still rules out responsibility because it rules out being the ultimate source of your action. Also tell me how much the robustness debate matters: whether the leftover alternatives have to be robust, and who gets to set the standard for robust enough. For each item, say whether a defender of the Frankfurt verdict would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as missing the point, and separate the things that would refute the verdict from the things that merely limit how much it shows. Then tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified enough that it no longer risks anything — and wether you think that's a problem for a view of this kind. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write the answer in American English.
