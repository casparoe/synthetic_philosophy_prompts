I am a novelist, and I am asking you to write a short story for me. I want literary fiction — or speculative fiction in a literary register — that works first as story and only second as thought.

The philosophical ground the story should work is this: sentience and moral status are not binary. Beings and systems differ along several independent dimensions at once: whether anything is felt from the inside (valenced experience, pleasure and pain); whether there is a unified subjective point of view; whether the being persists as a self over time; whether it has preferences or interests that can be satisfied or frustrated. A human being, a dog, an octopus, a honeybee, a sea anemone, a thermostat, a trained reinforcement-learning agent, a brain organoid — these do not line up on a single scale, and none of the usual thresholds is obviously principled. A creature can have nociception without suffering; a system can pursue goals without felt preference; an information-processor can respond to the world without there being anything it is like to be it. Yet practical life forces thresholds. Some things may be eaten, used, switched off, or destroyed, and some may not, and the line has to be drawn somewhere — often with the feeling that no defensible place exists.

Write a story whose central dramatic pressure is a graded-status decision of this kind. The protagonist must decide — or refuse to decide, or evade deciding — how much moral weight to give to an entity or system that sits in the disputed middle of the spectrum, somewhere between an insect and a thermostat as it were. The entity should be genuinely intermediate rather than an obvious person or an obvious thing: an insect, a swarm, a model organism, an organoid, a homeostatic machine, a simplified reconstruction of a nervous system, or something similar. Do not resolve the story by revealing that the entity was secretly fully conscious all along, and do not resolve it by revealing that it was secretly empty. The story should dramatize the continuum itself: the arbitrariness of any line, the cost of refusing to draw one, and the gap between what a character can know and what a character must do.

Sections:
1. Story (about 1,200 to 1,800 words). Give the story a title. Use a close third-person or first-person point of view, and keep it in the register of a literary short story; it should stand as fiction, not as a disguised essay.
2. Commentary on the choices made (about 300 to 450 words). In the voice of a writer explaining a draft to an editor, say (a) which dimension or dimensions of the graded-status problem the story dramatizes, (b) where the story places its threshold pressure and whether the ending resolves it, and (c) what the story does, as fiction, that a philosophical argument about graded moral status cannot.
