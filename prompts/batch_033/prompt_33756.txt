I teach a graduate seminar on American pragmatism, and every year we spend three weeks on the Peirce–James dispute about truth, and every year the same thing happens: the students can articulate both positions clearly, they can identify the passages where James seems to caricature Peirce and the ones where Peirce's followers accuse James of subjectivism, and then the discussion stalls. Nobody changes their mind about which pragmatist has the better account, and nobody can quite say what would settle the matter.

I have come to suspect that the impasse is not just a classroom phenomenon but reflects something deeper about the structure of the debate itself. Peirce defines truth in terms of the ideal limit of inquiry — the opinion destined to be agreed upon by all who investigate — and his account is built around the convergence of a community of inquirers using scientific method over the long run. James, by contrast, makes truth depend on what it is expedient for a particular believer to believe, on the practical difference a belief makes in that believer's life, and he is willing to call beliefs true when they help us cope with experience even if they outrun what has been rigorously established. The two accounts seem to pull in opposite directions: one ties truth to public, third-person, indefinitely extended inquiry; the other ties it to private, first-person, present satisfaction.

Both sides have standard replies. Jamesians say Peirce's account is vacuous unless you can specify what the end of inquiry looks like, and that Peirce smuggles in a correspondence intuition he claims to have abandoned. Peirceans say James conflates the criterion of truth with its nature, and that his view licenses wishful thinking if it happens to work for the believer. These replies have been on the table for over a century, and neither camp has conceded.

What I want to know — and what I want to ask my students — is what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it a substantive metaphysical disagreement about the nature of truth, a verbal dispute about the word 'true' that could be resolved by distinguishing senses, a clash of philosophical temperaments that rational argument cannot adjudicate, or two different projects — one descriptive (what does 'true' mean in our best scientific practice?) and one normative (when are we entitled to believe?) — that have been misleadingly placed under a single heading? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding about inquiry, or simply the admission that the two sides are answering different questions?
