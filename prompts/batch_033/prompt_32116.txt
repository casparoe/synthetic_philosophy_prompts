I teach a seminar on philosophical method, and this term we have been working through Wittgenstein's later philosophy — the *Philosophical Investigations*, the Blue and Brown Books, and a fair bit of secondary literature. One thing that has genuinely puzzled my students, and me, is why the discussion of family resemblance has not moved much since the 1960s. The basic move is familiar: Wittgenstein points out that many concepts — 'game', 'language', 'number' — resist classical definition in terms of necessary and sufficient conditions, and he proposes that what holds the instances together is a network of overlapping similarities, like the resemblances across a family. The anti-Wittgensteinian response, pressed early on by authors like Bambrough and Khatchadourian and then refined over the decades, runs roughly as follows: either family resemblance is just a metaphor and tells us nothing precise about how concepts work, or it is a covert theory of concepts — in which case it faces all the difficulties it was meant to avoid, such as having to specify which similarities count as relevant and why. The Wittgensteinian replies, in turn, have tended to insist that family resemblance is precisely not a theory but a reminder, a therapeutic device for loosening the grip of a certain picture of language, and that asking for criteria of relevance is to miss the point. And around it goes.

I have now read enough of this literature to suspect that the impasse is not going to be broken by another round of the same arguments. What I cannot tell is what kind of impasse it is. Is this a substantive disagreement about how concepts actually work, where one side is right and the other wrong, but the evidence that would decide it is not yet in view? Or is it a disagreement about what we are doing when we do philosophy — about whether the task is to construct theories of meaning or to dissolve the felt need for them — in which case the two sides are not really answering the same question? Or is it something more mundane, like a verbal dispute about the word 'theory' that has been mistaken for a deep philosophical divide? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, grounded in what each side actually says, not a survey of the positions. And I want an honest answer: given your diagnosis, is there anything — a distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, a change of question — that could actually move things forward, or is the sensible thing to stop expecting progress and treat the two sides as engaged in different projects? Please write the diagnosis in British English, and be as specific as you can about what the sides are committed to rather than trading in generalities about 'therapeutic' versus 'theoretical' philosophy.
