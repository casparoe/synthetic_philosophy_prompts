I'm an undergraduate taking a course in political philosophy this semester, and I've found myself in a situation that maps almost too neatly onto the material we've been covering. I sit on my university's student-faculty speaker committee, which reviews invitations for controversial external speakers. We're currently considering a request from a campus group to host a political commentator who has argued, in published essays and public talks, that members of certain religious minorities should be barred from holding public office and that their places of worship ought to be subject to heightened state surveillance. The group inviting him says he's a provocative voice worth hearing and that shutting him down would be an act of illiberal censorship. The students opposing the invitation argue that his views are not merely offensive but actively hostile to the equal standing of fellow students — that giving him a platform legitimizes the idea that some members of our community are presumptive threats. The faculty on the committee are split, and in our last meeting one of them said, almost in passing, "This is the paradox of tolerance, isn't it?" I nodded along, but honestly, I'm not sure what to do with that observation. We didn't discuss it further, and now I'm supposed to vote in two weeks.

I understand the basic idea as we covered it in class — that a tolerant society might need to be intolerant of intolerance in order to preserve tolerance itself. But I'm not a society; I'm a committee member at a university. Does the paradox actually give me guidance here, or is it just a way of naming the tension without resolving it? And if it does give guidance, what does it recommend in this particular case? I want to approach this vote with some philosophical clarity, not just a gut feeling, and I'm especially worried that whichever way I vote I'll be rationalizing a prior instinct rather than reasoning from principle.
