I'm a journalist working on a piece about rational choice theory, and I've gotten tangled up in an argument I reconstructed from the decision-theory literature. It seems to prove that the standard Allais preferences are irrational. Each step looks airtight to me, but the conclusion feels like it proves too much — it would condemn preferences that many thoughtful people, including economists who know the argument well, continue to find reasonable after reflection. I need to know exactly where this goes wrong, or whether I have to accept the conclusion.

Please approach this as a professional decision theorist. Work through the argument step by step, identify the weakest link, and explain what rejecting it costs — including which major positions in the literature map onto each possible move.

The Allais choices:

- **Choice 1:** A = $1M for certain. B = 10% chance of $5M, 89% chance of $1M, 1% chance of $0.
- **Choice 2:** C = 11% chance of $1M, 89% chance of $0. D = 10% chance of $5M, 90% chance of $0.

The standard finding is that most people prefer A over B and D over C. Now the argument that this pair of preferences is irrational:

(1) The sure-thing principle: if two gambles agree on what happens in some subset of possible states, your preference between them should depend only on the states where they differ.

(2) In Choice 1, A and B agree on tickets 1–89: both yield $1M. So by (1), your preference should depend only on tickets 90–100. On those tickets, A yields $1M for sure; B yields a 10/11 chance of $5M and a 1/11 chance of $0.

(3) In Choice 2, C and D agree on tickets 1–89: both yield $0. So by (1), your preference should depend only on tickets 90–100. On those tickets, C yields $1M for sure; D yields a 10/11 chance of $5M and a 1/11 chance of $0.

(4) The sub-lotteries described in (2) and (3) are identical. So by the sure-thing principle, you must prefer the sure $1M in both choices or the gamble in both choices. Preferring A over B while also preferring D over C is inconsistent.

(5) Inconsistent preferences are irrational.

(6) Therefore, the standard Allais preferences are irrational.

This conclusion seems too strong. The Allais preferences are not a casual mistake — they persist among sophisticated subjects, survive reflection, and have been defended by serious theorists. If the argument is sound, it wipes out a large class of intuitively attractive risk attitudes. Where does it go wrong? Is the sure-thing principle less innocent than it looks? Does the argument equivocate somewhere? Or is the right move to bite the bullet and accept that the Allais preferences really are irrational — and if so, what does that cost?
