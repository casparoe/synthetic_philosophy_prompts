I cover science and ideas for a living, so I end up in a lot of arguments about who owns what. I've been trying to figure out whether my gut calls on owning ideas actually hang together, and I'm not sure they do. Here are my verdicts.

- A food blogger reproduces a chef's exact recipe — ingredients, proportions, steps — without asking. Fine. Its a list of instructions, not a thing you can own.
- A producer lifts a distinctive three-second horn riff from an old soul record for a hit single. Not fine. That's someone's creative fingerprint, however small.
- Someone writes a full-length fanfic set in a living author's world, using her characters, and posts it free online. Fine. It isn't competing with the original; it's a conversation with it.
- An academic gives a keynote and presents another scholar's original argument as their own, with no attribution. Not fine, even though the argument itself isn't 'owned.' Credit is different from property.
- A generics company copies a drug molecule after the original patent lapses. Fine. That's the whole deal: a temporary monopoly, then the idea becomes public.
- A biotech firm patents a gene sequence it isolated from a patient's tumor. Not fine. Isolating something nature made isn't inventing it.
- A magazine runs a photographer's image without paying or asking. Not fine. That's there work, end of story.
- An engineer reverse-engineers a rival's software to build an interoperable clone, without copying the code itself. Fine. Interoperability matters more than the owner's control.

And here's the one I actually need help with: training a large language model on millions of copyrighted books without permission. One part of me says it's like the recipe — you read the books, learn the patterns, and the model doesn't store the originals. Another part says it's like the horn riff: it's ingesting an entire culture's creative output at scale to build a product that competes with the people who made it.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all of them? I'd rather you commit to the best principle you can defend, state it precisely, and tell me straight which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and what it says about the model-training case. Don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick.
