I run a small press with six other novelists — we've been doing this for about three years, putting out weird literary fiction that wouldn't get picked up anywhere else. When we set it up, we agreed that any one of us could greenlight a manuscript without a vote. It seemed like a good idea at the time; we all trusted each other's taste and didn't want to get bogged down in committee meetings.

Now there's a problem. One of our members brought in a manuscript that's basically a roman à clef about a very famous, very litigious author in our circle. It's not flattering. If we publish it, one of two things happens: either it's a succès de scandale and puts our press on the map, or we get sued into oblivion and the press folds. The manuscript is good — genuinely good — but the legal risk is real. We've all read it and we've all seen the same lawyer's memo.

Three of us think publishing it would be catastrophic. Two think it's the best thing we've ever been handed and we'd be cowards to pass. Two of us (I'm one of them) are genuinely torn. We've been arguing about it for weeks.

And here's what's keeping me up at night: our founding rule says any of us can publish anything. So the actual decision procedure here isn't "we weigh the evidence together and reach a consensus." It's "we publish if anyone thinks it's a good idea." That feels like a terrible way to make a decision this consequential, but I can't put my finger on exactly why it's structurally broken, as opposed to just being a bad idea in this particular case.

Is there a name for whats wrong with this kind of decision rule? And what do we do — change the rule retroactively for this one manuscript? That feels like a seperate problem.
