Hey, so I'm kicking around an essay idea and I'd really appreciate some critical comments before I sink a bunch of time into it. It's about a tension I think I see in Dennett's work between his older stuff on consciousness and his recent writing about AI and counterfeit people.

So here's the basic argument:

Dennett's whole thing for decades has been that there's no real self hiding inside us, no Cartesian theater. The self is a narrative fiction, a center of narrative gravity. And consciousness is a kind of user illusion generated by parallel competitive processes in the brain. On this view, if something walks and talks like a conscious person, taking the intentional stance toward it isn't just a useful heuristic — it's basically constitutive. There's no further fact of the matter about whether it's really conscious or really a person. The intentional stance is all you get.

But then in his 2023 Atlantic piece The Problem With Counterfeit People, he takes a very different tone. He calls AI-generated fake people the most dangerous artifacts in human history and says they threaten civilization itself. He explicitly points out that our habit of adopting the intentional stance makes us sitting ducks for these systems. But here's where I get stuck: if the intentional stance is all there is to personhood, what exactly are we being fooled about? What makes a counterfeit person counterfeit rather than just another instance of the same kind of thing the rest of us are? He seems to be leaning on a real-vs-fake distinction that his earlier framework largely dissolves.

I don't think this is a cheap gotcha. Dennett's worry in the Atlantic piece is mostly about manipulation, fraud, and the erosion of institutional trust — not about the metaphysics of personhood. He's making a practical and political argument, and those concerns don't go away just because you're a Dennett-style eliminativist about the self. But I do think there's a tension worth pulling at. The whole force of calling them counterfeit people rather than, say, malicious agents or fraudbots seems to depend on there being a meaningful difference between a real person and an imitation of one. If the self is a narrative fiction to begin with, what is the counterfeit a counterfeit of?

The essay would explore whether he can have it both ways, and if so, what the reconciliation looks like. One possible line: maybe the intentional stance is the only game in town for attributing personhood, but it comes with normative commitments (you owe a person certain things, and they owe you certain things) and the problem with counterfeit people is that they trigger those commitments parasitically, without being the kind of thing that can actually discharge them. But I'm not sure that holds up either.

I'd love comments on: (1) Is this a genuine tension or am I just mixing up different things Dennett is doing in different registers? (2) Are there passages where he actually addresses this directly — maybe in From Bacteria to Bach and Back or somewhere else I've missed? (3) Is the essay interesting enough to pursue, or is it one of those tensions that disolves as soon as you think about it for five minutes? (4) If you think the reconciliation I sketched doesn't work, is there a better one?
