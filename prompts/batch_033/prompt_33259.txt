Our reading group has spent the past several months stuck on a question that sounds merely taxonomic but keeps shaping what each of us thinks should be funded, built, or attempted first: is "the alignment problem" one problem or several? One camp — call them the unifiers — holds that there is a single underlying difficulty, something like getting advanced systems to robustly pursue what we actually intend, and that the familiar subproblems (specifying the goal, avoiding reward hacking, generalizing beyond the training distribution, keeping humans able to notice and correct failures) are best understood as facets or symptoms of that one difficulty, likely to be solved together or not at all. The other camp — call them the pluralists — holds that "alignment" is an umbrella term covering a loose cluster of problems with different failure modes, different success criteria, and plausibly different solutions, such that progress on one can leave the others fully intact, and treating them as one problem actively misleads us about where the risk is.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, about what any adequate account of alignment must cover and about what the observed disagreements in the field do and do not establish; (2) the precise formulations of their residual disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as a fair statement of its view; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect the cruxes will include something like whether success on held-out behavior in the training distribution is evidence of alignment in the relevant sense, and whether specifying what we want is separable in practice from getting a system to pursue it — but correct or refine these if the collaboration's logic demands it. If you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why, and put the genuinely technical or empirical part in the sharpest form you can. Format the result as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
