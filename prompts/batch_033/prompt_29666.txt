You serve on the board of a philanthropic foundation whose charter instructs you to maximize the good achieved per dollar spent. At your next meeting, you must vote between two grant proposals. The first funds a well-established malaria prevention program expected to save roughly 1,000 lives over the next five years, with a high degree of confidence in that estimate. The second funds a small research team working on the alignment of future advanced AI systems; their best estimate is that their work has something like a 0.01% chance of preventing an existential catastrophe that would foreclose the lives of trillions of future people. A straightforward expected-value calculation says the AI grant dominates by orders of magnitude. And yet you find yourself deeply reluctant to vote for it over the malaria program. Are you making a mistake, or is there a principled reason to resist the expected-value reasoning in a case like this?
