I was at a reading-group meetup last week where two friends got into an argument that I have been turning over in my head ever since. The case they were disputing was this: a junior researcher discovers that a senior colleague has been systematically leaving graduate students' names off papers to which they made substantial contributions. She reports it through the university's formal misconduct procedure. The report is justified, the behaviour is genuinely wrong, and she knows this. But she also happens to loathe this particular colleague — he once humiliated her at a department seminar — and she has privately admitted that part of her wanted to see him squirm. The question is: does her action have moral worth?

Here is roughly how the argument went.

Adam said no, it does not. The fact that she would have reported him even if the misconduct had been trivial — or even if, counterfactually, it had not been misconduct at all but merely something embarrassing — shows that the moral rightness of the action was not doing the motivational work. If the moral reason is not what makes the difference to whether she acts, then she is not acting *for* the right reason. She is merely doing the right thing, and doing it from spite. The moral features of the situation are epiphenomenal with respect to her decision.

Beth pushed back hard. She said Adam was smuggling in a counterfactual test for moral worth that almost no action could pass. Whenever someone acts from multiple motives, you can always ask 'would they have done it anyway, even without the moral reason?' and if the answer is yes, Adam's criterion strips the action of moral worth. But that is absurd. A parent who rescues their child from a burning building both because it is their child and because it is the right thing to do does not lose moral worth just because they would have done it for either reason alone. Overdetermination by a good motive and a bad motive does not cancel the good motive.

Adam replied that the parent case is different because the personal motive — love for one's child — is itself morally admirable, whereas spite is not. Beth said this just relocates the problem: Adam now needs a theory of which non-moral motives are admissible companions to moral motives, and why. She said the deeper issue is that Adam was treating moral worth as though it requires the moral reason to be a but-for cause of the action, when in fact moral worth only requires that the agent be *responsive* to the moral reasons — that the moral features of the situation play a genuine role in her practical reasoning, even if other factors also play a role.

Adam came back with a modification. He said it is not about but-for causation simpliciter, but about whether the moral reason is *sufficient* in the circumstances. If the moral reason alone would have been enough to move her, then the action has moral worth even if other motives were also present. But if the moral reason alone would not have been enough — if she needed the extra push of spite to get herself to act — then the action lacks moral worth. In the researcher's case, he argued, the fact that she sat on the information for weeks until the colleague slighted her again suggests the moral reason was not sufficient.

Beth said this was an empirical claim about the case that neither of them could settle, but more importantly, the sufficiency criterion has its own problems. Imagine someone who is so demotivated by depression that no single reason is ever sufficient for them to act — they always need multiple reasons to converge before they can get out of bed and do anything. If such a person does the right thing for the right reason plus some trivial additional reason, does their action really lack moral worth? That seems to confuse facts about someone's motivational architecture with the moral assessment of their action.

At this point they were going in circles, and I had to leave, but I cannot stop thinking about who was on stronger ground. Could you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move? I am especially interested in whether the counterfactual test, the sufficiency test, or Beth's responsiveness alternative is the right way to think about moral worth in cases of mixed motives — and whether Adam's retreat from the but-for test to the sufficiency test actually helped his case or merely shifted the bump in the carpet.
