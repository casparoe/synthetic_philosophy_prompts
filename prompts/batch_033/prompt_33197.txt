I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I've been working on the conceptual structure of the AI alignment problem. In a recent seminar, a visiting speaker presented alignment as a single, unified challenge — roughly, the problem of ensuring that advanced AI systems reliably pursue the goals we intend for them. I've come to think this framing is not just unhelpful but actively misleading.

I'd like you to make an argument for the following position: that what we call "the alignment problem" is best understood as a collection of several distinct problems — including at least outer alignment (specifying the right objective), inner alignment (ensuring the system's optimization target matches the specification), and corrigibility (ensuring the system remains open to correction) — and that these problems differ not just in degree but in kind, such that approaches that work for one may be irrelevant or even counterproductive for another. Please engage seriously with the obvious objection: that these sub-problems are all, at bottom, variants of the same fundamental challenge of value specification under uncertainty, and that fragmenting the problem risks obscuring deep structural connections. The argument should be rigorous enough to convince someone working in AI safety, but it should not rely on citation of recent papers or technical results that someone without web access would need to look up.
