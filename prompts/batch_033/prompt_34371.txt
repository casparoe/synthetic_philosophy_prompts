I'm a high school student writing my college application essays, and I keep coming back to Bernard Williams's "one thought too many" objection—the idea that if someone saves his drowning wife only after checking that a moral principle permits it, he's already had one thought too many, because the thought should simply be "it's my wife." This seems to challenge the whole way we're taught to think about ethical decision-making: step back, apply a rule, justify your action impartially. What I can't figure out is this: suppose Williams is right that impartial justification can alienate us from our deepest commitments. Which open question about the scope and limits of that objection is most worth settling first, if what you care about is actually living well rather than just doing academic philosophy? For instance, should we first get clear on whether the objection applies only to close personal relationships, or also to political and professional roles? Should we first work out whether it rules out ever using impartial reasoning, or only at the moment of action? Or is the real priority something else entirely—like figuring out whether the objection is really about the content of the thought or about the timing of it? I'm less interested in a survey of the literature than in a defended ranking: what's the question that, once answered, would most change how a thoughtful person actually navigates their life, and why does it deserve to go first?
