You are a professional philosopher specialising in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, with particular expertise on the extended mind thesis and the surrounding debates. Please think carefully and work through the logical space of the positions before writing.

I run a philosophy of mind reading group for advanced undergraduates and early-career graduate students. At our next meeting we are discussing Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis, and I want to begin with a diagnostic quiz that sorts participants according to what they actually believe about the boundaries of cognition—rather than by which label they find appealing.

Please write a 'Which position on the extended mind do you actually hold?' quiz with the following specifications.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete scenario. For each scenario, the respondent is asked whether a particular external resource is best described as (a) part of the person's cognitive process, (b) a mere tool that feeds into a wholly internal cognitive process, or (c) something in between that cannot be cleanly classified either way. No question should simply ask 'do you think the mind extends beyond the brain?'; the diagnostic work must be in the scenarios.

The positions the quiz should sort respondents into are, at minimum:

- **Intracranialism**: all cognitive processes are realised inside the skull; external resources are merely inputs to or outputs from internal processes.
- **Embodied cognition**: cognitive processes can involve the extra-cranial body (e.g., motor routines, sensorimotor loops), but they do not extend into artefacts or other people.
- **Extended cognition, parity-based**: an external resource counts as part of a cognitive process when it plays a functional role that, were it performed internally, we would unhesitatingly call cognitive—roughly, Clark and Chalmers's parity principle.
- **Extended mind, integration-based**: external resources can be part of the mind itself, not merely part of cognition, when they meet conditions of reliable availability, automatic endorsement, and easy accessibility—the so-called 'trust and glue' criteria.
- **Socially extended cognition**: other people can form part of an individual's cognitive system, not merely by providing inputs but by acting as components of ongoing cognitive processes.
- **Distributed cognition**: the individual is the wrong unit of analysis entirely; cognitive processes are inherently distributed across agents and artefacts, and should be studied at the level of the system rather than the person.

Choose cases that genuinely separate these positions. Include at least the following scenarios:

- The classic Otto-and-his-notebook case (a memory-impaired person who relies on a notebook as their external memory store).
- A case involving a smartphone used for navigation, scheduling, and fact retrieval.
- A case involving skilled use of a bodily tool (e.g., a blind person's cane during navigation).
- A case involving a couple or small team who routinely think together—finishing each other's sentences, jointly planning, or dividing cognitive labour in a way that makes the pair the natural unit of problem-solving.
- A case involving a brain-computer interface or neural implant that directly mediates between neural activity and an external computational device.
- A case involving a person working through a difficult problem on a whiteboard or with pen and paper, where the external marks are essential to the reasoning.
- At least one case involving a novice using a tool they have not yet integrated fluently (to test sensitivity to the trust-and-glue conditions).

Provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns to positions. The key should be transparent: explain which answers are diagnostic for which position and why, and flag any pairs of positions that the quiz cannot reliably separate. If some combination of answers corresponds to no defended position in the literature, note that as well—is the position unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored? If a respondent's pattern falls between positions, the scoring key should say so rather than forcing them into the nearest box.

The scenarios should be phrased so that someone can answer them without knowing the literature; the quiz should sort people by their pre-theoretical judgements about cases, not by whether they recognise the technical terms. Each scenario should be vivid enough that a respondent can imagine themselves in it and form a judgement without needing to reconstruct the philosophical dialectic.

Write the quiz and the scoring key in full.
