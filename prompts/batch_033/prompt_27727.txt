I spent most of my working life as a research scientist, and since retiring I have been reading contemporary epistemology with the same habit I brought to the laboratory: when I hold a view, I want to know in advance what would count against it. The view I currently hold is the knowledge account of assertion: the claim that assertion is governed by a rule to the effect that one may assert a proposition only if one knows it, and that this rule is not merely sound epistemic advice but part of what constitutes the act as an assertion in the first place.

I am not asking for an introduction to the view or a survey of the literature. Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works on speech acts and social epistemology, and think through the question carefully; I would suggest you begin by brainstorming candidate defeaters and then sorting them, before presenting the final map.

What I want is an honest assessment of the view's exposure. If I am committed to the knowledge account, which phenomena, arguments, intuitions, or experimental findings should move me? I already know about several of the standard pressure points, and I would like you to pressure-test them rather than merely list them: Moore-paradoxical conjunctions; lottery propositions and other cases where one has only statistical grounds; selfless assertion, in the style of a creationist teacher who faithfully presents evolution without believing it; cases in which a person learned something from a reliable source long ago but can no longer say how; and the familiar complaint that the knowledge rule is too strong because there are many things one knows but should not say. For each, tell me whether it cuts against the view, supports it, or can be absorbed as a violation of some other norm such as prudence, relevance, or politeness.

In particular, I want the candidate defeaters sorted according to whether they threaten the necessity of knowledge for proper assertion, the sufficiency of knowledge for proper assertion, or the stronger constitutive claim that the knowledge rule makes an utterance an assertion in the first place. Please rank them from those a committed defender would have to concede are serious to those that can reasonably be dismissed or handled with auxiliary hypotheses, and say where the view genuinely sticks its neck out by predicting something its rivals do not. For each serious defeater, indicate which rival account it would, if successful, leave in the strongest position. Finally, I want a candid answer to a methodological worry: once the view is formulated as a constitutive rule that coexists with all sorts of non-epistemic norms, has it been insulated to the point where no observation about how people actually speak could count against it? If so, is that a defect in this particular account, or simply the price of theorizing about norms rather than behavior?
