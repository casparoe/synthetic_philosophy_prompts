I've been going down a Moore's paradox rabbit hole and I keep hitting the same wall: the two main camps have had their arguments on the table for decades and nobody's budged. Take the standard sentence — It's raining, but I don't believe it. It can be true: rain and my not believing in it are perfectly compatible facts. But it can't be felicitously asserted, and nobody disputes that. One camp says the absurdity is a contradiction in disguise: asserting the first conjunct expresses, or commits you to, the belief that it's raining, so the whole performance amounts to something like representing yourself as believing it's raining while saying you don't believe it's raining. The other camp says no — the sentence is a perfectly consistent conjunction, and the defect is in the pragmatics of the act: it undercuts its own point, or violates the norm governing assertion, or can't be a coherent move in the assertion game. (The omissive version, It's raining but I believe it isn't, is usually treated as the easy case, since there you at least get contradictory beliefs; the commissive version is the one that splits people.)

My working theory is that this is, at bottom, a verbal dispute about the word 'contradiction' and about what assertion does: one side is answering a question about the contents and commitments involved in asserting, the other about the conditions for a coherent speech act, and they've been talking past each other under one heading. But I'm not confident — maybe there's a substantive disagreement about the norm of assertion hiding in there, or about how much the semantics has to explain. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, or is the question ill-posed? And what would count as progress: a new kind of case, a distinction, a formal result — or should I just stop expecting this literature to converge?
