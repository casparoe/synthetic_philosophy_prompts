Before I engage any further with the situationist critique of virtue ethics, I want to step back and ask what a theory of character traits actually needs to deliver in order to count as adequate. The debate between virtue ethicists and situationists often seems to turn on different implicit standards for what a character trait is in the first place, and I suspect some of the disagreement is about the desiderata themselves rather than about whether any extant theory meets them.

Please lay out the full list of requirements that a theory of character traits must satisfy to survive the situationist challenge — the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the empirical results it must be consistent with. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates treating it as a requirement. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable (any theory of character, virtue-ethical or otherwise, must meet them) and which are parochial commitments of one side of the debate that the other side would reject — and explain why. If any pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, or if meeting all the non-negotiable ones forces a theory into a position that already concedes the situationist's central claim, say so; that is the part I most want to see. If you think one particular requirement does the most work to exclude candidates, identify it and explain why.

Optionally, score the main positions in the debate — global trait realism, local trait or "fragment" views, and full eliminativism about character — against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
