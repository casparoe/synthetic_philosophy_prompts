I am a novelist, and while working on a narrative problem — how a reader decides what to credit in a first-person account, and whether the reader's default posture toward a narrator should be trust or suspicion — I found myself constructing the following argument. I now suspect it proves too much, but I cannot locate the misstep with any confidence.

(1) A hearer is justified in believing a speaker's testimony that p only if the hearer possesses independent reasons — reasons that do not themselves depend on accepting that very testimony — to think the speaker is both sincere and competent with respect to p on this occasion.

(2) These independent reasons must ultimately derive from perception, memory, or inference from what perception and memory supply, since any other source would either collapse into testimony itself (circular) or introduce a further mystery.

(3) In the vast majority of ordinary testimonial exchanges — asking a stranger for directions, reading a news report, consulting a reference work, hearing a colleague's account of a meeting one missed — the hearer lacks perceptual access to the facts reported and also lacks perceptual access to the speaker's sincerity and competence on this specific occasion. At most the hearer has statistical background beliefs about how often people in general, or people of a certain type, prove reliable — but these background beliefs are themselves largely acquired through testimony.

(4) Therefore, in the vast majority of ordinary testimonial exchanges, the hearer is not justified in believing what the speaker says.

(5) But this conclusion is absurd. We are plainly justified in believing a great deal of what we are told, and a philosophical account that denies this has parted company with the phenomenon it was meant to explain.

(6) So premise (1) — the reductionist requirement — must be false. But if we reject (1) and allow that testimony can justify belief on its own, without the hearer needing independent positive reasons, we seem to license gullibility: the hearer who trusts a stranger's word without checking has done nothing epistemically wrong, and by extension the reader who simply believes whatever a narrator says cannot be faulted. Yet we know that trust can be misplaced and that narrators can be unreliable; we sometimes discover we were too credulous, and it seems perverse to say that discovery never impugns our earlier epistemic conduct.

I find myself unable to accept either the reductionist horn — which makes our ordinary reliance on testimony irrational — or the gullibility horn — which makes our ordinary vigilance irrational. Which premise should I give up, and at what cost? I would particularly value an answer that does justice to the fact that we do sometimes discover we were too trusting without thereby being forced to conclude that we were irrational to have trusted in the first place.
