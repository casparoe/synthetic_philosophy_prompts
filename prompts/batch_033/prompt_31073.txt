I'm trying to get my views on individual responsibility for collective harms into a defensible shape, and I'd like an audit of whether they hang together. Here's where I currently stand:

(1) Individuals in affluent countries have a moral obligation to take significant steps to reduce their personal carbon footprint — flying less, eating less meat, and so on.

(2) The inefficacy objection is sound when applied to voting in large elections: the probability of any single vote being decisive is so small that there is no duty to vote grounded in expected causal impact.

(3) Nevertheless, there is a moral obligation to vote, grounded in considerations of fairness and civic expression rather than causal efficacy.

(4) The inefficacy objection fails for individual meat consumption because expected-impact calculations that model supply-chain threshold effects show a non-negligible expected harm per person who goes vegetarian.

(5) The following argument is basically correct: if you accept the inefficacy objection for individual climate actions like reducing your emissions, you would have to accept it for individual participation in a firing squad or a lynch mob — the marginal causal contribution of one person is similarly negligible. Since we rightly reject the objection in those cases, we should reject it for climate actions too.

(6) There is a morally relevant difference between harms that arise through market mechanisms (like the meat industry) and harms that arise through aggregative political procedures (like elections), such that the inefficacy objection can succeed in one domain and fail in the other.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in tension and how badly. For each tension, say what would have to give to resolve it, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to clean up the overall view. I am especially worried that (2), (4), and (6) might not all be jointly tenable, but I cannot tell whether the problem is real or whether I am simply failing to see how they fit together.
