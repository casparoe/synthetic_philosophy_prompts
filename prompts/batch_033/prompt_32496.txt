I'm a novelist working on a book about a mathematician who becomes convinced that her own consciousness is, in some deep sense, a formal system — and that understanding this is the key to understanding why she is falling apart. GEB has been my conceptual compass, but the more I sit with the book's central analogy, the more I suspect it either collapses into triviality or proves far too much. I've built an argument that presses this dilemma, and I need someone to tell me where the reasoning goes wrong — because I'm sure it does, but I can't find the exit.

Here is the argument:

(1) Hofstadter's project in GEB is to use Gödel's incompleteness theorems as a model for understanding how minds emerge from physical systems. The core idea is that consciousness arises from strange loops — self-referential structures in which a system represents and reasons about itself, much as a formal system equipped with Gödel numbering can construct sentences that refer to their own provability.

(2) If the analogy is to do genuine explanatory work, it must be more than a loose metaphor or a vivid illustration. It must identify some specific structural feature of Gödelian self-reference that is also present in conscious minds, and that feature must be non-trivial — it must not be a feature shared by every system that merely happens to refer to itself in some way.

(3) The specific feature Hofstadter emphasizes is the tangled hierarchy: the way that, in Gödel's proof, statements about the system are mapped onto statements within the system, so that the distinction between levels — object language and metalanguage — breaks down. The system gets caught up in itself. This tangled hierarchy, Hofstadter argues, is the formal analogue of the mind's ability to reflect on itself, to have beliefs about its own beliefs, to be an object to itself.

(4) But now the problem. If tangled hierarchies of this kind are the mark of consciousness, then any system that exhibits a tangled hierarchy should be conscious, at least to some degree. Yet tangled hierarchies are remarkably easy to produce. A computer program that can modify its own source code at runtime exhibits a tangled hierarchy: the program is both the object being modified and the agent doing the modifying. A legal system that contains procedures for amending its own constitution exhibits a tangled hierarchy. An organization whose leadership is elected by its own members exhibits a tangled hierarchy. The sentence "This sentence is false" exhibits a tangled hierarchy between language and metalanguage. Even a thermostat with a feedback loop that adjusts its behavior based on its own output exhibits a rudimentary tangled hierarchy.

(5) None of these are conscious. A self-modifying program is not aware of itself; a legal system does not have experiences; an organization does not have a unified point of view; a paradoxical sentence does not feel anything; and a thermostat is not a subject.

(6) The defender of GEB might reply that these are not the right kind of tangled hierarchy — that consciousness requires a tangled hierarchy of a specific sort, involving rich representations of the self as a self, embedded in a system that also interacts with a world, builds models of that world, and integrates those models over time. But this reply comes at a cost. Once we begin specifying the extra conditions, the Gödelian analogy recedes from view. The analogy was supposed to illuminate what kind of structure consciousness is; instead, we find ourselves appealing to a long list of further conditions — representational richness, world-modeling, temporal integration — that the analogy itself does not capture. The Gödelian strange loop becomes at most one ingredient among many, and perhaps not even a necessary one. The analogy is no longer doing the heavy lifting; it has become a suggestive picture rather than a model.

(7) So we face a dilemma. Either the Gödelian analogy does real explanatory work, in which case it overgenerates — attributing the seeds of consciousness to self-modifying programs, legal systems, and paradoxical sentences — or it does not do real explanatory work, in which case GEB's central analogy is a beautiful illustration rather than a substantive claim about the nature of mind. Neither horn seems faithful to the book Hofstadter actually wrote.

Where does the argument go wrong? I'm drawn to the idea that the flaw is somewhere around (2) — that the demand for a specific structural feature that is both necessary and sufficient misunderstands the kind of intellectual move Hofstadter is making, which might be neither metaphor nor model but something in between. But if I give up (2), I'm left without a clear sense of what the analogy is supposed to teach me, as a novelist, about how a mind might arise from the right kind of self-referential structure. What is the middle ground between a vivid picture and a reductive explanation, and does GEB actually occupy it?
