Consider the absent-minded driver problem (Piccione & Rubinstein 1997). A driver is on a road with two identical-looking intersections. He wants to exit at the second intersection. Payoffs: exit at first = 0, exit at second = 4, continue past both = 1. The driver cannot distinguish the intersections and has no memory of past choices. Let p be the probability he continues at any given intersection. (a) Compute the ex ante optimal p and the associated expected utility. (b) Now consider the driver's reasoning upon reaching an intersection. Using his posterior beliefs about which intersection he's at, determine whether the ex ante optimal strategy is stable under re-optimization at the action stage. If you find a discrepancy, explain what gives rise to it and how it might be resolved.

---

And here's the student's answer:

(a) From the planning stage, the expected utility as a function of p is: EU(p) = (probability exit at first)*0 + (probability continue then exit at second)*4 + (probability continue both)*1. The probability of exiting at the first is (1-p). The probability of continuing at the first and exiting at the second is p(1-p). The probability of continuing both is p^2. So EU(p) = 0*(1-p) + 4p(1-p) + 1*p^2 = 4p - 4p^2 + p^2 = 4p - 3p^2. Taking the derivative: dEU/dp = 4 - 6p = 0 → p = 2/3. So the ex ante optimal strategy is to continue with probability 2/3 at each intersection. Plugging back: EU = 4(2/3) - 3(4/9) = 8/3 - 4/3 = 4/3.

(b) Now suppose the driver actually arrives at an intersection and re-evaluates. He knows he is at some intersection but not which one. What are his posterior beliefs? The probability he is at the first intersection is the probability he did not exit at the first intersection earlier—but since he hasn't made any choice yet (he's deciding now), we use his planned probability of continuing, p. The probability he reached this intersection via continuing at the first is p, so P(second) = p and P(first) = 1 - p.

Now compute the expected utility of each action at the intersection. If he exits: he gets 0 if at the first, 4 if at the second. So EU(exit) = (1-p)*0 + p*4 = 4p. If he continues: if he's at the first, he proceeds to the second intersection where he'll follow the same strategy p, yielding expected payoff (1-p)*4 + p*1 = 4 - 3p. If he's at the second and continues, he gets 1. So EU(continue) = (1-p)(4-3p) + p*1 = (1-p)(4-3p) + p.

Now evaluate at the ex ante optimum p = 2/3:
EU(exit) = 4*(2/3) = 8/3 ≈ 2.67.
EU(continue) = (1/3)*(4 - 2) + 2/3 = (1/3)*2 + 2/3 = 2/3 + 2/3 = 4/3 ≈ 1.33.

So at the action stage, exiting strictly dominates continuing! The driver would want to deviate by exiting more often. But if he raises p (the probability of continuing), the ex ante EU drops—there's a conflict between what looks best at the planning stage and what looks best at the action stage. This is the paradox: the driver cannot implement his own ex ante optimal plan because at the moment of choice he'll want to defect from it.

The standard resolution is that the driver must precommit to the mixed strategy before setting out, perhaps by using a randomization device whose output he cannot overrule later. Another way to put it is that causal decision theory gives the wrong answer here and that evidential or timeless decision theory correctly recommends sticking with p = 2/3 even at the action stage, because the two intersection-choices are logically correlated via the driver's own decision algorithm.
