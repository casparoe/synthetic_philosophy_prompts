I keep coming back to the verisimilitude problem and realising I don't have a clean statement of what exactly goes wrong, so I'd like an explanation aimed at someone who knows the basics of Popper but not the technical litterature. Popper wanted a notion of being closer to the truth that lets one false theory be more truthlike than another: roughly, a theory should count as closer to the truth if it has more true consequences and fewer false consequences than its rival. The standard objection is that on Popper's actual definitions no false theory can ever be closer to the truth than another false theory, which makes the whole notion useless for its intended job. Could you walk me through why that happens: what feature of Popper's definitions produces the collapse, and what it shows about trying to compare false theories by their consequences? Then sketch what a post-Popper account has to do differently to avoid the problem, not a survey of accounts but just the conceptual fix. I'm especially interested in whether the real lesson is that truthlikeness can't be a function of consequence-classes alone, or whether the problem goes deeper than that.
