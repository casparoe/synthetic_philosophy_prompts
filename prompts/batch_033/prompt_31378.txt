I am a novelist, and I am drafting a novel whose central character is a magistrate in a small, long-settled city-state who rules on petitions to alter or abolish inherited institutions. I want his decisions to read as the work of one coherent mind rather than as plot convenience, so I am trying to recover the principle he has been implicitly applying.

His prior rulings are these. He refused to let a landowner demolish a derelict boundary wall whose original purpose no one could state, although its public upkeep was trivial. He granted a petition to repeal a docking ordinance after the city antiquary showed it had been enacted to quarantine plague ships and that inspection had since moved to the harbor mouth, while the old rule had become a costly obstruction to trade. He refused to abolish a twice-yearly bell-ringing of unknown origin, because it had become the occasion for markets and marriage contracts. And he allowed the gradual, compensated replacement of an inheritance custom after the archivist established that it had been created to keep estates undivided for militia service, a purpose the city's professional army had long since made obsolete, and that the custom was now generating costly litigation.

A petitioner now asks him to abolish a rule requiring every household to keep a lamp burning in its front window through the night. The city's historians have searched and cannot recover the rule's original rationale. The rule causes frequent fires and burdens poor households, and the city already maintains a night watch. If the magistrate is to be consistent with his earlier rulings, should he grant the petition or refuse it? Please identify the rule that best explains his prior verdicts, the other rule or rules that fit them just as well, what each candidate rule predicts about the lamp rule, your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, which of his earlier rulings is the closest analog, and the single further case that would most cleanly distinguish the candidate rules.
