Here is an argument that seems to prove too much, and I cannot find the step that fails.

(1) A rational agent chooses among the acts available to her so as to maximize expected utility relative to her beliefs.

(2) Suppose she is offered costless information before she chooses: a signal she may observe for free, whose observation affects her only by changing what she believes, and which she is free to ignore. Whatever act she would have chosen without the information remains available after she sees it, and if the information changes her choice, she will, by (1), choose the act that maximizes expected utility relative to her new beliefs. So, assessed before she sees the signal, her expected utility cannot be lower if she observes it: at worst she ignores it and does what she would have done anyway. This is Good's theorem, in effect — the expected value of costless information is never negative.

(3) Therefore, a rational agent never strictly prefers to remain ignorant of costless information and would never pay to avoid it; at most she can be indifferent.

(4) Yet a free, painless, confidential genetic test for a fatal and untreatable disease seems to be costless information, and many people decline such a test, saying they would refuse it even if paid. They do not expect the result to change any treatment decision; they simply prefer not to live with the knowledge. This preference seems rational, or at least not irrational.

(5) So (3) is false. Since (3) follows from (1) and (2), one of the premises, or the inference, must go. I would most like to keep (1) and the verdict in (4), but I worry those are exactly the two claims the argument will not let me keep together.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what, if anything, survives of the theorem. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
