I'm a junior scholar presenting my paper "The Bearing of the Diary: How Frege's Late Political Thought Reopens Questions About Sense, Objectivity, and the Third Realm" at a specialist workshop on the history of analytic philosophy next month. My core thesis is this: the political views expressed in Frege's 1924 diary—in particular, his arguments that Jews should be deprived of civil rights and expelled from Germany, and his proposed institutional mechanisms for doing so—are not merely an embarrassing biographical footnote, nor are they the unfortunate but philosophically irrelevant ravings of a declining mind. Instead, I argue that they bear on his philosophy in a specific and philosophically instructive way: the diary reveals Frege deploying a style of argument—about what a people is, about the conditions under which a legal order can claim authority, and about the nature of objective judgment—that structurally parallels key moves in his mature philosophical writings, and that attending to this parallelism exposes tensions in his account of sense and objectivity that are harder to see if one treats his political and philosophical writings as hermetically sealed from one another. I am not claiming that the logicist project is secretly a crypto-fascist enterprise, or that Frege's anti-psychologism in logic straightforwardly entails his political commitments. My claim is narrower: the diary's arguments draw on intellectual habits and presuppositions that also operate, less visibly, in Frege's philosophical work, and surfacing those connections enriches our critical understanding of both.

The audience will consist of roughly thirty people: about half are specialists in Frege's technical philosophy (logicians, philosophers of language, and philosophers of mathematics who work on sense and reference, the context principle, and the foundations of arithmetic), about a third are historians of early analytic philosophy whose primary interest is in situating Frege's work in its intellectual and cultural context, and the remainder are philosophers working on the ethics of the canon and the question of how—or whether—an author's politics should affect our engagement with their work. Michael Dummett's view that the diary reveals a shocking but philosophically irrelevant personal defect still commands considerable sympathy among the Frege specialists, though fewer of them defend it openly than did so twenty years ago. The historians tend to resist insulating the philosophy from the politics on methodological grounds, but they are also wary of reductive readings that treat the philosophy as "merely" an expression of political ideology. The ethics-of-the-canon people will be sympathetic to my project in principle but will press hard on the specifics: they want to know exactly how the diary bears on the philosophy, not just that it does, and they are alert to the danger of overclaiming.
