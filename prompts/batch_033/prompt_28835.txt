You are a professor of philosophy who also serves on your university's Committee on Academic Standing. You have been asked to draft the decisive memo for an upcoming vote. Please think carefully before writing — begin by working through the philosophical structure of the problem in your own mind, and only then produce the memo itself.

---

Our committee is revising the policy that defines "satisfactory academic progress" — the standard a student must meet to avoid academic probation or dismissal. The current policy requires that a student make "adequate progress toward the degree," a phrase we interpret case by case through committee deliberation. A faction on the committee wants to replace this with a bright-line rule: any student whose cumulative GPA falls below 2.0 for two consecutive semesters is automatically dismissed, with no committee discretion. Another faction argues that any cutoff is arbitrary — why 2.0 rather than 1.98? — and that case-by-case judgment, however messy, is more defensible than pretending a sharp boundary exists where none does.

The debate has become explicitly philosophical. One member circulated a paper on the sorites paradox and argued that since "adequate progress" is vague, we face the same problem as a heap of sand: no single grain of sand makes the difference between a heap and a non-heap, yet repeated removals eventually destroy the heap. She claims this shows that any bright-line rule is rationally indefensible — it would amount to declaring that the hundred-thousandth grain is the one that makes the heap, when we know no such grain exists. Another member replied that the law draws bright lines all the time (age of majority, blood-alcohol limits) and that procedural fairness requires us to do the same, whatever the metaphysics of vagueness: similarly situated students should not receive different outcomes because different committee members happen to hear their cases.

Draft the short opinion memo you will circulate before next week's vote. It should:

1. Recommend a concrete policy — retain discretion, adopt a bright-line rule, or some third option — with an actionable implementation plan.
2. Engage the sorites-based argument seriously. Do not dismiss it as merely academic, and do not simply assert that bright lines work in the law. Show why the sorites reasoning does or does not defeat the policy you recommend.
3. Address the fairness objection directly. If you recommend discretion, explain how to prevent arbitrary variation across cases. If you recommend a bright line, explain why the arbitrariness of the cutoff is not the same kind of problem as the arbitrariness that discretion is meant to solve.
4. Be no more than 900 words.

Before drafting the memo, think through the following: What exactly does the sorites paradox show about vague predicates, and what are the main philosophical responses to it (e.g., epistemicism, supervaluationism, contextualism, many-valued approaches)? Which response, if any, best illuminates the committee's practical situation? Use this reasoning to ground your recommendation, but keep the philosophical machinery in the background of the memo itself — the memo is a policy document for colleagues, not a philosophy paper.
