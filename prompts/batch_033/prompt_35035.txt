I'm a second-year student sitting on my university's mitigating circumstances commitee this term — the panel that meets weekly to decide whether students get extensions, late-penalty waivers, resits without penalty, and so on — and I'm taking a political philosophy course at the same time. We've just done luck egalitarianism: Dworkin's distinction between option luck and brute luck, the thought that inequalities are fine when they trace to choices and not fine when they're just bad luck, and the Anderson-style objection that applying this strictly would be cruel and involve intrusive judgements about what people deserve. It struck me that the panel is basically doing applied luck egalitarianism every week without realising it. A student who missed a deadline because they were in hospital gets a waiver; a student who missed it because they were at a festival doesn't. But the real cases are much murkier: a mental health flare-up that is partly outside the student's control but partly about whether they sought help early, a family bereavement, a broken laptop with no backup, a chronic condition flaring up after a night out, a student taking on too many paid shifts and falling behind.

What I'd like is a decision procedure the panel could actually use, not a theory essay. Something like: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and which steps are really option-luck/brute-luck judgements dressed up as neutral procedure. It has to be runnable in about fifteen minutes per case by people who aren't philosophers, and it has to produce decisions we can defend to the exam board, with a senior tutor who signs off but mostly defers to our reasoning. Then run it on two or three worked cases, including at least one where the procedure tells us to do something the panel would find harsh or uncomfortable — I want to know where the theory actually bites. Also say explicitly which argument or kind of case would change the procedure's verdict or show that its reasoning is most likely to fail, because I'll have to defend this to the rest of the panel.
