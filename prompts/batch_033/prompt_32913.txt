The debate over the "equality of what?" question — the currency of egalitarian justice — has been running since at least the late 1970s, and it seems to me to have settled into a stable impasse. Welfare egalitarians (or equal-opportunity-for-welfare theorists) hold that what matters is how well people's lives go for them; resource egalitarians following Dworkin hold that people should be held responsible for their tastes and ambitions, so we should equalize resources, not the welfare those resources produce; and capability theorists following Sen and Nussbaum hold that both welfare and resources are the wrong metric because what matters is what people are actually able to do and be. Each side has well-known moves against the others: the welfarist points out that a disabled person may need more resources to reach the same welfare level, the resourcist responds that this is exactly why Dworkin introduces hypothetical insurance markets, the capability theorist objects that both sides are still thinking in terms of means rather than freedoms, the resourcist replies that capabilities are either reducible to resources or collapse into welfare, and so on. Everyone seems to acknowledge the same cases and the same force of the other side's objections, yet nobody changes their position.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. My working hypothesis is that the three camps are answering different questions under one heading: the welfarist is answering "what ultimately makes a life go well?", the resourcist is answering "what is it fair for a political community to distribute?", and the capability theorist is answering "what should we measure to assess a society's justice?" — and that the impasse reflects the fact that these are all legitimate questions that do not need to compete. But I am not sure that is right, and I want an honest assessment that engages with what the actual positions say. If my hypothesis is wrong, what kind of disagreement is it instead — a clash of bedrock intuitions, a disagreement about the normative relevance of responsibility, a hidden empirical question about what can be measured, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a new kind of case, a formal result, or a change of question?
