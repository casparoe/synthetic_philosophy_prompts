The sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to keep investing in something because you've already put resources into it, even when the future costs outweigh the future benefits. Standard examples include finishing a meal you're already full from just because you paid for it, or continuing to fund a failing project because you've already spent so much. The standard view in economics is that this is irrational: rational agents should make decisions based only on marginal costs and marginal benefits going forward. Past expenditures are gone either way, so they shouldn't factor into your decision. This is why it's called a fallacy.

However, I think there are cases where honoring sunk costs can actually be rational.

First, consider self-binding. Thomas Schelling argued that people can use sunk costs strategically to solve self-control problems. If you pay for a year-long gym membership upfront instead of monthly, the fact that you've already spent the money might push you to actually go to the gym. You end up fitter than you would have otherwise. Since the outcome is better, the decision to let the sunk cost influence your behavior seems rational.

Second, reputation. If a company cancels a project they've poured millions into, investors and clients might see them as unreliable. So finishing the project — even at a net loss — can be rational because it protects the company's reputation for future dealings. In this case the sunk cost isn't really sunk, because walking away creates new costs in the form of lost trust.

Third, personal meaning. Some philosophers, like Bernard Williams, have argued that abandoning projects you've invested heavily in can fragment your sense of identity over time. If you constantly walk away from things you've committed to, your past efforts lose their meaning, and that's a genuine harm. So sticking with something because of your past investment in it can be a rational way of maintaining the integrity of your life.

In conclusion, while people definately fall into the sunk cost fallacy in irrational ways all the time, there are at least three kinds of cases — self-binding, reputation, and personal meaning — where honoring sunk costs is fully rational.
