I'm taking an aesthetics course and we've been doing the paradox of fiction, and honestly the standard solutions all feel too neat to me. I started writing down my actual gut reactions to different cases and they're kind of a mess. I'd love for you to act as a professional philosopher who works on this stuff — think through it carefully, maybe start by mapping out what the main theoretical options are, and then see which one best fits my pattern of verdicts.

Here's what I've got:

1. Crying at a sad movie. Fine. Not even slightly puzzling to me.
2. Heart racing, gripping the armrest during a horror film. Fine, that's literally why people watch them.
3. Being genuinely angry at the actor who played the villain — like, you see them in a different movie later and feel hostile toward them. Not fine. That's a category mistake.
4. Feeling guilty for a couple days after doing an "evil" playthrough of an RPG where you betrayed an NPC who had dialogue about trusting you. This one bothers me because I want to say it's irrational — it's pixels and scripted lines — but the guilt also feels very real and I can't just dismiss it. It's different from the movie cases, but I can't articulate how.
5. Having what amounts to a crush on a fictional character (not the actor). I think this is fine, or at least not irrational in the way #3 is. But I struggle to explain the asymmetry — in both cases you're directing an interpersonal attitude at something that doesn't exist, so why is one okay and the other isn't?
6. Feeling a rush of satisfaction when the villain finally gets killed at the end. Fine.
7. Sending hate mail or death threats to the author because a character did something awful. Obviously not fine.
8. Reading a creepy story and then feeling uneasy in your own apartment, getting up to double-check the door is locked, even though you know perfectly well the story was fiction. I do this. I think it's fine, or at least understandable. But how is it different in principle from #3? In both cases an emotion generated by fiction is spilling over onto real-world objects.
9. Genuinely grieving a character's death for weeks — like, it sits with you the way a real bereavement would. This is the one I can't settle. Sometimes I think it's beautiful that fiction can matter that much; other times I think it's a failure to properly track reality.

So my question: is there a single principle that captures all or most of these? I have this vague sense that the key is something about whether the emotion is directed "at the content" versus "at the world," or maybe about whether you're treating the fiction as fiction while you feel it. But I can't make that precise. If no clean principle gets all nine, tell me which verdicts I should reconsider and why. And definitely tell me what your principle says about #9, because I need someone to settle that for me.
