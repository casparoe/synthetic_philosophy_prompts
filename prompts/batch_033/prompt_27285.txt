I spent my career in neurobiology, studying the mechanisms of perception and belief fixation, and only in retirement have I turned seriously to philosophy. I have been working through the literature on self-deception—the paradox that to deceive oneself one must simultaneously know and not know the thing one is hiding—and I find myself returning again and again to a novel I first read years ago: Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*.

The narrator, Stevens, is an English butler who has given his life to serving Lord Darlington, a man who drifted into Nazi sympathy in the 1930s. Stevens has also spent decades suppressing—or something—his feelings for the housekeeper, Miss Kenton. The novel is his first-person account, and it is a monument of evasion: he will not say what he plainly knows, or once knew. The question the philosophical literature forces me to ask is whether Stevens is actually deceiving himself, or whether he is doing something else—wishful thinking, akrasia, plain hypocrisy, or simply lying to the reader. If self-deception is impossible because it requires a single mind to partition into deceiver and deceived, then what is Stevens doing, and why does it feel so much like self-deception?

I would like a reading of the novel through the philosophical problem of self-deception. What does the book show about the phenomenon that the arguments do not? Where does Stevens's case fit among the main philosophical positions—intentionalism, non-intentionalism, partitioning views, deflationary accounts—and where does it resist them? I am particularly interested in whether the novel suggests that self-deception is something one does over time rather than in a single moment, and whether that temporal dimension dissolves or merely relocates the paradox. If the novel has something to teach the philosophers, I want to know what it is; if the philosophers expose something the novel's form obscures, I want to know that too.
