I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy putting together a one-year dissertation fellowship application, and I need a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea concerns moral compromise. Economists have a well-worked-out notion of gains from trade: two agents with different preference rankings can each be made better off by their own lights through voluntary exchange, without either party adopting the other's ranking or appealing to a shared standard of value. I want to know whether that structure carries over to compromise between value systems. In a legislature, a religiously divided school board, or an international negotiation, parties with deeply incompatible commitments sometimes reach agreements that each side regards as a genuine improvement over the status quo; at other times one side experiences the same agreement as a betrayal of its commitments even when it has won concrete concessions. The difference cannot be only the size of the concession, since small concessions are sometimes felt as more corrupting than large ones. I keep oscillating between two framings. On one, a value system can be treated, for purposes of compromise, as a partially ordered set of commitments with some substitutions permitted and others forbidden, and the question is decision-theoretic: when does a compromise Pareto-dominate the status quo from within each system? On the other, the analogy with preferences is fundamentally misleading, because some commitments are constitutive of the person or community that holds them, so that trading them away does not better satisfy one's values but changes which values one holds.
