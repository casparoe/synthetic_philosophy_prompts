I'm a novelist—I've published a couple literary novels, nothing huge—and I'm teaching a weekend workshop for other fiction writers, about twelve people, one three-hour session with a break in the middle. The workshop is called "Writing the Hard Choice," and the whole point is that the most interesting decisions in fiction aren't the ones where a character picks the obviously right thing over the obviously wrong thing. They're the ones where the character is torn between two goods that can't be weighed against each other on any single scale—like, do I stay with my dying parent or take the fellowship abroad? Do I turn in my friend for something they did or stay loyal? Those choices where you can't just do the math.

I've been reading around in some philosophy—Ruth Chang's essay on hard choices, a bit of Isaiah Berlin on the clash of values—and I'm conviced that the best way to get these writers to understand this isn't to lecture at them. I want them to *feel* it. So I want to design an in-class activity, maybe a game or a structured role-play, where they actually face a series of choices that bring them up against incommensurability. The idea is: they run into the problem themselves—they feel the vertigo of having to choose when there's no common measure—and then we debrief and connect it to craft. How do you dramatize that vertigo on the page?

So here's what I need: a runnable activity for 12 people, with clear rules, any materials I'd need to prep (cards, handouts, whatever), and a timeline that fits within about 75 minutes (leaving time for the debrief). The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical idea—that some values are genuinely incommensurable, that this isn't the same as being uncertain about outcomes, and that the experience of *making* the choice anyway (what Chang calls "committing" or "putting your agency behind" one option) is something a character does, not something a calculation does for them. Also, I need you to tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this the first time. Every workshop exercise has a failure mode—people get too meta, or they try to game it, or the stakes feel fake and they disengage. Be honest about what'll break and how I should handle it in the moment.
