I'm putting together a batch of prompts on Bayesian epistemology and I want one on the problem of old evidence, but run in the disconfirming direction. The usual examples are all about a known fact that seems to support a newly proposed theory; I want a case where a known fact should intuitively count against a new theory even though the theory is logically consistent with it.

Construct me a thought experiment to the following specs. Two positions should come apart in it:

- A strict conditionalizer, who treats the old evidence as known with probability 1 and therefore says it's confirmationally neutral: it can't raise or lower the new hypothesis.
- A counterfactual-prior Bayesian, the standard old-evidence fix, who says you temporarily set aside your knowledge of the evidence, assign the prior it would have had before it was observed, update on it, and then fold the rest of your knowledge back in; this position should say the evidence disconfirms the new hypothesis.

Constraints: keep it to an ordinary scientific setting (a lab, a field station, a clinical dataset — nothing science-fictional). The evidence has to have been sitting in the published literature for at least a decade before the hypothesis is proposed. The hypothesis must be logically consistent with the evidence, and its defender should have a coherent story about why the evidence doesn't refute it — so the disagreement is about evidential weight, not about a straightforward failed prediction. And the two sides can't get thier different verdicts just because one of them has a silly or gerrymandered prior; both should look like reasonable scientists.

After you lay out the scenario, walk through the reasoning for each position with enough of the informal likelihoods that I can see why each gets it's verdict. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if I remove that feature, and how the strict conditionalizer would push back on the counterfactual verdict without just denying that the case has intuitive pull.
