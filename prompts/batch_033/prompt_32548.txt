I'm a director of engineering at a mid-sized software company, and like many people in this industry who read Slate Star Codex and similar blogs, I've internalized Chesterton's fence as a heuristic: don't tear down a fence until you understand why it was built. But I keep running into the problem that this is too vague to actually guide decisions. My team regularly proposes deprecating old internal services, rewriting legacy modules, removing features that seem to have low usage, and eliminating process steps that look like bureaucratic cruft. When I say "let's make sure we understand why it's there first," the response is often "we looked, and as far as we can tell, it's just historical accident." Sometimes that's true, and sometimes they've missed something important — the legacy service handles an edge case that only triggers at 3 a.m. on the last day of the quarter, the code review step catches a class of error that is rare but catastrophic, the rarely-used feature is the only thing keeping a handful of enterprise customers from churning. I want something more structured than a one-line heuristic, and I want my team to internalize the epistemic humility that the heuristic is pointing at, not just pay lip service to it.

I've been reading Burke and some of the secondary literature on epistemic conservatism — the idea that existing practices and institutions encode information that no single individual can fully articulate, that the bare fact that something has survived is weak evidence of its functional value, and that the burden of proof should fall on the reformer rather than on the inherited arrangement. I want to translate this into a decision procedure my team can actually run when they're proposing to remove or replace something. The procedure should be a checklist or flowchart they can work through in under an hour before bringing a proposal to me. It should force them to distinguish between "we understand why this was created and the reason no longer applies" and "we can't figure out why this was created, so we're assuming it was pointless." It should identify the cases where the procedure can't give a clear answer and escalation to human judgment is necessary. And it should be honest about the points where the procedure itself is making a judgment call dressed up as a mechanical step.

Once you've written the procedure, run it on two or three worked examples from software engineering: say, deprecating a legacy authentication service that everyone agrees is a mess, removing a code review step that adds two days to every deployment cycle, and killing a feature that 3% of users still use. For at least one of these examples, the procedure should recommend against the action my team wants to take. Also, flag the limits: what kinds of change proposals does this procedure simply not speak to, and where does the Burkean framework itself break down as a guide to software engineering decisions?
