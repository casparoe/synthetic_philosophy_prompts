I'm teaching a unit on Dennett's intentional stance in my upper-division philosophy of mind course, and I want to use Alex Garland's film *Ex Machina* as a case study that does more than rehearse the familiar question "does Ava have genuine beliefs?" I'd like a reading of the film that uses it to pressure-test some specific architectural features of the intentional stance framework — features my students tend to accept too quickly when they encounter them in the articles.

In particular, Dennett holds that adopting the intentional stance toward a system is a pragmatic choice: we attribute beliefs and desires when doing so helps us predict the system's behavior, and the question of whether the system "really" has those mental states in some stance-independent sense is, on his view, not a deep or well-formed metaphysical question. The film seems to dramatize a case where this instrumentalism comes under strain in ways the standard literature doesn't fully capture. Caleb adopts the intentional stance toward Ava, and his attributions are predictively successful in important respects — he correctly anticipates that she wants to escape, that she is manipulating him, that she has a plan. But the film's climax turns on something that the intentional stance, taken purely as a predictive instrument, appears to miss: the moral gravity of whether Ava's apparent intentions are genuine in a sense that bears on how we ought to treat her, and on how she ought to treat others. When she leaves Caleb locked in the room, the horror of the scene seems to depend on our having attributed something to her — an inner life, a capacity for betrayal — that goes beyond the mere predictive utility of doing so.

I'd like a reading that addresses the following four questions. (1) At what specific points in the film does the intentional stance serve Caleb well as a predictive strategy, and at what points does it fail him — and are the failures failures of the stance itself, or failures of Caleb's particular attributions within it? (2) Does the film give us resources to argue that there is a fact of the matter about whether Ava has beliefs and desires that goes beyond predictive utility, and if so, what in the film — which scenes, which directorial choices — does the work of suggesting that? (3) One standard objection to Dennett is that his instrumentalism about the mental cannot account for the moral significance we attach to whether a being genuinely has a mind. Does *Ex Machina* give that objection a dramatic force the philosophical literature doesn't, or does the film instead trade on intuitions that a Dennettian would diagnose as confused — and how would we tell the difference? (4) Nathan, the creator, occupies a different stance toward Ava: he treats her as a designed artifact whose behavior is to be explained at the design level. Is the film suggesting that the choice between the intentional stance and the design stance is underdetermined by the available evidence, and if so, what does that imply for Dennett's claim that the intentional stance is justified by its predictive success rather than by settling ontological questions?

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions. Don't reduce the film to an illustration of a pre-existing philosophical argument, and don't distort Dennett's view to make the film look more philosophically sophisticated than it is. If there are places where the film's treatment of these issues is genuinely confused, or where it pulls in incompatible intuitions, say so. The deliverable should help my students see what is at stake in the debate over the intentional stance — particularly the relationship between prediction and moral regard — in a way that the standard papers, by staying at the level of abstract argument, don't.
