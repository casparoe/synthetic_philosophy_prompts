I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term, and we've spent several weeks on the literature concerning introspection — roughly, the question of how reliable we are at knowing our own conscious mental states, our reasons for action, and the occurrent contents of our own thoughts. What's striking to me, reading through the back-and-forth, is that the debate seems to have been running in place for decades. On one side, you have the pessimists — the line of work that runs from Nisbett and Wilson's classic studies in the 1970s through to more recent defenses by philosophers like Eric Schwitzgebel — who argue that introspection is systematically unreliable: we confabulate explanations for our own behavior, we are often wrong about what we are thinking or why we acted, and the subjective sense of direct access to our own minds is largely an illusion. On the other side, you have the optimists, who grant that introspection is fallible but maintain that it is broadly trustworthy, that blanket skepticism about it is self-defeating or empirically overblown, and that the pessimists' experimental paradigms are themselves question-begging in various ways. Both sides have their stock studies and their stock rebuttals to the other side's studies, and after forty-plus years of this, I'm not sure anyone has significantly moved.

What I want to understand is not who is right, but what kind of disagreement this is in the first place. Is it fundamentally an empirical disagreement that could in principle be settled by better experiments, and the field simply hasn't run the right ones yet? Is it a disagreement about what counts as an error in introspection — that is, are the two sides tacitly operating with different standards for when introspection has "failed," such that the same data look like a minor glitch to one side and a damning indictment to the other? Is it a disagreement about the burden of proof — pessimists treating every error as evidence of systematic unreliability, optimists treating every success as evidence of general reliability, and neither side having argued for why their burden is the right one? Or is it something deeper: a disagreement about what introspection even is, such that the two sides are answering different questions under a single label — one asking about the accuracy of a quasi-perceptual inner faculty, the other asking about the coherence of self-narratives, and the apparent clash being an artifact of talking past each other? I'm not looking for a survey of the literature or a verdict on who wins. I'm asking for a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself, and an honest assessment of whether anything — a new experimental paradigm, a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question — could actually move it, or whether we should expect the impasse to persist indefinitely.
