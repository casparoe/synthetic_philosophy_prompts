I am trying to work out what I actually think about the unity of consciousness, and I would like you to find the principle behind my verdicts on cases, or tell me which verdicts I should abandon. Here are the cases and my verdicts. A split-brain patient whose right hemisphere is shown a command and whose left hand carries it out, while the speaking left hemisphere invents a reason: I think there is one person, not two; the hemispheres are simply not sharing information, and the speaking half confabulates. A hypnotized subject who carries out a post-hypnotic suggestion and then confabulates a reason: plainly one subject, and the explanation is something like self-deception. A patient with anosognosia who denies that a paralyzed arm is theirs: again one subject, with a damaged self-model. But in the thought experiment in which a person's two hemispheres are transplanted into two different bodies, each preserving the original's memories and character, I find myself wanting to say that there would be two persons, each equally continuous with the original, and that the original person has ceased to exist. And in an ordinary split-brain patient I am not sure whether to say there is one subject with two streams of consciousness that are not co-conscious, or two subjects sharing one body and one biography. Is there a single principle about what unifies experiences into one subject that captures all of these, or nearly all? What does it predict about a patient who has had a hemisphere removed, or about someone in deep meditation who reports that the self has dropped away? And which of my verdicts does it force me to give up?
