I'm a journalist working on a piece about algorithmic risk prediction — the kind of thing courts use for recidivism, or doctors use for survival odds — and I've fallen down a rabbit hole I can't climb out of. It's this thing called the reference class problem, and I tried to write it up as a tight argument for my editor, and now I think the argument might be too good. Here it is:

(1) Assigning a probability to a one-off event requires placing that event in a reference class — a set of relevantly similar events whose frequencies give you the numbers.
(2) Any particular event falls into many reference classes at once. This patient is "a 67-year-old man," "a 67-year-old man with atrial fibrillation," "a 67-year-old man with atrial fibrillation who walks 30 minutes a day," and so on, and these classes yield different survival probabilities.
(3) There is no principled, non-arbitrary rule for selecting the uniquely correct reference class. Narrowing the class buys you more specificity but smaller sample sizes and noisier frequencies; widening it buys you statistical stability but less relevance. The tradeoff is real, and there's no objective cut point.
(4) So there is no unique, objective probability for any singular event.
(5) If there's no unique objective probability, then the whole enterprise of assigning risk scores to individuals — in medicine, in criminal justice, in insurance — rests on an arbitrary choice.
(6) That's an absurd conclusion. Risk scoring isn't perfect, but it's not literally arbitrary; some risk models are clearly better than others, and we can improve them.

So the argument has to break somewhere. But I can't figure out where. Premise (1) seems like what probability is, at least if you're not a pure subjectivist. Premise (2) is just a fact. Premise (3) seems right — people have been banging on this for 150 years and nobody's produced the rule. (4) follows from the first three, and (5) seems to follow from (4). So the only out is rejecting (6), which I can't do and still write the article I'm trying to write.

You're an expert philosopher of probability. Think carefully, maybe start by sketching what each premise commits you to, and then tell me: which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'm especially curious if there's a way to keep (1) and (2) but block the conclusion — I'm not ready to go full Bayesian and say probabilities are just credences in my head.
