So I got into this huge argument with a friend last night about whether a soldier fighting for an unjust cause is morally in the wrong for killing enemy soldiers. My friend kept saying no — the soldier was conscripted, or he reasonably believes his side is just, or he's just trying to protect the guys in his unit. And I kept saying: but if you apply that reasoning anywhere else, it falls apart. Imagine a mafia enforcer who was born into the family, genuinely believes the people he kills had it coming, and does it to protect his crew. We don't say "well he's morally in the clear then." Or a cult member who helps kill someone because the leader told him to and he was raised in it and genuinely thinks it's righteous. We don't give him a pass. But my friend insisted war is different, and he couldn't quite say why — he just kept coming back to "but its war."

I've been turning this over and I can't tell if there's an established concept for this thing where we treat combat as a special moral domain with its own rules, or for the asymmetry between how we judge soldiers and how we judge everyone else who kills under similar epistemic and situational pressures. Like, is there an actual name for this carve-out, and more importantly, what is supposed to justify it? Is it a principled distinction or just a gut feeling that war is special? I don't want just the label; I want to know whether the thing itself holds up under scrutiny, or if the people who defend it are just refusing to follow their own reasoning to where it leads. And if there are nearby concepts that almost fit but not quite, I want to know what the differences are.
