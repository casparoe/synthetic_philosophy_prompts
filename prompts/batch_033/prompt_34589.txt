I'm a novelist — mid-career, literary fiction, the kind of thing where my characters spend a lot of time talking at dinner parties — and I've been trying to nail down a social dynamic I want to build a character around, but I don't have the word for it and I'm not sure philosophy has one either.

Here's the thing. Someone in my circle hasn't read a book, hasn't seen the film, hasn't listened to the album. But they've marinated in the discourse. They've read the reviews, they've heard the podcast discussions, they've scrolled the arguments on Twitter or Bluesky or wherever. And when the work comes up in conversation, they hold forth with a fully formed aesthetic verdict — "oh, it's overrated, the third act collapses, the prose is self-indulgent" — delivered with the same cadence and confidence as someone who spent six hours with the thing. If you ask directly, they'll admit they haven't experienced it, but they'll say it like it's almost a point of pride: "I haven't read it, but I know enough." And here's the part that interests me: their judgment has a different texture from the judgment of someone who actually read the book. The actual reader's take is messy — they liked it but they're not sure why, the ending worked for them but they can't defend it, they keep circling back to one scene they can't quite interpret. The discourse-absorber's take is never messy. It's crisp, it's confident, it sounds like a pull quote. It's in the register of a review, never in the register of a confession. And paradoxically, this crispness makes it more fragile — you can't push on it without the whole thing collapsing, because there's no texture underneath, no memory of a specific passage or a moment of boredome or a line that landed wrong. There's nothing to push against.

I'm dimly aware there's a whole literature on "aesthetic testimony" — whether you can learn about art from other people's descriptions. But that debate, as I understand it, is largely about whether testimony can justify aesthetic belief. What I'm after is narrower and more sociological: it's the practice of treating discourse-absorption as functionally equivalent to firsthand experience, and the distinctive rhetorical posture that comes with it — the overconfidence, the review-like register, the brittleness. It feels like a specific epistemic stance, maybe even a specific kind of bullshit in the Frankfurtian sense, but I'm not sure.

Is there a name for this? If it's just a corrolary of the aesthetic testimony problem, tell me that, but also tell me whether anyone has analyzed it on its own terms — the social practice, not just the epistemological question. If nobody has named it, give it a name and draw its boundaries: what counts, what's adjacent but different (is it just being well-informed? is it intellectual posturing?), and where the line is between this and someone who legitimately knows a lot about a work they haven't consumed. Keep it to about 400 words — I need the concept, not the whole history of the debate.
