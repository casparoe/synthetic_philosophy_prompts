I'm taking an advanced physics elective at my high school, and we've been working through the formalism of quantum mechanics — operators, eigenstates, the Schrödinger equation, the Born rule. Last week, after class, I stayed behind to ask my teacher what she actually thinks about the measurement problem and whether she leans toward Copenhagen, Everett, or Bohm. She smiled, waved her hand, and said: "Shut up and calculate!" Then she added that all the interpretation stuff is a distraction and that real physicists just get on with the math.

I've since learned that this slogan has a history — it was coined by N. David Mermin as a caricature of the attitude some physicists take toward foundational questions. But the more I think about it, the less clear I am about what exactly it's supposed to be claiming. It could be a pragmatic rule of thumb ("don't let philosophical tangles stop you from solving problems"), or a hard positivist thesis ("only statements about measurement outcomes have meaning"), or a claim about empirical underdetermination ("Everett, Copenhagen, and Bohm make all the same predictions, so the choice between them isn't physics"), or even a kind of quietism ("there is a fact of the matter, but we can't access it, so stop asking"). Those feel very different in their plausibility and their consequences.

I want to go back to my teacher with something clear. Could you — acting as a philosopher of physics — lay out the distinct precise theses that the slogan "Shut up and calculate!" might be expressing? For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false. Most importantly, tell me which of these readings would genuinely justify dismissing questions about the interpretation of quantum mechanics, and which would instead be an evasion — a way of shutting down a legitimate question without engaging it. If it helps, start by brainstorming all the possible things someone might mean when they say it, then sort them into a menu I could bring to a conversation.
