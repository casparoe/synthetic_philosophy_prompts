For a feature I am writing about the current state of consciousness science, I have been struck by how differently otherwise careful researchers use the label the hard problem. Some of my sources treat the hard problem of consciousness as a genuine explanatory gap: there is something it is like to be in a conscious state, this phenomenal character is not entailed by any physical or functional description of the brain, and the gap will not be closed by further neuroscience because it is not an empirical gap in the first place. Others treat the whole framing as a conceptual artifact, comparable to the way vitalism dissolved once biochemistry matured; on this side the prediction is that the problem will be dissolved rather than solved, either because phenomenal consciousness gets eliminated as a real phenomenon, because the distinction between the physical and the phenomenal comes to seem misguided, or because a future conceptual scheme stops asking the question in this form.

I want a number rather than a both-sides paragraph. What is your credence that the hard problem, in the sense just specified, names a real and persistent explanatory gap rather than a temporary artifact of our current concepts? Please decompose the estimate into at least these terms: (a) your probability that phenomenal consciousness exists at all, that is, that illusionism or eliminativism about phenomenal states is false; (b) your probability, conditional on its existing, that phenomenal facts are not entailed by the physical or functional facts, so that no reductive or functional identification of the kind standard elsewhere in science will capture them; and (c) your probability, conditional on both of those, that the apparent gap will not eventually be dissolved by a conceptual revolution rather than remaining a permanent feature of our understanding. Show how you combine these into the final number, then tell me which term the final credence is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would move that term the most. A philosopher I interviewed put the overall claim at around 0.9; a researcher on the other side put it at around 0.1. On your decomposition, which sub-question does that kind of disagreement most plausibly live in?
