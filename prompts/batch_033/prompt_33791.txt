Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind who disagree about how to interpret the colour phi phenomenon. Set the scene by having them agree on the empirical facts: two briefly illuminated spots are flashed in quick succession at different positions, the first green and the second red, and a normal observer reports seeing a single spot that moves continuously from the first position to the second and changes from green to red somewhere around the midpoint of its trajectory. The puzzle is that the content of the experience seems to have been fixed only after the second flash occurred, even though the experience itself presents the colour change as happening before the second flash.

One philosopher defends the Orwellian account: the observer first has a conscious experience of, say, a green spot beginning to move, and then, once the red flash arrives and is processed, this earlier experience is retroactively edited or overwritten, so the observer's memory and report reflect only the revised version. The other defends the Stalinesque account: the onset of conscious experience is itself delayed until the second flash has been processed, so the observer never consciously experiences the unrevised version at all; the first version to reach consciousness is already the integrated moving-and-changing spot.

The dialogue should be a genuine engagement between equals, not a sequence of prepared speeches. In particular:

- The Orwellian should press the Stalinesque on the length and function of the delay: what is consciousness for, and what happens during the waiting period, if it is systematically held back until after the events it reports have already occurred?
- The Stalinesque should press the Orwellian on evidence: if the alleged pre-revision experience leaves no trace in memory or report, by what right does anyone posit it, and what experiment could in principle reveal it?
- Each should respond to the other's actual objections rather than restating their own position.
- At least once, they should consider whether the disagreement is empirically decidable in principle, and at least one of them should propose a concrete experimental modification that would, if feasible, favour their side.
- Each should be forced to concede at least one genuine difficulty in their own position.

End the dialogue without declaring a winner. As a coda, have each character state the single finding that would most reduce their confidence in their own view, and why. Write the entire dialogue in British English, in a formal but lively register.
