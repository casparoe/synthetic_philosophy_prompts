I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of physics seminar, and I have a ninety-minute session in which students must come to see why the Born rule is a genuine problem for the many-worlds interpretation rather than an axiom that simply carries over from collapse theories. Don't explain the problem to me; plan how I should explain it. Give the order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the question 'what do the amplitude weights mean if every branch is real?' feels forced rather than merely posed: what has to be said about measurement before anything is said about branching, and what must be in place before the word 'probability' can even be used without begging the question. Pick the one worked example that should carry the session and say why it needs unequal amplitudes. List the misconceptions students will form — I expect at least 'probability is the fraction of worlds with each outcome' and 'all branches are equally real, so each should be equally likely' — and specify where in the sequence each should be headed off and with what distinction. State what to refuse to cover at this level, which I take to include the full decision-theoretic derivation and the decoherence formalism. End with one question whose answer would show a student understands why 'just add the Born rule as an extra postulate' does not dissolve the problem, and one answer that would show they don't.
