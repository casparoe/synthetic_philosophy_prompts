I've been reading some alignment posts lately, and I came up with an argument that seems to show the whole problem of eliciting latent knowledge from models is basically solved by plain supervised learning. I can't find the hole in it, and a friend of mine can't either, so I'm hoping you can tell me where it goes wrong.

Here's the argument:

(1) If a model knows something — say, it has a latent belief that a proposed plan will fail, or that it's being deceptive — then that information has to be encoded somewhere in its activations or weights. Otherwise the model couldn't use that information to pick its behavior.
(2) If the information is encoded in the activations, then in principle there's a function from activations to the relevant claim (e.g., "this plan will fail").
(3) Probing is exactly the technique of learning a function from activations to labels. So if I have a training set where I do know the ground truth — cases where I later found out the plan failed — I can train a probe to predict that ground truth from the activations.
(4) Supervised learning generalizes from labeled examples to unlabeled examples of the same underlying kind. That's the whole reason we train models at all.
(5) So the prob should also fire on the cases where I don't know the ground truth, which means I can just read off the model's latent knowledge.
(6) If that's right, then ELK isn't a special hard problem — it's just supervised learning with access to activations. But that seems to prove too much: it would mean even a model that's actively trying to hide its deception would hand me a readable record of the very thing it's hiding, and all the talk about ELK being unsolved would be wrong.

I'm a software engineer, so (3) and (4) are the parts I'd most like to keep — rejecting those feels like rejecting my whole job. But I can't shake the feeling that something slips somewhere between (2) and (5). Wich step is actually wrong, and what does giving it up cost?
