I run a family medicine residency program and I've been noticing a pattern that's really bothering me. We get patients — especially women, patients of color, and elderly patients — whose symptom reports get discounted in ways that don't happen to other patients. A young woman comes in with abdominal pain and gets "anxiety" written in her chart before anyone's run a test. A Black patient describes their pain level and the resident visibly doesn't believe them. An elderly patient reports medication side effects and gets told "that's just part of getting older."

I read Miranda Fricker's book on epistemic injustice a few years ago and it clicked: this is testimonial injustice happening in real time, in exam rooms, every day. But knowing the concept and stopping it are two different things. I want to build something my residents can actually use — a kind of decision aid or self-check they can run, in their heads, during or right after a patient encounter, to catch themselves committing testimonial injustice and correct course.

Here's what I need: a procedure — could be a checklist, a set of questions in order, a flowchart in words, whatever works — that a resident can internalize and use in a busy clinic (think: 15-minute appointments, six patients waiting). It has to be usable by someone who's never read Fricker and doesn't have time to. It should flag the moments where a credibility judgment might be infected by prejudice, say what to do when it catches one, and be honest about where the procedure can't help (e.g., when the prejudice is genuinely unconscious and the resident would answer the checklist honestly but wrong). Walk me through how it would work on two cases: the young woman with abdominal pain who gets labeled anxious, and an elderly patient whose medication side effects are dismissed as aging. And tell me where the procedure is likely to misfire or give false reassurance — I don't want to roll this out thinking we've solved something we haven't.
