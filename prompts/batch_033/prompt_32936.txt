I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar of about fifteen students, meeting for ninety minutes. We are about to begin the unit on the epistemic regress problem — the Agrippan trilemma — and the three classical responses to it: foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. I do not want to begin by lecturing on the trilemma; I want the students to discover the structure of the problem for themselves and to feel, viscerally, why each of the three responses has both appeal and cost.

Design a structured in-class activity — a game with rules, roles, and a scoring system — that makes participants run into the regress directly. The core mechanism should be that participants must justify claims, those justifications can themselves be challenged, and the process iterates under constraints that make the three responses emerge as natural strategies rather than as theories named in advance. The rules should be simple enough to explain in under five minutes, and the activity should run for about forty minutes, leaving ample time for debrief.

Specify: the rules and materials (any handouts, cards, whiteboard setup), the precise framing I should give the students at the start (which must not mention foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism, or the regress problem itself), the timeline, and a detailed debrief script that maps what happened in the activity onto the three positions — giving each its strongest case, not a caricature. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for instance, students fixating on the truth of the claims rather than their justificatory structure, or one group exploiting a loophole in the rules) and how I should handle those failures. Finally, give an honest note on which features of the philosophical debate the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, the distinction between doxastic and propositional justification, or the internalist/externalist dimension — and how I should address those gaps in the lecture that follows.
