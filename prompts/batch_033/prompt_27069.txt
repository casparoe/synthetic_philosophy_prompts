I'm a retired geophysicist who started reading moral philosophy about four years ago, and I keep a small discussion group with other former scientists in my area. Lately we've been stuck on the question of whether pleasure and pain are genuinely symmetric in ethics — whether a unit of suffering avoided is morally equivalent to a unit of pleasure gained, or whether the two have fundamentally different weights or structures. I'd like to produce a handout that sorts readers into a position on this question without letting them pick by label.

Please write a taxonomy of positions on the moral asymmetry between pleasure and pain, structured as a decision tree. Each branching node should present a concrete scenario or a yes/no claim that someone can evaluate without knowing the philosophical terminology. The tree should route a reader to a named leaf position based on their answers.

Here are the leaves I want included:

- Classical symmetric utilitarianism
- Prioritarianism (suffering counts more heavily than pleasure, but no amount of suffering is strictly incomparable with pleasure)
- Moderate negative utilitarianism (extreme suffering has lexical priority — no quantity of pleasure can outweigh it — but mild suffering does not)
- Strong negative utilitarianism (all suffering has lexical priority over pleasure)
- Antifrustrationism (only frustrated preferences or unsatisfied desires count negatively; satisfied preferences carry no positive value)
- An asymmetry view restricted to creation: we have stronger reasons not to bring miserable lives into existence than to bring happy lives into existence, but this does not entail antinatalism and does not apply to already-existing beings

Also include any additional leaves you think are well-enough defended to belong, but do not inflate the tree with positions nobody holds.

For each unoccupied combination of answers — that is, a path through the tree that no named position occupies — add a brief note diagnosing why: is the combination logically incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the literature?

The diagnostic questions should use concrete tradeoff cases rather than abstract principles. For instance, instead of asking "do you believe in a lexical threshold?", present a case: "You can either (a) prevent one person from experiencing an hour of the worst agony imaginable, or (b) bring about a mild but genuine pleasure for N people who would otherwise experience neither pleasure nor pain. Is there any finite N for which you would choose (b)?" Vary the magnitudes across questions to locate the breakpoints between positions.

At least three questions should probe the distinction between preventing suffering in existing beings and avoiding the creation of new suffering beings — the commission/omission and identity-affecting dimensions — since several positions in this space turn on whether these are treated alike.

The audience is scientifically literate but has no formal philosophy training. The questions themselves should be jargon-free; the leaf labels can and should use standard terminology. Keep the whole thing to roughly eight to twelve branching nodes.
