I'm teaching a unit on the ethics of consent in an upper-division applied ethics course, and I want to give students a diagnostic instrument that sorts them into positions based on what they say about cases, not on which label they instinctively prefer. Please write a decision tree (or taxonomy in tree form) of the main positions on what makes consent morally valid. The tree should be built from the logical space of possible answers to diagnostic questions, not from a pre-existing list of named positions.

Each branch node should be a concrete case or a sharply stated claim that positions genuinely come apart on, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing the terminology. The cases should span the central dimensions of the debate: deception (about the nature of the act, about its consequences, about the identity of the partner), coercion (threats, offers one cannot refuse, background structural pressure), and competence (intoxication, cognitive disability, age, temporary emotional distress). Make sure at least one case involves deception about something other than the partner's identity, and at least one involves a hard-to-classify pressure that is neither a paradigmatic threat nor a paradigmatic offer.

The leaves should carry the name of the position if it has a standard name in the literature, and one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment — the case it struggles with or the implication its defenders least want to admit. If there are logically possible combinations of answers that no one defends, mark those leaves as unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Flag any pair of positions your tree cannot separate, and explain why the diagnostic questions fail to pull them apart.

The audience is philosophy majors who have read some applied ethics but not the specialist consent literature. Aim for roughly six to eight leaves, including both occupied and unoccupied positions.
