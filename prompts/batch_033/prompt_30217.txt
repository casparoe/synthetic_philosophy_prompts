I direct a regional juried art prize for emerging visual artists. Our entry rules require that a submitted work be the entrant's own original creative work, a clause we have never had to interpret because its meaning seemed obvious. This year roughly a third of the entries were made with image generators: some entrants disclose it, some do not, and our two volunteer jurors now spend as much time disputing whether a piece is really the entrant's creative work as they do judging its quality. The prize is small but respected; an incorrect eligibility ruling either lets in work that devalues the award or excludes a serious entrant and invites a public dispute. I make the final eligibility call. The jurors screen around 250 entries at ten to fifteen minutes each, and neither has any background in philosophy. I need whatever the philosophy of creativity actually has to say translated into something we can run under those constraints.

Please write me a decision procedure for determining whether an entry satisfies the own-original-creative-work requirement. It should be a checklist or sequence of questions in words: the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with accept or reject, and where it must hand the decision to me because the remaining question is one of values or judgment rather than fact. Keep three questions distinct, since they come apart in practice: whether the image is creative at all, whether it is the entrant's creative work, and whether it is the entrant's own original work. The procedure should preserve the distinctions from philosophical accounts of creativity that actually carry weight here, for instance between the novelty of the product and the agency exercised in producing it, and between novelty relative to the entrant's own past work and novelty relative to a wider field, and it should drop the distinctions that cannot survive a ten-minute screening by non-specialists. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure cannot settle.

Then apply it to these three entries:

1. A photographer shoots a portrait in a studio, then uses a generative tool to produce several candidate backgrounds, chooses one, and adjusts the lighting and color grading by hand so the final image is a seamless composite.

2. An entrant fine-tunes a model on a thousand of their own drawings, generates four hundred images, selects twelve, and submits those twelve without further alteration.

3. An entrant generates an image from a text prompt, selects the one they find most striking, and then spends forty hours producing a faithful oil painting of it by hand.

For each, say what the procedure returns and why. Be explicit about which of the three returns a verdict that I, given my likely instincts as a practicing curator, will least want to accept, and say why the procedure is nonetheless right. I am not asking for a survey of positions: take a definite view of what creativity consists in for the purposes of this rule, build the procedure around that view, and defend the choices you made in operationalizing it.
