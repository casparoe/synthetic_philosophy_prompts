I'm a program officer at a mid-size foundation, and I keep getting proposals where the whole pitch is "there's a tiny chance this does an absolutely enormous amount of good." Some of them are AI-safety or biosecurity projects with claimed probabilities like 0.01% of averting a catastrophe, and some are stranger long-shot bets. The trouble is that if I just multiply probability times claimed impact, these proposals dominate everything else in our portfolio, and I cant tell the serious ones from the Pascal's muggings. I need a reusable decision procedure I can run on any proposal where the headline expected value depends on a tiny probability of a huge outcome — a checklist in words, with the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to me becuase the remaining question is one of values rather than arithmetic.

I'm especially worried that any finite grant can be made to look good by a small enough probability of a big enough payoff, so the procedure should say when to stop redoing the expected-value math and just cap or reject on principle, and also when that would be a mistake.

A bit of context so the procedure is actually usable: I see a couple of these a month, grants run from about $50k to $2m, I have maybe 90 minutes per proposal, I'm not a statistician, and our board has to sign off on anything I recommend. So write this for a grant officer, not for a seminar.

Please act as an expert in decision theory under uncertainty who has spent time inside foundations. Before you write the final procedure, spend some time brainstorming the hard cases and the places where the procedure is most likely to break, then give me the finished procedure. Then run it on two or three worked examples: (1) a proposal claiming a 0.01% chance of meaningfully reducing existential risk from AI for a $500k grant, (2) a $200k grant to a local food bank with boring but very reliable benefits, and (3) a proposal claiming something like a one-in-a-billion chance of preventing an astronomically large amount of future suffering. Make sure at least one of the worked examples ends in a conclusion I'm not going to like, and be explicit about where the procedure will misfire or simply can't settle the question.
