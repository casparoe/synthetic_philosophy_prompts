I have been teaching a graduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility this term, and one of my students presented a reconstruction of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument that has stayed with me, because I find each step hard to deny and the conclusion impossible to live with. The argument, as she laid it out, runs like this. (1) Whatever you do in a given situation, you do because of the way you are: your beliefs, desires, values, dispositions, and capacities. (2) Therefore, to be truly morally responsible for what you do, you must be truly morally responsible for the way you are, at least in the respects of mind and character that issue in your choice. (3) But you cannot be truly morally responsible for the way you are. (3a) To be truly responsible for the way you are, you would have to have intentionally brought it about that you are that way. (3b) But any act of bringing it about that you are a certain way would itself have to issue from the way you already were, with whatever preferences and principles of choice you already had. (3c) If you are to be truly responsible for that prior way of being, the same demand applies to it in turn, and so on without end. (4) Therefore no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything they do. What unsettles me is that the argument appears to be entirely a priori and independent of the determinism question: injecting an undetermined event into the story would not supply the missing self-creation, since an event that is not determined by anything is not one you controlled or authored either. So the argument seems to establish that true moral responsibility is impossible no matter what the metaphysics of free will turns out to be. I am most reluctant to give up the thought that at least some people are sometimes responsible in the way that makes resentment, gratitude, and blame fitting. But I cannot tell whether the weak point is the move from responsibility for actions to responsibility for character, the regress that is supposed to rule out responsibility for character, the assumption that responsibility for the way one is requires intentional self-creation, or somewhere else entirely, and I would like to know what rejecting each of these would cost. Would you walk me through where the argument goes wrong, if it does, and tell me which premise a defender of ordinary moral responsibility should reject and what that rejection commits her to? I would be grateful if you would write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, the way one would write a letter.
