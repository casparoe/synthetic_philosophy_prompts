I'm an undergraduate serving on the allocations committee of my university's Effective Altruism chapter, which administers a modest annual grant of about $25,000 from alumni donors. I'm also taking a seminar this term on philosophical critiques of effective altruism, and the readings on measurability bias, the institutional critique, and earning to give have made our current decision feel considerably more difficult than it did a year ago. We have three finalists. The first is a direct global-health intervention: a partnership that would fund insecticide-treated bed nets through a well-established charity whose randomized-trial evidence of mortality reduction is, by our usual standards, about as strong as evidence gets. The second is a campus-based systemic-change project: a campaign to pressure the university endowment to adopt stronger fossil-fuel exclusion screens and create a standing committee on socially responsible investment. Its advocates argue that it targets a structural driver of harm rather than a downstream symptom, but its expected impact is nearly impossible to quantify and its probability of success is widely agreed to be low. The third is an earning-to-give pipeline: funding career mentorship, interview preparation, and networking for students who commit to entering high-earning fields and donating a fixed share of income, on the argument that the marginal donations generated will eventually exceed the grant several times over. Half the committee wants to fund only the bed-net proposal, since it alone has a measurable, evidence-backed counterfactual; the other half says this is exactly the measurability bias our readings warn about, and that treating measurability as a tiebreaker quietly bakes in a conservative deference to existing institutions.

I have been asked to draft the short decision memo the committee will vote on next week. The memo should recommend one proposal to fund, or a split if it can be justified by principle rather than by compromise. It should state criteria that we could apply to future rounds, and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side — including the worry, pressed by several members, that our chapter's entire decision procedure assumes a kind of expected-value maximizing that may itself be part of the problem. Please draft the memo.
