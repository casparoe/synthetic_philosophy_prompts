Hey—so I'm teaching an upper-level aesthetics seminar this term and we've spent the last three weeks on the paradox of fiction. I want to write a final exam question that actually makes them think, rather than just regurgitate "Radford says we're irrational, Walton says it's make-believe, the end." Here's what I have in mind.

The question:

"It's often said that the paradox of fiction is an inconsistent triad: we have genuine emotional responses to fictional events; genuine emotion requires belief in the existence of its object; and we don't believe fictional events are real. But there's a quieter, trickier version of the problem that doesn't turn on the belief requirement at all. Even if we grant that emotions toward fictions are possible, we still have to explain why they seem to violate the norms that govern ordinary emotions—why, for instance, it seems fine to feel pity for Anna Karenina while doing nothing to help her, or why we seek out tragedies that make us sad when we'd avoid that sadness in real life. In other words, the deeper challenge might not be about the possibility of fictional emotions but about their rationality.

Choose one major theory of fictional emotion (e.g., Walton's make-believe theory, the thought theory, the illusion theory, or any other you find compelling) and explain: does it handle the rationality problem better than it handles the possibility problem, or vice versa? In your answer, you must (a) clearly state what the rationality problem is and how it differs from the standard possibility problem, (b) show how your chosen theory addresses both problems, and (c) argue that the theory is stronger on one front than the other, with a concrete example—say, a specific scene from a novel or film—that illustrates the asymmetry you're pointing to."

Model answer (roughly 600 words):

The standard paradox of fiction is framed as a problem about the possibility of emotional responses to what we know doesn't exist. The rationality problem, by contrast, asks whether such responses—even if we grant they occur—are appropriately governed by the norms that make ordinary emotions intelligible. Ordinary fear motivates avoidance; pity for real suffering motivates assistance. When we pity Anna Karenina, we don't send her a pamphlet on divorce law. When we watch a horror film, we don't flee the theater. This isn't just a quirk—it looks like a failure of the functional role that partly constitutes the emotions in question. A theory of fictional emotion that can't explain why this failure doesn't impugn our rationality has left the harder work undone.

Kendall Walton's make-believe theory handles the rationality problem more successfully than the possibility problem. On Walton's view, what we call "fear" of a cinematic monster isn't genuine fear but a quasi-emotion experienced within a game of make-believe: we are make-believedly afraid, just as a child playing monsters is make-believedly in danger. The props (the film images, the soundtrack) prescribe certain imaginings, and our physiological arousal is part of the game, not a genuine emotional state directed at a nonexistent object.

This theory dissolves the possibility problem at a stroke: there's no puzzle about how we can fear a monster we know isn't real, because we aren't genuinely fearing it at all. But this is also where the theory is weakest. The phenomenology of watching a horror film doesn't feel like pretending—our hearts race, we flinch, we cover our eyes. Walton's response is that these physiological responses are real even if the emotion they're embedded in is make-believe, but the line between "real arousal inside a quasi-emotion" and "genuine fear" is hard to draw without begging the question. The possibility problem persists for anyone who insists that the experience feels like the real thing.

The rationality problem, however, is where Walton's theory shines. If our responses to fiction are quasi-emotions within a game of make-believe, then the norms governing them are the norms of the game, not the norms of practical rationality. It's not irrational to fail to help Anna Karenina because the game of reading Tolstoy doesn't prescribe helping-her imaginings; it prescribes pitying-her imaginings. The functional role of quasi-pity is circumscribed by the conventions of the fiction and the imaginative framework it establishes. Similarly, seeking out tragedy is no more irrational than children seeking out scary stories around a campfire: the quasi-sadness is part of a valued imaginative activity, not a malfunction of our hedonic system.

Consider the final scene of Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, where Kathy stands in a field watching garbage blow across the fence, having accepted her fate. The reader feels a deep, quiet devastation. But we don't close the book and start googling how to donate to clone-rights organizations. On Walton's theory, this is exactly what we should expect: the quasi-grief is prescribed by the narrative and contained within it. The rationality problem—why we find this experience valuable rather than perverse—is answered by the fact that the imaginative engagement itself is what we value, not the emotional state considered in isolation. The possibility problem is trickier for Walton precisely because the grief feels so real that calling it "quasi" can seem like a denial of the experience itself.

Marking scheme (out of 20):

1. (4 marks) Clearly distinguishes the rationality problem from the possibility problem. The student must say more than "one is about whether fictional emotions exist, the other about whether they're rational." An A answer identifies that the rationality problem concerns the normative fit between the emotion and its characteristic action-tendencies and hedonic valence, while the possibility problem concerns the cognitive preconditions of emotion. A B answer states the distinction but doesn't unpack it. Typical error: students frame the rationality problem as just "is it rational to feel these things?" without connecting it to the functional role of emotions.

2. (4 marks) Accurately presents the chosen theory and shows how it addresses both problems. The theory must be described with enough precision that its commitments are clear. An A answer applies the theory to both problems explicitly rather than summarizing the theory and gesturing. Typical error: students spend a paragraph explaining what Walton (or whoever) thinks and then say "so it solves both problems" without showing the mechanism.

3. (5 marks) Argues convincingly that the theory handles one problem better than the other. This is the crux: the student must make an asymmetric claim and defend it. An A answer identifies a specific tension or gap in the theory's handling of the weaker problem and explains why the same theory handles the stronger problem more cleanly. Typical error: students hedge and say the theory handles both equally well, which fails to engage the prompt.

4. (4 marks) Uses a concrete, well-chosen example that illustrates the asymmetry. The example must be specific enough to do argumentative work—not just namedropped. An A answer shows how the example makes the asymmetry vivid. Typical error: students mention a movie or book but don't connect it to the theoretical point, so the example floats there as decoration.

5. (3 marks) Clarity, structure, and philosophical prose. An A answer is well-organized, avoids sloppy formulations, and doesn't equivocate on key terms like "emotion," "belief," or "rational." Typical error: sloppy use of "rational" to mean three different things across the essay.
