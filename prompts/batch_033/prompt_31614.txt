I'm in a reading group that's been working through epistemic logic — mostly philosophy grad students, with one computer scientist who keeps asking what any of this buys you computationally — and we got stuck last week on the problem of logical omniscience. Next week I have to write a short response paper on that session, and I still don't know what I think, so I'd like help sorting through the actual back-and-forth. Two members, Dana and Priya, argued for a while and then asked the rest of us to referee.

(1) Dana said the possible-worlds treatment of knowledge isn't meant to describe real humans; it's an idealization, like frictionless planes or massless ropes. The model just isolates what follows from knowing something, and complaining that it makes agents logically omniscient is like complaining that introductory physics ignores friction. (2) Priya replied that friction is a good idealization because you can say how far a real rope is from massless and add friction as a small correction, whereas logical omniscience isn't like that: there's no parameter you can turn to make an agent only a little non-omniscient, so the model isn't conected to the real target in any controlled way. (3) Dana came back that the logic is normative, not descriptive: an ideally rational agent should believe the consequences of what they believe, so the so-called problem is just part of what ideal means. (4) Priya said a norm that requires believing infinitely many consequences is one no finite agent could even aproximate, and anyway not having considered some complicated tautology isn't a rational failing, so the normative story doesn't rescue the framework. (5) Dana then said fine, if you want bounded agents you go syntactic — make belief a relation to sentences rather than sets of worlds — but then you lose the clean semantics and have to say when two sentences express the same belief, which just reintroduces the problem. (6) Priya admitted syntactic models handle the original objection, but said they're ad hoc: they fix the logic by shrinking the objects of belief to match our cognitive limits, without explaining why some consequences get missed and not others. They asked me to referee and I honestly didn't know. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who is more right overall about the problem of logical omniscience.
