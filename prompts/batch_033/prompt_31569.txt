I work on the survey design team at a large public-health research institute. We run longitudinal studies tracking health outcomes across populations, and we're constantly fighting about which demographic categories to include on our intake forms and how to ask about them. The fights are usually between people who say "we need to track race/gender/etc. because health disparities are real" and people who say "these categories are socially constructed and by using them we're reifying them and doing harm." Both sides are right about something and we have no framwork for resolving this.

I want a decision procedure my team can use when we're designing a new survey or revising an old one. For each candidate category (say, a race question, a gender question, a disability-status question, an income bracket question), the procedure should walk us through: should we include it at all? If yes, should we use fixed checkboxes or a free-text field? Should we let people select multiple? And when should we push back on a funder or a PI who insists on a category we think is harmful?

Ground this in whatever the social-construction literature actually implies for practical classification decisions — not the metaphysics for its own sake, but what follows for someone who has to build a form that real people will fill out. The procedure has to be something a team with no philosophy background can run in an hour-long meeting. Flag the steps that are genuinely judgment calls vs. steps where the theory gives a clear answer. And be honest about where the procedure will give an answer that makes everyone unhappy.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a question about race/ethnicity on a study of maternal health outcomes, where we know there are real disparities but the standard OMB categories are a mess; (2) a question about gender identity on a study of adolescent mental health, where our IRB is nervous and some parents will see the form; (3) a question about "household income" on a study of food insecurity, where the category seems straightforward but the construction is actually pretty loaded (what counts as a household? whose income?).
