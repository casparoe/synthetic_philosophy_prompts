I serve on the ethics committee of a large teaching hospital. We are reviewing a proposed revision to our mass-casualty triage protocol. Under the current protocol, when ventilators are scarce, a triage officer may withhold a ventilator from a patient with a poor prognosis in order to allocate it to one with a better chance — but may not withdraw a ventilator already in use, even if doing so would save more lives. The proposed revision would permit withdrawal in specified circumstances: when the patient currently on the ventilator has a less than 10% chance of survival even with continued ventilation, and at least two other patients would die without it who each have a greater than 50% chance of survival with it.

The committee is sharply divided. One faction argues that the distinction between withholding and withdrawing is morally irrelevant — both result in death, and the only thing that matters is the number of lives saved. They say the current policy is incoherent and that refusing to withdraw is just squeamishness dressed up as principle. The other faction argues that withdrawing life support from a patient who is not yet dying is an act of killing, not merely allowing to die, and that a physician's role absolutely forbids intentionally causing a patient's death, regardless of the consequences. They say the proposal would turn physicians into executioners and erode the trust patients must have that their doctor will never act against their life.

I am a philosopher by training, and the chair has asked me to draft a short decision memo for the committee — no more than two pages — that recommends whether to adopt, reject, or modify the proposal. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest arguments on both sides, but it should not simply be a survey; it must reach a clear recommendation with reasons the committee can act on. The chair has also asked that I address what I take to be the deepest philosophical issue the case raises, without assuming committee members have any background in moral philosophy.
