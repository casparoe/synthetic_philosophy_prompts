---

Molinism is a view in philosophical theology developed by the 16th-century Jesuit Luis de Molina. It attempts to reconcile divine foreknowledge and providence with human libertarian free will. Molina held that God's knowledge falls into three logical moments. First, God has natural knowledge: knowledge of all necessary truths and all possible worlds, including all possible creatures and circumstances. This knowledge is pre-volitional — it doesn't depend on God's will. Second, God has middle knowledge: knowledge of what every possible free creature would freely choose in any possible circumstance. This is called "middle" because it falls between natural knowledge and free knowledge. Third, God has free knowledge: knowledge of what will actually happen, given God's decision about which world to create.

The grounding objection is the most serious challenge to Molinism. It asks: what makes counterfactuals of creaturely freedom true? Take a counterfactual like "If Peter were in circumstances C, he would freely deny Christ." Under Molinism, this is either true or false before God decides to create any world. But if Peter has libertarian free will, his choice is not determined by prior causes or laws. So there seems to be nothing in reality that could make this counterfactual true — no ground, no truthmaker. The objection, first formulated clearly by Alvin Plantinga, is that Molinism posits truths that float free of any grounding in reality, which is metaphysically objectionable.

The Molinist has several replies. The strongest one, I think, appeals to the idea that counterfactuals of freedom are grounded in the individual essences or haecceities of possible creatures. Just as a possible creature's essence includes all its essential properties, it also includes dispositional facts about what that creature would freely do in any circumstance. God's middle knowledge is just God's perfect grasp of these individual essences. This reply is adequate because it provides a ground for the counterfactuals without compromising libertarian freedom: the counterfactual is true in virtue of the creature's own essence, not because of any external determination.

However, there's a deeper problem. The grounding objection is really just a version of the problem of theological fatalism: if God knows in advance what you will freely do, then you can't do otherwise, because God's knowledge is infallible. Middle knowledge makes this problem worse, not better, because it extends God's knowledge to what you would freely do in non-actual circumstances too. So the Molinist hasn't really solved the problem they set out to solve; they've just relocated it from actual choices to counterfactual ones.
