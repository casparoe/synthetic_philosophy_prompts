I've been going back and forth on the common prior assumption in Bayesian epistemology and game theory, and I'd like someone to unpack it for me in a way that gets past the surface-level summaries.

The assumption, as I understand it, says that any two ideally rational agents who share the same information should have the same beliefs — or, in the stronger form that underpins Aumann's agreement theorem, that rational agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree once they have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities. The idea is that all differences in belief should be traceable to differences in private information, not to differences in starting points. That picture has a lot of intuitive appeal: it suggests that if you and I disagree about something after talking it through, at least one of us must have made a mistake, and the only respectable source of persistent divergence is asymmetric access to evidence.

But here's what bothers me. The common prior assumption seems to be either trivially true (if you define rationality as including a specific uniquely rational prior, you've just stipulated the conclusion and the interesting question is which prior that is) or clearly false (if you grant that different agents can have different inductive biases, different cognitive architectures, or different ways of carving up the hypothesis space, none of which seems obviously irrational — a Solomonoff prior is not the same thing as a finite agent's feasible starting point). And yet the assumption is treated as foundational in a lot of economic theory. It's built into the Harsanyi doctrine, it's used to motivate the no-trade theorem and the idea that speculative trade shouldn't happen, and it shows up in arguments about peer disagreement in epistemology. People seem to be getting a lot of mileage out of something whose status is deeply unclear.

I'm not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature. What I want is a conceptual map. What are the strongest arguments that the common prior assumption is a genuine requirement of rationality, not just a modeling convenience? What are the strongest arguments that it's just a modeling convenience — or that it's actively false? And what does the answer imply for how we should actually update when we disagree with someone who seems roughly as smart and as well-informed as we are? If the common prior assumption is false, does that mean we should be more stubborn in our beliefs (since the other person's differing prior might just reflect a different but equally reasonable starting point), or does the argument from disagreement get reconstructed on some other basis?

If I'm confused about the whole framing — if the common prior assumption isn't really one thing but several distinct claims that get conflated, or if the real action is somewhere else entirely — tell me that too. I'd rather have the question reframed than get a clean answer to a messy question.
