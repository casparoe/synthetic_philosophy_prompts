I've been keeping notes on how a friend of mine—a software engineer who reads philosophy on the side—adjudicates the Meno problem across different cases. The question is whether knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief, and if so, why. Below are the verdicts I've collected from him (in each case, the question is whether you should prefer having the knowledge state over the corresponding merely-true-belief state, holding practical outcomes equal where they are equal):

1. Knowing the way to Larissa (you've walked it) vs. merely truly believing the way (a stranger told you, and you happened to be right): knowledge is more valuable.
2. Knowing a mushroom is safe because you're an expert who can identify the species vs. truly believing it's safe because you like its shape and luck into being correct: knowledge is more valuable.
3. Truly believing your flight is delayed because you're generally cynical about airlines vs. knowing it's delayed because the airline sent you a status alert: knowledge is more valuable.
4. A veteran chicken-sexer who correctly sorts chicks by sex at near-perfect accuracy but cannot articulate how vs. a trainee who applies a written decision tree and gets the same results: neither state is more valuable; they're on par.
5. Truly believing a mathematical theorem because a trustworthy colleague told you it holds vs. knowing it because you worked through the proof yourself: knowledge is more valuable.

Is the engineer's knowledge more valuable than the captain's true belief, or are they on par?
