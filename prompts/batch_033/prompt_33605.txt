I'm in a discussion group that meets every couple of weeks to talk about moral psychology and responsibility — we read a mix of news stories, legal cases, and the occasional philosophy article, and then argue about who deserves blame. One of the regulars, a woman named Elena, isn't an academic and doesn't use any theoretical vocabulary, but she has unusually consistent instincts about when it's appropriate to resent someone or hold them responsible. Over the past year I've kept a mental list of her verdicts, and they've been remarkably stable across quite different cases:

- A driver has an unforeseeable epileptic seizure at the wheel and causes a fatal accident. Elena says resentment is not appropriate — it was a medical event, not an action.
- A neurotypical adult embezzles money from a charity over several months. Elena says full blame is appropriate; this is a paradigm case of moral failure.
- A five-year-old lies about breaking a neighbor's window. Elena says it's not appropriate to resent the child; they're still developing the relevant understanding.
- A person with schizophrenia, during a psychotic episode, attacks a stranger they believe to be pursuing them. Elena says blame is not appropriate; the psychosis makes it impossible to see the situation for what it is.
- A college student voluntarily gets extremely drunk, then vandalizes a storefront. Elena says the student is blameworthy — they chose to impair their own judgment.
- A person develops a brain tumor that causes dramatic personality changes, including uncharacteristic aggression toward their spouse. Elena says the spouse should not resent the person; the tumor altered who they were.
- A person with a severe intellectual disability takes merchandise from a store without paying, understanding it's against the rules but not fully grasping why it's wrong. Elena says resentment is not appropriate; the moral dimension of the situation isn't accessible to them in the relevant way.
- Someone commits a minor crime under a credible threat of death. Elena says they are somewhat blameworthy but the duress is a strong mitigating factor — they're not off the hook, but the blame is substantially reduced.

Last week we read about a criminal case that has been bothering me ever since. A man diagnosed with antisocial personality disorder — what used to be called psychopathy — defrauded several elderly people of their life savings. He had a stable upbringing with no abuse. By all clinical accounts, he has never experienced empathy, guilt, or remorse, and the condition is understood to have strong neurological and genetic underpinnings. He understands right from wrong in an intellectual sense: he knows what the rules are, he knows other people consider his actions wrong, and he can follow rules when it serves his purposes. But he does not feel the wrongness of what he does. He had full behavioral control — he could have chosen not to run the scheme — but he had no internal emotional brake. Elena hasn't weighed in on this case yet, and I want to figure out what she'd say if she were being consistent with everything she's already committed to. The problem is I can see at least two or three different principles that would fit her earlier verdicts, and they pull in different directions here.

Please approach this as a careful moral reasoner trying to reconstruct someone's implicit rule from their case judgments. Start by brainstorming candidate principles that could generate Elena's pattern, then narrow them down. Structure your response in these sections:

1. **The pattern in the precedents** (roughly 150 words): Summarize the consistent thread in Elena's verdicts and note where the ambiguity lies — what the cases so far leave unsettled about the boundary between those we can properly resent and those we cannot.

2. **Candidate principles** (roughly 250 words): Articulate two or three distinct principles that are each consistent with all of Elena's earlier verdicts. Give each principle a clear, non-technical formulation and explain how it handles the earlier cases.

3. **The new case under each principle** (roughly 200 words): Apply each candidate principle to the psychopathy case and state what verdict each would recommend. Highlight exactly where and why they diverge.

4. **Best prediction** (roughly 150 words): Give your best guess about what Elena would say, with the reasoning that supports preferring one principle over the others given her particular pattern of verdicts and anything you can glean about her reasoning style from the cases.

5. **The tiebreaker case** (roughly 100 words): Describe a further case — not yet discussed — that would cleanly distinguish the remaining candidate principles, and explain what you would learn about Elena's rule from her verdict on it.
