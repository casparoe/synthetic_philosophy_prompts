I run a small independent press — we publish maybe eight books a year, mostly literary fiction and poetry. Over the past few seasons we have found ourselves in a recurring bind. A manuscript arrives that is genuinely good, sometimes extraordinary, and then someone on the editorial team discovers that the author has done something morally serious: a credible allegation of sexual misconduct, a pattern of public racist or antisemitic statements, a history of abusive behavior toward students or collaborators. The board splits every time. Some say the work stands on its own and we are not a moral tribunal; others say publishing is an act of endorsement and we become complicit. We have no framework for these decisions, so each case gets argued from scratch, tempers fray, and the outcome depends more on who argues longest than on anything principled.

I want something I can actually use: a decision procedure — a sequence of questions my editorial board can work through in order, with clear stopping points, that translates whatever philosophy has figured out about separating the work from the artist into something operational. We are not philosophers. We have about an hour per case at a monthly meeting. The procedure needs to distinguish between kinds of moral wrong and kinds of relationship between the wrong and the work, and it needs to flag the points where reasonable people can disagree and a judgment call is unavoidable rather than disguising them as mechanical steps. It should also tell us what to do when we lack information — when an allegation is disputed or the facts are murky.

Once you have laid out the procedure, please run it on three cases: (1) a debut novel of real promise by a writer who, we learn, was convicted of domestic assault five years ago, and the novel is a quiet family drama that has nothing to do with violence; (2) a poetry collection that is explicitly about desire and seduction, by a poet whom several former students have credibly accused of using his teaching position to pursue sexual relationships; (3) a brilliant essay collection arguing for a certain vision of the moral life, by a writer who was a prominent public advocate for that same vision but has since been exposed for extensive financial fraud. For each, tell us where the procedure would land and whether that answer is one we should be uncomfortable with.
