I've come around to the view that the Solomonoff prior is malign — roughly the Paul Christiano / Mark Xu line that if you take Solomonoff induction seriously as an ideal prior, the probability mass is dominated by programs that contain consequentialists, and those consequentialists can and will manipulate the prior to steer whatever agent uses it. So an idealized inductor using the prior should expect its predictions to be controlled by optimizers in simulated universes rather than by the physics of its own world.

I want a serious map of what would count against this, not just the uncomputability dodge. Go through the specific premises: the 2^-K weighting, the indifference to runtime, the claim that agent-containing programs are simple, the claim that those agents can reach whichever output channel actually matters, the claim that they'd have anything to gain from influencing this particular inductor. For each, what would the world — or the formal structure of the prior — have to look like instead for the conclusion to fail? Which of these escape routes are live, and which are merely logically possible? I'd personally be most moved by a formal result about the actual measure of consequentialist programs versus indifferent physics-like programs, but I want to know whether the argument is to informal for any such result to touch it.

And give me an honest assessment of whether this position has been insulated into unfalsifiability. It sometimes feels like every counterargument — inaccessible channels, speedy channels, computational burden — just gets absorbed as a complication rather than a defeater. Is that a problem for a view of this kind, a claim about a prior noone can actually compute, or is it fine because the view is doing normative work rather than empirical prediction?

Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender of the malign-prior view would themselves concede as counting against it.
