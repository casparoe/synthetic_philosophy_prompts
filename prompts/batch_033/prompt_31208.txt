I keep returning to the socialist calculation debate — Mises and Hayek on one side, Lange and Lerner on the other — about whether a centrally planned economy could allocate resources rationally. I can't tell whether it's a live technical question or a disagreement that was never really about what it claimed to be about.

Mises's side argued that without markets in the means of production there are no prices for capital goods, and without those prices there is no way to compare the costs of alternative uses of a resource, so rational allocation is impossible in principle, not just difficult. Hayek shifted the emphasis from prices as such to the underlying information problem: the relevant knowledge is dispersed across millions of people and bound up with local conditions and particular moments, and the price system is a mechanism for compressing and transmitting exactly that information. A central planner, on this view, cannot collect what it would need, because much of it never exists in a collectible form.

Lange's side replied that a planning board does not need to collect all of that information. It can set prices parametrically, watch for shortages and surpluses, raise prices where demand exceeds supply and lower them where supply exceeds demand, and iterate. In principle this trial-and-error process converges on the same allocation a competitive market would reach. On this picture the planner is essentially running an optimization procedure from the center, and the only genuine obstacle is computational cost, which falls over time.

The debate has now run for roughly a century and it seems stuck. The Mises-Hayek side keeps saying the Lange side has assumed away the very thing at issue; the Lange side keeps saying the Mises-Hayek side has mistaken a practical difficulty for an impossibility. The core arguments have barely moved, and I mostly see people re-citing them rather than extending them.

Coming at this as a software engineer rather than a philosopher, my working hypothesis is that several different disagreements are sharing one name. There is (a) an empirical question about whether any actual planning process, with realistic information and incentives, could track a market allocation closely enough; (b) a conceptual question about what counts as solving the allocation problem — whether prices that exist only inside a planner's model count as prices, and whether convergence in an idealized model counts as feasibility; (c) a question about incentives and authority that neither side's formal model addresses head-on; and (d) a normative disagreement about markets, property, and coercion that the technical language may be partly obscuring. I am not confident this decomposition is right, and I cannot tell which layer, if any, is doing the real work.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on planning or markets. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is it substantive but empirical, substantive but conceptual, verbal, or a mix — and if a mix, which strands are which? Is there a formal result in computation, mechanism design, or information economics that would settle or dissolve any one strand? And what would progress look like, or is the debate structured so that no progress should be expected?
