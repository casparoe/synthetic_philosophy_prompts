I'm a physics PhD student, and I've been trying to get clear on something I keep noticing in how physicists talk about the wavefunction, but I lack the philosophical vocabulary to nail it down. I'm hoping someone with expertise in philosophy of physics can help me map this terrain.

Here's what I observe. In my quantum mechanics course, the instructor treats the wavefunction as a calculational instrument: you take its squared modulus, you get a probability density for measurement outcomes, end of story. The wavefunction isn't a "thing" — it encodes what we can predict. When pressed on what's happening between measurements, the answer is some version of "don't ask." Meanwhile, in my condensed matter group, people routinely talk about the wavefunction's topology, its phase structure, its behaviour under symmetry operations — as if it's a real field with genuine physical features that explain conductance and edge states. And in a foundations reading group, the split is even sharper: some members insist the wavefunction is a literal physical entity evolving in configuration space, while others call that a category error and say the wavefunction is nothing but a compact summary of an agent's information.

When I try to sort people into camps, I get something like this. On one side, the wavefunction corresponds to something in the world — a field, a nomological structure, a genuinely existing thing whose properties do explanatory work. On the other side, the wavefunction is epistemic through and through: a credal state, a representation of knowledge, something that lives on the subject side of the subject-world divide. But the more I listen, the more I notice that people don't stay neatly in these boxes. Some say the wavefunction is "real" but only in the sense that a probability distribution is real — it's a real feature of an agent's epistemic situation, not a fiction. Some say it's a law rather than a substance, which seems to cut across the real-versus-epistemic axis entirely. And some say the whole question presupposes a picture of representation that quantum mechanics itself undermines.

So my question is: does this fault line — roughly, wavefunction-as-worldly versus wavefunction-as-epistemic — have an established name and a systematic treatment in the philosophy of physics? I'm not after a one-word label. I want to know whether the distinction as I've described it holds up, what the strongest versions of each position are, whether there are intermediate or orthogonal positions that the simple binary misses, and whether the question itself might be badly framed in ways the literature has diagnosed. If there is an established distinction, how precisely is it drawn, and does it match what I'm gesturing at or only overlap with it? If there isn't, or if the existing terms don't quite capture the thing I'm seeing, what would you call it and how would you define its boundaries?

Please take your time with this — start by disentangling the different things someone might mean when they call the wavefunction "real" or "epistemic," and then work toward the cleanest possible carving of the conceptual space.
