I am preparing to interview Simone Weil for a radio program devoted to her political thought, and I want the conversation to center on her wartime essay calling for the general suppression of political parties. I know the broad shape of the position: a political party is, by its nature, an organization oriented toward the conquest and exercise of power; it generates and mobilizes collective passion rather than independent judgment; it exerts collective pressure on the thinking of its members; and this holds even for parties with democratic or progressive programs. On this basis she concludes that parties should be abolished by law, with political life reorganized around temporary, freely formed associations that do not seek to capture the state.

Draft nine or ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate the view. Press where the position is actually strained, and give, for each question, the follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give given her own texts. I want at least one question on each of the following, though the order and grouping are yours: (a) how her characterization of a party is meant to distinguish parties from other durable political associations, such as trade unions or revolutionary committees, and whether her criteria would also condemn associations she would want to preserve; (b) her insistence that thought belongs to individuals and not to collectivities, given that the associations she proposes are themselves collectivities — is the difference one of kind or of degree; (c) whether a legal prohibition on parties is not itself in the form of a party program, and how it escapes the very structure it condemns; (d) whether the totalitarian tendency she attributes to parties is a necessary feature of them or an empirically corrigible one, and what would show a party to be an exception; (e) what happens to the functions parties actually perform — organizing conflict, sustaining coordinated action over time, protecting individuals against the state — once parties are suppressed, and whether her alternative would simply hand power to informal or bureaucratic forms of coordination; and (f) how the suppression of parties fits with the rest of her thought, especially her ideas about attention, obligation, and the danger of idolizing the social.

Do not give me a menu of possible questions. Commit to a definite set of nine or ten, in the order in which you would ask them, and defend that order: say why each question comes where it does, which reply it is designed to draw out or to cut off, and where you expect her to deflect. Where a question is one her texts can answer, say so; where her texts leave it underdetermined, say what you think the most likely answer is anyway and why the question is still worth asking.
