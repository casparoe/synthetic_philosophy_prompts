In an interdisciplinary working group on reduction and emergence, one member habitually ends disputes by announcing that "the whole is more than the sum of its parts." Last month he used it to dismiss the claim that an ant colony's collective behavior is fully determined by the behaviors of the individual ants and their interactions; this week he used it to dismiss the claim that the temperature of a gas is nothing over and above the mean kinetic energy of its molecules. The two dismissals feel different in strength, and I suspect the slogan shifts its content to suit the target. I want a disambiguation rather than a survey of the literature. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be used to express, ordered from the trivially true to the metaphysically radical — for example, non-additivity, practical unpredictability, in-principle non-deducibility, irreducibility of higher-level laws, novel causal powers, downward causation, and any further readings you think are in play. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or false. Then apply the menu to the two cases above: which readings would actually license each dismissal, which would be too weak to do the job, and which would overshoot into claims the slogan-wielder probably does not want to defend — and say which reading he is most likely tacitly relying on in each case.
