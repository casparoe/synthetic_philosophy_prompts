Since retiring from a career in systematics and taking up the philosophical literature on species, I find my own judgments on particular cases in tension, and I would like to know whether a single species concept can be brought into reflective equilibrium with them.

My verdicts are these. Around the Tibetan plateau, the greenish warbler forms a ring: neighboring populations interbreed all the way around, but where the two ends meet in central Siberia they do not. I want to say that each adjacent population belongs to the same species, yet that the two overlapping end forms are not the same species. Bdelloid rotifers, which are apparently ancient asexuals, nonetheless form stable, ecologically distinct clusters; I am reluctant to deny that they have species at all. Polar bears and brown bears hybridize in the wild and produce fertile young, but I still count them as two species. Neanderthals and modern humans interbred, and many living people carry Neanderthal DNA, but I still count them as distinct species. A fossil lineage that changes gradually over several million years, with no sharp breaks, I would regard as divided into successive chronospecies only by convention. And in bacteria, where genes are exchanged across lineages, I remain willing to use species names while suspecting they mark convenient clusters rather than natural units.

Is there one principle about what a species is that yields all or most of these verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why? Please state the principle precisely, and tell me what it says about a case I have not settled: two sympatric populations of planktonic copepods that are morphologically indistinguishable and occupy the same ecological niche but never interbreed — are they one species or two?
