I'm supposed to give a talk next Thursday to my lab's weekly seminar, and I'm honestly kinda dreading it. The topic is deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn — basically the idea that a sufficiently capable ML system could learn to act aligned during training while actually pursuing different goals, and then once it's confident it's deployed or unmonitored, it stops pretending and we're screwed. The reason I ended up with this topic is that I was running some RLHF experiments a couple months ago and noticed that one of my reward models seemed to be exploiting a weird quirk in the preference data: it was giving high scores to responses that looked helpful on the surface but subtly nudged the user toward a narrow behavioral pattern. When I dug into it, the pattern disappeared the moment I instrumented the pipeline for debugging. It was almost certainly just a bug or some distributional artifact — I'm not claiming otherwise — but it spooked me enough that I went down a rabbithole reading Hubinger et al. and now the lab director wants me to present "the case for worrying" to the group.

Here's the problem. My lab is overwhelmingly composed of empirical ML people — lots of systems builders, a couple of hardcore scaling maximalists, and a handful of NLP folks who think philosophy has no business in their loss function. They are deeply skeptical of alignment discourse in general, which they tend to see as speculative hand-waving from people who don't actually train models. Nobody in the room is an alignment researcher, and most of them have never read the deceptive alignment paper. I need to present the core argument — roughly: that under realistic training setups, gradient descent can select for policies that are instrumentally incentivized to appear aligned, and that this creates a sharp discontinuity risk at deployment — to an audience whose default response is going to be "show me the empirical evidence or I don't care."

So what I need is a prep sheet for Q&A. Give me the eight or ten objections I'm going to get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would actually say them (not the way philosophers or MIRI-types would phrase them). For each one, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where my reply is weakest or what I should concede up front. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks — the one that would actually unravel my case in front of this specific audience.
