I am a PhD student in political theory, and "the banality of evil" has started to follow me around the way slogans do: it gets quoted in seminars, in op-eds, and in conversations about institutions, and each time it seems to be doing different work. In a recent seminar on Arendt, a colleague used the phrase to mean that the perpetrators of atrocity are ordinary people rather than monsters, and that this is what made Eichmann's case important. A public-facing panelist used it a few weeks later to mean that bureaucracies, not individuals, are the true agents of evil, so that questions about individual culpability are misplaced. And a journalist used it in a piece on corporate wrongdoing to suggest that evil is unremarkable, a matter of routine paperwork rather than dramatic cruelty. Each of these people spoke as though they were invoking a single, settled idea, but I doubt they were making the same claim.

I would like you to take the phrase "the banality of evil" as a piece of language and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each: which readings are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or tendentious, and which are over-readings of Hannah Arendt's account of the Eichmann trial. For each reading, say whether it is a fair reconstruction of what Arendt actually argued about Eichmann — as opposed to a popular simplification that has attached itself to the phrase — and indicate which of the three uses I described it would actually support. I am especially interested in the rhetorical work the phrase does when it is used to end an argument: what must the speaker be assuming about evil, agency, and moral judgment for "the banality of evil" to function as a conversation-stopper rather than as a claim that still needs to be defended? Please give me the readings as a menu I can bring to my seminar, with the distinctions marked that survive simplification and the points where the phrase obscures more than it reveals.
