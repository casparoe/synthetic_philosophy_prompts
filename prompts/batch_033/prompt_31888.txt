So I'm in this reading group that's been working through Mill's On Liberty, and last week we got into a whole thing about campus speech. Someone — the guy who always has the confident takes — dropped "your right to swing your fist ends where my nose begins" and sat back like he'd won the argument. The context was whether a student group should be allowed to invite a speaker who says genuinely vile things about trans people, and his line was being used to say: yes, free speech is absolute until it physically harms someone.

But the more I've chewed on it, the more I think that one sentence is smuggling in about five different claims under the cover of folksy wisdom. When someone says it in a free speech debate, are they saying harm means only physical harm? Are they saying the "nose" is individual and not collective? Are they channeling Mill, or are they actually saying something Mill explicitly rejected? And what about the fact that the original image is about *fists*, not *words* — does the whole analogy beg the question against speech being capable of harm in the first place?

I want to go back to the group with something sharper than "I don't think that's right." So: lay out the different precise claims that "your right to swing your fist ends where my nose begins" could be making, from the ones that are basically truisms to the ones that are genuinely controversial. For each one, say whether it's something Mill would actually endorse, whether it's defensible on its own terms, and — crucially — which of these readings would actually carry the argument my interlocutor was trying to make about deplatforming. I'm especially interested in any reading where the slogan looks persuasive but actually collapses under its own weight once you try to state it clearly.
