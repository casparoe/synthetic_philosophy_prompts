Write the joint statement that a committed Harsanyi-style utilitarian and a committed non-utilitarian critic of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem (a prioritarian or egalitarian, for example) could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. For the purpose of the document, take Harsanyi's theorem in its standard form: if every individual's preferences and the social preference relation satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern expected-utility axioms, and the social preference satisfies Pareto indifference, then the social utility function is an affine function of the individual utility functions (a weighted sum, up to a positive affine transformation), with all weights positive if a strong Pareto condition is added. The Harsanyi-style utilitarian holds that this shows a rational, Pareto-respecting social evaluator must be a weighted utilitarian. The critic denies that it shows any such thing, holding either that the social-rationality axioms are not normatively neutral, or that interpersonal comparisons and the weights are presupposed rather than derived, or both.

The document should have two parts.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These should include: exactly what the theorem assumes and proves; what the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation theorem itself gives and does not give; what Pareto indifference adds and what it leaves open; and where the theorem is silent — in particular about the interpersonal comparability of the individual utilities, about how the weights are determined, and about whether the social preference relation is the sort of thing that should be required to satisfy the expected-utility axioms. Each claim should be precise enough for a decision theorist, and should state in plain language what philosophical work it does.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give: the issue; the claim each side would sign, phrased in that side's own vocabulary and not as a caricature; the crux that separates them; and a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction of what it shows. Include at least these cruxes: (i) whether the independence axiom is a requirement of rationality for a social evaluator choosing among lotteries over distributions, or only a consistency condition for a single person's preferences; and (ii) whether the theorem's weights, or the impartial-observer construction used to interpret them, presuppose interpersonal comparisons of utility rather than derive them. For the first crux, include a case with the following shape: a social evaluator is indifferent between two people receiving a benefit, but strictly prefers a fair lottery that gives the benefit to one of them over a policy that gives it to one of them for certain. Record how each side would treat that case.

End with a short note on which residual disagreements, if any, are verbal rather than substantive, and which ones would actually move if the cruxes were settled.
