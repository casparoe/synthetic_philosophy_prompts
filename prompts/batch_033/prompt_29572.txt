For a graduate seminar paper in philosophy of biology, I want a comparison of two notions of genetic information, not a survey of the literature. On the first, causal notion, a gene carries information about a trait because the sequence and the trait covary under suitable conditions, much as tree rings carry information about a tree's age. On the second, semantic or teleosemantic notion, a gene carries information the way a belief or an instruction does: it has content, can be right or wrong, and can misrepresent. I want the comparison pushed through two stress tests rather than left at definitions. First, the arbitrariness of the genetic code: the codon-to-amino-acid mapping could have been otherwise, and defenders of the semantic reading often take that arbitrariness to be decisive; does the causal reading actually fail here, or can it absorb arbitrariness as a contingent covariance? Second, misrepresentation: if a mutation causes a gene to produce a protein that fails to fold, has the gene misrepresented its product, or is this merely a new causal fact with no correctness conditions? The paper should conclude by saying which notion, if either, licenses the claim that DNA codes for traits, and what is being projected onto the molecule when the claim is read semantically. If you think the two notions are not genuine rivals, or that I have chosen the wrong stress tests, say so plainly and reframe the task rather than forcing the comparison.
