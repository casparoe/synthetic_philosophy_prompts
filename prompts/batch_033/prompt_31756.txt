I'm preparing a lecture on the interpretations of probability for my philosophy of science course, and I keep running up against a difficulty with the subjective Bayesian view. On that interpretation, probability is a measure of an agent's degree of belief, and the only rational constraint is that one's credences obey the probability axioms. But if that is right, then any coherent set of priors is permissible, and two agents faced with the same evidence can assign wildly different probabilities to the same hypothesis without either of them making a mistake. Doesn't this mean that the subjective interpretation cannot distinguish between beliefs that are well-grounded in evidence and those that are merely internally consistent, and therefore cannot explain why we ought to revise our beliefs in response to data rather than simply choosing priors that insulate our pet theories from refutation?
