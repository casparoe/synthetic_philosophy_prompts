**Question:** Explain the paradox of phenomenal judgment as it arises for epiphenomenalism about consciousness. Describe at least one response available to the epiphenomenalist, and evaluate whether that response succeeds.

**Student answer:**

The paradox of phenomenal judgment is a challenge to epiphenomenalism, which is the view that phenomenal consciousness is caused by physical processes in the brain but exerts no causal influence of its own. The paradox asks: if consciousness has no causal powers, how can we form judgments about our own conscious experiences? Our judgments about consciousness seem to be about something — they refer to our phenomenal states — but if those states are causally inert, they cannot be what causes us to form those judgments. This makes it mysterious why our judgments about consciousness should be accurate at all.

One prominent response is the common-cause or "correlation" reply. According to this response, both our conscious experiences and our judgments about them are caused by the same underlying neural processes. The neural processes carry information, and when they cause judgments, those judgments end up tracking the very conscious states that the same neural processes also produce. There is no need for consciousness itself to cause anything; the reliability of our phenomenal judgments is secured by their sharing a common physical cause with the experiences they are about.

This response, however, faces a serious problem. It relies on treating consciousness and neural processes as distinct relata that happen to be correlated, but epiphenomenalism actually identifies consciousness with physical processes — it is a form of physicalism. If consciousness just is a physical process, then the common-cause model collapses: there are not two things being correlated, but one thing described in two ways. The response therefore misconstrues what epiphenomenalism claims. A better approach is to accept that our phenomenal judgments are caused directly by physical processes and to note that, because these are the very same processes that constitute our conscious experience, the judgments are reliably about their own physical causes. The paradox thus dissolves once we abandon the dualist framing that made it seem puzzling in the first place.
