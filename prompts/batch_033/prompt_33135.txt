The question I set:

"In the iterated prisoner's dilemma, why does a strategy like tit-for-tat do well, and what does that show about the conditions under which cooperation can evolve? Be precise about what 'doing well' means, and about what can and cannot invade a cooperative population."

The student's answer (upper-level undergrad, untimed):

"In the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defection strictly dominates cooperation, so rational players defect and cooperation can't get going. When the game is repeated indefinitely, conditional strategies can sustain cooperation. Tit-for-tat — cooperate on the first move, then copy whatever the other player did last round — won Axelrod's computer tournaments. It does well because it's nice (it never defects first), retaliatory (it punishes defection immediately), and forgiving (it returns to cooperating as soon as the other player does). Because it's forgiving, two tit-for-tat players that suffer an accidental defection get back to mutual cooperation quickly. Tit-for-tat is also an evolutionarily stable strategy: a population of tit-for-tat players cannot be invaded by unconditional defectors, because a defector gets punished in the next round and ends up with a lower average payoff. This is taken to show that cooperation can evolve without kin selection or group selection, at least in principle. One limitation is that in a game repeated a known finite number of times, backward induction unravels cooperation, so tit-for-tat only works when the horizon is indefinite."
