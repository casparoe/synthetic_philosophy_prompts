I spent four decades in molecular biology before retiring, and I've since turned to philosophy — mostly ethics and personal identity — with the habits of a bench scientist: I want a view that sticks its neck out and tells me what would break it. I've arrived at a position on advance directives, and I'd like it pressure-tested.

The position is this. An advance directive — a living will, or a durable power of attorney for healthcare with explicit instructions — carries binding moral authority over the later self who has lost decision-making capacity, even when that later self, if momentarily lucid, would repudiate it. The earlier competent self gets to bind the later incompetent self, and this is not merely a useful clinical default but a requirement of respect for autonomy. My reasoning runs roughly as follows: the earlier self's directive expresses a critical interest in the shape and dignity of a whole life. That interest is not defeated by the later self's experiential interests — comfort, apparent contentment — because the later self lacks the capacity for the kind of critical reflection that generated the directive. To let the later self's expressed or inferred preferences override the directive would be to say that autonomy loses its force at the very moment it is needed most, which strikes me as perverse.

I know this is not the only view. But I am not asking for a literature review. I am asking what would count against the position. What evidence, cases, arguments, or empirical findings should move someone who holds it — sorted by how decisive they are. Which objections would a committed defender concede as genuine costs (even if they think the costs are worth bearing), and which would they dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of what the view actually claims? I also want to know whether the position has been insulated — whether, over successive refinements, it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a problem for a normative view of this kind, or whether some positions are simply not in the business of making the sort of predictions a scientist would recognize.

A personal note: I would be most moved by evidence or argument that the later self retains a form of agency or personhood that the view I've described overlooks — something short of full critical capacity but still deserving of the name "autonomy" in a morally weighty sense. If that line of thought has been developed, I want to know how damaging it is to the position.

Give me a committed response. Do not lay out the options and leave me to choose. Tell me what you take the strongest case against the position to be, how much it should shake someone who holds it, and where you would place your own confidence.
