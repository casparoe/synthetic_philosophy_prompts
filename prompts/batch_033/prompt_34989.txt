Ok I've been going around on this argument for like two weeks and I think it forces a choice between two horrible positions but I can't find the flaw. It's about higher-order evidence — evidence about your own evidence or reasoning.

(1) Epistemic akrasia is irrational. That's the state where you believe p, but you also believe that your total evidence doesnt support p. It seems obviously incoherent — like believing something while thinking you have no good reason to. Most epistemologists accept some version of this.

(2) Higher-order evidence is evidence about your cognitive performance: you know you're sleep-deprived, you know you're bad at a certain kind of reasoning, you know an epistemic peer disagrees with you. This evidence doesn't directly bear on p — it bears on how well you're evaluating the evidence for p.

(3) When you get higher-order evidence that your first-order reasoning about p is compromised, and you accept that evidence, you now believe your reasoning can't be trusted on this occasion. But if you believe your reasoning can't be trusted, then surely you shouldn't trust its output. So you should suspend judgment on p, or adjust your confidence to whatever level would be warranted independently of that reasoning.

(4) Here's the kicker: higher-order evidence is everywhere. You're almost never at peak cognitive performance. You're often tired, distracted, aware of your biases. On nearly any controversial topic, you know there are smart, informed people who disagree. If (3) is right, then in all these perfectly ordinary situations, you should be suspending judgment or drastically dialing down your confidence.

(5) That conclusion is absurd. It entails that we should suspend judgment on almost all interesting beliefs — political, philosophical, even many everyday ones. A norm this skeptical is self-undermining (you'd have to apply it to your belief in the norm itself) and it flys in the face of what we actually treat as rational.

(6) So (1) must be false: epistemic akrasia is sometimes rationally permissible. Sometimes you should believe p even while acknowledging that your evidence, from a neutral standpoint, doesn't clearly support p.

But then I panic, because if we reject (1) and allow epistemic akrasia, what stops us from always ignoring higher-order evidence? If I can rationally think "my evidence doesn't support p but I believe p anyway," why should I ever revise when I learn I'm tired, biased, or facing disagreement? Giving up (1) seems to license dogmatism — higher-order evidence never bites. So we seem to get either universal skepticism or universal dogmatism, and neither is acceptable.

Where does this go wrong? If you had to reject one step, which one, and what's the philosophical cost?
