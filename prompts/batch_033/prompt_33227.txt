My colleague is a curator with firm and consistent-sounding views about authenticity and the value of originals, and I want to know what she would say about a new case. Her past verdicts, given over several years:

(1) A modern copy of a famous Renaissance painting, made by an expert copyist and visually indistinguishable from the original even to trained eyes: she calls it almost worthless as art, though useful for teaching and documentation.

(2) A seventeenth-century studio painting, composed and supervised by the master, who painted the faces and hands himself while assistants executed the rest, signed with his approval: she values it well below a fully autograph work but far above the modern copy in (1).

(3) A forgery painted in the 1920s in the style of a seventeenth-century master, which hung in a museum for decades, was praised by serious critics, and moved ordinary viewers; after its exposure she says its value as art collapsed, though she still finds it interesting as a document of its own period.

(4) A small, spontaneous autograph sketch by a great painter — not especially beautiful, but securely documented and full of visible decision-making: she values it highly, more than the technically polished studio piece in (2).

(5) A meticulous hand-painted replica of one of the great painter's own works, made during the painter's lifetime by a studio assistant using the painter's materials, and intended for sale as a copy: she values it below any autograph work but above a modern photographic reproduction.

The new case: a celebrated modern painter, late in her career, produces a faithful replica of her own most famous early masterpiece — same size, same composition, produced largely by tracing and mechanical transfer, with no new compositional or painterly decisions — and signs it as a replica made by her own hand.

If my colleague is to remain consistent with herself, what should she say about this object, and which of her past cases is the closest analog? The bare prediction matters less to me than the principle she is implicitly applying — origin and causal history, embodied creative achievement, expression or agency, perceptible properties, or something else — along with the rival principles that fit her past verdicts equally well, what each predicts about the autograph replica, your best guess about her verdict and why, and the single further case whose verdict would most cleanly settle which principle she actually holds.

Please end with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...}, where the braces contain only a short phrase giving your predicted verdict for the new case (for example, \answer{high autograph value} or \answer{low value as a mere replica}).
