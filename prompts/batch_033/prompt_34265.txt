I recently sat in on a metaethics seminar where two graduate students got into a long argument about the Frege–Geach problem, and I've been turning it over in my head ever since. I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange.

The first student, Elena, opened by saying that the Frege–Geach problem remains fatal to expressivism, and that the last forty years of responses have been a series of increasingly elaborate epicycles. Her core claim: expressivism says moral judgments like "lying is wrong" express non-cognitive attitudes — disapproval, say — rather than beliefs. But in an unasserted context like the antecedent of "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong," the speaker does not express disapproval of lying. So the meaning of "lying is wrong" differs between the two occurrences, which means the argument form "lying is wrong; if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong; therefore, getting your brother to lie is wrong" is equivocal — and modus ponens fails. Since competent moral reasoners plainly do make valid inferences of this form, expressivism is false.

The second student, David, replied that this is the problem statement, not a refutation. Blackburn's solution, he said, is that the conditional as a whole expresses a single higher-order attitude: disapproval of simultaneously disapproving of lying while failing to disapprove of getting your brother to lie. The attitude expressed by "lying is wrong" is the same in both contexts; what changes is the role it plays in the larger attitude expressed by the conditional. So there is no equivocation, and the inference is valid because anyone who holds the attitudes expressed by the premises but fails to hold the attitude expressed by the conclusion is in a state of attitudinal inconsistency — which is the expressivist's analogue of logical contradiction.

Elena answered that this just relocates the problem. Attitudinal inconsistency is not the same as logical inconsistency, she said, and the expressivist owes us an account of why the particular inconsistency Blackburn identifies counts as a logical mistake rather than a mere psychological clash. She then said that Gibbard's norm-expressivism fares no better: Gibbard treats moral judgments as expressing acceptance of systems of norms, but once you've built enough structure into your "systems of norms" to support genuine logical inference, you've effectively reintroduced representational content, and the view collapses into a kind of descriptivism.

David pushed back: that collapse argument only works against pure expressivism, and pure expressivism is a strawman. The interesting views, he said, are hybrid: they grant that moral judgments have both a belief-like component and an expressive component. The Frege–Geach problem is fatal only if you insist that moral language does nothing but express attitudes. Once you admit that it also represents, the logical behavior of moral terms in embedded contexts is handled by the representational component, and the expressive component does its work elsewhere — in accounting for motivation, for instance. The Frege–Geach problem, on this view, rules out pure expressivism but leaves hybrid views untouched.

Elena replied that this is a Pyrrhic victory. If the representational component handles all the logical work, then the expressive component is idle with respect to inference, and the hybrid expressivist has no answer to the question: what exactly does the expressive component add that couldn't be captured by a purely representational account paired with a separate theory of moral motivation? She said the Frege–Geach problem doesn't kill hybrid views outright, but it forces them into a dilemma: either the expressive component contributes to the logic of moral discourse (in which case the original problem recurs), or it doesn't (in which case it's redundant).

At this point the seminar ended, and I never got to hear a resolution. I'd like you to go through their exchange move by move and tell me who is right about what, and whether either of them has the better overall case. I'm particularly interested in Elena's final dilemma for hybrid views: does it land, or is there a way for the hybrid expressivist to slip between its horns?
