Two engineers at my company have been debating whether a large language model we've been testing internally actually "understands" anything. The model recently solved a fairly involved scheduling problem — it produced a multi-step plan with constraints, trade-offs, and a justification for each choice that referenced specific requirements in the prompt. The output was genuinely useful, and nobody had given it a worked example of that exact kind of problem.

(1) David argued that none of this shows understanding. The model is trained to predict the next token, and what we're seeing is a statistical mimic of reasoning, not reasoning itself. The model has no beliefs about the scheduling constraints, no desires about the outcome — it's just pattern completion at scale. (2) Maya pushed back: if the output walks like reasoning and talks like reasoning, at what point do we stop calling it mimicry? She noted that the model handled a novel constraint combination that none of us had anticipated, and its justification was internally coherent. If a human junior engineer had produced the same output, nobody would hesitate to say they understood the problem. (3) David replied that this is exactly the mistake people made about biological complexity for centuries. An eye is an extraordinarily well-organized organ, and for a long time it seemed obvious that only an intelligent designer could have produced it. But we now know that a blind, mindless process — variation plus selection — can generate apparent design without any comprehension at all. He said the model is the same kind of thing: a mindless optimization process that produces outputs that look intelligent, and we're falling for the same illusion. (4) Maya said the analogy actually cuts the other way. Evolution has no internal model of the world — it doesn't represent anything. But the model does have internal representations: it encodes facts about the world, relationships between concepts, and it manipulates those representations when it generates text. That's not just blind variation and selection — it's something closer to what we do when we reason. (5) David said calling them "representations" and "manipulations" already assumes the conclusion. All we actually observe is a function from input tokens to output tokens. The internal activations are just high-dimensional vectors being transformed by matrix multiplications. There's no more "representation" there than in a pocket calculator's registers — and nobody thinks a calculator understands arithmetic. (6) Maya said the calculator comparison proves her point, not his. A calculator can't explain why it gave the answer it did, can't handle ambiguity, can't revise its approach when you point out a mistake, and can't transfer its "knowledge" to a different domain. The model does all of these things. If David wants to maintain that it's all just mimicry, he needs to say what would count as genuine understanding — and she suspects any criterion he gives will either be met by the model already or rule out humans too.

They've asked a few of us to weigh in, and I'm honestly not sure how to adjudicate this. Could you go through their exchange move by move and assess who has the better of each point, and what — if anything — both of them are missing?
