I'd like you to write a short story that explores the philosophical problem of advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. The core issue is this: if I, while competent, sign an advance directive refusing life-sustaining treatment in the event of severe dementia, and later I develop dementia and seem content — even happy — in a way my earlier self would have found undignified, which self gets the final say? The earlier self made the directive precisely to prevent the later self from overriding it, but the later self is the one who actually exists now, with preferences and pleasures that are real, not hypothetical.

A good story on this theme would not simply take sides. It would make the reader feel the force of both positions: the earlier self's horror at the prospect of a life she would not recognize as her own, and the later self's uncomplicated, present-tense desire to keep living. The philosophical puzzle is partly about personal identity — is the demented later self the same person as the earlier self in the relevant sense? — and partly about the normative question of whether a competent person's prior wishes should bind her later, incompetent self even when that later self has interests that diverge from those wishes.

The story should be self-contained, not a philosophical essay in disguise, but it should be clear to a reader familiar with the debate which ideas are being dramatized. After the story, please include a brief author's note (a paragraph or two) indicating where the story takes liberties with the philosophical positions, which contested reading of the problem it leans on, and what you are least confident about in the way you rendered the issue.
