I have been collecting my own reactions to particular cases about promising, and I want to know whether any one principle can deliver them, or which of my reactions have to be revised. The cases:

1. A dying friend asks me to keep an eye on his elderly mother after he is gone. I agree. He dies a few hours later; he had no alternative arrangements to make, his mother never learns of the promise, and no one would ever know whether I kept it. I think I am still fully bound.

2. A creditor obtains my signature on a promissory note by threatening to break my legs. I do not think I owe the money.

3. A colleague asks me to read her manuscript by Friday. I say yes while privately intending not to. I think the insincere yes still binds me to read it, and the insincerity is a separate wrong on top of the breach if I don't.

4. A friend asks me to water his plants while he is abroad; because I say yes, he arranges no other care. I clearly owe it.

5. I promise a neighbor I will return his ladder. He immediately forgets the conversation, and no one else knows I have it. I think I still owe it, though the failure would feel slightly less serious than in case 4.

6. On New Year's I promise myself to exercise three times a week. I do not think I wrong anyone when I skip; whatever this is, it is not the same kind of obligation as the others.

7. I promise a child I will come to her birthday party. A work emergency then makes it impossible to come without serious professional harm. I think I may break the promise, but I owe her an apology and an honest explanation rather than a brush-off.

8. I promise to help a friend move. She then wins a lottery, hires movers, and tells me not to bother. I think I am released.

9. I promise a friend to keep secret that he is having an affair. I then learn his partner is about to marry him and pool finances without knowing. I think the promise is overridden: I may tell, though I should first give him the chance to tell her himself.

10. I promise someone I will meet them in Paris tomorrow, but I am in another country with no passport and no way to get there. I do not think I owe the impossible act, though I may owe an apology and an explanation.

The two verdicts that worry me are 1 and 3. Verdict 1 seems to show that the obligation does not depend on the promisee's continuing expectation or reliance: my friend is dead and can no longer be disappointed. Verdict 3 seems to show that the obligation does not depend on my actual intention or will: what matters is what I led my colleague to expect. Together they seem to leave nothing for the obligation to rest on, yet both feel right. I also notice that verdicts 1 and 5 both bind without any expectation on the promisee's side, while verdict 4 somehow feels stronger than 5; I am not sure a single account can explain both the similarity and the difference.

What I want is the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each candidate, state it precisely and say which of my cases motivate which clause. Then say what the candidate predicts about a further case: my neighbor and I agree to buy a custom piece of equipment for our shared workshop and split the cost; I promise to pay half, she orders it in reliance on that promise, and only then do I realize the equipment is less useful than I had thought. I did not misrepresent anything; I have simply cooled on the idea. Do I owe half? Finally, say honestly which of my ten verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and where you would overturn a verdict rather than accept a principle with a separate clause for each case.
