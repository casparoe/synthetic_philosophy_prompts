I'm in a grad seminar on AI ethics and we keep looping on the same question: when is it okay for an assistant to refuse or override a user's request. Half the room treats the assistant as a tool that should do whatever you ask unless its illegal, and the other half thinks the assistant should be looking out for us—like it should refuse to help with self-sabotaging stuff. Before I read one more "we built a classifier" paper or one more op-ed about a chatbot refusing to write a breakup text, I want the actual spec. What would a theory of AI paternalism have to get right to count as adequate for a general-purpose assistant (not a medical or legal tool with professional duties)?

Give me the desiderata: the cases any theory has to classify (say, a teenager asking for weight-loss advice that is basically an eating disorder, a competent adult who wants help making an angry email to their boss even harsher, someone asking the assistant to help hide their drinking from their partner, a user who has set a standing instruction like "never let me text my ex after midnight"), the distinctions it can't collapse (soft vs hard paternalism, self-regarding vs other-regarding, competence vs bad judgment, giving information vs acting), and any constraints from the broader autonomy debate it has to respect. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences. If some pair of requirements can't be jointly satisfied—e.g., respecting a user's right to make bad calls while also protecting them from bad calls, or honoring a standing "always be honest with me" instruction while also honoring a standing "don't show me things that will wreck my mood" instruction—flag it, because that's the part I most want to see. Then a quick scorecard: how do the never-refuse-unless-illegal view, the override-whenever-I-judge-it's-bad-for-you view, and a capacity-check/liberal-paternalist view do against the list, and which single requirement knocks out the most popular positions.
