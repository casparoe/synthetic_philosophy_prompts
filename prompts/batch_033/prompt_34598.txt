In metaethics, expressivists hold that moral sentences like "Lying is wrong" do not express beliefs or report facts but instead express non-cognitive attitudes — disapproval, for instance. The Frege–Geach problem points out that moral sentences embed in complex constructions: "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong," or "It is not the case that lying is wrong." Explain why these embeddings pose a special difficulty for expressivism that they do not pose for cognitivism. In your explanation, address the following: why can't the expressivist simply say that the attitude expressed by a complex sentence is a function of the attitudes expressed by its parts, the way a cognitivist says the truth-condition of a complex sentence is a function of the truth-conditions of its parts? What is it about the nature of attitudes, as opposed to truth-conditions, that blocks this straightforward compositional move? And how have expressivists attempted to get around the difficulty — for instance, by treating moral language as expressing higher-order attitudes, or by developing expressivist-friendly notions of logical consequence?
