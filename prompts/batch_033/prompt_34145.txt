I am preparing to interview a philosopher who defends the position that epistemic akrasia is always irrational. By "epistemic akrasia," I mean the state of believing that p while simultaneously believing that one's total evidence does not support believing that p — or, in its graded form, having a high credence in p while assigning a high credence to the proposition that one's credence in p is not the one supported by one's total evidence. The guest holds, roughly, that there is a structural incoherence in this state that makes it rationally impermissible, and that higher-order evidence — evidence about the quality or import of one's first-order evidence — cannot rationally produce it.

I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having the guest restate the view, and instead press on the points where the position faces its most serious challenges. Please make sure the questions cover at least the following territory:

(1) The charge that the anti-akrasia position requires something like a "level-bridging" principle — a principle that licenses moving from facts about what one's evidence supports to facts about what one should believe, or vice versa — and that any such principle is either too strong or question-begging. What is the guest's preferred way of blocking this charge, and where does that response leave the view vulnerable?

(2) The relationship between epistemic akrasia and practical akrasia. In the practical case, we seem to recognize that an agent can judge that she ought to φ while failing to φ — that is the very structure of akrasia. But in the epistemic case, the anti-akrasia theorist denies the analogous possibility: you cannot rationally judge that your evidence supports believing p while failing to believe p on that basis. What is the relevant disanalogy between the practical and epistemic domains that licenses this asymmetry?

(3) Cases of peer disagreement. If I learn that an epistemic peer — someone equally intelligent, equally well-informed, and equally likely to have reasoned correctly — disagrees with me about p, I seem to acquire higher-order evidence that my own assessment of the first-order evidence may be flawed. The anti-akrasia position appears to permit, or even require, me to retain my original belief in the face of this higher-order evidence, since to do otherwise would be to believe p while thinking my evidence does not support it. How does the guest handle the intuition that this is objectionably dogmatic? And if the guest's answer appeals to the idea that peer disagreement merely makes the evidence equivocal in a way that suspending judgment would itself violate the anti-akrasia norm, I would like a follow-up that asks what work the notion of peerhood is doing on that account — if any doxastic response to peer disagreement would violate the norm, the view seems to lose its power to discriminate among responses.

(4) The question of which norm is violated. Is the akratic agent violating a synchronic coherence requirement on belief sets, a diachronic requirement about how beliefs should be updated, or a constitutive norm tied to the very nature of belief? What turns on this classification, and does the guest's answer create problems elsewhere — for example, by making it hard to explain why epistemic akrasia feels like a distinct kind of failure from, say, believing an outright contradiction?

(5) The challenge from deliberately misleading evidence. Suppose an experimenter gives you a drug that you know makes people temporarily worse at probabilistic reasoning, then presents you with a body of evidence. You assess the evidence and conclude that p is very likely. But you also know you are under the influence of the drug, so you have higher-order evidence that your assessment is unreliable. It seems that the rational response is to reduce your confidence in p — yet this looks like a case where your higher-order evidence rationally requires you to believe p while believing your evidence does not support p, or at least does not support it with the confidence you would otherwise assign. How does the guest handle such cases?

(6) The question of whether the anti-akrasia requirement is an ideal of substantive rationality or merely a requirement of structural coherence. If the latter, then it seems compatible with the agent's being substantively irrational in other respects — for instance, by having terribly misguided first-order beliefs while being internally coherent. Does the guest accept that the anti-akrasia norm is silent about the quality of first-order reasoning, and if so, does this not make the norm distressingly thin as an epistemic ideal?

For each question, please include a follow-up that anticipates the reply the guest is most likely to give, given the commitments of the position, and pushes the dialectic one step further. The aim is to produce a sequence of questions that feels as though it is genuinely advancing the debate, rather than rehearsing familiar opening moves.
