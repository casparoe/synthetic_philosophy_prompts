I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar and we've been going over the etiological vs. causal-role accounts of function, and I've built myself into a corner with an argument that seems to show the etiological account can't distinguish functions from accidents in a principled way. The conclusion feels wrong — surely we can say the heart's function is to pump blood and not to make thumping sounds — but I can't see which step to reject.

(1) On the etiological account, a trait T has function F iff T's performing F explains why T was selected for and persists in the population.

(2) So to determine whether some effect E of a trait is a function, we need to know whether E figures in the selection history of T.

(3) But selection doesn't operate on effects one at a time. What gets selected is the whole organism, and traits typically have many effects bundled together. The heart pumps blood, makes sounds, produces certain hormones, takes up space in the chest cavity — all at once.

(4) So the question "was the heart selected for pumping blood or for making sounds?" presupposes we can disentangle which effects drove selection. But natural selection doesn't come with labels. We infer selection pressures retroactively from comparative data, engineering analysis, and so on.

(5) The standard move here is to appeal to counterfactuals: the heart was selected for pumping blood because, in the relevant ancestral environments, hearts that pumped blood better increased fitness, while hearts that made different sounds didn't. But this requires us to hold everything else equal while varying one effect — which is biologically unrealistic, since you can't vary the heart's pumping without varying its sounds, its hormone production, etc.

(6) If we can't cleanly isolate which effects were selected-for, then on the etiological account we can't cleanly say which effects are functions and which are side effects.

(7) But we clearly can and do make this distinction. Cardiologists don't study heart sounds as a heart function; they study them as diagnostic signs of the heart's actual function.

(8) So either the etiological account is false, or our everyday function talk doesn't track the etiological account — in which case the account isn't capturing what we mean by "function."

The thing is, I don't want to give up the etiological account, because the causal-role account has its own famous problem — it can't explain why the function of the nose isn't to hold up glasses, since holding up glasses is a causal role noses play. The etiological account handles that beautifully: noses weren't selected for holding up glasses. So I'm stuck. Which premise would you reject, and what's the cost?
