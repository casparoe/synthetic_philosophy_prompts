I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long-form piece about Bertrand's paradox and whether the principle of indifference can be salvaged as a tool for turning ignorance into probability assignments. I've come to a position I'm inclined to defend: the paradox is not a local technical nuisance but a decisive refutation of the principle as a foundational rule of probabilistic reasoning. The principle tells us to assign equal probability to possibilities among which we have no reason to discriminate. But in any continuous space of possibilities, the same state of ignorance can be redescribed in multiple, equally natural ways, and the principle yields different probabilities depending on the description. In the standard chord problem, treating a random chord as a pair of points on the circumference yields probability one-third that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle; treating it as a radius together with a point along that radius yields one-half; treating it as a point in the disk chosen as the chord's midpoint yields one-quarter. All three descriptions are compatible with the same underlying ignorance, so the principle appears unable to fix its own partition or parameterization. I'm therefore inclined to conclude that the principle of indifference should be abandoned as a foundational principle rather than patched up.

Before I commit to that in print, I want to know what would count against this position. What would have to be true for the verdict to be wrong or overstated? I'm looking for a map of the position's exposure, not a survey. In particular: which formal results, examples, or arguments would show that the paradox can be dissolved without smuggling in information — by identifying a privileged parameterization, by showing that the conflicting answers come from a misapplication of the principle rather than from the principle itself, or by deriving the equal-probability assignment from independent constraints? Which of these would be decisive, and which would only show that the principle survives in a narrower role — as a heuristic, for finite partitions, or under an explicitly stipulated symmetry? How seriously should I take the cost of giving the principle up, such as the prospect that no principled way of setting non-empirical priors remains? Please sort the considerations from fatal to merely awkward, say which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible, and give an honest assessment of whether my position is at risk of being insulated — set up so that no result could count against it — and whether that would itself be a problem.

Finally, tell me what should change my mind: which single argument or consideration, if it held up, would most likely lead me to a different verdict, and where my reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write the response in American English.
