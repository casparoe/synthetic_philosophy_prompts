I keep noticing a pattern in how people — including me, if I'm honest — evaluate testimony, and I'm wondering if there's an established concept for it, or if not, what it should be called. Here's the thing.

A friend of mine, call him Dan, reads a news article reporting on a climate science study. The journalist cites a climate scientist. Dan accepts the finding — he says "well, the expert says so, and the journalist is just reporting what the expert said." The journalist is, in Dan's view, a transparent conduit. A few days later, the same journalist writes a piece on the economic effects of a minimum wage hike, citing an economist whose findings cut against Dan's priors. Now Dan suddenly starts talking about the journalist's publication's editorial slant, about how the economist they chose to quote probably has a think-tank affiliation, about how you'd need to check what other economists say before believing this. The journalist is nolonger a transparent conduit; suddenly Dan needs positive reasons to take the testimony seriously.

It's not just Dan. I've seen this in academic seminars, on social media, in my own head. The structure is: when testimony aligns with what someone already believes, they treat it as if the default entitlement to believe holds (the speaker is competent and sincere until proven otherwise). When testimony cuts against prior beliefs, they flip into a mode where the burden of proof is on the testifier to earn credibility. And the weird thing is, the epistemic standards being applied genuinely are different — it's not that they are being inconsistently applied to the same set of facts. The person really does, in the one case, think only a minmal threshold needs to be met, and in the other, thinks a much higher threshold is apropriate. And both judgments feel principled from the inside.

I know this lives somewhere in the neighbourhood of the reductionism / anti-reductionism debate about testimony — Hume versus Reid, whether we need positive reasons to accept testimony or have a default entitlement. But the asymmetry is the thing I cannot locate. It's not cherry-picking (which is just selecting favourable evidence from a mixed pool). It's not mere confirmation bias (which is about searching for or weighting evidence differently). This is about which epistemic policy — reductionist or anti-reductionist — gets deployed when, and the policy itself seems to shift based on the content of the testimony rather than on features of the speaker or the context that would justify a shift. 

Is there a name for this? How exactly does it relate to the reductionism debate — does it count as a problem for anti-reductionism, for reductionism, or for both? And if there is no label, what would you call it, and how would you define it so it's not collapsed into those neigbouring concepts?
