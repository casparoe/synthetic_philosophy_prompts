Most animal-welfare regimes extend some protection to vertebrates but none at all to insects. Under most research-ethics rules, a lab needs committee approval before it experiments on a mouse or a zebrafish, but the same project can kill hundreds of thousands of fruit flies without any review. A farm must stun a pig before slaughter, while insects raised for food are killed by boiling, freezing, or grinding with no welfare requirement, and pest control can eliminate an entire colony as a routine part of a building project without any accounting. Yet insects outnumber vertebrates by many orders of magnitude, and whether insects are sentient is treated by the relevant science as genuinely unsettled, not ruled out. Reconstruct the implicit theory of sentience and moral status that this pattern of practice presupposes. In particular, face the scale argument: if there is even a modest probability that insects are sentient, the expected number of sentient individuals affected by these practices is vast. Does the practice strictly presuppose that insects are certainly not sentient, or is it also compatible with discounting low probabilities of sentience, with denying that insect welfare matters, or with a tractability story on which insect welfare is real but not actionable? Distinguish what the practice presupposes from what it merely tolerates, say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and describe what a consistent expected-welfare approach would have to change in, for example, insect farming or pest control.
