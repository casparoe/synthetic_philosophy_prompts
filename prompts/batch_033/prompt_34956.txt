I am a philosophy professor currently revising a paper on conceptual unity, and I have been wrestling with a passage from Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* that I hope you can help me think through. The passage is from sections 66 and 67 of Part I, in the Anscombe translation. Rather than quote from memory, I will paraphrase the stretch that has been giving me trouble, since I do not have the published text in front of me.

Wittgenstein opens section 66 by asking us to look at the various proceedings we call "games" — board games, card games, ball games, Olympic games, and so on — and to consider what is common to all of them. He immediately warns the reader not to assume that there must be something common to them all simply because we apply the same name. Instead, he urges us to look and see. As we survey the range of activities, he writes, we find that similarities emerge and then fade away. There is no single feature present in every instance. What we see instead, he says, is a complicated network of similarities that overlap and criss-cross, sometimes similarities in the large and sometimes only in fine-grained detail.

In section 67 he proposes that the best way to characterize these similarities is through the idea of "family resemblances," since the various resemblances among members of a family — build, features, eye color, gait, temperament — overlap and criss-cross in just this way. He then declares that "games" form a family. He extends the point to numbers, asking why we call something a number. He suggests it is because it bears a direct relationship to several things we already call numbers, and through those, an indirect relationship to still others. He then offers the analogy of spinning a thread: we twist fiber on fiber, and the strength of the thread does not depend on any single fiber running through its entire length.

What I am stuck on is the precise role of the interlocutor's objection that Wittgenstein entertains just before this, at the very start of section 65. There, an imagined critic objects that Wittgenstein has been talking about language games but has failed to say what the essence of a language is, what is common to all these activities. Wittgenstein's response across sections 65 through 67 is clearly meant to challenge the demand for an essence, but I am trying to work out whether he is rejecting the demand outright or instead showing that the demand is satisfied in an unexpected way — namely, by overlapping similarities rather than a single common property. Put differently, is the family resemblance analysis meant to replace the notion of essence, or to reconceive it? I would be grateful for a careful reading of this stretch of text that clarifies what Wittgenstein thinks he is doing with the interlocutor's challenge.
