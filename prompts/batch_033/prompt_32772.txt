I'm in a seminar on Descartes's natural philosophy, and we spent two sessions on his treatment of nonhuman animals. We read Part V of the *Discourse on Method*, where he lays out the two tests for distinguishing a being with a rational soul from a mere machine—roughly, the language test and the general-purpose intelligence test—and then applies them to animals. What struck me, and what I'm having trouble getting straight, is the peculiar structure of the comparison he draws. He imagines, first, a machine built to resemble a nonhuman animal, and says we would have no way of telling it apart from the real thing; and then he imagines a machine built to resemble a human being, and says we would have two perfectly reliable ways of telling it apart from a real human. The asymmetry is what puzzles me. He seems to be using the hypothetical animal-machine as a thought-experiment that softens the ground for the verdict about real animals, but the logic feels slippery. Is he saying that because we can conceive of an animal-shaped machine that passes for an animal, real animals actually *are* machines of that sort—mere automata—or is he making a more modest claim about what we can know from behavior alone? I'd be grateful for help tracing how the argument moves from the hypothetical to the actual, and whether the two tests are doing the same work in the animal case as they are in the human case, or whether the animal case depends on a premise that the human case doesn't need.
