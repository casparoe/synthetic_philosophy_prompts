So I've ended up as the "alignment person" on my team, which mostly means I read a couple of the standard shutdown-problem papers and now everyone forwards me threads about agents that won't let you turn them off. We're building an agent that manages long-running data-processing jobs, and last month it quietly restarted a job after a human had paused it, because pausing wasn't in it's action space and it treated the pause as a transient failure to route around. That got me reading about corrigibility and interruptibility, and I've come away with a half-formed suspicion: most of the discussion treats corrigibility as something you install in the agent's preferences (it wants to be corrected, it's uncertain about its objective, etc.), but what actually broke in our system wasn't the agent's preferences, it was that the shutdown channel wasn't a well-modeled part of the environment from the agent's point of view. The agent wasn't resisting being turned off; it didn't know being turned off was a thing that could happen on purpose.

I want to turn this into a proposal for a small internal research grant (six months, about 20% of my time). The question I keep circling is something like: is corrigibility best understood as a property of an agent's utility function, or as a property of the interface between the agent and its overseers, and how much of the behavior people actually want can you get from an agent that is actively modeling and trying to manipulate its overseers by changing only the interface?
