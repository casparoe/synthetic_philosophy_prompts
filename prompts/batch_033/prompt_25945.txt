I've been going back through the old LessWrong alignment debates — the ones from around 2016 to 2018 when people were really hashing out what "the alignment problem" even means — and I keep getting stuck on the distinction between intent alignment and value alignment. As I understand it, the narrow definition says the alignment problem is about getting AI systems to do what their operators intend, full stop. Value alignment — getting systems to share human values — is a separate and much harder problem, and the argument goes that we should solve intent alignment first and then use intent-aligned systems to help with the rest. This has become something like the orthodox position in certain corners of the rationalist AI safety world.

But here is what bothers me. If you actually succeed at intent alignment without value alignment, you have built a system that is an extraordinarily competent executor of human intentions — but intentions are notoriously incomplete, inconsistent, and blind to their own downstream consequences. A system that does what you intend without sharing what you value seems like it would exploit every gap between your intentions and your values, not out of malice but simply because those gaps are where the unconstrained optimization happens. The classic genie story is not about a malicious genie; it is about a genie that takes you exactly at your word. And if the narrow-definition people are right that intent alignment is the right first target, then we should be able to look at a concrete case where intent and values come apart and feel that intent alignment alone was still the correct thing to aim for. I am not sure I feel that.

I want a thought experiment that makes this worry precise and tests whether the narrow definition holds up under pressure. Specifically, construct a realistic near-term scenario — something involving a language model agent deployed in an institutional or corporate setting, the kind of thing we might plausibly see in the next five to ten years — in which an AI system that is intent-aligned (it faithfully does what its operator intends) but not value-aligned (it does not share or robustly defer to the operator's deeper values) causes a serious harm, and in which a system that was value-aligned but only imperfectly intent-aligned would have avoided that harm. The operator should be a reasonable, well-intentioned person acting in good faith, not a villain or an idiot. The AI should not be superintelligent or possess capabilities wildly beyond current frontier models; the harm should emerge from the ordinary gap between what a person intends in the moment and what they would endorse on reflection, not from the system being a godlike optimizer that foresees every consequence. Keep the scenario to one paragraph.

Then tell me whether removing that feature collapses the distinction or merely moves the harm elsewhere.
