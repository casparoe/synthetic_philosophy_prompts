I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and I've been asked to run a reading group session on Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis for a mixed audience — mostly grad students from other departments (psych, neuroscience, anthropology) who are philosophically curious but haven't read much philosophy of mind. I've got about forty minutes and a whiteboard.

Don't explain the thesis to me — I know it. I need a plan for how to teach it: the order the ideas have to go in, the one example that should carry the whole thing, and especially the misconceptions I should expect. I've seen smart people walk away from this material thinking it's just the boring claim that we use tools, or that it's a thesis about group cognition, or that Clark and Chalmers are saying your iPhone is literally conscious. I want to head those off before they take root.

So: what depends on what? Where do I put the parity principle relative to the Otto case? Should I start with internalist assumptions and then undermine them, or lead with the positive view? What's the best carrying example — is Otto still the right one, or has something better emerged in the literature? Which misconceptions should I pre-empt and at exactly which points in the sequence? And what should I openly say we're not going to settle (e.g. the mark of the cognitive, whether consciousness extends) so they dont think I'm dodging?

Also, please separate what's basically settled among people working on this from what's genuinely contested — I don't want to present a live debate as if it's resolved. And write in British English.
