I'm taking a course on rational choice theory and we covered Kavka's toxin puzzle last week. I've been turning it over in my head and I think I might be missing something, because the puzzle doesn't seem nearly as deep as my professor made it sound.

The setup: a billionaire will give you a million dollars if you intend at midnight to drink a toxin tomorrow. The toxin will make you sick for a day but causes no lasting harm. The money is yours if you have the intention at midnight, regardless of whether you actually drink the toxin the next day. Kavka's claim is that you can't form the intention, because you know that once midnight passes and the money is yours, you'll have no reason to drink the toxin, and you can't intend to do something you know you won't have reason to do.

But here's my thought: why can't you just hire a hypnotist to make you intend to drink the toxin? Or take a pill that temporarily makes you forget the structure of the deal? Or even just tell yourself a convincing story about how drinking the toxin will build character? If the intention can be induced by any means whatsoever, then the puzzle is just an engineering problem about how to get yourself into the right mental state. It's not a paradox about rationality — it's a practical question about which psychological techniques reliably produce intentions. Philosophers have spent decades on this puzzle, so I assume I'm missing something. What is it?
