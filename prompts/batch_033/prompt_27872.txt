So I'm in this reading group and we spent like three weeks on Parfit's dust speck thing — you know, would you rather one person be tortured for fifty years or some astronomical number of people each get a single dust speck in their eye. Most of us had the intuition that the dust specks don't add up, but one guy, Derek (not that Derek, just some guy named Derek), has been pushing this analogy that's been eating at me.

He says the structure of the problem is exactly the same as risk aggregation in cost-benefit analysis. The way he puts it: government agencies like the EPA routinely treat a one-in-a-million risk of death spread across a million people as morally equivalent to one statistical death. That's how we justify environmental regulations — we multiply tiny probabilities by large populations and treat the product as a real harm worth preventing. So, Derek argues, if we're fine aggregating tiny probabilities of harm across people into something death-equivalent, we should be fine aggregating tiny certain harms (dust specks) across people into something torture-equivalent. He says anyone who rejects dust speck aggregation is committed to rejecting the entire framework of risk assessment that underpins public health policy, which would be absurd.

Another person in the group, Lena, pushed back hard. She said risk is different because ex ante nobody knows who the victim will be, so the process is fair in a way that deliberatly inflicting dust specks on identified people isn't. Derek shot back that fairness is a side issue — the underlying arithmetic of harm is what matters, and the arithmetic is identical. She said that's not true because probabilities aren't the same kind of thing as actual pains, and aggregating them isn't the same operation. He said that's a distinction without a difference.

I've been turning this over and I can't tell whether Derek's analogy is genuinely illuminating or whether it's smuggling in a bunch of stuff that doesn't survive inspection. Can you audit it? I want a proper breakdown: what corresponds to what in the mapping, where the analogy breaks, which features of risk assessment it quietly imports that don't apply to dust specks, and — the real question — whether Derek's conclusion (that rejecting dust speck aggregation commits you to rejecting standard risk assessment) holds up once the disanalogies are on the table. And if the analogy is bad, is there a better one? Please write the answer in American English.
