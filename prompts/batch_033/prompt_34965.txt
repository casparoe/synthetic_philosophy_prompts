Exam question: State the Condorcet jury theorem in its classical form, making explicit what each assumption says and what work it does. Then explain what the theorem does and does not establish about the epistemic reliability of majority rule in large electorates, and say what happens to its conclusions when the equal-competence assumption is dropped while every voter remains better than chance, or when the independence assumption is relaxed.

Student's answer: The theorem concerns a group choosing between two alternatives, exactly one of which is correct. There are n voters, n odd, each voting for the correct alternative with probability p > 1/2, and the votes are independent of one another conditional on the correct alternative. Under these assumptions three things follow. First, the probability that the majority votes correctly is greater than p, so the group is more reliable than any single member. Second, this probability increases with n. Third, it converges to 1 as n goes to infinity. If p < 1/2, the majority's reliability converges to 0 as the group grows, and if p = 1/2, majority rule does no better than a fair coin.

The assumption doing the real work is that each voter is more likely right than wrong. Independence matters as well, but mainly to ensure that errors do not systematically line up. In real electorates the equal-competence assumption is plainly false, yet this does little damage to the theorem's epistemic upshot. As long as every voter is better than chance, each additional voter still contributes some independent information, so the majority's reliability must keep increasing with the size of the electorate even when voters are not equally competent. Relaxing independence is likewise not fatal: correlation among voters can slow convergence, but so long as each individual remains better than chance, it cannot reverse the theorem's conclusion that sufficiently large majorities are almost certain to be correct. The theorem thus supplies a conditional epistemic case for majority rule: whenever its assumptions hold, larger groups are more reliable than smaller ones, and very large groups are nearly infallible.
