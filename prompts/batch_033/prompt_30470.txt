I am a teaching assistant for an upper-division undergraduate course in philosophy of mind, and I am grading a batch of midterm essays. The exam question the students received was this:

"In their 1998 paper 'The Extended Mind,' Andy Clark and David Chalmers argue that cognitive processes are not confined to the brain but can extend into the external environment. Reconstruct their central argument, making sure to explain the role of the parity principle and the thought experiment involving Otto and his notebook. Then evaluate the thesis: does the argument succeed in showing that the mind extends beyond the skull, or does it merely show that external objects can play a causally supporting role in cognition? Defend your assessment."

Below is one student's answer. I have my own sense of how strong it is, but I want a second opinion before I assign a grade and write up my comments. Please read the question and the student's answer carefully, then give me a grade on whatever scale you think appropriate, as long as you explain it, and provide a point-by-point assessment. I am not looking for a general impression; I need the specific things the student gets right, the specific things they get wrong or miss, and the reasoning that justifies the grade. In particular, I suspect there may be one or two mistakes that are easy to overlook on a quick read, and I want to make sure I have not missed anything myself. Please write your assessment in continuous prose, the way you would write an email to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.

The student's answer:

Clark and Chalmers defend what they call the extended mind thesis: the view that cognitive processes can extend beyond the boundaries of the skull and skin, into the external environment. Their central argument relies on the parity principle, which says that if an external process plays the same functional role as an internal process that we already count as cognitive, then we should count the external process as cognitive too. To treat them differently would be mere prejudice about location.

The key thought experiment involves Otto, a man with Alzheimer's disease who carries a notebook everywhere. Otto writes down information he needs to remember — addresses, facts, appointments — and consults the notebook whenever he needs to retrieve something. Clark and Chalmers ask us to compare Otto with Inga, who has a normal biological memory. When Inga hears that the Museum of Modern Art is on 53rd Street, she stores this information in her memory and later recalls it. When Otto hears the same information, he writes it in his notebook and later consults it. The functional role is the same: both are storing information for later retrieval, both rely on the information to guide action, and in both cases the information is reliably available when needed. The only difference is that Otto's storage happens to be outside his head. By the parity principle, if Inga's memory counts as part of her cognitive system, then Otto's notebook should count as part of his.

The argument is powerful because it challenges a deep assumption in philosophy of mind: that the mind is located entirely inside the head. Clark and Chalmers show that this assumption is not supported by argument but is simply taken for granted. Once we focus on what cognition does rather than where it happens, the case for external cognitive processes becomes compelling. The extended mind thesis also has striking implications: it suggests that our conscious experience itself can be partly constituted by external objects, since the boundary between self and world is more porous than we ordinarily suppose.

However, the thesis does face objections. One might argue that Otto's notebook is merely a tool that causally supports his cognition, rather than being genuinely constitutive of it. But this objection misses the force of the parity principle: if we redescribed Inga's memory as merely a tool that causally supports her cognition, we would be changing the subject. The parity principle demands that we treat like cases alike. Another objection is that the information in Otto's notebook is not automatically endorsed in the way that memories usually are — Otto might write something down and later reject it. But Clark and Chalmers can reply that we sometimes distrust our own memories too, and this does not disqualify memory from being a cognitive process. On the whole, the extended mind thesis succeeds in showing that the mind extends beyond the skull, and it forces us to rethink what a mind is.
