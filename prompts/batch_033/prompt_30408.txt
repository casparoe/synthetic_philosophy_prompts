I've been working on a longform piece about theodicy — the problem of evil — and I keep returning to Leibniz's solution because it seems to contain a trap I can't disarm. I'm hoping someone with sharper philosophical instincts can help me locate the flaw.

Here is the argument as I understand it. (1) God, being omnipotent and perfectly good, would create the best of all possible worlds. (2) In the best of all possible worlds, nothing could be otherwise without making the whole worse — otherwise it would not be the best. (3) So every particular evil that occurs is, in the strictest sense, necessary for the world to achieve its maximal goodness; remove any actual evil, and the total goodness declines. (4) If an event is necessary for the world to achieve its maximal goodness, then it would be wrong to prevent it, since preventing it would make the world worse than it could have been. (5) Therefore, for any evil you encounter — a child's suffering, an injustice, a genocide — it would be wrong to prevent it. (6) But that is morally monstrous; we are plainly obligated to prevent evils when we can. So something has gone wrong.

I know Leibniz himself did not draw this conclusion — he thought we should actively fight evil, and that our doing so is itself part of the best possible world. But I cannot see how that reply works without collapsing the argument. If my preventing an evil is part of the best possible world, then I will prevent it — the best world already includes my intervention. But then premise (4) seems to survive: the evil I prevent was never going to occur in the best world anyway, so the argument only applies to evils that actually occur. And for those, the conclusion still seems to stand: they are necessary, and preventing them would make things worse. So the reply merely relocates the problem rather than solving it.

I am not committed to Leibniz's theology; I am trying to understand whether the argument fails on its own terms, and if so, exactly where. Is there an equivocation on "necessary"? A scope error in the move from the whole to the parts? Or is the Leibnizian forced to bite the bullet and say that, yes, for the evils that actually happen, preventing them would be wrong — and we simply have to live with that? I want to know which premise to reject, and what rejecting it costs for the broader Leibnizian picture.
