I help run a community discussion group — about 16 people, mostly working adults with no special background in philosophy or political theory. We meet for two hours every other week. Over the last year I've noticed something that bothers me and I can't quite get a grip on.

Here's the pattern. We sometimes make decisions by voting on separate parts of a proposal and then combining the results. A while back we voted on whether to (a) move our meetings to Wednesdays, and (b) switch to a new venue. A majority voted yes on (a), and a majority voted yes on (b), so we adopted both. But when I looked at the individual ballots afterwards, I realized that almost no one had actually voted for the combination — most people wanted either the new day or the new venue, but not both together, because the combination created a conflict with childcare or commuting that neither change alone created. The group as a whole endorsed a package that almost no individual member endorsed.

I've since noticed that this seems to be a general feature of any procedure that votes on issues one at a time and then aggregates: the group can end up with a position that is inconsistent with what any individual holds, or that no one would have chosen if they'd been asked about the whole package. It feels like there's something deep here about whether a group can really be said to have "a view" at all, or whether majority voting on separate issues just manufactures a phantom collective opinion.

I want to design a structured activity for our group that makes the participants feel this problem directly — not just hear about it from me, but run into it themselves. The activity should be the first thing we do in a session, before I name the problem or give any lecture. By the end of the debrief, I want them to see that there's a genuine tension between two reasonable ideas: that a group should respect majority opinion on each issue, and that a group's overall position should be coherent.

Here's what I'm imagining: a simulation where the group plays the role of a committee — a hiring panel, a board, a jury — that has to make a decision with several logically connected parts. They vote on each part separately, and the rules are set up so that the aggregation produces a result that is logically inconsistent, or at least one that no individual member endorses. I want the participants to notice this themselves, without me pointing it out.

Constraints: about 16 people, two hours total. I'd like the activity itself — setup, any role assignments, voting, and the initial collective reaction — to take about 45 minutes, leaving the rest of the time for debrief. No real money or stakes; just roles and paper ballots or a show of hands. The case should be simple enough that people can grasp it in five minutes and complex enough to generate the tension.

In the debrief, I want to walk them through what happened and connect it to the bigger idea: that there's a structural problem in aggregating individual judgments into a collective one, and that this problem isn't a quirk of our particular voting procedure — it shows up in any procedure that takes majority votes on logically connected propositions. I'd also like to discuss what, if anything, a group should do about it: should they vote on whole packages instead of separate issues? Should they deliberate until they reach consensus? Should they just accept that groups don't have coherent "views" in the way individuals do?

Please give me the full activity: the scenario, the exact propositions they vote on, the ballot design, the step-by-step timeline, the debrief structure, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how to handle it. Also tell me which feature of the underlying idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply in the debrief myself.

One more thing: I came up with all of this on my own through observation and I've never studied philosophy. If I'm misunderstanding the problem, or if the activity I'm asking for can't actually be made to work the way I'm imagining, please tell me plainly rather than trying to force it. I'd rather be told I'm confused than walk into the session with a broken activity.
