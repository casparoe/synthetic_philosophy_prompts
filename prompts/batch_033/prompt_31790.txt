Consider the Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment. Two cosmological theories fit all non-indexical evidence equally well and differ only in the number of observers they predict: T1 predicts N observers, and T2 predicts (for concreteness) a trillion times as many. Starting from equal credence in T1 and T2, a philosopher claims that the mere fact of your own existence should shift your credence in T2 to near-certainty (say, at least 0.99), because T2 makes that fact vastly more probable. What is your credence that the philosopher's inference is rationally correct? Give a single number, then decompose it into the sub-questions on which it depends: the probability that the self-indication assumption (SIA) is the correct rule for updating on de se evidence; the probability that the self-sampling assumption (SSA) is correct and would block the inference; the probability that some third rule is correct — full non-indexical conditioning, a view on which your own existence is not evidence at all, or another account — and what verdict it gives in this case; the probability that the case or the question is underdetermined; and any further term you think belongs. Assign each term a probability and a one-line justification, show how the terms combine, and then identify the term you are least confident in and what evidence or argument would most move it.
