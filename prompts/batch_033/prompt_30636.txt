I'm on the board of a small wildlife trust in the south of England, and I've got to write something ahead of our meeting next month that I keep putting off. A proposal has come to us from one of our wardens: a one-year pilot to treat sarcoptic mange in urban foxes by leaving medicated bait at a handful of sites and monitoring the animals, with the aim of reducing slow deaths from a disease that's common in our area. Last winter I watched a fox with bad mange in our garden over several weeks, and it got under my skin more than I expected; I've always thought of myself as someone who accepts that wild animals suffer and die and that conservation isn't about playing favourites, but seeing the decline up close has made me less sure what that acceptance actually commits us to. I've since read a bit on wild animal suffering, and honestly it's made the question feel more urgent rather than clearer. Its not as if foxes are a conservation priority, so the strongest objection on the board is that this is mission creep, or worse, that we'd be distorting a wildlife trust into an animal hospital for individual wild animals. The strongest reply is that we already intervene in nature constantly (habitat management, reintroductions, culling), so the hands-off line isn't obviously available to us, and a slow death from mange is the kind of thing we wouldn't tolerate in a domestic animal. I need to circulate a short written opinion: recommend whether we fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with a timeframe, and the reasoning should engage the mission-creep and unintended-consequences worries honestly rather than just asserting that suffering is bad. Could you draft it for me? British English please, and keep it under about 800 words.
