Antitrust doctrine in market economies draws a sharp line at the boundary of the firm. When two independent manufacturers agree on the price of a component, the law calls it price-fixing and treats it as a serious offense; when the same two manufacturers merge and the surviving corporation sets that same price by internal directive, the law calls it management. Inside the merged firm, resources move by command and are valued by internal transfer prices that no one discovered in a market; outside it, the same resources move only by market prices. This is a puzzle: if market prices are indispensable for rational economic calculation, why is allocation by command permitted the moment coordination is moved inside a single legal entity? And if command can allocate resources rationally inside the firm, why is coordination by agreement among independent entities condemned rather than merely regulated? Reconstruct the implicit theory of economic knowledge that this practice presupposes — what it assumes about where the information needed for rational allocation lives, who can possess it, and whether a central decision-maker can reproduce what prices do. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: is the doctrine committed to the Mises–Hayek view that only markets can mobilize the dispersed knowledge required for calculation, or is it equally compatible with a Lange-style view on which a central board could set prices by trial and error? Finally, say what would have to change in antitrust doctrine, or in the theory of the firm, if the Lange-style view were adopted consistently.
