In a seminar on information cascades and rational herding, a classmate waved off a worry about a bank run by saying that "everyone can't be wrong." His move was this: if enough depositors are withdrawing, the best explanation of their behavior is that each has good private reasons, so a rational observer should withdraw too, and the run itself becomes evidence that the bank really is in trouble. I am fairly sure that slogan is doing more work than he has acknowledged, but I cannot yet say exactly which work it is doing. I want you to separate the distinct precise claims "everyone can't be wrong" could be used to express — for instance, claims about the accuracy of independent judgments pooled across many people, claims about markets or crowds aggregating dispersed private information, claims about what a rational agent may infer from observing the actions of others, and claims about the improbability of a large group converging on a falsehood. For each reading, spell out the conditions it assumes (independence of signals, observability of actions, common knowledge of rationality, and the like), whether it is true under those conditions, how it fares once agents observe and imitate one another rather than decide independently, and whether that reading would actually support the inference from a run on the bank to the bank's being in trouble. Lay the readings out as a menu rather than defending one interpretation, flag which are trivial, and say which ones would genuinely do the work my classmate's argument needs.
