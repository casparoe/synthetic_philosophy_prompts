A theory of self-deception faces a conceptual puzzle before it even gets to the empirical cases: if deception requires that the deceiver knows the truth and intentionally keeps it from the deceived, and in self-deception deceiver and deceived are the same person, then the person must simultaneously believe p and believe not-p, which looks like a contradiction. Any adequate theory has to dissolve this — or explain why it is not a genuine requirement — without losing the phenomenon. But that is only the first item on the list.

Write the full set of desiderata that a theory of self-deception must meet to count as adequate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps. I want to see at least the following covered, though the list should not be limited to them:

- What cases drawn from ordinary life and clinical psychology the theory must get right: the husband who ignores obvious signs of infidelity, the terminally ill patient who insists they are recovering, the student who sincerely believes they prepared adequately while evidence of underpreparation is plainly available, and the "twisted" cases where someone deceives themselves into believing something unwelcome rather than comforting.
- What distinctions the theory must respect and not collapse: self-deception vs. wishful thinking, vs. akrasia, vs. simple false belief arrived at by biased evidence-processing, vs. hypocrisy, vs. outright lying to others while half-believing the lie oneself.
- What the theory must say about whether self-deception is blameworthy or instead excuses, and whether the answer varies by case.
- Whether the theory must characterize a process (how one gets into the self-deceived state) or may remain purely structural (what the state consists in), and what the minimal constraints on either are.

Flag any subset of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. Then score the three main families of theory — intentionalism, deflationism, and divided-mind (partitioning) accounts — against the list, briefly and candidly about where each falls short.
