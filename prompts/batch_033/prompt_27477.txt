Write a dialogue between a philosopher who defends epiphenomenalism about consciousness and a philosopher who thinks the position is self-undermining because of the paradox of phenomenal judgment. 

The epiphenomenalist holds that conscious experiences are caused by physical processes but exert no causal influence of their own: they accompany the neural machinery the way the whistle accompanies the locomotive's engine, contributing nothing to the work. The critic argues that if this is true, then every judgment we make about our own conscious experience—that we are in pain, that red looks a certain way, that we are conscious at all, and crucially, that epiphenomenalism is the correct account of the mind—must be produced entirely by non-conscious physical mechanisms, with the experiences themselves playing no role in shaping those judgments. But then, the critic presses, what reason could we possibly have to trust that those mechanisms reliably deliver true beliefs about consciousness? If experiences never cause anything, the correlation between experience and judgment looks like an enormous coincidence, or at least like something we have no entitlement to rely on. And if we cannot trust our judgments about consciousness, we cannot trust the judgment that epiphenomenalism is true—the view saws off the branch it sits on.

The epiphenomenalist should offer real responses rather than deflections. They might argue, for instance, that a common physical cause explains the correlation without consciousness doing any causal work (the same neural process causes both the experience and the judgment, so they will naturally covary), or that the critic's demand for a causal connection between experience and judgment already assumes that only causal connections can confer justification, which is itself a premise the epiphenomenalist need not grant. The critic should engage these moves seriously rather than restating the initial objection. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth in which each character refines their position in response to the other's arguments, and neither side should be a strawman. Avoid easy resolutions; the point is to explore the pressure points on both sides.
