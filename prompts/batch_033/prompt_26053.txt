I'm currently taking a course on Hume's moral philosophy, and we've been working through his essay "Of Suicide." I'm struggling with a specific stretch of argument and would appreciate help unpacking it. Here is the passage (from the section where Hume begins his case that suicide is no transgression of our duty to God):

"If Suicide be criminal, it must be a transgression of our duty, either to God, our neighbour, or ourselves.

To prove, that Suicide is no transgression of our duty to God, the following considerations may perhaps suffice. In order to govern the material world, the almighty creator has established general and immutable laws, by which all bodies, from the greatest planet to the smallest particle of matter, are maintained in their proper sphere and function. To govern the animal world, he has endowed all living creatures with bodily and mental powers; with senses, passions, appetites, memory, and judgment; by which they are impelled or regulated in that course of life, to which they are destined. These two distinct principles of the material and animal world continually encroach upon each other, and mutually retard or forward each other's operation. The powers of men and of all other animals are restrained and directed by the nature and qualities of the surrounding bodies; and the modifications and actions of these bodies are incessantly altered by the operation of all animals. Man is stopt by rivers in his passage over the surface of the earth; and rivers, when properly directed, lend their force to the motion of machines, which serve to the use of man. But tho' the provinces of the material and animal powers are not kept entirely separate, there result from thence no discord or disorder in the creation: On the contrary, from the mixture, union, and contrast of all the various powers of inanimate bodies and living creatures, arises that surprizing harmony and proportion, which affords the surest argument of supreme wisdom."

What I cannot get clear on is the precise logical structure of the argument Hume is making here against the claim that suicide violates a duty to God. In particular: is he offering a single argument or several distinct ones? How exactly does the claim that God governs through general laws rather than particular volitions do the work of showing that suicide is permissible — is the idea that since God does not specially intervene to stop me from diverting a river or knocking down a house, I am equally free to end my life? And what role does the "harmony" observation at the end play: is it doing genuine argumentative work, or is it a rhetorical concession to the religious reader before the more subversive implications set in? I'd be grateful for a step-by-step reconstruction of what Hume takes himself to have established by the end of this paragraph.
