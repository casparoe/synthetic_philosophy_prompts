I've been trying to get my head around something about the sorites paradox that still bothers me, and I'd love a clear explanation.

The standard story we all know: you start with a heap of sand, remove grains one by one, and at some point—without any single grain removal feeling decisive—it's not a heap anymore. The paradox is that if there's no precise number n such that n grains make a heap but n-1 don't, we seem forced into one of three uncomfortable positions: (a) there actually is a sharp cut-off but we can't know where it is (epistemicism), (b) the concept 'heap' is semantically defective in some way (supervaluationism, fuzzy logic, etc.), or (c) classical logic has to go.

What I specifically want to understand better is the dispute between the epistemicist and the supervaluationist. Both views seem, at least on the surface, to accommodate exactly the same data about how we use vague terms. In neither case can a speaker locate the boundary. In neither case do we get paradoxes in ordinary life. In both cases, pushing on borderline cases produces the same sort of confusion. So here's what nags at me: is there a substantive difference between saying "there is a sharp but unknowable boundary" and saying "the boundary is indeterminate"? If both views predict the same linguistic behaviour, the same patterns of assent and dissent, the same phenomenology of puzzlement, then what exactly is at stake? Is this just a verbal dispute about whether to stick the label "sharp" on something that, for all practical purposes, behaves exactly like a vague boundary? Or is there a real disagreement hiding here?

And if there is a real disagreement, I'm curious what kind of consideration could possibly settle it. The epistemicist's main move seems to be that their view lets us keep classical logic untouched, wherees supervaluationism has to give up things like bivalence (even if it keeps excluded middle) and some classical inference patterns. But then the question becomes: why is preserving classical logic worth the price of admitting sharp, unknowable cut-offs in the world? That feels like a trade-off rather than a knockdown argument. Is there something else that actually tips the scales, or does the whole debate just kind of peter out into a clash of philosophical temperaments?
