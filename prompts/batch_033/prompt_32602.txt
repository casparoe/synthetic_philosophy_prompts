I've been going down a rabbit hole on discount rates, and there's something I can't shake about how the UK government appraises long-term policy, climate stuff included. The Green Book — the Treasury's cost-benefit manual — doesn't use a single discount rate. It uses 3.5% a year for the first 30 years, then 3% for years 31–75, then 2.5% after that. On it's face this gets presented as a technical adjustment for uncertainty about future growth, but it looks to me like it's quietly taking a stance on how much people who don't exist yet count. What theory of intergenerational justice does this practice actually presuppose? Is the declining schedule coherent, or is it a kludge that papers over the fact that any constant positive rate effectively writes off anyone a few centuries out? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely happens to fit, and tell me what a policy built on a cleanly stated principle would have to change.
