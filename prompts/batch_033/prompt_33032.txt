I work in quality control at a small type foundry, and my team reviews submitted glyphs in which a designer intends a form as the letter 'a' — or 'g', 'e', or another letter — but reviewers disagree about whether it is still that letter at all, as opposed to a decoration, a different letter, or a private mark. I've been thinking on my own about what makes every 'a' an 'a' across wildly different styles. The idea I keep returning to is that a letterform's identity lives in the roles its parts play — an enclosed counter, a stem, a terminal — rather than in any particular shape, and that a style is a systematic way of varying those parts while preserving their roles. Please write a reusable decision procedure my reviewers could actually run on a disputed glyph, with roughly ten minutes per case and no background in the theory: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a decision that the form is not the intended letter, and where it hands the case to a person because the matter is one of judgment rather than fact. The procedure should distinguish a stylistic variant from a change of category. Then run it on three invented cases: one clearly the intended letter, one clearly not, and one where the procedure returns an answer the submitting designer will not like. Finally, say where your reasoning is most likely to fail and which consideration would lead you to revise the procedure.
