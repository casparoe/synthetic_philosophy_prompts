I'm grading for an upper-level epistemology course and I've got a student answer on Jeffrey conditionalization that I need a second pair of eyes on. The question on the problem set was:

"Standard Bayesian conditionalization is commutative: if you learn E and then learn F, your final credences are the same as if you learned F and then E. Jeffrey conditionalization generalizes this to cases where evidence is uncertain (you don't learn a proposition for sure, you just shift your credence in it). Is Jeffrey conditionalization commutative? If not, explain why and give a toy example that shows the order mattering."

Here's what the student wrote:

"Jeffrey conditionalization is not generally commutative. The reason is that Jeffrey updating works by shifting probabilities across a partition while keeping the conditional probabilities on each cell of the partition fixed (this is the rigidity condition). When you do two Jeffrey updates in sequence, the second update's rigidity condition applies to the probabilities that result from the first update. But if you reverse the order, the first update's rigidity condition is applied to the original probabilities instead. Since the intermediate probabilities are different in the two orders, the conditional probabilities that get held fixed by the second update are different, and you end up with different final distributions.

For example, suppose you have a bag with two coins: one fair (F) and one double-headed (D). You draw a coin and flip it. Initially your credence in F is 0.5 and in Heads is 0.75 (since D always gives heads and F gives heads half the time). Now suppose you Jeffrey-update on the coin-type partition {F, D}, raising your credence in F from 0.5 to 0.8 (maybe the bag looks like it has more fair coins than you thought). Then you Jeffrey-update on the outcome partition {H, T}, raising your credence in H from 0.6 to 0.9 (the flip sounded like it landed heads). If you reverse the order — first updating on {H, T} and then on {F, D} — you get a different final probability for F. This shows that order matters.

There is one exception: if the two evidence partitions are probabilistically independent, then Jeffrey updating is commutative. This is because independence means the conditional probabilities across the partitions don't change after the first update, so the rigidity condition 'sees' the same numbers regardless of order."
