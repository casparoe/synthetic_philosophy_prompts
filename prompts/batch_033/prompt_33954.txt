I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've spent a fair amount of my free time reading about algorithmic information theory and its philosophical implications — mostly papers and essays by people like Marcus Hutter, Shane Legg, and various writers in the LessWrong orbit. I've developed a set of views on Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Occam's razor that seem individually reasonable to me, but I don't trust my ability to spot when commitments I hold pull against each other. I'd like an audit.

Here is where I currently stand:

(1) Solomonoff induction is the right formalization of Occam's razor. Weighting hypotheses by 2^{-K(h)}, where K(h) is the Kolmogorov complexity of the hypothesis, captures exactly what we mean by "simpler theories deserve more prior probability." The formalism gets the intuitive cases right and has a principled mathematical foundation.

(2) Kolmogorov complexity is the right measure of simplicity for hypotheses. The fact that it's defined relative to a reference universal Turing machine is not a serious problem, because the invariance theorem guarantees that switching reference machines changes K(h) by at most an additive constant, and for hypotheses of any interesting complexity that constant is negligible.

(3) Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, so it cannot serve as a practical algorithm. But this is irrelevant to its role as a normative standard. It tells us what an ideal reasoner ought to do, in the same way that Bayesian conditionalization is the normative standard for belief updating even when exact computation is intractable.

(4) Occam's razor is an epistemic principle, not merely a pragmatic one. Simpler theories are not just more convenient or more likely to generalize well for practical purposes — they are genuinely more probable. If theory A and theory B both fit the available data and A is simpler, I ought to believe A is more likely to be true, full stop.

(5) The no-free-lunch theorems are sometimes invoked against Occam's razor, since they show that no learning algorithm outperforms any other when averaged uniformly over all possible data-generating processes. I think this objection fails: the uniform average over all possible environments is not the right distribution to use. The Solomonoff prior — which is heavily non-uniform and biased toward simple environments — is the correct one, and no-free-lunch results have no bearing on its justification.

(6) But I worry about circularity. If I ask myself why the Solomonoff prior is the right one rather than some other prior, the natural answer seems to be that the world we inhabit is itself simple — that the true data-generating process has low Kolmogorov complexity. Yet how could I know that, except by having already done some induction? It looks as though I need to assume the conclusion (that induction works, that simplicity tracks truth) in order to justify the prior that is supposed to underwrite induction in the first place.

(7) Despite that worry, I do not think we can coherently jettison Occam's razor. Any system that learns from finite data must embody some inductive bias, and simplicity-based biases are the only ones that have ever been made to work. Even if the justification bottoms out in something circular, the alternatives are worse — giving up Occam's razor means giving up learning altogether, and there is no third option.

(8) Finally, I take the Minimum Description Length principle, and the related practices in machine learning — L1 and L2 regularization, early stopping, the preference for smaller networks that generalize better — to be practical approximations of the same underlying idea as Solomonoff induction. They are not mere engineering heuristics; they are implementations of a genuine epistemic principle.

Rank the tensions by severity rather than giving each equal weight.
