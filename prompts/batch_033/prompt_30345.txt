I'm giving a colloquium next month at a philosophy department where I'll be defending a broadly Dennettian line in the Malcolm–Dennett debate over whether dreams are experiences. My thesis is that the question as usually posed rests on a false assumption: that there must be a determinate fact, during sleep, about whether a stream of experience is occurring, which the waking dream report then either accurately or inaccurately records. I argue that neither the received view that dreams are waking-like experiences occurring during sleep nor Malcolm's rejection of that view is forced. Dream reports are better understood as retrospective narrative constructions produced on waking, shaped by the request to report a dream, and the familiar empirical findings about REM sleep and lucid-dream signaling are compatible with this account without settling the philosophical question. My argument turns on indeterminacy considerations: the timing and content of any supposed experience during sleep are systematically underdetermined by the evidence, and the inference from a waking report to a prior episode relies on exactly the criterion that is in dispute.

The audience is mostly philosophers of mind with ordinary language sympathies, two of whom have written on Malcolm and will press the conceptual side; there are also two philosophers of cognitive science who work on sleep and memory and will treat the REM literature as decisive unless I give them a reason not to; and there is one phenomenologist who will defend the authority of first-person experience. They are not uniformly hostile to Dennett, but they will resist anything that sounds like changing the subject or dismissing empirical work as irrelevant.
