I have been working through Harsanyi's aggregation theorem on my own, and I find that I cannot tell how much it actually proves. Let me lay out the argument as I understand it, and then ask you for a probability rather than a survey.

Suppose each individual has preferences over gambles that satisfy the standard expected-utility axioms, so each individual can be represented by a utility function. Suppose further that the social ranking of gambles also satisfies those same axioms. Harsanyi's theorem says that if the social ranking is indifferent between two gambles whenever every individual is indifferent between them, then the social utility function must be an affine combination of the individual utility functions, that is, a weighted sum plus a constant. If we also require that society prefers one gamble to another whenever at least one individual prefers it and no one prefers the alternative, then the weights must all be positive. Harsanyi then adds an impartiality move: an evaluator who imagines having an equal chance of being any of the affected individuals would, by the same expected-utility logic, give everyone equal weight, which yields utilitarianism.

I want your actual credence that this line of reasoning is a sound argument for utilitarianism as a standard of social evaluation. I am not asking only whether the mathematics is correct; I am asking whether the theorem, together with the impartiality step, genuinely establishes that a rational and impartial social ranking must be the sum of the individuals' utilities.
