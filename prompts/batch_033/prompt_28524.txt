I'm on the research ethics board at a teaching hospital. We've got a protocol from a neuroscience group that wants to study the sensory/affective dissociation in pain. They propose using rTMS to transiently disrupt posterior insula activity in healthy volunteers (they build on the pain asymbolia literature), then applying calibrated thermal pain stimuli. The idea is that subjects would still feel the painful sensation — they'd identify it as pain — but the intervention is expected to strip away the 'hurt' or aversive quality. The researchers argue this is no more ethically concerning than a standard pain-induction study, because the affective component is what makes pain bad, and that's precisely what they're suppressing.

The board is split. One faction thinks this is dangerously wrong: pain is intrinsically harmful regardless of how it's experienced moment-to-moment, and you can't just consent away that harm. The other faction thinks the protocol is methodologically elegant and that the objection rests on a conceptual confusion about what pain is. I need to draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasoning that engages both sides seriously. It should have a clear bottom line we can actually vote on.
