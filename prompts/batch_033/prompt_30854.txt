A legal system that would never fill its legislature by lot nonetheless entrusts twelve randomly selected citizens with the power to imprison a defendant, and treats their verdict as a legitimate exercise of state coercion. The jury is deliberately emptied of expertise — lawyers and people with specialized knowledge are routinely excluded — and its members are accountable to no electorate once the trial ends. The legislature, by contrast, is chosen through competitive elections meant to produce accountable, politically skilled representatives. What theory of political competence, representation, or legitimacy does the institution of trial by jury presuppose? Reconstruct that theory as precisely as the practice allows, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then say whether the surrounding electoral system shares those presuppositions or quietly contradicts them. Finally, if a society took the jury's presuppositions seriously, would it be committed to selecting legislatures and other political bodies by lot as well, or is there a principled difference between adjudication and legislation that blocks that inference?
