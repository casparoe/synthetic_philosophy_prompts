I've been going down a rabbit hole with the situationist critique of virtue ethics and I've constructed an argument that's been keeping me up. I'm taking a moral psychology seminar and we read a bunch of Doris and Harman, and the more I think about it the more it seems like the situationist argument, if it works, proves too much. Like, way too much. I tried to lay it out cleanly and now I can't find the exit.

(1) Virtue ethics, at least the Aristotelian kind, needs people to have stable character traits (virtues and vices) that reliably produce trait-consistent behavior across different types of situations. If you're compassionate, it's not just that you help sometimes when it's convenient — you help across a range of circumstances because compassion is a settled disposition.

(2) If people actually possess such traits, we should observe fair cross-situational consistency in morally relevant behavior. Not perfect consistency, but enough that you can't flip someone's behavior with a trivial change to the situation.

(3) But the social psychology literature is full of cases where trivial situational factors swamp any apparent personality effects: Darley and Batson's seminary students walk right past someone groaning in a doorway when they're told they're late, but stop to help when they're not in a hurry. Isen and Levin's subjects who find a dime in a phone booth are dramatically more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers. And Milgram's participants — ordinary people, not monsters — deliver apparently lethal shocks because a guy in a lab coat tells them to. If behavior were driven by stable traits, a dime or a mild time pressure shouldn't make that much difference.

(4) So people don't have the kind of stable character traits that virtue ethics needs. The traits are, at best, weak and highly situation-specific.

(5) So virtue ethics rests on a false empirical picture of human psychology and should be rejected.

(6) But if (4) is right — if there are no robust, cross-situationally stable character traits — then the problem isn't just for virtue ethics. It's for any ordinary practice of moral evaluation. When we praise someone as "a good person" or blame someone as "cruel," we seem to be attributing a stable disposition to them. If no such dispositions exist, then moral praise and blame are systematically unfounded.

(7) But it can't be that moral praise and blame are entirely unfounded. That's not a conclusion anyone can live with, and it's not what the situationists themselves claim — they still want to hold people responsible.

(8) So something has gone wrong in (1) through (6). But every premise looks plausible to me, and the studies are real.

I'm not sure which step to give up. I want to keep virtue ethics, or at minimum keep the idea that moral praise and blame are justified, but I can't figure out where the argument overreaches. Is there a step that looks solid but actually smuggles something in? And if I have to reject a premise, what's the cost — what else do I lose?
