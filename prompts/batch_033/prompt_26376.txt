I just quit a PhD program two years in, and I've been thinking about sunk costs nonstop. So at a party last weekend when I overheard two friends going at it about exactly this issue, I basically just listened and tried to sort out who was right. They weren't talking about academia — they were arguing about a mutual freind who flew to another city for a concert, the headliner cancelled, the opening acts were mediocre, and he stayed for the whole four hours anyway. They got into the general question of whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Here's the exchange as I remember it:

Clara: "Staying was textbook irrational. The ticket and the flight were sunk — he should've just asked whether he'd enjoy the rest of the show more than whatever else he could do in an unfamiliar city, and the answer was obviously no."

Ben: "Okay but that's too clean. If he left, he'd be the guy who flew to another city and bailed. There's value in not being that person — in being someone who commits and follows through. That's not a sunk cost, that's a real consideration."

Clara: "Sure, but it's a forward-looking consideration. He's weighing the future benefit of self-respect against the future boredom. The sunk money and time still don't figure in. My point is just that past costs shouldn't drive the decision, and they don't on your account either."

Ben: "I'm not sure they don't. Think about it like this: the past effort is part of what makes the whole thing matter. If he leaves, the story of his weekend becomes 'I wasted a trip.' If he stays, it's 'I had a weird, bad concert experience but I stuck it out.’ The past investment changes the meaning of the present choice. Stories aren't just forward-looking expected-value calculations."

Clara: "So you're saying it's rational to suffer through a bad show to improve the story you tell later? That seems like you're just relabeling the cognitive bias — you feel bad about wasting money so you throw good time after bad to avoid feeling like a sucker."

Ben: "No, I'm saying the feeling of having wasted something is itself a real cost you have to account for. If leaving would make him feel worse than staying — regret, stupidity, whatever — then staying might actually maximize his welfare. You can't just declare those feelings inadmissible."

Clara: "But those feelings are generated by the fallacy. It's like saying the itchiness of a rash justifies scratching it. The whole point is to notice that the regret about sunk costs is a misfiring and discount it."

Ben: "Maybe. But some things we value precisely because we've invested in them — relationships, careers, skills. If you treat every decision as a fresh start with no weight given to past investment, you'll churn through things and never get deep at anything. Sunk-cost reasoning might be necessary for depth."

They went on like this and I still don't know who has the better argument. Can you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate — who's right about which specific claim, where each person's reasoning goes wrong, and what they're both missing? And overall, is honoring sunk costs always irrational, or are there principled exceptions?
