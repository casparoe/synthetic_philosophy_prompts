**Question**

Explain the Sleeping Beauty problem and the two main positions in the debate about it. Set out what you take to be the strongest argument for each position. Then assess whether the problem shows that standard Bayesian epistemology requires revision in order to handle self-locating belief, or whether it can be satisfactorily resolved within the standard framework. Defend your assessment.

**Student Answer**

The Sleeping Beauty problem, introduced by Adam Elga, concerns how rational agents should assign credences to self-locating propositions. The setup is as follows. Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, and a fair coin is tossed. If the coin lands heads, Beauty is awakened on Monday, interviewed, and the experiment ends. If the coin lands tails, Beauty is awakened on Monday and again on Tuesday, but between the two awakenings she is administered a drug that erases her memory of the Monday awakening. On each awakening she is asked: 'What is your credence that the coin landed heads?' Beauty knows all these details in advance. The puzzle is what answer she should give.

The halfer position, defended by David Lewis, holds that upon awakening Beauty's credence in heads should be 1/2. The central argument is that Beauty knew on Sunday that she would be awakened at least once regardless of the coin's outcome, so the experience of awakening provides no new evidence that discriminates between heads and tails. Since her credence on Sunday was 1/2 and the evidence she gains upon awakening is evidentially neutral with respect to the coin, her credence should remain 1/2. Moreover, if Beauty is later told that it is Monday, the halfer maintains that her credence in heads should remain 1/2, because the halfer treats all possible awakenings as reflecting the same underlying chance set by the coin toss.

The thirder position, originated by Elga, holds that upon awakening Beauty's credence in heads should be 1/3. The argument is that there are three possible awakening events — Monday-and-heads, Monday-and-tails, and Tuesday-and-tails — and Beauty, upon awakening, has no way to distinguish among them. By the principle of indifference, she should assign each an equal probability of 1/3. Since heads occurs in only one of these three cases, her credence in heads is 1/3. The thirder is committed to the Self-Indication Assumption, which says that one should reason as if one is randomly selected from the set of all possible observer-moments, weighted by their probability of existing.

The strongest argument for the thirder is the betting argument. If Beauty is offered a bet on heads at even odds on each awakening, she will lose money in the long run: over many trials, she will be awakened twice as often on tails as on heads, so a policy of betting on heads at 1:1 odds yields a net loss. Since credences ought to guide betting behaviour, and the fair betting odds for heads are 1:2 (corresponding to a credence of 1/3), Beauty's credence must be 1/3. The halfer's reply to this argument is that the betting setup is not independent of the hypothesis being tested — the number of bets placed depends on the coin outcome — and standard Bayesianism does not require an agent's credences to match betting odds when the betting arrangement is entangled with the truth of the proposition in question.

I think the thirder position is more defensible. The problem does show that standard Bayesian epistemology requires revision, because the halfer cannot account for the fact that upon awakening Beauty genuinely has more reason to think it is Monday than Tuesday, and the halfer's insistence on sticking with a credence of 1/2 even after being told it is Monday seems to ignore relevant centred evidence. The Sleeping Beauty problem reveals that credences about self-locating propositions do not behave like credences about ordinary, uncentred propositions, and that a theory of rational belief must expand its framework to accommodate them.
