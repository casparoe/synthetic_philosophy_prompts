You're a philosopher of medicine who's worked on the naturalism/normativism debate about health and disease. I keep running into a version of it in ordinary life and I want to know how much weight the argument can actually carry. A friend of mine, a cardiologist, insists the body is basically a machine and disease is just a part failing to do its job: the way a carburetor that doesn't mix fuel properly is broken, a thyroid that doesn't produce the right amount of hormone is diseased. On his view, that makes health and disease objective biological facts — you figure out what the part is *for*, check whether it can do it, and your values never enter the picture. He uses this to dismiss people who say "disease" is partly a social or evaluative category, and to claim the whole normativist side of the debate is confused.

I half-agree with him, but I suspect the machine analogy is doing a lot of hidden work. Could you audit it carefully? I'd like: (1) an explicit mapping — what corresponds to the machine, the part, the designer's specification, the repair manual, the breakdown, maybe the recall notice; (2) the places where the analogy breaks, and please seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the naturalism-vs-normativism question from the ones that are just superficial; (3) what the machine picture quietly imports that organisms might not have — design, a spec, a single intended function, a designer who set the performance standard; and (4) a verdict on whether his conclusion — that disease is value-free biological dysfunction — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the machine analogy was carrying it the whole time. If there's a better analogy that would do more honest work for the naturalist, tell me what it is and where it too would start to creak. Take your time, and start with some brainstorming on the correspondences before you commit.
