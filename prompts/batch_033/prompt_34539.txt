I've been running a reading group on Kierkegaard's *The Present Age* — we're a mix of philosophy postgrads, a couple of journalists, and one person who works in content moderation at a social media company. The discussion keeps circling the same drain: everyone agrees Kierkegaard's critique of the press and "the public" is eerily prescient for the social media era, but we can't seem to agree on what the actual diagnosis *is*, let alone what follows from it. Some of us think the problem is structural (something about the medium itself), others think it's about anonymity, others think it's about scale, and one guy insists Kierkegaard is just wrong — that the public isn't a phantom at all, it's a real epistemic agent.

I want to bring a sorting instrument to the next session so we can actually figure out where we all stand relative to each other, rather than talking past each other for two hours. Could you write me a taxonomy or decision tree of the positions one can take on the relationship between the press (or mass media generally) and the public, with Kierkegaard's critique as the central reference? The tree should be built from the logical space — first the branching questions, then the positions located in the space they carve out. I don't want a list of Great Thinkers with labels; I want the diagnostic questions that genuinely split the views, phrased so someone can answer them without knowing whose position they're walking into.

Some things I'd like you to make sure are included: the question of whether the problem (if there is one) is intrinsic to mass communication as such, or whether it's a contingent feature of particular media forms; whether anonymity is a bug or a feature; whether the "public" has any normative or epistemic standing the individuals in it lack; and whether the press creates the public, or merely reflects one that already exists. At least one leaf should be Kierkegaard's own position (as you read it). Flag any pairs of leaves the tree can't cleanly separate, and note any logically possible combinations of answers that nobody seems to occupy — and say why you think that is (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). I'm not fussy about the number of leaves, but I'd like it to be rich enough to do justice to the debate — eight or ten leaves, maybe?

I'd like you to take a definite position on the structure of the tree: don't just lay out options for how one might carve it. Commit to the branching order and justify it, even if you're acknowledging that another order would highlight different things. And if you think Kierkegaard's own view is mischaracterised in the standard ways people talk about it, say so — the group will enjoy that.
