So I'm a novelist and I've been watching what Kindle Unlimited's payment model does to the books people actually write. For context: KU pays authors by pages read — not per sale, but per page a reader actually flips through. What's emerged is this whole ecosystem of books optimized to keep people turning pages: chapters end on micro-cliffhangers every few pages, prose is deliberately skimmable, books get serialized into endless installments, and there's a cottage industry of advice about "writing for the algorithm."

I'm trying to figure out what theory of literary value this payment model presupposes. Like, any compensation system has to have some implicit notion of what's worth paying for — even if nobody sat down and articulated it. And what's strange to me is that the same industry that built this also runs the Pulitzer and the National Book Award, which seem to presuppose a totally different notion of value. Is there a coherent view here, or is the industry just holding two incompatible theories at once? And if we actually took the awards' implicit theory seriously, what would we have to change about how authors get paid?
