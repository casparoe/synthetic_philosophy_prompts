I've been thinking a lot lately about this idea I came across — the "expanding circle" of moral concern. The basic thought is that over history, who we count as morally relevant has slowly widened: from kin to tribe to nation to all humans, and now maybe to animals. I think Peter Singer popularized it, though I gather it goes back further. I'm not an academic, just someone who reads around.

Anyway I rewatched District 9 the other night and I can't stop thinking about it in connection with this idea. For anyone who hasn't seen it: an alien ship ends up parked over Johannesburg, the aliens (derisively called "prawns") get shoved into a squalid camp, and the protagonist Wikus is this mid-level bureaucrat who treats them with this casual, bureaucratic contempt — until he accidentally gets sprayed with their DNA-goo and starts transforming into one of them.

What I'm trying to work out is whether the film actually dramatizes the expanding circle thesis or whether it's doing something darker that the theory doesn't capture. Because Wikus does eventually come to care about the prawns, and even risks himself for them — but only after his own body starts changing. It's not that his moral imagination expands through reasoning or exposure or empathy; it takes his own flesh becoming alien for him to see them as beings that matter. That seems like a pretty damning comment on the whole idea of moral progress through the gradual widening of concern. He doesn't expand his circle so much as get dragged into a different one.

And then there's the humans. They don't expand their circle at all — if anything they contract it, because once Wikus is part-alien they treat him as disposable, a medical resource to be harvested. The Nigerian gangsters in the film are even worse. So the film seems to be saying that the circle doesn't naturally expand; it stays rigidly drawn around "us," and "us" is defined in the most visceral, bodily way possible.

The ending too. Wikus is fully transformed, hiding out in the slum he used to help evict, folding little metal flowers for his wife who doesn't know it's him. And we're waiting, like him, for Christopher the prawn to return with help. Is that hopeful — a suggestion that moral progress is agonizingly slow but real, that the circle can expand if we wait long enough? Or is it bleak — the only person whose circle expanded is the one who literally stopped being human, and the rest of humanity learned nothing?

I guess what I'm asking for is a reading of the film that takes the expanding circle idea seriously but also lets the film push back. I don't want the movie reduced to an illustration of the theory, and I don't want the theory dismissed just because the movie is dark. Where does the film confirm something the theory gets right, where does it complicate it, and does it ultimately suggest the theory is naive about how moral concern actually works — that it's not reason but bodily vulnerability that redraws the line between us and them?

And please, no bullet points or headings or anything like that — just write it out in ordinary paragraphs, the way you'd think through it if we were talking.
