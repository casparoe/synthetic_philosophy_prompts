I've been teaching a seminar on Moore's paradox this semster — you know, "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" — and we've been working through the standard moves: the speech-act account, the belief-expression account, the normativity-of-assertion account, all that. But I've been trying to push my students to see whether the paradox has any real traction outside the usual toy examples, and I keep coming back to the credit rating agencies in the run-up to the 2008 financial crisis.

Here's the thought. The agencies — Moody's, S&P, Fitch — were publicly stamping AAA ratings on mortgage-backed securities that were, as we now know, garbage. And yet we have internal emails and testimony (the "let's give this deal a AAA even if the underlying assets are crap" kind of thing) showing that plenty of analysts didn't believe the ratings they were issuing. So in effect, they were saying to the market: "These securities are extremely safe" while simultaneously — in their own internal communications, and plausibly in their own heads — not believing it. That looks an awful lot like a Moore-paradoxical assertion, just with a corporate mouth rather than an individual one.

I want you to analyze the ratings-agency episode through the lens of Moore's paradox. But I'm not looking for a simple "yep, it's a Moorean absurdity" take. I want a careful mapping. Which of the standard accounts of the paradox — the speech-act story, the belief-expression story, the knowledge-norm story — best captures what was wrong with what the agencies did? Where do the accounts break? For instance, the agencies weren't exactly sincere liars, since many of them seem to have been in some kind of self-deceptive state rather than cynically lying. Does Moore's paradox have anything to say about institutional or collective assertion, or does it only work for individual speakers? And what does the episode tell us about the paradox itself — does it reveal that the paradox is really about something deeper than assertion, maybe about the relationship between public commitment and private mental state? 

One more thing: I'm not interested in the legal or regulatory analysis of what the agencies did wrong. I want the philosophical analysis. Where does Moore's paradox illuminate, and where does the episode force us to revise or extend the standard philosophical treatments?
