Analyze the rise and fall of the Phillips curve — from its early postwar formulation as an empirical trade-off between wage inflation and unemployment, through its adoption in the 1960s as a menu for macroeconomic policy, to the stagflation of the 1970s — as a case study in Friedman's F-twist, the methodological thesis that a theory should be judged by the accuracy of its predictions rather than by the realism of its assumptions.

Begin with a disciplined mapping. On the F-twist's own terms, what in this episode is the theory, what are its assumptions, what count as its predictions, and what would the methodology have told economists and policymakers to do at each stage? Then address the apparent tension: Friedman is standardly read as both the source of the F-twist and one of the first serious critics of the Phillips curve's foundations, arguing via adaptive expectations and the natural rate that the exploitable trade-off was at best short-run and that the long-run curve is vertical. Was that critique a violation of his own methodology, a consistent application of it, or evidence that the methodology is too blunt to capture how macroeconomics actually proceeded?

Close by saying what the episode establishes about the F-twist itself. Does stagflation vindicate the view that models are judged by their predictions, since the Phillips curve was abandoned only when its predictions failed? Or does it vindicate the view that unrealistic assumptions matter, since the critics located the flaw in the expectations mechanism before the predictions broke down? Or does the case show that the F-twist draws the distinction in the wrong place? Be explicit about where the episode leaves the F-twist silent.
