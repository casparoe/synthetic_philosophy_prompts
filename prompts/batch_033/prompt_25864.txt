I'm putting together a little piece on the sunk cost fallacy and I want to sanity-check my overall position before I go further. I'm a grad student and this is for a draft I'm shopping to my advisor, so please think carefully and don't pull punches. Here's where I'm at:

(a) The standard line: sunk costs are sunk. They should never, in themselves, factor into forward-looking decisions. If you wouldn't start something now knowing what you know, the fact that you've already invested in it is irrelevant — that's just good decision theory.

(b) But I also think: if I've already paid $200 for a non-refundable weekend workshop and then I wake up feeling lousy (not contagious, just tired and unmotivated), I actually *should* be more inclined to drag myself there than if the workshop were free. The reason isn't that the $200 somehow changes the future costs and benefits of attending. It's that going anyway helps me maintain a self-conception as someone who follows through. If I skip, I learn something bad about myself that I'd rather not learn.

(c) I think precommitment strategies — Ulysses contracts, burning bridges, public pledges with social costs for backing out — are rationally justified, and this isn't in tension with (a). Precommitment changes your future incentive structure, so you're not honoring sunk costs; you're just responding to altered payoffs.

(d) When a friend says "I've put three years into this relationship, I can't leave now," I think they're reasoning badly. The years are gone either way. If the relationship is making them unhappy going forward, the past investment shouldn't weigh on the decision at all. I find this obviously right in a way I don't find myself hedging.

(e) Past investment can be rational *evidence*: if you've put four years into a PhD, that's evidence you have the ability and persistence to finish, so continuing can be rational on purely forward-looking grounds. This is compatible with (a) and I take it to be uncontroversial.

So that's the package. I want to know: does this actually hang together? Specifically — is there a real tension between (a) and (b), or can I fend that off by saying (b) is about self-signaling and not about sunk costs per se? And if (b) is legitimate, why doesn't the same move rescue my friend's relationship reasoning in (d)? Walk through which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look that way, and what the cheapest fix is. Be precise about what feature, if any, is doing the work to distinguish the cases.
