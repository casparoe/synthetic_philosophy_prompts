I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I edit both long reported features and shorter news analyses. The recurring problem on my desk is knowing when a story is ready to file. Some writers treat every piece as if it needs one more source, one more study, one more rival interpretation; others stop the moment the piece is publishable and miss the reporting that would have changed the argument. Deadlines and word counts are fixed, but so is the cost of being shallow or wrong.

I have read enough about Herbert Simon's work on satisficing and bounded rationality to believe that a threshold of acceptability, rather than an exhaustive search for the best possible version of a story, can be a rational stopping rule when search is costly and the full set of options cannot be surveyed. What I have not seen is that idea turned into a procedure an editor without a background in the theory can actually run. Please write one for me: a decision procedure, grounded in satisficing and bounded rationality, for deciding when a reported story is ready to file, which my desk could use repeatedly.

Make it a procedure in the strongest sense. It should be a sequence of questions or tests in a fixed order, with a clear account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each; it should say where the procedure returns "file", where it returns "keep reporting", and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of editorial judgement rather than mechanics. It should specify how to set an aspiration level for a piece before reporting begins, how to revise that level when the reporting environment turns out to be richer or poorer than expected, and how to prevent revision from becoming a quiet lowering of standards. It should distinguish satisficing from settling, and it should make the stopping rule sensitive to the costs of further search, not only to the quality of what has already been found.

Build in the constraints of my context: a fixed word count and deadline for each piece; writers of varying experience; fact-checkers who are available but expensive; sources who will speak only on background; and the impossibility of covering every angle. The procedure should be usable by someone who has never read Simon, so translate the concepts rather than invoke them.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A 3,000-word feature on a contested epidemiological claim. The writer has two strong sources on one side, one on the other, and a central document that neither side will release.

2. A 900-word news analysis of a new paper on AI safety. The writer says it is ready, but has read only the paper itself and one critical blog post.

3. A 2,500-word reported essay that meets its original brief, is factually clean and well written, but does not yet say anything surprising. The writer has already interviewed nine people. My instinct is to commission more reporting; I want the procedure to be capable of telling me, on its own terms, that the problem is the aspiration level I set at the start rather than a shortage of search, and to say "file" if that is what follows.

Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot do: the cases where it will misfire, the steps that are judgement dressed as mechanics, and the assumptions it makes about the newsroom that may not hold elsewhere. Please write the answer in British English.
