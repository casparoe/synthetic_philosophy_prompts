Write a sonnet about Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. The octave should develop the thesis that the probability of an indicative conditional 'If A, C' is the conditional probability of C given A, and it should make that identification feel natural and powerful: it lets us assign a probability to a conditional the way we do to any ordinary proposition. The volta should be the step where the identification begins to collapse. Under modest closure assumptions — once the conditional can combine with negation, conjunction, and disjunction like an ordinary proposition — the equation forces any probability function that satisfies it to be trivial, and the elegant thesis gives out. The sestet should not merely announce the collapse but work through its shape, and the closing couplet should state exactly what a theorist has to give up: the unrestricted equation, the claim that conditionals express ordinary truth-conditional propositions, or some other commitment. Someone who knows the results should recognize them as rendered faithfully rather than gestured at, and someone who does not know them should still find the poem a good poem.
