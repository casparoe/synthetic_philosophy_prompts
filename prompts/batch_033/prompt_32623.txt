So I just got back from a three-hour argument with my roommate about wether it's hypocritical to go to climate marches but fly home twice a year and eat meat pretty much daily. She had this whole defense worked out, and by the end I couldn't tell if it was actually a good argument or just confidence. Her version went roughly like this: the inefficacy objection shows that a single individual act, like buying a burger or taking a flight, makes no difference to climate outcomes, because the total amount of harm is fixed by millions of aggregated choices and no single choice shifts that total. Since you can't be obligated to do something that won't change anything, there's no individual duty to cut your emissions or stop eating meat; it's like how one vote never decides an election, so staying home on election day isn't a moral failing either. The only real obligations are collective ones, like supporting a carbon tax or organizing for systemic change, and harping on personal consumption is a distraction that lets fossil fuel companies off the hook. She said the inefficacy objection basically refutes the idea that individuals have climate duties.

I kept wanting to say something about how a tiny chance of being the deciding vote still matters, but she just said I was doing expected-value cope. What I want to know is: how much of her summary would a philosopher working on collective harm and individual responsibility actually sign off on? Which parts are right, which are subtle misreadings, and does the conclusion that there are no individual climate duties actually follow from the parts that are true?
