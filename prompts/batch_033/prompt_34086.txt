A few of us in my reading group got into a long argument about Dennett's "Quining Qualia," and I'm hoping you can help sort out who was right about what. The sticking point was the Chase and Sanborn intuition pump.

For context: Chase and Sanborn are two coffee tasters who both used to enjoy their company's blend but no longer do. The coffee hasn't changed. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the same to him as it always did, but his tastes have changed—he no longer likes that flavor. Sanborn says his tastes haven't changed at all; rather, the coffee now tastes different to him—his perceptual system has altered, and if it still delivered the old taste experience, he'd like it just as much. Dennett's point is that nothing in either man's experience could settle which of them is right, and that this reveals something deeply problematic about the very idea of qualia.

Here's how the argument went:

Maya said the thought experiment is genuinely devastating to qualia realism. Her argument was that if qualia are supposed to be immediately apprehensible—if you're supposed to have direct, incorrigible access to the intrinsic character of your own experience—then the fact that Chase and Sanborn can't even tell whether their own qualia have changed or their evaluative standards have changed shows that there's no such thing as this kind of direct access. She said the two hypotheses "the taste quale is the same but I evaluate it differently" and "the taste quale is different but my evaluation is the same" are distinct in the quale-theorist's own terms, and yet the subject himself has no way to tell them apart. If the very person who supposedly has the qualia can't say which hypothesis is true, then the concept of qualia does no explanatory work and we should discard it.

Evan pushed back hard. He said Maya was conflating an epistemic limitation with a metaphysical conclusion. The fact that Chase can't tell whether his perceptual system or his standards changed doesn't mean there's no fact of the matter—it just means introspection isn't fine-grained enough to decompose the total shift into sensory and evaluative components. He offered an analogy: you might not be able to tell by introspection alone whether your headache is worse today because the pain quale is more intense or because you're less tolerant of pain today, but that doesn't mean there's no difference between pain intensity and pain tolerance. The concepts are still meaningful; we just can't disentangle them from the inside. He said Dennett's argument only works if you assume that qualia must be perfectly transparent to introspection, and that's a straw man—no serious qualia realist thinks we have infallible access to every relational fact about our qualia.

Maya replied that Evan's headache analogy actually proves her point. She said the reason we can make sense of the distinction between pain intensity and pain tolerance is that we have independent behavioral and physiological criteria for each—we can measure pain thresholds, observe coping behavior, look at neurological correlates. The concepts earn their keep through a network of third-person criteria. But in the Chase and Sanborn case, Dennett's challenge is precisely that there is no third-person criterion, even in principle, that could distinguish a change in the quale from a change in the evaluative standard. She said that if the quale-theorist insists there's a fact of the matter anyway, then "qualia" become a wheel that plays no part in the mechanism—they're epiphenomenal posits that explain nothing because they're consistent with every possible observation.

Evan said that was too quick. He argued that the Chase and Sanborn scenario only seems to lack a criterion because Dennett has artificially restricted the evidence. In a real case, we could do psychophysical testing—vary the stimulus, run discrimination tasks, track adaptation—and build up a profile that would let us distinguish a sensory shift from a preference shift. He said that if Chase's gustatory system changed, he'd likely show different discrimination thresholds and different similarity judgments across a range of coffees, whereas if only his preferences changed, his discrimination capacities would remain stable while his hedonic ratings shifted. So there is a fact of the matter, and there are third-person criteria for it—Dennett just ignores them by presenting the case as a single, isolated introspective report.

Maya said that even if Evan's proposed testing worked, it would miss the deeper point. She said Dennett isn't just claiming that Chase and Sanborn can't tell the difference; he's claiming that the very idea of a quale as an intrinsic, ineffable, private property is incoherent because we can't even specify what it would mean for the quale to be the same while the judgment changes, or vice versa. She said Evan's psychophysical approach just replaces the old-fashioned quale with a complex dispositional profile—which is exactly the kind of functionalist, relationally-defined notion of experience that Dennett is happy to accept. So Evan hasn't defended qualia in the traditional sense; he's just changed the subject.

Evan said that was a rhetorical move, not an argument. He said that if Dennett's conclusion is just that we should reject an extravagantly Cartesian picture of qualia as perfectly private, intrinsic, and infallibly known, then fine—but that's not a quining, it's a friendly amendment. The real fight is whether there's any phenomenal residue at all that isn't exhausted by functional or dispositional facts. And the Chase and Sanborn case doesn't touch that question—it only works against someone who thinks qualia are both perfectly transparent to introspection and completely independent of behavioral dispositions. That's a position almost no one holds.

Neither of them would budge after that, and the rest of us couldn't agree on who had the better of the exchange. Can you go through their argument move by move and adjudicate? I want to know who's right about which specific points, where each person's reasoning goes wrong (if it does), and what the overall verdict is—not just who "wins" but what a careful analysis of the dialectic reveals.
