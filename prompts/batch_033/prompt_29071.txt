"Pascal's mugging is a thought experiment where a mugger asks you for $5. When you refuse, he says that if you give him the $5, he will use his powers to give you an astronomically large amount of utility tomorrow — say, a payoff so huge it makes a googolplex look tiny. You think the chance he is telling the truth is vanishingly small but not zero, maybe one in 10^100. The expected utility of paying up is that tiny probability times the enormous payoff, which comes out larger than the certain loss of $5, so expected utility theory says you should hand over the money. Since that seems crazy, the thought experiment is supposed to show that expected utility theory gives crazy advice. The standard fix is to make utility functions bounded, so no outcome can be assigned an astronomically large utility. Once utility is bounded, the expected value of the mugging stays small, and in particular it cannot exceed the certain utility of keeping your $5, so the problem goes away. Pascal's mugging therefore shows that rational agents should have bounded utility functions. The only real cost is that we can't assign infinite value to things like an eternal afterlife, but that seems like a price worth paying."
