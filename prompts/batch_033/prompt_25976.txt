I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in moral philosophy, and the topic of promissory obligation has recently become personally significant to me. A close friend made a serious promise to me earlier this year — to help care for an ailing family member during a difficult stretch — and then withdrew from the commitment at the last moment with what struck me as a rather thin rationale. I've found myself returning repeatedly to the question of what, exactly, makes a promise binding in the first place, and whether my sense of having been wronged is best explained by the philosophical accounts I've been teaching. I'm now grading the midterm essays and would appreciate a second opinion on one student's answer. Below is the question and the student's response. I'd like you to assign a letter grade and provide point-by-point comments justifying it — please be specific about what the student gets right and where they go wrong.

**Question:**

According to Hume, the obligation to keep promises is not grounded in any natural duty or act of the will, but rather arises from a convention. Explain Hume's argument for this claim, and contrast it with at least one contemporary account of promissory obligation. Which account do you find more persuasive, and why?

**Student's answer:**

Hume argues that promises are an artificial virtue, meaning they depend on social conventions rather than natural sentiments. His key argument is that a promise is unintelligible outside of a social practice: the words "I promise" have no meaning and create no obligation unless there is already a convention in place that gives them force. Hume compares promising to other conventions like property rights — just as there is no natural "mine" and "thine," there is no natural obligation to keep one's word. The convention arises because it serves everyone's mutual interest: by participating in the practice of promising, we can cooperate with strangers and make commitments that extend beyond immediate self-interest.

Hume also argues that promises cannot be grounded in a mere act of will or mental resolution. Simply resolving to do something does not create an obligation, and adding words to that resolution doesn't change its nature unless those words have conventional force. This is his argument against the natural law view that promises bind because they involve the voluntary transfer of a right.

T.M. Scanlon offers a contemporary alternative. According to Scanlon, the obligation to keep a promise derives from the principle that one should not lead others to form expectations about one's conduct and then disappoint those expectations. On this view, when I promise to do something, I intentionally cause you to expect that I will do it, and it is the act of raising and then violating that expectation that constitutes the wrong. This is different from Hume because for Scanlon, the wrong of breaking a promise is fundamentally about deception — I have misled you about my intentions.

I find Scanlon's account more persuasive because it captures the interpersonal dimension of promising better than Hume does. When someone breaks a promise to me, what bothers me is not that a useful social convention has been undermined, but that they have wronged me personally by failing to do what they led me to count on. Hume's convention-based account seems to make the wrong too impersonal and diffuse.
