I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I keep noticing a pattern across several domains that I don't have a word for. I'm hoping someone can help me identify it — or, if it doesn't have an established name, help me carve out what exactly the phenomenon is and where its boundaries lie.

The pattern is this: something appears to be responding intelligently to its situation, but on closer inspection it's executing a fixed sequence of steps. The tell is what happens when you interrupt it mid-sequence. Instead of diagnosing the interruption and picking up from the relevant point, it abandons its progress and restarts the entire routine from step one — even when restarting makes no sense given what caused the interruption.

I first noticed this in code. I was debugging a deployment script that, on failure at any stage, would roll back everything and retry the whole pipeline from the beginning. If step 7 of 10 failed because of a permissions error on a directory, the script wouldn't check the permissions and resume at step 7 — it would tear down all the infrastructure it had already built, recreate it from scratch, and hit the exact same permissions error again, looping until it exhausted its retry budget. The script looked sophisticated: it had error handling, retry logic, and logging. But it had no representation of why it failed, only a rule that said "on failure, start over."

Then I saw the same structure in an institutional process. A friend works at a company where, if a purchase order gets rejected at the final approval stage because someone forgot to fill in a cost-center code, the entire PO is voided and you must submit a new one from scratch — which then routes through all the same approvers again, including people who already signed off. The process looks thorough and multi-layered, but it's mechanically restarting a ritual rather than addressing the specific defect.

And then I started noticing it in people, including myself. When I'm deep in a debugging session and someone interrupts me with a question, I sometimes lose my place entirely and restart my mental checklist from the beginning — re-verifying things I had already ruled out ten minutes earlier. Or consider someone who, when a recipe goes wrong mid-preparation, doesn't diagnose the specific mistake but throws everything in the trash and starts over from measuring the flour.

What interests me is that these cases sit in an odd middle ground. The behavior is too structured and multi-step to call merely reflexive, but too rigid to call intelligent. It has the surface features of goal-directed action — it's a sequence of operations oriented toward an outcome — yet it can't adapt when the sequence is broken. It's as though the system has a representation of the goal but no representation of its own state, so the only way it knows how to reach the goal is to run the whole tape from the beginning.

I'm aware of terms like "cargo culting" and "path dependence," but those don't seem to capture the specific thing I'm describing — the combination of apparent sophistication with underlying rigidity, exposed by the restart-from-scratch response to interruption. Is there an established concept for this? If there are several in the vicinity, I'd want to understand how they differ and which maps most precisely onto what I'm seeing. And if nothing established fits, I'd be curious how you would characterize it: what are its necessary features, what are its boundary cases, and what distinguishes it from mere habit, mere reflex, or genuine flexibility?
