I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a short essay on the relationship between chaos, predictability, and determinism. Before I develop it further, I'd appreciate comments on the working notes below. I'm particularly interested in whether the conceptual distinctions are drawn correctly and whether any of the claims overreach.

Working notes for the essay:

My central claim is that determinism and predictability are properties of quite different kinds, and that much public discussion of chaos theory runs them together. Determinism, as I understand it, is a thesis about the world's dynamics: given the complete state of a system at a time and the laws that govern its evolution, exactly one future is possible. It says nothing, by itself, about what anyone can know or compute. Predictability, by contrast, is an epistemic and computational notion: it is always relative to an agent's data, the precision of that data, and the resources available for turning data into forecasts.

Chaos, on my account, is a phenomenon within deterministic systems: sensitive dependence on initial conditions means that arbitrarily small differences in a starting state grow at an exponential rate. Because any actual measurement has finite precision, a real observer's ability to forecast decays over time, no matter how good the instruments. This is a failure of predictability in practice, but it leaves determinism untouched.

I want to push one step further, though. Even if an agent were granted exact initial conditions, prediction could still fail in a stronger sense. Some deterministic systems are computationally universal, and their long-term behavior can be undecidable in the way the halting problem is undecidable: no finite procedure, given the initial data, can settle the question in all cases. If that is right, there is a sense of unpredictability that survives unlimited precision and is not merely a consequence of measurement error. Yet this still is not indeterminism, because the dynamics remain single-valued; the failure is computational, not dynamical.

I also want to include the converse point: predictability does not require determinism. A stochastic process can be statistically predictable even if its individual outcomes are not determined.

The essay would then argue that two common inferences are both mistaken: from "we cannot predict" to "the world is not determined," and from "the world is determined" to "everything is in principle predictable." The first ignores chaos and computational limits; the second assumes an ideal of prediction that finite agents cannot meet.

One worry I have: the stronger unpredictability claim may depend on treating the initial state as an exact real number, which may be a mathematical idealization rather than a physical possibility. If physical states are always finitely specified, the claim may need reformulation, and I'm not sure how much damage that does to the essay's argument.

I'd welcome comments on any of the following: whether the determinism/predictability distinction is drawn correctly; whether the stronger claim about exact initial conditions and computational limits is sound or overstated; whether the two inferences I call mistaken are in fact as cleanly separable as I suggest; and where a philosopher of physics or a mathematician would most likely object.
