I spent my career in computational neuroscience — modeling how real neural systems solve the problems that artificial systems stumble over — and in retirement I've turned to philosophy of mind, partly to see whether the questions I skirted around in the lab had been settled by people with more time to think. They haven't. The frame problem is a case in point. I first encountered it in its original AI formulation, then watched Dennett, Fodor, and Pylyshyn pull it into philosophy, and now I find the literature has splintered: some treat it as a narrow problem in nonmonotonic logic, others as a deep epistemological puzzle about relevance, and still others as a clue that the whole representationalist picture of mind is broken. Before I read any more, I want to know what I should be asking of a purported solution.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of the frame problem — whether it claims to solve it, dissolve it, or show that it's the wrong problem to begin with — must get right. Start with the original computational formulation (updating a representation of a changing world without an explosion of frame axioms specifying what doesn't change) and work outward to the philosophical versions (how any cognitive system determines what is relevant to a given belief revision or action without an infinite regress of relevance criteria). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Then flag which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable — the ones any account must satisfy regardless of theoretical commitments — and which are parochial, insisted upon by one research program but reasonably rejectable by an outsider. I'm especially interested in whether any pair of desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable: if the computational formulation and the philosophical formulation demand incompatible things, that would explain a good deal about why the literature has felt stuck for decades. A brief scorecard for the main approaches — the situation calculus and its descendants, circumscription, relevance logics, and the embodied/embedded cognition line that the problem simply doesn't arise for situated agents — would be welcome, but the criteria themselves are what I'm after, since I suspect that clarifying the requirements might do more work than evaluating the contenders.
