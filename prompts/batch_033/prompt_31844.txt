I'm teaching a third-year undergraduate seminar on theories of well-being, and we're spending two weeks on hedonism and the heterogeneity objection. I'd like a diagnostic instrument — a sorting quiz or decision tree — that I can give to students at the start of the second week, after they've read the core papers but before we discuss the heterogeneity problem in depth.

The question the instrument should sort them on is: what, if anything, do all pleasures have in common in virtue of which they are pleasures? The positions I want to appear as leaves are:

- Qualitative (phenomenal) hedonism: pleasures share a distinctive phenomenal quality or hedonic tone.
- Attitudinal hedonism: what makes a state a pleasure is that the subject takes a favourable attitude toward it (desires it, likes it, is pleased by it).
- Functionalism about pleasure: pleasures are states that play a certain causal or functional role (e.g., states we seek to sustain, or that reinforce the behaviour that produced them).
- The fitting-attitude view: pleasures are states it is fitting or appropriate to desire or be pleased by.
- The family-resemblance view: pleasures have no single common feature; they are united only by overlapping similarities.
- Pluralism about pleasure: pleasures are genuinely heterogeneous, and this is a reason to doubt that hedonism can serve as a monistic theory of well-being.

The instrument should sort by what students believe about cases, not by which label they like. Each question should present a concrete scenario and ask for a verdict or a judgement, worded so that someone who hasn't yet encountered the taxonomy can answer it. The scenarios should include at least the following kinds of pleasure, because these are the ones that pull different positions apart:

- The pleasure of solving a difficult intellectual problem.
- The pleasure of eating something delicious.
- The pleasure of a sensory experience that the subject neither desires beforehand nor seeks to prolong (e.g., an unexpected pleasant smell that passes).
- A case of masochistic enjoyment, where the subject finds an experience pleasurable despite its being painful in the ordinary sense.
- A drug-induced state that the subject sincerely reports as pleasurable but has no inclination to sustain or repeat.
- The pleasure of anticipating a future good.
- A case where someone takes satisfaction in something they believe to be objectively worthless (e.g., malicious pleasure at another's misfortune).

The tree should be built from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of named positions. Lay out the diagnostic questions first, then locate the six positions in the space of answer combinations. If there are answer combinations that no one defends, note them and briefly diagnose why: is the position incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Also flag any pair of positions your instrument cannot separate — for example, if two views make the same predictions about every case you've included — and explain where they come apart and why the instrument couldn't capture it.

The audience is third-year philosophy undergraduates who have done introductory ethics and some philosophy of mind. The instrument should be usable in twenty minutes. Please write the answer key and the diagnostic commentary in British English.
