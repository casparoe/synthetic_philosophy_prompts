Then evaluate the following objection: hypothetical intentionalism either collapses into actual intentionalism (because the ideal reader's best inference just tracks the actual author's intention) or collapses into anti-intentionalism (because the 'best inference' is constrained by the text alone). Does hypothetical intentionalism occupy a stable middle ground? Defend your answer."

And here's one student's answer (unedited):

"Actual intentionalism is the view that the meaning of a literary work is whatever the author intended to communicate by it. This view is intuitive — when we read a novel, we're trying to understand what the author is saying — but it runs into two main problems. First, the epistemic problem: we often don't have access to the author's intentions, especially for authors who are dead or who haven't commented on their work. Second, the failed intention problem: an author might intend to write a biting satire but produce something that every competent reader takes as sincere. It seems wrong to say the work 'really is' a satire when no one reading it could tell.

Hypothetical intentionalism, developed by Wimsatt and Beardsley in their famous paper 'The Intentional Fallacy,' tries to solve both problems. On this view, the meaning of a text is not what the actual author actually intended, but rather what an ideal reader — one who knows the language, the relevant literary conventions, and the historical context — would most reasonably infer the author intended. This handles the epistemic problem because we don't need to access the author's private mental states; we just need to be competent readers. And it handles the failed intention problem because if the author's execution failed, the ideal reader won't infer the intended meaning from the publicly available evidence.

So the motivation for the move is that hypothetical intentionalism preserves the intuition that meaning is tied to intention and communication, while avoiding the messiness of actual authorial psychology.

Now, does hypothetical intentionalism hold a stable middle ground, or does it collapse into one of the two views it's trying to split the difference between?

Against the collapse-into-actual-intentionalism worry: the hypothetical intentionalist doesn't care about what the author actually intended, only about what the best-supported hypothesis is given the evidence. Even if we found a diary entry where the author explicitly stated their intention, if the text doesn't support that reading, the diary entry doesn't change the work's meaning. Meaning is constituted by the hypothetical reader's best inference, not by the facts about the author's mental states.

Against the collapse-into-anti-intentionalism worry: the anti-intentionalist says meaning is just what the text says, full stop, with no reference to authorial intention at all. But the hypothetical intentionalist is still asking a question about intention — 'what did the author most likely intend?' — not just 'what does the text say?' The text is the primary evidence, but the question is fundamentally about intention.

One might object that the ideal reader is just a disguised way of getting at the actual author's intention, since with enough information the ideal reader would converge on what the author actually intended. But this confuses epistemology with metaphysics. The hypothetical intentionalist is making a claim about what constitutes meaning, not about how we best discover it. Even if the ideal reader's inference usually lines up with the actual intention, meaning is still constituted by the inference, not by the actual intention. So the middle ground is stable."
