I've been working through Moore's paradox and have ended up with an argument that seems to prove far too much — that we cannot be wrong about our own beliefs — and I cannot locate the misstep. Here it is:

(1) Asserting a sentence of the form "p, but I don't believe that p" is always absurd. Not merely surprising or conversationally odd, but somehow self-undermining in a way that ordinary falsehoods are not.

(2) The standard diagnosis is that the absurdity is pragmatic rather than semantic. To assert p is to represent oneself as believing p. So the act of asserting the second conjunct ("I don't believe that p") contradicts what the act of asserting the first conjunct already represents. The proposition expressed, however, is perfectly coherent: it could be true that p while I mistakenly think I don't believe it.

(3) Now shift from assertion to judgment. Suppose I try not to utter the Moorean sentence but to judge it true of myself — to quietly think, "p, but I don't believe that p." To succeed, I would need to judge the first conjunct (that p) and also judge the second (that I don't believe that p).

(4) But consider what happens when I judge that p. In judging that p, I come to believe that p. Moreover, my judgment that p gives me immediate grounds to self-ascribe the belief that p. First-person belief ascription is transparent in the following sense: to answer the question "Do I believe that p?" I do not introspect; I answer the question "Is p true?" and if I find myself judging that p, I thereby know I believe it.

(5) So judging the first conjunct automatically puts me in a position to judge that I do believe that p — which directly contradicts what I would need to judge for the second conjunct. The cognitive act required to verify the first conjunct destroys my grounds for the second.

(6) Therefore I cannot coherently judge a Moorean proposition true of myself. It is not that I try and fail; the structure of first-person judgment makes the attempt incoherent from the start.

(7) But if a proposition about my own mind is one I cannot even judge to be true — not because of contingent ignorance but because of the constitution of first-person thought — then that proposition cannot be true of me. A state of affairs that I am structurally barred from recognizing as actual is not a genuine possibility for my mental life. The mental is, in one's own case, minimally self-intimating.

(8) Hence the Moorean proposition is not merely unassertable; it cannot be true of the subject. First-person belief ascription is infallible: I cannot be wrong about what I believe.

(9) But this conclusion is absurd. People hold false beliefs about their own mental states all the time: through self-deception, through inattention, through plain confusion about what they think. The infallibility thesis denies this obvious fact.

The argument thus proves too much. Where does it go wrong? I am especially suspicious of the move from unjudgeability to impossibility in (7), but I cannot articulate the objection without risking the collapse of first-person authority into something merely contingent. If I must reject a premise, which one, and what is the cost? I would also like to know whether the argument can be blocked earlier — at the transparency claim in (4), for instance — and what that alternative would commit me to.
