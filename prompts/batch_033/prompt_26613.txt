I'm a retired physicist — spent 35 years doing spectroscopy, not AI — and in retirement I've been reading philosophy of mind, and I keep coming back to the frame problem. I now sit on the advisory board of a small company that make autonomous mobile robots for hospital logistics (delivering linens, meds, lab samples). The engineers has a recurring bug: the planner either checks absurd consequences (before moving, will the corridor wall still be the same color? I made that up, but you get the idea) or misses something that matters (moving a cart pulls the IV line or the power cord; opening a door changes which room is pressurized). They've asked me, the "philosophy guy," to turn the frame problem literature into a relevance filter: a procedure, run on every action the robot considers, that decides which consequences of the action the planner has to check before doing it.

So here's the ask. Act as a philosopher who works on the frame problem and philosophy of AI. Please write me that procedure. Constraints: it has to run in something like tens of milliseconds on an embedded processor; it has to be implementable by engineers who have no patience for jargon; and a hospital safety officer has to be able to read it and see where it can fail. It should be honest that the frame problem may not have a full solution — if the right answer is "you can't solve this in general, here's the best satisficing procedure and here's where it breaks," then say that. Where the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as an algorithm, mark it as such.

Ground it in what philosophers of mind and AI have actually said about the frame problem — the old robot-and-the-bomb-in-the-wagon story, the idea that relevance is the hard part, and the back-and-forth about whether there's a general solution at all. I want the distilled ideas, not a reading list, and no citations needed.

Then run the procedure on three of our real cases: (1) the robot is about to pass through a doorway while carrying a tray of infectious samples; (2) the robot is about to move a patient's overbed table out of the way; (3) the robot is about to plug itself into a wall charger in a corridor where patients are sometimes wheeled past. I expect the procedure will tell us something we don't want to hear about at least one of those.

Please think carefully before you write — maybe start by brainstorming what a relevance filter would even need to do, then work from there. And when you give me the procedure, tell me how confident you are in each main part of it, and single out the point you're least sure about. I'd rather have a self-critical answer than a confident wrong one.
