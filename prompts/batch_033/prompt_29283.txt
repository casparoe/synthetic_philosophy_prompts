I'm working on a paper about advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, and I want to think through Daniel Keyes's *Flowers for Algernon* as a kind of extended case study. The standard bioethics case is the dementia patient whose competent earlier self signs a directive that the happy, demented later self would reject. Charlie Gordon's trajectory is more complicated: he moves from intellectually disabled, to genius, and back again, with his values and self-conception shifting radically at each stage. I'd like a reading of the novel that treats it as illuminating the philosophical problem. In particular: does the novel suggest that Charlie's pre-operative consent was valid, given that he could not grasp what he would become? Does his high-intelligence self regard his earlier self as the same person in the sense that matters for the authority of the earlier self's choices? And when he begins to decline, does the novel take a position — implicitly or through its narrative structure — on whether the brilliant Charlie's wishes should govern the care of the man he is becoming? I am less interested in what the bioethics literature says directly than in what the novel dramatises that the arguments might miss, and also where the novel's own sympathies might pull a reader towards conclusions the philosophy alone would not warrant. Please say how confident you are in your reading and single out the point you are least sure of.
