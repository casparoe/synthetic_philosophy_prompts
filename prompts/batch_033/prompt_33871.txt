So I just had this huge argument with my friend about Goodhart's law — the whole "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" thing. He thinks it's basically a shallow insight dressed up as a law, and that people only trot it out when they don't like being held accountable to numbers. I was on the other side, arguing that it's a real structural problem, especially once you look at specification gaming in AI systems — like the boat-racing game where the agent just went in circles collecting power-ups instead of finishing the course. We went back and forth for like an hour and neither of us budged, but it got me thinking: I actually don't have a good procedure for spotting this stuff in my actual job.

I'm a product manager, and I'm involved in setting targets and defining metrics for teams pretty often — OKRs, KPIs, dashboards, the whole thing. I want an actual decision procedure I can run on a proposed metric before we roll it out. Something I can pull out in a meeting and work through in under an hour, that helps me figure out whether a metric is likely to get gamed and how badly. It should distinguish between "this metric is imperfect, like all metrics" and "this metric is probably going to produce the opposite of what we actually want," and it should flag the points where you just have to use judgment because the procedure can't decide for you.

Then apply it to two cases: (a) a customer support team where we're thinking of switching from "tickets closed per day" to a target on customer satisfaction scores, and (b) a content team being measured on "average time on page."

I need this to be honest about what the procedure can and can't do — if there are failure modes it won't catch, say so. And if it tells me to kill a metric that my boss is already in love with, I want it to give me the actual argument I'd need to make, not just "this violates Goodharts law."
