I am a philosophy professor, and next term I am giving a paper at a workshop on realism and anti-realism in the philosophy of language. The paper defends a restriction strategy for Fitch's paradox of knowability. I argue that the anti-realist should not assert the unrestricted knowability principle—the claim that every truth is knowable—but should restrict it to a privileged class of basic propositions: propositions whose truth can be made manifest in a canonical verification and which do not embed claims about what is or is not known. The Church–Fitch derivation, I claim, depends on applying the principle to a proposition of the form p and it is not known that p, which falls outside that class; once the restriction is in place, the derivation is blocked and there is no collapse to the conclusion that every truth is actually known. I also argue that the main alternatives—moving to intuitionistic logic, rejecting the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, or reformulating the principle with an actuality operator—either concede too much to the classical logician or fail to track the anti-realist's underlying motivation.

The audience is mixed. Several participants work in the Dummettian tradition and are already sympathetic to intuitionistic logic; they will suspect that my restriction is ad hoc and that the honest anti-realist should simply go intuitionist. A few are classical logicians who think the knowability principle was always a bad idea and that Fitch's result is a reason to abandon anti-realism outright. There are also two epistemologists who work on closure and who will press the point that the real lesson concerns the logic of knowledge rather than the scope of the knowability principle.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I want the objections ordered by how likely they are and by how much damage they would do, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, and, where it matters, indicating who is likely to raise each. For each objection, give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest—what a well-prepared member of the audience could say next. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should refuse to concede, and include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. If, in your judgment, the restriction I am defending cannot be made to work as stated, or the request rests on a mischaracterization of the alternatives, tell me that plainly rather than writing a prep sheet for a doomed position; I would rather revise the paper now than discover it in the room.
