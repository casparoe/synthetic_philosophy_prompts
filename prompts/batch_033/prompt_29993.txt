I've been thinking about hard choices — the kind where you're torn between two things that matter in completely different ways, like whether to take a demanding job you'd find meaningful or stay in a quieter role that leaves time for family. I've arrived at a handful of views I find plausible, but I can't tell whether they actually fit together. I'd be grateful for an audit.

Here's where I've landed:

(a) Some values are genuinely incommensurable. There is no single scale — not even a rough or implicit one — on which, say, career fulfilment and family intimacy can be weighed against each other.

(b) When you face a hard choice between incommensurable options, it is rationally permissible to choose either one. Neither choice is a mistake; you aren't failing to respond to reasons correctly whichever way you go.

(c) If two options are incommensurable, and you take one of them and improve it slightly along a dimension it was already strong on, the improved version remains incommensurable with the other option. A tiny nudge doesn't suddenly make one option better than the other.

(d) The 'better than' relation is transitive: if X is better than Y, and Y is better than Z, then X is better than Z. Giving this up seems too costly.

(e) Incommensurability is not the same as equality. If two options are equal, you should be indifferent between them — swapping one for the other costs you nothing. But in a hard choice between incommensurable options, something is genuinely lost whichever way you go, and that's why the choice hurts.

Do these five claims hang together? If not, which ones are in tension, how severe is each tension, and what's the least costly way to restore consistency?
