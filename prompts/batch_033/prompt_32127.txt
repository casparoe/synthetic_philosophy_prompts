I am a novelist by trade, and I have stumbled into a piece of reasoning that has been troubling me for months. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

The argument concerns the reality of virtual and fictional harms. It runs as follows:

(1) A harm is real if and only if it consists in the setting back of a genuine interest of a subject.

(2) Subjects can and do form genuine interests in entities and states of affairs that are not physically instantiated: a player cares about her in-game house, a reader cares about the fate of a fictional character, a person in a virtual world cares about her reputation there.

(3) When those interests are set back—when the in-game house is destroyed, when the character suffers a tragic fate, when the virtual reputation is ruined—a genuine interest has been set back, and so a real harm has occurred.

(4) The moral weight of a harm is proportional to the weight of the interest set back, not to the physical substrate of the states of affairs involved.

(5) Some interests in virtual or fictional states of affairs are weightier than some interests in physical states of affairs. (I care more about the integrity of my novel's central character than I care about a stranger's car getting scratched.)

(6) Therefore, at least some harms involving merely virtual or fictional objects are morally weightier than some harms involving physical objects.

(7) If that is true, then in writing a novel in which a character suffers greatly, I am causing a real harm of nontrivial moral weight—and I do so deliberately, for aesthetic or commercial reasons.

(8) But that conclusion is absurd. Novelists are not wrongdoers merely for writing tragic fiction.

The argument seems to show that virtual and fictional harms are real harms carrying real moral weight, and that I am implicated in producing them. I cannot accept the conclusion, yet each premise looks plausible to me individually. I am not asking whether the conclusion is true—I am confident it is not. I am asking which premise or step to reject, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, I would like to know:

- Which premise or step is the weakest, and why?
- Is there an equivocation somewhere—perhaps on "genuine interest" or on "real harm"?
- If I reject premise (2) by denying that interests in the non-physical are genuine, do I lose the ability to say that, e.g., a malicious hacker who destroys a player's years of in-game work has done something genuinely wrong?
- If I reject premise (4) by insisting that physical substrate matters, how do I draw the line without its looking ad hoc?
- If the argument is valid and the only escape is to bite the bullet on (8), what does a world look like in which novelists do bear genuine moral responsibility for the suffering they write into their fiction?

I would prefer to keep premise (1) and premise (5) if possible, but I am open to being told that is not possible.

Please conclude with a one-sentence summary of the diagnosis, formatted as: \answer{...}
