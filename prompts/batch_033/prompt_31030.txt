Two members of my philosophy reading group, Daniel and Mara, recently argued about Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language (1982), and neither would budge. I am setting out their exchange as faithfully as I can. Neither of them is here, and I honestly cannot tell who has the better of it; I would like you to adjudicate the dispute move by move.

The background they set up is this. Kripke's sceptic notes that I have performed only finitely many sums in my life. Suppose I have never added numbers as large as 57. The sceptic proposes that by 'plus' I meant 'quus', where x quus y equals x + y if both numbers are below 57, and equals 5 otherwise. When I now encounter 68 + 57, I confidently answer 125. But all my past behaviour is equally consistent with my having meant quus rather than plus, since every pair I have ever summed falls below the threshold. The sceptic concludes that no fact about my past use, mental images, or dispositions determines that I meant plus rather than quus.

Then the exchange:

(1) Daniel: The sceptic's challenge has a straightforward answer. What I meant by 'plus' is fixed by my dispositions. I was disposed, had I been asked, to answer 125 rather than 5 for that sum. So there is a fact about me — a dispositional fact — that selects plus over quus.

(2) Mara: Dispositions are the wrong kind of thing to fix meaning. A disposition describes how I would in fact respond, but I might be disposed to make mistakes when tired, distracted, or faced with very large numbers. A rule tells me how I ought to respond, not merely how I am caused to respond. The dispositional story cannot capture the normativity of meaning — the distinction between conforming to a rule and merely being prompted to say something.

(3) Daniel: That only shows we need to idealise. We should say I meant plus because, under normal conditions and in the absence of interference, my dispositions converge on the plus function. Error is simply a matter of the disposition being blocked or distorted. The idealisation is no more mysterious than any other scientific idealisation.

(4) Mara: But the idealisation is doing all the work, and it is illegitimate. 'Normal conditions' and 'interference' must be specified so that every divergence from plus counts as interference. That is exactly the gerrymander the sceptic exploits: relative to one specification of normal conditions, my dispositions select plus; relative to another, they select quus. There is no fact about me that privileges one specification over the other. So the dispositionalist has not answered the sceptic; he has presupposed the answer.

(5) Daniel: Still, the sceptical conclusion is absurd. I do not, and never did, mean quus. Anyone who knows me knows that. If your argument forces us to deny this, the argument is a reductio of its own premises, not a discovery about meaning.

(6) Mara: You are misstating the conclusion. The sceptic does not claim you meant quus. The claim is that no fact about your past use, mental images, or dispositions constitutes your having meant plus rather than quus. It is a denial of a certain kind of fact, not a rival hypothesis about which function you meant. The point is that meaning is not a self-standing state of an individual.

(7) Daniel: But then you owe us an account of why meaning talk is legitimate at all. If there is no fact of the matter, then your own words in this argument are no more meaningful than mine — and the sceptic's challenge itself presupposes the very meaning it purports to undermine. That is self-defeating.

(8) Mara: That is why Kripke's Wittgenstein offers a sceptical solution: we keep the practice but change the metaphysics. To say someone means plus is not to describe a hidden state with truth conditions; it is to license or correct their responses against the community's practice. Your answer of 125 is correct because the community, having been trained in arithmetic, would give that answer. Normativity enters at the social level: the community can hold you to a pattern of use.

(9) Daniel: But the community is just a collection of individuals. If no individual's dispositions fix a unique rule, aggregating them cannot magic one into existence. And the community could all be disposed to answer 5 for large sums; by your criterion, quus would then be correct. Correctness cannot be a majority vote, because whole communities can be wrong about things.

(10) Mara: You are treating the community as if it were one more large individual that could be right or wrong against an independent standard. But for basic rule-governed practices, there is no independent standard; the shared practice constitutes the standard. An individual can be wrong by diverging from it. The community's agreement is not evidence of a prior fact; it is the background against which correctness is attributed. That asymmetry is what makes individual error possible and community error, at the ground level, an empty notion.

They asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate the dispute move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, partly right and partly wrong, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and explain what, if anything, both parties are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
