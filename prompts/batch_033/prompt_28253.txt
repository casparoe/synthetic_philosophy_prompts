I'm at this week-long epistemology workshop in Edinburgh — the kind where by day three evryone's brain is completely fried from talking about Bayesian vs. frequentist approaches — and we're all at the pub afterwards, as you do. Someone brings up Hempel's raven paradox, you know the one: "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens", so technically every time you spot a white shoe or a red apple you're gathering evidence that ravens are black. The table gets into a proper row about whether this is a genuine problem for confirmation theory or just a quirk of first-order logic we've all agreed to politely ignore.

Anyway, my mate dares me to write a joke about it — not just some throwaway pun where you swap "raven" for "craven" or whatever, but something where the punchline actually turns on the logical structure of the paradox itself. The kind of joke that would land with people who've actually thought about Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition, but would still be funny rather than just clever. It should make sense too: nobody hearing it should think I've misunderstood what the paradox is even about.

Oh, and write the whole thing in British English, ta.
