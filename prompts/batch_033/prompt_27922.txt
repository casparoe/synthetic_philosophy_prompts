Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four is often read as the fictional counterpart of the argument in his essay Politics and the English Language: that political and linguistic decay are reciprocal, and that a sufficiently controlled language could narrow the range of thought itself. I'd like a critical reading of the novel's treatment of Newspeak—especially Syme's enthusiasm for the dictionary project and the Appendix's account of the principles of Newspeak—set against the essay's actual argument, with attention to where the two texts diverge rather than simply illustrate each other. The essay recommends a kind of individual linguistic discipline: avoiding stale figures, inflated diction, and words that carry no clear meaning, and preferring concrete, active language. The novel, by contrast, presents a language deliberately engineered by an institution to make certain thoughts, and ultimately the thought of rebellion, unavailable. Is the novel merely dramatizing the essay's thesis, or does it alter the thesis by relocating the source of linguistic corruption from careless habit to deliberate design? Does the Appendix, which describes Newspeak from the outside, in Standard English and in the past tense, quietly concede that a totalitarian language cannot fully close off the standpoint from which it can be described and criticized? And what should a reader make of the essay's optimism—its conviction that the decay of language can be reversed by voluntary effort—once the novel is taken seriously? I'm teaching the two texts together and want an account of where Orwell the essayist and Orwell the novelist are not quite saying the same thing.
