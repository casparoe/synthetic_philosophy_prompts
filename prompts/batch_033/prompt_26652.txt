I've been following the AI alignment discourse on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for years, and one thing that keeps bothering me is a tension I see in how the major AI labs actually build and talk about their systems. On the one hand, the official line from labs like Anthropic and OpenAI is that they are building "tools" or "assistants," not "agents" — systems meant to answer questions and follow instructions, not to pursue open-ended goals in the world. On the other hand, the actual training pipeline tells a different story: these models go through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), where they are quite literally trained to maximize a reward signal — human preference scores. That is the classic setup for creating an agent: a policy that selects actions to maximize expected cumulative reward. And the models are increasingly given affordances like chain-of-thought reasoning, tool use, and the ability to take multi-step actions, all of which blur the line further.

What I want to understand is: what theory of agency — or what theory of the tool/agent distinction — does this practice actually presuppose? When a lab trains a model via RLHF to be "helpful, harmless, and honest" and then calls the result a tool, are they implicitly committed to the view that agency requires something more than reward-maximization (e.g., self-awareness, preferences about the world, a persistent identity)? Or are they committed to the view that the tool/agent distinction is not a binary but a spectrum, and that RLHF'd models fall below some threshold? Or is the practice philosophically inconsistent — does the training methodology presuppose an agent while the deployment framing presupposes a tool, and the labs are simply hoping no one notices? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) that would make this practice coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change — in the training, the deployment, or the rhetoric — if the labs adopted each of the main rival views about what separates a tool from an agent.
