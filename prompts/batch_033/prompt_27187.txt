And here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

"The absent-minded driver problem (introduced by Piccione and Rubinstein, 1997) describes a driver who plans to exit a highway at the second exit. The driver knows they will become absent-minded — when they encounter an exit, they won't remember whether it's the first or second. The payoffs are: exit at first exit = 0, exit at second = 4, miss both = 1.

Since the driver cannot condition their action on which exit they're at, they must adopt a uniform behavioral rule: 'when I see an exit, I exit with probability p.' The expected payoff of this policy is: EU(p) = p·0 + p(1-p)·4 + (1-p)²·1 = 4p - 4p² + 1 - 2p + p² = 1 + 2p - 3p². Maximizing: d/dp = 2 - 6p = 0, so p = 1/3, giving EU = 4/3.

Now what does CDT recommend? At the moment of decision the driver faces an exit and doesn't know which one. CDT says to maximize expected utility given your beliefs, treating your action as causally independent of the state. Since the driver has no information distinguishing the two exits, they should assign P(exit 1) = P(exit 2) = 1/2. Then EU(exit) = (1/2)(0) + (1/2)(4) = 2. For EU(continue): if at exit 1 and continue, you'll reach exit 2 and face the same decision; if at exit 2 and continue, you get 1. But if continuing dominates at exit 1 it also dominates at exit 2, so the driver continues at both and gets 1. So CDT effectively recommends p = 0, yielding EU = 1, which is worse than 4/3. CDT gives bad advice here.

EDT handles this better. EDT evaluates actions by how the outcome would correlate with them evidentially — it uses P(state | action) rather than unconditional P(state). If you plan to exit with probability p, then conditional on actually exiting at an encounter, you're more likely to be at exit 2 than exit 1, because at exit 1 you might have already exited. The equilibrium calculation under these conditional probabilities yields p = 1/3, the optimal policy. EDT gets the right answer.

The lesson is that CDT fails when an agent's action provides evidence about a relevant state, even absent a causal link — the same lesson as Newcomb's problem. The absent-minded driver is structurally Newcomb-like, and EDT is the superior theory."
