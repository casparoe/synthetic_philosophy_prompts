I've been dipping into the endurantism vs. perdurantism debate in metaphysics — you know, the one about how objects persist through time. Endurantists say an object is wholly present at each moment it exists, so change is just the very same thing having incompatible properties at different times (the banana itself is green, then the banana itself is yellow). Perdurantists say objects are four-dimensional, spread out in time the way they're spread out in space, with temporal parts that have the properties simpliciter — so there's a green temporal part and a yellow temporal part, and the banana is the whole worm. Both camps have been at this since at least the 80s, and I've been reading through a bunch of the back-and-forth for a reading group I'm in.

Here's what's bugging me: as far as I can tell, nobody's moved. Both sides can handle the standard puzzle cases — the statue and the lump of clay, the Ship of Theseus, amoeba fission — by adding the right epicycles. Both sides have a semantics that captures our ordinary talk about objects persisting and changing. Both sides are consistent with special relativity (or at least both have stories about why they are, even if the endurantist's story is more strained). And yet people still write papers and seem to think there's a fact of the matter about whether objects endure or perdure. I'm starting to suspect the whole thing is really a disagreement about what we want from a metaphysical theory — elegance, match with ordinary language, parsimony of kinds, continuity with physics — and not about how the world actually is. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a substantive question hiding in there that I'm just not seeing because I'm new to this.

So my question: diagnose this impasse. Is it a verbal dispute, a clash of theoretical virtues, or is there a genuine factual question that neither side has managed to settle? And whatever your diagnosis, tell me what argument or consideration would change your mind about it — where's your reasoning most likely to go wrong here?
