(2) I'm a pluralist about scoring rules. The Brier score, the log score, and the spherical score are all legitimate measures of inaccuracy. I don't think there's a uniquely correct one.

(3) The accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism succeeds. Credences that violate the probability axioms are accuracy-dominated by credences that satisfy them, and this gives a decisive epistemic reason to conform to the axioms.

(4) The expected-accuracy argument for conditionalization succeeds. If you know you'll learn which member of a partition is true, conditionalizing on that evidence uniquely maximizes expected accuracy from your current perspective.

(5) Rational credences are immodest: a rational agent should not expect any alternative credence function to be strictly more accurate than her own. Her credences should be self-recommending.

(6) The Principle of Indifference can be given a genuine accuracy-first justification. When your evidence is symmetric with respect to a partition, the uniquely rational prior is the one that maximizes entropy — equivalently, the one that minimizes worst-case expected inaccuracy.

(7) The Reflection Principle holds: your current credence in any proposition should equal your current expectation of your future credence in that proposition.
