I'm a novelist working on a book set in a consciousness research lab. The characters have different philosophical positions—some are realists about the hard problem, some are illusionists, one is obsessed with the meta-problem—and I want the intellectual architecture to be philosophically sound even if the characters are messy. In the process of researching this I've ended up with my own set of views, and I want to know if they actually hang together before I build the whole book's stakes on them.

Here's where I've landed:

(a) I'm a physicalist. Consciousness is physical, full stop.

(b) The hard problem is genuine. It's not a pseudo-problem or a confusion. There's a real explanatory gap between physical facts and phenomenal facts, even if the gap is ultimately closable.

(c) The meta-problem—explaining why we think there's a hard problem, why we have phenomenal intuitions—is a distinct and worthwhile project. It's not just the hard problem restated.

(d) Illusionism is false. Consciousness really exists; we aren't merely under the impression that we have phenomenal experience.

(e) Phenomenal concepts are special in kind—recognitional, or constitutively tied to experience, or something like that—and this explains why the explanatory gap feels so unbridgeable.

(f) A successful solution to the meta-problem would substantially demystify the hard problem. It would show that the hard problem arises from features of our own cognitive architecture rather than from anything ontologically spooky.

I keep going back and forth on wether (f) is compatible with (b) and (d), and whether (e) undercuts the need for (c)—if phenomenal concepts already explain the gap, what's left for the meta-problem to do? Go through these and tell me: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest way to restore consistency. Rank the tensions by severity.

For the response, structure it as: "Genuine tensions" (ordered by severity, ~150 words each), "Apparent tensions that dissolve" (~150 words each), and "Cheapest fix" (~200 words on the single revision that would clean up the most).
