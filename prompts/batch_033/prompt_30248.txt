I am a philosophy professor preparing a single graduate seminar session on Fitch's paradox of knowability for students who have had only an introductory logic course and no exposure to modal logic. Do not explain the paradox to me; give me a teaching plan. Specify the order in which the ingredients must be introduced — the knowability principle, the factivity of knowledge, the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, and the claim that p is true though no one knows it — and say what each later step depends on. Choose one carrying example of an unknown truth and say what it must and must not have; for instance, a fact the audience already knows would fail because the premise that it is unknown would be false, and a conjecture whose truth-value is unsettled would fail because the example must be accepted as true. Identify the misconceptions I should expect — such as sliding from 'some truth is unknown' to 'some truth is unknowable' before the derivation has licensed that step, or dismissing the argument as a self-reference trick — and say at which step of the sequence each should be headed off. End with one question or short exercise that would show whether the students understand why this is a paradox for verificationism rather than a piece of logical trickery, and state explicitly what I should leave out at this level.
