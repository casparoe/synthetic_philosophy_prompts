I work as a software engineer at a company that builds and operates autonomous delivery vehicles. After any incident — a collision, a near miss, a routing failure that stranded a customer — we run a formal post-mortem. The process is well established: we reconstruct the system state, trace the decision pathway through the model, and identify contributing factors. What I find puzzling is the way the post-mortem process handles responsibility differently in two cases that seem structurally similar.

Case A: The vehicle was operating in fully autonomous mode. The perception system misclassified a pedestrian as a static object, the planner chose an unsafe trajectory, and the vehicle struck the pedestrian. The post-mortem concluded with a set of engineering fixes — retrain on edge cases, add a redundant sensor modality, adjust the planner's cost function. No individual was held responsible in any meaningful sense. The failure was attributed to "the system," and the response was purely technical.

Case B: The vehicle was operating in the same mode, but a remote safety operator had been monitoring the feed and had a three-second window to intervene. The operator did not intervene in time. The same underlying perception error occurred. The post-mortem still recommended engineering fixes, but it also resulted in a formal performance review for the operator, a note on their employment record, and in a previous instance someone was dismissed.

The operator in Case B could not realistically have detected the misclassification in those three seconds — the feed showed grainy video with nearly a second of latency, and the misclassification was subtle. The operator's failure was, in practice, as inevitable as the system's. Yet the process treats the two cases as fundamentally different: one is an engineering problem, the other is a personnel problem.

I want to understand what theory of responsibility this practice presupposes. It seems to treat the mere presence of a human in the loop — even an ineffective one — as transforming the nature of the failure. What implicit view of moral or legal responsibility would make this distinction coherent? And is the practice consistent with itself, or does it rely on incompatible ideas in different parts of the process?
