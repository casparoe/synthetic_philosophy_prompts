**Question:** Explain Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and how it bears on the principle of epistemic closure. Does the tracking theory's departure from closure represent a net gain or a net loss for our overall picture of knowledge? Defend your assessment.

**Student Answer:**

Robert Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge proposes that the traditional justified true belief analysis is incomplete and that knowledge should instead be understood in terms of subjunctive conditionals that track the truth. On Nozick's view, S knows that P if and only if four conditions hold: (1) P is true, (2) S believes that P, (3) if P were false, S would not believe that P (the sensitivity condition), and (4) if P were true, S would believe that P (the adherence condition). Conditions (3) and (4) are the distinctive contribution of the theory; they require that S's belief covaries with the truth of P across nearby possible worlds.

The principle of epistemic closure under known entailment states that if S knows that P, and S knows that P entails Q, then S knows (or is in a position to know) that Q. Closure underwrites much of our ordinary reasoning—we routinely extend our knowledge by deduction. However, Nozick's tracking theory forces us to abandon closure. The reason is that the sensitivity condition is not closed under entailment. I can be sensitive to P without being sensitive to Q, even when P entails Q. Nozick's central example is the skeptical hypothesis: I know that I have hands, because if I did not have hands (having lost them in an accident, say), I would not believe that I have hands—the belief is sensitive. But consider the proposition that I am not a brain in a vat being fed experiences of having hands. This proposition is entailed by my having hands, yet my belief that I am not a brain in a vat fails the sensitivity condition: if I were a brain in a vat, I would still believe I was not one, so my belief does not track the truth.

The payoff of this departure from closure is a direct and elegant response to external-world skepticism. The skeptic argues that since I do not know I am not a brain in a vat, and I know that having hands entails not being a brain in a vat, I cannot know I have hands. Nozick blocks the skeptic by denying closure: I can know I have hands without knowing the denial of the skeptical hypothesis. This is a significant theoretical benefit because it avoids the skeptical conclusion without requiring us to adopt heroic epistemological measures.

Importantly, the cost of abandoning closure is smaller than critics allege, because the tracking theory only severs closure in cases involving remote skeptical hypotheses. In ordinary chains of deductive reasoning—such as moving from "this is a zebra" to "this is a mammal"—closure is preserved, because the same sensitivity that secures knowledge of the premise typically carries through to the conclusion. The worry that Nozick's view would prevent us from extending knowledge by deduction in everyday contexts is therefore overstated.

Additionally, Nozick's theory has a further advantage that is sometimes overlooked: the adherence condition helps handle Gettier cases. In a standard Gettier scenario, a belief happens to be true by luck, and in nearby worlds where the same proposition is true (but the luck is absent), the subject would not continue to hold the belief. The adherence condition rules out such lucky true beliefs, which gives the tracking theory resources to address the Gettier problem as well as the skeptical one.

For these reasons, the tracking theory's departure from closure is a net gain: the loss is confined to a narrow class of cases that we have independent reason to treat as epistemically special, while the gain is a clean dissolution of the skeptical paradox that has troubled epistemology since Descartes.
