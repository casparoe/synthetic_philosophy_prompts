A popular philosophy of science book I read summarizes the underdetermination thesis like this: "Quine showed that for any scientific theory, no matter how well confirmed, there are always infinitely many alternative theories that fit the exact same data equally well. This means that evidence never forces us to accept one theory over another, that theory choice is always underdetermined by the data, and that the success of science is therefore a kind of miracle — or, more likely, a sign that we should be antirealists about scientific theories. The history of science supports this: for every successful theory, from Ptolemaic astronomy to Newtonian mechanics, there was always an empirically equivalent rival that was discarded for non-empirical reasons, which shows that theory choice is guided by values and conventions, not by evidence."

I'm suspicious of a few things here. The claim that there are "infinitely many" alternatives that fit the "exact same data equally well" sounds stronger than what I remember from the literature, and the jump from underdetermination to antirealism seems too quick. But I'm not a specialist, and the author is a well-regarded philosopher. Can you go through this summary and tell me what a philosopher of science would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the antirealist conclusion follows from the accurate parts?
