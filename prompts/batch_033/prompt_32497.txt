I've been running a reading group on consequentialism and its critics, and we've spent the last three sessions on the demandingness objection. One of the members — a grad student sympathetic to consequentialism — built an argument that's been bothering me ever since, because it seems to show that the demandingness objection cuts far deeper than its proponents usually admit, and I can't tell whether that's a feature or a bug.

Here's the argument, reconstructed from his handout:

(1) The demandingness objection says: if a moral theory requires agents to sacrifice everything that gives their lives shape and meaning — close relationships, personal projects, leisure, even modest self-care — simply because the world contains vast unmet need, then that theory is too demanding to be correct.
(2) What drives the objection is not the maximizing structure of consequentialism in particular, but the combination of two ideas: (a) everyone's welfare matters, and (b) there are no principled limits on how much one person can be required to sacrifice for others when the need is great enough.
(3) Any moral theory that includes an impartial duty of beneficence — a duty to promote the good of others that is not limited to special relationships or nearness — will contain both (a) and (b) in some form, unless it artificially caps the duty at a level that makes it trivial.
(4) Given the actual scale of global suffering and inequality, an uncapped impartial duty of beneficence will, under realistic empirical assumptions, require agents to sacrifice nearly everything they value, because the marginal good one can do for the global poor with a dollar or an hour of one's time is enormous, and remains enormous across a very wide range of giving.
(5) So the demandingness objection, if sound, rules out not just act-consequentialism but any moral theory that contains a non-trivial impartial duty of beneficence — including moderate deontology, contractualism, and many pluralist views.
(6) But a moral theory with no serious duty to help distant strangers in need is itself implausible; it would mean that morality has almost nothing to say about global poverty, famine, or preventable disease.
(7) Therefore, either the demandingness objection proves too much (and we should be suspicious of it), or there is some feature peculiar to consequentialism — not just impartial beneficence — that generates the extreme demands, and the objection doesn't generalize.

I'm genuinely stuck. The argument seems to show that anyone who rejects consequentialism on demandingness grounds must either gerrymander their own theory to avoid the same fate, or quietly accept that their theory has nothing meaningful to say about distant strangers. But I don't know whether the right move is to accept the argument and give up on the demandingness objection, or to locate a flaw in it and pay the price.
