I'm reading Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (1895) and I keep half-getting it but then loose the thread. The setup is that Achilles has agreed that anyone who accepts A and B must accept Z, and now the Tortoise dares him to force Z on someone who accepts A and B but won't accept the hypothetical. The part I'm stuck on is this:

    "I'm to force you to accept Z, am I?" Achilles said musingly. "And your present position is that you accept A and B, but you don't accept the Hypothetical --"
    "Let's call it C," said the Tortoise.
    "-- but you don't accept
    (C) If A and B are true, Z must be true."
    "That is my present position," said the Tortoise.
    "Then I must ask you to accept C."
    "I'll do so," said the Tortoise, "as soon as you've entered it in that note-book of yours. What else have you got in it?"

Then the Tortoise has Achilles write C into the notebook like it's just another premise, and as soon as C is accepted the same problem reappears: the move from A, B, and C to Z gets treated as a further premise D, then E, and so on. The lines that really throw me are near the end:

    "Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down," said the Tortoise. "So enter it in your book, please. We will call it
    (E) If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true. Until I've granted that, of course I needn't grant Z."

What exactly is the Tortoise doing? Is he making a real point about the difference between a premise and a rule of inference, or is he just being a sophist? And I've seen people connect this dialogue to the epistemic regress problem — foundationalism vs. coherentism vs. infinitism — but I can't tell whether the Tortoise's regress is literally the same regress or only a loose analogy. You're an epistemologist who works on the structure of justification. Start by brainstorming exactly what the Tortoise has granted and what he's still withholding, then walk me through the passage slowly, and say which parts carry over to the regress problem for justification and which don't.
