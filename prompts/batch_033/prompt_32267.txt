I've gotten myself tangled up in this argument and I can't figure out where the slip is. The conclusion seems way too strong, but each step looks right to me.

(1) The standard semantics for conditionals says: "If A, C" is true just in case C is true in all the closest possible worlds where A is true.

(2) This semantics is supposed to work for both indicative conditionals ("If A, C") and counterfactual conditionals ("If A had been, C would have been"). They're both "if"-sentences — why would the word "if" mean something different in each case?

(3) Take the classic pair. Indicative: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did." True. Counterfactual: "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have." False.

(4) For the indicative to come out true under (1), the closest worlds where Oswald didn't kill Kennedy must be worlds where Kennedy was still killed (so someone else pulled the trigger). That means we hold fixed the fact that Kennedy was killed.

(5) For the counterfactual to come out false under (1), the closest worlds where Oswald didn't kill Kennedy must be worlds where Kennedy wasn't killed at all — the assassination just didn't happen. So we don't hold fixed that Kennedy was killed.

(6) But "closest worlds" is supposed to be an objective matter — similarity to the single actual world. There's only one actual world, so there can only be one ordering of which worlds are closest to it.

(7) So (1) plus (2) plus (6) forces the indicative and the counterfactual to have the same truth value. But they don't. So at least one of (1), (2), or (6) is false.

(8) If (2) is false, then "if" is ambiguous — it has one meaning in indicative mood and a different meaning in subjunctive mood. But that's wild: it's the same word, kids learn it without noticing any ambiguity, and we can mix indicatives and counterfactuals in the same sentence ("If he had come, and if I'm right about his motives, then..."). How can a single lexical item be ambiguous in a way nobody notices?

(9) If (6) is false, then there's no objective closeness ordering — the ordering depends on the conversational context or the speaker's epistemic state. But then conditionals don't have objective truth values; they're true-relative-to-a-context in a way that makes them not really propositions at all. That's the Gibbardian conclusion: conditionals don't express propositions. But that seems nuts — we embed them under negation, in propositional attitude reports, and so on.

(10) If (1) is false, we lose the best semantics anyone has for conditionals, and we're back to the material conditional analysis (which gives us the paradoxes of material implication) or something even worse.

So I'm stuck. Where does this argument go wrong, and what's the least bad fix?
