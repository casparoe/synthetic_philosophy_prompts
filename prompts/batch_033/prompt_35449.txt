I'm a UX research lead at a mid-size tech company, and I keep noticing that our user interview protocols assume something like a Cartesian theater — we ask people "what was your experience using feature X" as if there's a single, stable experience they can just replay and report. I've read enough Dennett to know this is probably wrong, that what we get is a draft constructed at the moment of asking, shaped by the question itself. But I don't know how to turn that insight into something my team can actually use. Could you write me a practical interview-and-interpretation procedure grounded in the multiple drafts model? It should be a step-by-step guide my researchers can follow: what questions to ask instead of the standard ones, what follow-ups probe for the construction process rather than pretending to access a fixed memory, what kinds of answers should be treated as artifacts of the method rather than data, and where the procedure forces a judgment call that can't be offloaded onto the theory. Then walk it through two example interviews — one where the procedure catches something the standard approach would miss, and one where the procedure itself probably creates a misleading draft and you have to flag that. My team has about 30 minutes per interview and moderate qual-research training but no philosophy background.
