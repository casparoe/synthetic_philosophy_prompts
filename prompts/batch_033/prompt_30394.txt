I've been teaching a grad seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and I've found myself defending the common prior assumption more than I expected to. For context, the CPA says roughly that rational agents starting from the same background information should have the same prior probability distribution — any differences in their posterior beliefs should be fully explainable by differences in the evidence they've each encountered. Aumann's agreement theorem is the canonical formal result behind this, but the philosophical version of the claim goes beyond the theorem's conditions.

I'm not a fanatic about it — I don't think every violation of the CPA is a failure of rationality. But I do think the assumption is the right default: if two people disagree, the interesting explanation is almost always that they have different information, not that they have incommensurable fundamental outlooks. And I think appeals to "different priors" are too often a conversation-stopper.

Here's what I want: Tell me what would count against this view. Not just the standard counterarguments people always give — I know the usual moves. I want a map of what the view actually risks. What kinds of evidence or arguments should make me substantially revise it, and which are merely awkward? Distinguish between:

- Things that would be fatal if established (e.g., a clear case where we have every reason to think two people share all relevant evidence and are fully rational, yet their disagreement persists and isn't resolvable by further information-sharing).
- Things that are awkward but the view can absorb with reasonable qualifications.
- Things that critics treat as problems but a defender of the CPA should not concede as counting against it at all.

Some specific things I'd like you to address: Does the view make empirical predictions that distinguish it from rivals (like permissivism about rational credences), or has it been insulated so that no possible observation could disconfirm it? If it has been insulated, is that a problem for a normative claim of this kind, or is that just what we should expect? Also, I find the "impracticality" objection — that real agents can't compute the common prior even if it exists — to be pretty weak, but maybe I'm wrong about that. I'd like to know if a defender of my position should be more worried about it than I am.

Basically, I want an honest assessment of how exposed this view is, from someone who isn't just trying to score points against it. If the honest conclusion is that it's mostly a methodological commitment rather than a falsifiable thesis, say so — and say whether that's fine or whether it undermines the whole thing.
