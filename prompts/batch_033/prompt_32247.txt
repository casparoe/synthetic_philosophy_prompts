Question:

Explain the Dutch book argument for probabilism. What does the argument purport to show, and what assumptions does it rely on? Present one significant objection and assess how much damage it does.

Student answer:

The Dutch book argument is an argument for probabilism, the claim that rational degrees of belief must satisfy the axioms of probability. It works by showing that if an agent's degrees of belief violate the axioms, then there is a combination of bets, each of which is fair according to the agent's own degrees of belief, such that the agent would accept each bet individually but the combination guarantees a loss no matter what happens. The argument is standardly paired with the converse result that no such sure-loss package can be constructed against an agent whose credences satisfy the axioms. Since a rational agent cannot be vulnerable to a guaranteed loss of this kind, any agent whose credences violate the axioms is irrational, and so rational credences must be probabilities. The argument assumes that the agent's fair betting price for a bet that pays one unit if the relevant proposition is true and nothing otherwise is equal to her degree of belief in that proposition, that she is willing to take either side of any bet at that price, and that the payoffs are measured in a currency whose marginal value is constant for the agent. The main objection I want to discuss is that the argument presupposes that every proposition has a single precise degree of belief. But many rational agents have imprecise credences: their doxastic states are better represented by a set of probability functions than by one function. Because such agents do not have a unique fair price to attach to each bet, the argument cannot get started against them, so it fails to establish probabilism.
