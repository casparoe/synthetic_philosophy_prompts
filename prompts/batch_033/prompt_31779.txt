I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give a ninety-minute masterclass for fiction writers on constructing characters who face genuinely hard choices — the kind where there is no right answer, where the character loses something irreplaceable no matter what they do. I want to ground the session in the philosophical concept of incommensurable values, because I've found that most writers tacitly reach for commensuration: the character weighs pros and cons in some implicit common currency, and that flattens the drama. But I need to teach the concept itself, not the fiction techniques, so the writers understand what they are reaching for. The audience is intelligent but philosophically untrained; they have read novels, not philosophy. Plan how I should teach the idea of incommensurable values as it bears on hard choices. I need the order of ideas: what has to be in place before I can even say "incommensurable," what one example or case should carry the whole session (and what features it must have and lack), the misconceptions these writers will predictably form — I suspect "incommensurable just means incomparable" and "if you can't compare them, any choice is arbitrary" — and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. I also want one question I can pose near the end to check whether they have grasped the idea rather than a watered-down version of it. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and why.
