I'm teaching an undergraduate course on paradoxes this semester, and next week we reach the Liar. I've found that students can follow Tarski's hierarchy, Kripke's fixed points, and the dialetheist's willingness to countenance true contradictions when we walk through them slowly on the board, but a month later the distinctions blur together. I want to give them something that does philosophical work in a different register — a poem whose form itself enacts the problem, so that remembering the poem means remembering the structure of the paradox and the shape of its main responses.

Specifically, I'd like a villanelle about the Liar paradox. The villanelle seems almost built for this: two refrains that keep returning, alternating, neither winning, until the final couplet forces them into the same room. That is the Liar's own structure — the way the paradox reasserts itself no matter which exit you take, the way each proposed resolution seems to generate a revenge version of the problem. Here is what I have in mind. Let one refrain capture the hierarchical, Tarskian impulse: the thought that truth must be stratified, that a sentence cannot contain its own truth predicate without generating nonsense, that we must ascend to a metalanguage and leave the paradoxical sentence behind as ill-formed. Let the other refrain capture the dialetheist's counter-move: the thought that the Liar sentence is simply both true and false, that the real mistake was insisting on consistency, that we should learn to live with true contradictions rather than carve up language to avoid them. The body of the poem should move through the experience of encountering the paradox — the initial confidence that every declarative sentence is either true or false, the vertigo of the Liar, the relief of the hierarchical ascent, the creeping realization that this ascent leaves us unable to say anything about the truth of our own language taken as a whole, and then the dialetheist's offer, which at first feels like surrender and then, perhaps, like honesty. The refrains should alternate in a way that makes the reader feel why the debate is not settled: each time one position asserts itself, the other comes back not as a refutation but as a remainder, something the first could not quite digest. In the final couplet, the two refrains should meet — not to resolve, but to show that the paradox is still there, that the two responses have been talking past each other, that the Liar outlasts both of them. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who works on formal theories of truth would recognize the positions and their interplay, but it should also succeed as a poem for a student who has only just met the material — the philosophical content should be carried by the form, not explained in asides.
