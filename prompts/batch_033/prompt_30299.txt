One recurring target of Bernard Williams's integrity objection is a courtroom picture of moral reasoning: the moral point of view just is the point of view of an impartial judge or spectator, and to ask what I ought to do is to ask what someone would prescribe who counted every life and interest equally, my own included as one among the rest. The picture is put to work to license a strong conclusion — that my loyalty to particular people and my own ground projects count as moral considerations only to the extent that they can be translated into, and defended from, that impersonal standpoint. Audit this analogy. What corresponds to the judge, the parties, the evidence, the verdict, and the person who must live with the verdict? Where does the mapping break down? What does the courtroom quietly import about the relation between a deliberator and the life she is leading — for instance, the assumption that she can stand outside her own commitments and adjudicate among them without already being the person who has them? Once those disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion the analogy was meant to support survive, or was the courtroom doing the work all along? And if the courtroom frame is the wrong one, what would a better analogy be?
