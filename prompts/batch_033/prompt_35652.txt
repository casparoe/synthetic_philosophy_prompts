I'm a novelist. I've served on three literary prize juries now — one for a major national award, two for smaller prizes — and I'm increasingly unsettled by something about the practice that I can't quite articulate. Here's the thing: we sit in a room for hours arguing about which book is best, we use words like "masterful," "uneven," "luminous," "fails to earn its ending," and eventually we vote and a winner is declared. The press release says the winning book is "the finest novel of the year." But we all know that if the jury had been five different writers, a different book would have won. And yet we don't write press releases that say "the book that happened to command a majority among this particular group of readers." We act as if we've discovered something about the book, not about ourselves.

I want to understand what theory of aesthetic value — what view about whether literary merit is objective or subjective — this whole practice presupposes. Does the institution of juried literary prizes only make sense if you think there's a fact of the matter about which book is best, the way Hume thought a properly situated judge converges on the right verdict? Or is the practice compatible with a more subjectivist view, where the prize just registers the contingent overlap in taste among this year's jury? And here's what really bothers me: if the practice presupposes something like Hume's ideal-judge view, does it actually deliver on that? That is, are the institutional conditions of prize juries — the rushed reading, the horse-trading, the pressure to represent different demographics and aesthetics — even remotely the conditions Hume had in mind for a "true standard of taste"?

I'm not looking for a defense or an attack on literary prizes. I want a reconstruction of the implicit aesthetics the practice commits itself to, whether that commitment is coherent, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where it could be run on several different theories of aesthetic value without anyone noticing a difference in the procedures.
