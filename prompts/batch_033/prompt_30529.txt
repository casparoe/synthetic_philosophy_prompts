I've been trying to get a handle on the moral uncertainty stuff — the question of what you should do when you're not sure which moral theory is right — and the more I read the more the subfield feels like one of those carnival games where you whack a mole and two more pop up. One paper shows that if you maximize expected choiceworthiness and one of the theories you assign positive credence to has an unbounded value function, then a tiny sliver of credence can dictate everything you do; another says you can't even get off the ground because a utilitarian and a deontologist don't report their verdicts in a common currency, so expected choiceworthiness was never well-defined; a third says whatever rule you adopt for acting under moral uncertainty is itself something you could be unsure about, and now you're in a regress. Each proposed fix seems to quietly break a different part of the machine.

So before I read yet another proposal, I want the full specification. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of how to act under moral uncertainty — for one agent trying to decide what to do, not a social planner. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement: what concretely goes wrong if a theory can't meet it. I especialy want a verdict on which requirements are genuine and which are just one camp's taste (is avoiding fanaticism an actual constraint, or a preference people smuggle in because they don't like Pascalian conclusions?), and whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself amounts to a result. Then, briefly, score the main families of view against the list — expected-choiceworthiness maximization over a weighted parliament of theories, the do-only-what-every-live-theory-can-tolerate approaches (maximality and its relatives, which I'll admit I'm partial to), and the bargaining-style views. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria are what I'm actually after. A table is fine.
