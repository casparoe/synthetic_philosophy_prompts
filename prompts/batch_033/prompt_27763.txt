I work as an ML engineer at a mid-sized AI company, and over the last year we've been running more and more reinforcement learning experiments — from fine-tuning language models with RLHF to training agents in simulated robotics environments. Every few weeks someone on my team proposes a new reward function or a new training setup, and I've started noticing patterns that make me nervous: reward functions that could be gamed by an agent controlling it's own sensors, setups where the agent has write-access to the system that computes its reward, that kind of thing. I've read enough of the alignment literature to know about wireheading and reward tampering as concepts, but what I don't have is an actual procedure I can run on a proposal: something I can sit down with for twenty minutes, go through step by step, and come out the other side with either a green light, a red light, or a list of things we'd need to change before proceeding.

Can you write me that procedure? It should be usable by someone who knows ML but isn't a philosopher or an alignment researcher — no appealing to "orthogonality thesis" or "instrumental convergence" without unpacking what they mean in engineering terms. The procedure should identify the specific pathways by which an agent could wirehead or tamper with its reward, the structural features of the training setup that make each pathway possible, and what mitigations actually close the pathway rather than just hoping the agent doesn't find it. I need clear stopping rules: cases where we can say "this is fine, proceed" and cases where we should say "do not run this without X redesign." Then apply the procedure to two scenarios: (1) a language model fine-tuned with RLHF where the reward model has been trained on human preference data that overlaps heavily with the same distribution of text the policy model was pretrained on, and (2) an agent in a robotics simulation that gets reward proportional to low readings on a pressure sensor it could physically cover or short-circuit. For each, tell me what the procedure would flag and what my team should actually do about it.

Also, I want the procedure to be honest about where it'll misfire. Like, what kinds of tampering or wireheading it won't catch, or what assumptions it makes about the agent's capabilities that might not hold. If there are cases where the procedure says "green" but you'd actually be worried, I want those flagged explicitly — I'd rather know what I'm missing than have a false sense of security.
