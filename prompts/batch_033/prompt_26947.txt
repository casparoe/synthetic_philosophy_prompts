I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been invited to give a talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on moral cognition jointly hosted by a philosophy department and a psychology department. My talk defends a particular response to the paradox of deontology. The paradox, as I frame it, is this: if you believe it is wrong to kill one innocent person to save five, because each person has a right not to be killed, then you seem committed to caring more about the right itself than about what the right protects — since five rights-violations are worse than one, yet you refuse to minimize rights-violations. I argue that the way out is to reject the assumption that rights function as a value to be maximized, and to defend instead a view on which rights are constraints that arise from the kind of relationship an agent stands in to each person. On this view, the reason I may not kill one to save five is not that one violation is somehow worse than five, but that I would be the agent of the one violation, and agency matters in a way that cannot be cashed out in terms of states of affairs. The audience will be roughly forty people: about half are moral psychologists who study folk moral judgment and tend to treat deontological intuitions as heuristic shortcuts or emotional noise that a proper normative theory should correct; a quarter are philosophers, including two who have published on the paradox and are likely to press on whether my appeal to agency can be made precise without circularity; and the remaining quarter are legal scholars who work on criminal law and are interested in what philosophical accounts of rights imply for doctrines like necessity and duress. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I am most likely to get, ordered by how much damage they would do if I handle them poorly, phrased the way each constituency would put them, with a concise two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me what I should concede in the talk itself to neutralize the strongest objection before Q&A begins.
