I'm in a metaethics class this term and the Frege–Geach problem has been eating at me. The way we set it up: expressivism says "murder is wrong" doesn't report a fact but expresses a non-cognitive attitude — disapproval, acceptance of a norm, a plan, whatever. Fine. But then the same sentence shows up unasserted in the antecedent of "If murder is wrong, then getting your little brother to murder is wrong," and the whole modus ponens argument seems valid, and validity seems to require the very same content in the premise and the antecedent. So expressivism needs a real compositional story about what embedded moral sentences contribute, and it can't just say they express attitudes, because nothing is being expressed in the antecedent. That's the bind as I understand it.

We read Blackburn's idea that the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude toward combinations of attitudes, and Gibbard's world–norm pairs, and I honestly can't tell whether these are real solutions or descriptivism with better branding. So here's what I want. Give me your actual credence — a number from 0 to 1 — that the Frege–Geach problem is fatal to expressivism, where "fatal" means: no recognizably expressivist view will end up with a compositional account of embedded moral language that both validates the ordinary logic of moral discourse (modus ponens, negation, inconsistency) and stays genuinely distinct from a deflationary form of cognitivism. Decompose that number into the sub-questions your credence actually depends on. I'd expect terms like: (a) how likely the core expressivist picture of moral judgment as non-representational is correct in the first place; (b) conditional on that, how likely it is that a compositional semantics can be built without smuggling truth-conditions back in; (c) conditional on a semantics existing, how likely it is to preserve the logic without collapsing into descriptivism; and maybe a seperate term for whether Frege–Geach is even a distinct problem rather than just the general problem of embedding non-descriptive content anywhere. Give each term a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification, then show how they combine. Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move that term the most.

If I've set the whole thing up wrong — like "stays genuinely distinct from deflationary cognitivism" is doing sneaky work, or asking for a number here is the wrong kind of question — say so plainly instead of humoring me. I'd rather have the framing fixed than a confident number built on a confused one.
