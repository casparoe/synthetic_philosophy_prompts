I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of intellectual property, and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when one person may use another's ideas or creative work without permission. Going by my reactions to particular cases: posting a non-commercial fan fiction story online using a living author's characters and setting seems fine to me, but publishing an unauthorized commercial sequel using those same characters seems wrong. Sampling a two-second drum break from an old record without clearance and building an otherwise new track around it seems fine, but lifting another song's chorus, melody and lyric, into a new song seems wrong. Reverse-engineering a restaurant's signature sauce and selling a near-identical sauce under one's own label seems fine. Hearing a colleague's unpublished experimental design at a colloquium and then running and publishing the study first seems wrong. Independently proving a theorem that someone else proved a year earlier, without knowledge of the earlier proof, seems fine. And a company sequencing a naturally occurring gene and patenting the isolated sequence so that it can charge diagnostic labs license fees seems wrong.

My working principle is that one can have rights in particular expressions and in confidential information, but not in ideas, methods, or facts themselves. That fits the sequel, the chorus, the sauce, the colloquium case, the theorem, and the gene. But it seems to condemn the fan fiction and the drum break, which I don't want to condemn, and it gives no account of why the fan fiction and the commercial sequel differ when both use the same protected expression. Is there a principle that captures all or most of these verdicts, which of them would it force me to abandon, and what would it say about a designer who copies the cut and construction of a couture dress and sells a lower-priced version?
