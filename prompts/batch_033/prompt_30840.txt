I host a small philosophy of mind podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a two-component account of pain. On his view the unpleasantness of pain isn't part of the sensation itself; it's a seperate affective-motivational component, and he leans hard on pain asymbolia to make the case — the patients who can locate and rate a stimulus fine but don't withdraw, don't complain, sometimes laugh, and say things like it hurts but they don't mind. I want eight to ten interview questions that push him instead of letting him restate the view. I'd like at least one question on each of these pressure points, and for each a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give:

- the objection that asymbolics don't really have pain, just a pain-like sensation, and his usual reply that the sensory component is enough for pain and critics are confusing pain with suffering;
- what the affective-motivational component actually is — a felt quality with its own phenomenology, or a motivational/attitudinal state — and whether either answer leaves the badness of pain looking like something added to the pain rather than part of it;
- the fact that asymbolics typically only report the sensation when prompted, which might suggest the sensory component alone isn't introspectively pain-like;
- the worry that the view makes pain's badness contingent or detachable, and what that does to the idea that pain gives you a reason to withdraw your hand;
- how his view is supposed to beat the evaluativist line that the sensory component already represents damage as bad, so no separate badness component is needed.

End with a question about what evidence would actually count against the two-component view. No generic questions like what drew you to pain research, and the follow-ups should be the kind that are hard to deflect once he's given his standard answer.
