I'd like you to write a short story that explores the philosophical terrain around self-locating belief — the question of what you should believe when you are uncertain not just about how the world is, but about where you are within it. Here is the setting I have in mind. A researcher in her late thirties, a neuroscientist by training, has built a device in her basement laboratory that can induce a perfectly dreamless sleep, selectively erase short-term memories, and wake her at precise intervals. She decides to run the protocol on herself. On Sunday evening she flips a fair coin, takes a mild sedative, and goes to sleep. If the coin landed heads, the device wakes her exactly once, on Monday morning, and then the experiment ends. If the coin landed tails, the device wakes her on Monday morning, lets her go about her day, then that night erases her memory of the entire Monday waking period, puts her back under, and wakes her again on Tuesday morning. The erasure is flawless: upon waking, her subjective experience is identical regardless of whether it is Monday or Tuesday, and regardless of whether she has already been woken once before. She cannot feel stubble on her legs, cannot smell coffee from yesterday, cannot detect any trace of the previous day. Each time she wakes, before she can reach for a clock or a calendar, a calm synthetic voice asks her a single question: "What is your credence that the coin landed heads?"

The story should follow her subjective experience across one full run of the experiment — what she thinks and feels in the disorienting moment of waking, how she reasons about her situation, and what answer she gives, and whether that answer shifts across wakings. But I do not want the story merely to dramatize a puzzle. I want the fiction to press on something deeper: what it means to locate a self when the relationship between a person, a moment of consciousness, and a possible world comes apart. There might be more than one of her, spread across branches of a coin toss, and she knows this. The story should treat that fact not as a logic problem to be solved but as a lived confrontation with the nature of identity, memory, and the first-person perspective. You have freedom with tone and style — it can be clinical and claustrophobic, or lyrical and strange, whatever serves the material — but please write in continuous prose, as you would in a short story: no headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold text.
