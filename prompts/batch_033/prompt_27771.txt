I keep tripping over a distinction in ordinary language that I don't have a name for. Compare:

"If the gardener didn't do it, the butler did."
"If the gardener hadn't done it, the butler would have."

These feel different. The first one I can accept even if I'm not sure anyone did it — it just says the possibilities I'm considering don't include one where neither did it. The second seems to commit me to something about how the world works causally. But then there are sentences where I can't tell which reading is intended, like "If you press that button, the bomb goes off" — said before you press it, it seems to work one way, but said after the fact when we know you didn't press it, it seems to work the other. And I've noticed people slip between the two in arguments without noticing: someone will say "If she was at the party, she saw the theft" (sounds like the first kind) and then later treat it as if they'd established "If she had been at the party, she would have seen the theft" (the second kind). Is there an established name for this distinction, and what are the standard diagnostics for telling them apart in messy cases? Also, are there contexts where the distinction genuinely collapses, or is that always a confusion?
