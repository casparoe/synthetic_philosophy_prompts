So I'm working on a novel where the protagonist has to choose between staying in her marriage (stable, affectionate, but intellectually deadening) and leaving to pursue painting seriously in another city (uncertain, possibly lonely, but creatively alive). I've been reading around in the philosophy of practical reason to get the psychology right — not to have characters lecture each other about metaethics, god no, but because I want the internal experience of the choice to feel true on the page. And I've ended up with a set of views I didn't exactly plan to have. I'd like someone to tell me if they actually hang together.

Here's where I've landed: (1) The values at stake in this kind of choice — say, security and creative fulfillment — are genuinely incommensurable. There's no common currency they both reduce to, no hidden exchange rate. (2) But incommensurability doesn't mean incomparability. You can still sometimes say one option is better than another even when the values don't share a scale; it's not just that all incommensurable choices are ties. (3) In the genuinely hard cases though — the ones where you feel torn in a way that isn't just indecision or ignorance — the options are on a par, in Ruth Chang's sense: neither is better, and they're not equally good either. (4) When options are on a par, you can still choose rationally by committing to one set of values over the other, and that commitment is what justifies the choice — there's no pre-existing ranking your discovering, you're creating the ranking by choosing. (5) This is exactly why hard choices make for good fiction: the character isn't solving an equation, she's defining herself through the commitment, and that's the stuff novels are made of.

I've read these over and they feel like a coherent picture to me, but I've also been living inside this novel for two years and I might be blind to the joints. Are any of these in tension? I'm especialy unsure about how (2) and (3) relate — if incommensurable values can be compared sometimes, what makes the hard cases different, and is that difference principled or just a psychological fact about when we feel stuck? And does (4) actually follow from (3), or am I smuggling in a kind of voluntarism about reasons that doesn't fit with the rest? Rank whatever tensions you find by how much trouble they cause — I don't want a list where everything is equally bad, I want to know which ones I actually need to worry about.
