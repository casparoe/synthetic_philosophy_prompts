I'm a journalist preparing a long-form essay on the history of logic and metaphysics across different philosophical traditions, and I have been spending a great deal of time with the ancient Chinese School of Names — in particular, with Gongsun Long's 'White Horse Dialogue' and its central thesis that a white horse is not a horse. When I first came across this, it seemed to me that the argument could only be one of two things. Either Gongsun Long is making a banal linguistic point: the complex term 'white horse' is not synonymous with the simple term 'horse', which is true but philosophically uninteresting. Or he is exploiting an equivocation — sliding between different senses of 'is' (the 'is' of identity and the 'is' of predication, or perhaps between a horse-simpliciter and a horse-with-properties) — to generate a spurious paradox, the sort of thing a first-year philosophy student learns to diagnose and set aside.

But the deeper I go into the text and its commentaries, the less satisfied I am with either of these dismissive readings. There seems to be something genuinely probing at work: an argument about whether a particular object can be identical with the universal or the kind under which it falls, or about whether a thing-plus-property can ever be the same entity as the thing-simpliciter. And yet I cannot quite articulate, to my own satisfaction, what the argument actually is and whether it succeeds. Am I overcorrecting — is the deflationary reading in fact correct, and the standard contemporary dismissal of the School of Names as mere wordplay basically warranted? Or is there a substantive philosophical point here that the Anglophone tradition has been slow to recognise? I would like the answer to distinguish carefully between what is settled in the scholarship and what remains a live dispute, and I should prefer it in British English.
