I keep seeing "sphexish" used as a one-word dismissal in discussions of large language models — the thought being that a system which loops or re-commits to a script when the environment shifts is thereby exposed as merely mechanical — and I've been trying to turn my unease about that move into a short essay. Below is a draft. I would like comments in a particular order: first, on whether the central inference is valid; second, on whether the essay is unfair to the position it means to attack; third, on which part would be most worth expanding if I wanted to make the essay genuinely useful rather than merely provocative. I'm not looking for stylistic notes or for reading suggestions; I want to know where the reasoning goes wrong or moves too fast.

The working title is "Sphexishness Is a Property of Environments, Not Agents."

The digger wasp behind the term "sphexish" is typically introduced as follows. It stings a cricket into paralysis, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves the prey outside, goes in to inspect the burrow, comes back out, and then drags the cricket inside. If an experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp drags it back to the threshold and repeats the inspection routine; if the experimenter keeps moving the cricket, the wasp keeps repeating the cycle. The standard moral is that the wasp is not deciding to inspect the burrow. It is executing a fixed routine, and the routine's blindness shows up in the loop.

My essay argues that this moral is drawn too quickly, and that drawing it too quickly obscures what the example actually shows. The wasp's behavior is not rigid in general; it is rigid with respect to one particular perturbation, namely displacement of the prey during the inspection step. Under other perturbations the same behavioral system is strikingly flexible: the hunting, paralyzing, transporting, and burrow-maintenance sequence has to cope with uneven terrain, struggling prey, and variable burrow geometry. So sphexishness is not a property of the wasp's behavioral mechanisms considered in isolation. It is a property of the fit between those mechanisms and the environment in which they are deployed. The experimenter constructs a condition outside the envelope the mechanism was shaped to handle, and the mechanism fails by looping.

That much, I think, is familiar. The move I want to stress-test is the next one. If sphexishness is environment-relative in this way, then the free-will use of the example is unstable. The contrast we are invited to draw between the wasp's mechanical rigidity and our own flexible, reason-responsive agency cannot be a contrast between two kinds of underlying causation or two kinds of stuff. It can only be a contrast between two shapes of behavior-environment fit: ours degrades gracefully across a wider class of perturbations, while the wasp's degrades catastrophically across a narrower one. And if that is right, the free-will question, as prompted by the wasp, has quietly become an engineering question about robustness and generalization rather than a metaphysical question about whether our actions are caused or up to us. I do not yet know whether to treat that as a reductio of the example's use or as the right way to dissolve the question; the essay currently ends by suggesting the latter, with a line to the effect that we would do better to taboo "free will" and talk instead about the width of the perturbation class over which a system remains goal-directed.

Specific comments I would like:

(1) Is the inference from "rigidity is relative to a perturbation class" to "the free-will contrast collapses into an engineering question" valid? I worry it slides from a point about explanation — we explain the wasp's loop by citing a misfit between mechanism and environment — to a point about metaphysics — that there is nothing else at stake in the free-will debate. Is that slide defensible, or does it need an extra premise?

(2) Am I being unfair to the standard use of the example? If someone invokes the wasp only to motivate a weaker claim — that some behavioral patterns are obviously not free and the difference lies in the mechanism, not in the environment — does my argument engage them, or have I swapped in a stronger target?

(3) Is the dissolution too cheap? A libertarian could accept everything I say about environment-relativity and still insist that the wasp is an automaton in a sense I have not touched. What is the strongest version of that reply, and does the essay have an answer to it?
