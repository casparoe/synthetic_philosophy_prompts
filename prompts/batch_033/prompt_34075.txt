I'm teaching a graduate seminar on transformative experience this fall, and I'm planning to devote one session to a close viewing of Denis Villeneuve's *Arrival* (2016), based on Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life." The film seems almost tailor-made for L.A. Paul's framework: Louise Banks learns the heptapod language, and in doing so undergoes an experience that is not merely epistemically but personally transformative — it changes her very experience of time, such that she can "remember" the future. The most striking moment, of course, is when she knows, before she and Ian have a daughter, that Hannah will die of a rare disease in adolescence, and she chooses to go ahead anyway.

I want to use the film to push on Paul's argument in a serious way, not just to illustrate it. In particular, I'm interested in whether the film offers a model of rational choice under transformative experience that Paul's framework rules out. Louise doesn't seem irrational — the film presents her choice as intelligible, even wise — but she makes a decision whose outcome she knows (in some sense) without having lived through it. Does the film thereby suggest that Paul's epistemic challenge can be met, or does it simply change the subject by giving Louise a quasi-perceptual access to the future that no real agent has? And if the latter, is there a way to read the film that doesn't reduce it to a fantasy about circumventing the problem, but instead treats the heptapod language as a metaphor for something we actually do when we listen carefully to the testimony of those who have undergone a transformative experience?

I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical argument seriously, that identifies where the film genuinely complicates Paul's position and where it trades on a piece of science-fictional magic that can't do the philosophical work, and that ends by saying what the film adds to the debate that the papers alone don't capture.
