I'm in a metaphysics course this term, and we've been working through the debate about laws of nature. After spending weeks on it, I've found myself genuinely convinced by the Humean picture — roughly, the view that laws of nature are not primitive governing relations or necessary connections between properties, but rather the theorems or axioms of the deductive system that strikes the best balance between simplicity and informativeness in describing the total distribution of particular matters of fact across all of space and time. What drew me in is that it doesn't posit anything beyond the mosaic of local, categorical facts: there's no mysterious "oomph" or nomic necessitation relation hovering above the particles. The laws are just the most elegant summary of everything that actually happens. I also find it appealing that the view makes no unexplained distinction between laws and accidents — the difference is a matter of degree, of where a regularity sits in the best system — and that it seems to fit neatly with a broadly empiricist epistemology, since the only facts we need to learn about are the particular ones.

What I'd like, before I commit to this view in my term paper, is a systematic map of its exposure. I want to know what would count against the Humean picture. I'm not looking for a standard list of objections — I've read those. What I want is an assessment of which considerations should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be if they turned out to be right. For each, I want to know: is this something that would force a revision of the view, or just something awkward that a Humean can learn to live with? Which of these would a committed Humean themselves concede as real costs, and which would they dismiss as question-begging or as resting on intuitions that the view was designed to overturn? I'd also like to know whether the Humean position, as it has been developed and defended, has been gradually insulated by qualifications to the point where it no longer risks anything — and if it has, whether that is a serious problem for a metaphysical view of this kind, or whether we should expect theories about the fundamentals to be empirically underdetermined in just this way. If there are developments in the metaphysics of grounding, fundamentality, or the semantics of counterfactuals that would tilt the balance, I want to hear about those too.
