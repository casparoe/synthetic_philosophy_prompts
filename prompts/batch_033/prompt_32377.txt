I'm a retired population geneticist — spent forty years working on salmon and trout, mostly — and I've recently got into philosophy of biology. It's been great, but one thing keeps nagging at me: heritability. I've used the concept my whole career without thinking too hard about it, and now that I'm reading the philosophical literature I'm realising that outside of quantitative genetics, the concept seems to cause almost as much confusion as insight. I've been trying to work out what I actually think by going through particular claims people make and checking my gut. I'm hoping you can help me extract whatever principle is behind my reactions.

Here are my verdicts on a bunch of claims about heritability. (I'm using h² in the narrow sense — additive genetic variance over total phenotypic variance — though I don't think the narrow/broad distinction matters much for what I'm asking about.)

Case 1. A newspaper article says "the heritability of IQ is 0.5, so IQ is 50% genetic." My verdict: this is wrong. Heritability is about variance in a population, not about partitioning causes within an individual. It's not saying half of your IQ comes from your genes and half from your environment; it's saying half the variation in the population is associated with genetic variation. Those are different claims.

Case 2. Someone else says "heritability doesn't tell you anything about genetics at all." My verdict: also wrong. It does tell you something — it tells you the proportion of phenotypic variance accounted for by genetic variance in that population. That's not nothing. It's a real quantity.

Case 3. A policy paper argues that because the heritability of educational attainment is substantial, efforts to close socioeconomic gaps in attainment are doomed. My verdict: this is a non sequitur and I find it genuinely offensive. Heritability says nothing about the likely efficacy of environmental interventions. High heritability is perfectly compatible with large environmental effects. Height has h² around 0.8 in developed countries, and yet average height has shot up with better nutrition. If we gave every child the same diet, heritability would go up, not down, and the environmental effect would be smaller because the environment would be more uniform. The whole inference is backwards.

Case 4. But then I read a sociologist who says heritability is a useless concept for human traits and should be scrapped entirely. My verdict: I get the frustration, but I think this goes too far. Heritability is genuinely useful in plant and animal breeding — it tells you expected response to selection — and it seems to me it can be useful in human genetics too for understanding trait architecture, if you're careful about what the numbers mean.

Case 5. A study finds that the heritability of BMI in the US went up between the 1970s and the 2000s. My verdict: this is exactly what you'd expect if the environment became more uniformly obesogenic. Reducing environmental variance increases heritability. It's not a surprising finding, but it's a useful one to have documented properly.

Case 6. Someone says "the heritability of divorce is about 0.4, so there's a gene for divorce." My verdict: this is so wrong it's hard to know where to start. There is no gene for divorce. Heritability of a complex behavioural outcome doesn't mean there's a simple genetic cause, and it certainly doesn't mean there's a gene "for" the behaviour in any meaningful sense. The genetic variance is going to be distributed across hundreds or thousands of variants with tiny effects, mediated by personality, temperament, all sorts of things.

Case 7. A behavioural geneticist I respect says heritability estimates are useful because they tell us where to look for gene–environment interactions. My verdict: I'm not sure about this one anymore. I used to think it, but now I worry that heritability estimates are too coarse and too environmentally labile to guide the search for specific G×E interactions. They tell you that genes matter for variation, but not which ones or how they interact with the environment. That's a lot of inferential distance between "h² is non-zero" and "here's a specific G×E interaction."

Case 8. I read a paper arguing that the concept of heritability, when applied to human social traits, is so routinely misunderstood by the public and by policymakers that scientists have a responsibility to either stop using it or to use it only with the most extensive caveats. My verdict: I think this is probably right, and it makes me uncomfortable because I've spent a career using the concept without worrying much about how it would be misused.

The tension I can feel in my own views is between cases 4 and 8. I think heritability is a legitimate and useful concept, but I also think it's so routinely misused in human contexts that maybe we should stop using it there. I can't tell if that's a coherent position or just a failure to think it through.

So my question: is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures these verdicts? What does it say about the following case I haven't made up my mind about: a research team finds that the heritability of a cognitive trait is high in a wealthy country but low in a poor country, and they conclude that genetic differences between individuals matter more for that trait in the wealthy country. Is that a reasonable interpretation, or is it yet another misuse?

I'd also like you to say which of my verdicts, if any, your principle forces me to give up, and whether the tension between cases 4 and 8 is resolvable or whether I really do have to pick a side.

- "verdicts_accommodated": list the case numbers the principle handles as-is
