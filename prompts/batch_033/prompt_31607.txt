I retired from physics a few years back and have been reading philosophy of probability and rational choice. I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a clean name for, and I'd appreciate some help situating it.

Here's the simplest case. Someone offers you a bet on a fair coin: heads you get back 2.5 times your stake, tails you lose 40% of it. The bet has positive expected value — about 5% per dollar — so if you just maximize expected value, you should put your whole bankroll on it. But if you actually do that repeatedly, you'll go broke almost surely. The reason is that your wealth multiplies by a factor each round, and the relevant average isn't the ordinary arithmetic mean of the outcomes (which is above 1) but the multiplicative one: a 150% gain followed by a 40% loss leaves you at 90% of where you started. Over many rounds, the typical path shrinks to zero even though the "expected" path across all possible worlds grows to infinity.

Now, I know there's a formula for exactly how much to bet to maximize long-run growth in cases like this, and I know that formula has a name. But I'm after the broader phenomenon. It shows up whenever you have a quantity that grows multiplicatively under random shocks — populations, investment portfolios, reputations. The strategy that maximizes the expected value of the quantity itself gives you a different answer from the strategy that maximizes what actually happens to a single trajectory over time. And there's a stronger claim some people make: that this isn't just about being risk-averse, that any agent whose wealth compounds should act as if it had a logarithmic utility function, not because of its psychology, but because the math of repeated multiplication forces it.

I have a few questions. (1) Is there an established name for this whole cluster of ideas — the thing about ensemble averages diverging from time averages in multiplicative settings, and the associated claim that this somehow grounds rationality in the structure of the process rather than in preferences? (2) How exactly does this differ from just saying "use a concave utility function"? The math looks similar, but the justification seems different, and I want to know whether that difference holds up. (3) Is there a name for the specific mistake of optimizing the wrong average — using the arithmetic mean when the geometric one is what matters? And (4), what's the best case against the strong claim that this makes expected utility theory redundent or derivative?
