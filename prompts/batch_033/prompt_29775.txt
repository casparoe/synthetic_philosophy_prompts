I retired a few years ago after a career in chemistry, and I've been slowly working my way through decision theory and Bayesian epistemology in my spare time. Recently I read a popular book that summarized I.J. Good's theorem on the value of information, and I keep turning it over in my head. As I understood the book, the upshot was this: Good proved mathematically that a rational person should always want more information before acting, because more information can never make you worse off. The book then drew some practical morals. It said this is why it's irrational to avoid finding out unpleasant facts, like reading your medical test results: by Good's theorem the information can only help your decision, so refusing to learn it is just leaving expected value on the table. It also applied the point to smaller cases, saying that if you're deciding wether to check the weather before a trip or ask a colleague's opinion before choosing a protocol, Good's theorem shows checking is always at least as good as not checking, so the only rational excuse for skipping it is that you don't care about the outcome.

Something about this feels too strong, but I can't pin down where. In the lab there were times when a noisy preliminary measurement sent us down the wrong path, and in hindsight we'd have been better off never seeing it. But I suspect "worse off in hindsight" is compatible with the theorem, which is presumably a claim about expectation. Could you do an accuracy audit of the book's gloss? I'd like to know which parts match what Good actually showed, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and whether the practical conclusions (about avoiding unpleasant facts, checking the weather, asking colleagues) survive once the theorem is stated correctly. Please also flag where the theorem's assumptions do real work—cost, signal quality, how you update on what you learn, whether acquiring the information changes the situation—and be explicit about which of these points are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
