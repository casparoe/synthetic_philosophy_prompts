The thing is I've accumulated opinions on each one individually but I've never checked whether they all fit together, and I'm supposed to write up my "positions doc" for a discussion group next week.

Here's where I've landed, roughly:

(1) Newcomb's problem: one-box. The dominance argument feels like it's assuming something about the causal structure that just isn't true once you have a perfect predictor in the picture. The evidential link between my decision and what's in the box is all I need.

(2) Smoking lesion: don't smoke. Unlike Newcomb, here the correlation runs through a common cause (the gene), so the evidential link is spurious. This is where EDT falls on its face and I need something more causal.

(3) Counterfactual mugging: pay the damn mugger. I know the coin has already been flipped, but conditional on Omega asking me for $100, the expected value of paying is huge. The fact that in the other branch I would have received $10,000 feels irrelevent to my choice in this branch.

(4) XOR blackmail: don't pay. I get that this looks a lot like counterfactual mugging — in both cases someone is exploiting knowledge of my decision procedure. But in XOR blackmail the whole thing feels like it unravels once you realize the blackmailer can't actually make the threat stick against an agent who simply doesn't give in to blackmail. (Though I'll admit I'm shakier on this one than the others.)

(5) Implementation insensitivity: a good decision theory shouldn't care about when or how the decision gets made. Whether I precommit ahead of time, deliberate on the spot, or hand the decision to an AI I built — the theory should give the same answer in all three setups. If it doesn't, then it's tracking something about implementation rather than about what makes a choice correct.

I have a nagging feeling that (3) and (4) are doing a lot of work to look different when they might not be, and also that (5) might be pulling against whatever move lets me distinguish them. And then there's the whole thing where (2) pushes me toward causal decision theory but (1) and especially (3) seem to require something more like functional decision theory or updateless decision theory.
