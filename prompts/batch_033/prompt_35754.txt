In my philosophy of language seminar we have been working through the debate between descriptivism about proper names and the anti-descriptivist direct-reference picture associated with Kripke, and I keep getting stuck on an analogy the instructor uses. The instructor presents the direct-reference view by saying that a proper name is like a tag tied directly to its bearer, the way a name tag is pinned to someone at a conference: the tag does not describe the person, does not pick them out through any set of properties, and remains attached to that same individual no matter what they do or what properties they acquire or lose. From this picture the instructor moves to two claims — that a name contributes only its bearer to what is said, and that names are rigid designators — and then uses those claims to explain why, if Hesperus and Phosphorus are simply two tags attached to the same planet, the statement that Hesperus is Phosphorus is necessary, while the fact that it took empirical astronomy to discover the identity shows only that the proposition is known a posteriori and not that it is contingent. A friend in the seminar insists the tag picture is obviously right, another says it is only a picture and not an argument, and I would like to sort out which of their reactions is closer to the truth.

Could you audit the analogy for me? First, give an explicit correspondence between the source and the target: what corresponds to the tag, the act of pinning it on, the conference-goer, the possibility of two tags on one person, and the possibility of someone wearing no tag. Second, identify the main places where the analogy breaks down or quietly imports features of tags that names lack — empty names such as Vulcan or Pegasus, names in fiction, negative existentials such as the claim that Vulcan does not exist, Frege's puzzle about how a true identity can be informative, belief reports in which substituting one co-referential name for another seems to change truth, and anything else you regard as important. For each disanalogy, say whether it is superficial or whether it threatens the work the analogy is being made to do. Third, and most importantly: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the instructor's conclusion survive — that proper names are rigid designators and that identity statements like the one about Hesperus and Phosphorus are necessary a posteriori — or was the tag analogy doing more than it can legitimately bear? If you think some other analogy would do better, I would be glad to hear what it is and why.
