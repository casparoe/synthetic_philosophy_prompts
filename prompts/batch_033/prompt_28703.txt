I've been working through the doomsday argument and I'm stuck on what seems like a straightforward practical implication. As I understand it, the core move is this: you treat your own birth rank as a random sample from the set of all humans who will ever live. Given that I find myself roughly 60 billionth in line, I should strongly update against hypotheses on which the total human population reaches, say, a hundred trillion — because on those hypotheses, my observed birth rank would be a wildly improbable fluke, like drawing the number 7 and learning that the lottery goes up to a million. The argument doesn't pick out an exact date, but it seems to compress our credible future into something relatively short: a few more generations, maybe a few centuries at the outside, well before any of the truly long-term projects humanity might attempt.

Here's what bothers me. If I actually buy this argument, shouldn't it radically reorder my priorities? In particular, it seems to undermine the entire rationale for working on existential risk reduction. The case for asteroid deflection, pandemic preparedness, or AI alignment rests heavily on the astronomical number of future lives at stake — trillions upon trillions of potential people whose existence we'd be foreclosing. But if the doomsday argument is sound, that astronomical future was never in the cards to begin with. The expected value calculation collapses: there simply aren't enough future people left to save for the intervention to pay for itself. At the limit, it looks like the argument tells us to stop worrying about the long-term future and focus exclusively on making the short time we have left as good as possible. Yet I've noticed that many people who discuss the doomsday argument seriously — including some who seem to grant its probabilistic force — continue to work on exactly these long-term risks. What am I missing? Is there a way to accept the doomsday argument's update and still justify directing resources toward preventing human extinction?
