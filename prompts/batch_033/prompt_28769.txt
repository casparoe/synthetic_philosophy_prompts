The debate between illusionists and realists about phenomenal consciousness has now run long enough that each side can predict the other's moves in advance, and neither side seems to move. The realists hold that phenomenal consciousness is the datum a theory of mind must answer to before anything else: when I attend to my own experience I find qualitative character, a way things are for me, and any view that denies this has denied the one thing we know most directly. The illusionists hold that phenomenal consciousness so understood is an artifact of introspection: there are experiences, in the sense of states that respond to stimuli, guide behavior, and issue in reports, but introspection systematically misrepresents those states as having private, non-physical, qualitative properties. Each side regards the other as not merely wrong but confused. The realist says illusionism is self-refuting, because an illusion is itself an experience, so the view presupposes what it denies. The illusionist replies that the state producing the false introspective representation is real, while the property the representation attributes is not, so no regress follows. The realist presses the explanatory gap: no account of functions and mechanisms seems to reach conscious experience itself. The illusionist answers that the gap is exactly what a false introspective picture would lead us to expect, and points to change blindness, choice blindness, and confabulation as cases where introspection is demonstrably unreliable.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is less about the brain than about what is allowed to count as data: the realist treats the first-person appearance as a datum to be preserved, while the illusionist treats it as a theoretical interpretation of data that ultimately consist in reports and behavioral dispositions. But I do not trust that hypothesis, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

Diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Which parts, if any, are verbal, turning on the expression phenomenal consciousness? Which parts are substantive, and over what: the reliability of introspection, the contents of introspection, the standards of explanation, or bedrock intuitions? Are the two sides answering different questions under one heading? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that would move at least one side, or is the question ill-posed? Support the diagnosis by engaging the actual moves each side makes, including the self-refutation charge, the illusionist reply to it, the explanatory-gap argument, and the appeal to introspective error. Then say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
