I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on consent and moral psychology, and I somehow ended up on my university's IRB (they like having at least one ethics person on it). We're stuck on a case and I've been asked to draft an opinion memo for the committee before we vote.

The study: social psychologists want to look at how people react when their contributions are systematically devalued in a group. They recruit participants for what's described as a "group discussion study on communication patterns." Each session has four people — except three are confederates following a script. Whenever the real participant speaks, a confederate interrupts, or changes the subject, or gives a dismissive "hmm, anyway—" and redirects. The real participant's contributions never get uptake. The researchers measure frustration, subsequent willingness to contribute, and self-reported mood. The manipulation isn't traumatic — it's low-grade social friction, the kind you'd get in a bad meeting — but it's designed to be unpleasant. There's a careful debriefing afterward.

The consent form describes the study as "examining communication styles in group discussion" and says participants will "engage in structured conversation with other participants." It doesn't disclose the confederates, the script, or that the participant will be systematically shut down. The researchers argue that full disclosure would make the study impossible, the deception is minimal, the debriefing adresses any concerns, and this is entirely standard in their field.

The committee is split. One side: this is textbook routine deception, been approved a thousand times, harm is trivial — approve. The other side: you can't validly consent to a social interaction when you're fundamentally mistaken about what kind of interaction it is and who you're interacting with. The fact the field does this routinely doesn't settle the ethics — it might just be a collective blind spot.

I need to draft a short memo (say 800–1200 words) recommending approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, and it has to take the consent question seriously. Don't just cite the federal regulations — I want the memo to work through the underlying consent theory. Specifically: is there a principled difference between being deceived about the _nature_ of what you're agreeing to versus being deceived about its purpose or peripheral details? Does debriefing actually fix a consent problem, or does it only address harm after the fact? And is "standard practice" just question-begging, or is there a real argument from disciplinary conventions?

Also: flag your confidence level on the core normative claims, and tell me which step in the argument you're least sure about. I need to know where the memo is on firm ground and where someone's going to come at me in the meeting.
