The question was:

"Ryle argued that knowing-how is not reducible to knowing-that. Explain Ryle's distinction and his regress argument, then present the contemporary intellectualist response (e.g., Stanley and Williamson). Which side do you think has the stronger case, and why?"

Here's the student's answer verbatim:

"Ryle introduced the distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that to challenge what he called the 'intellectualist legend' — the idea that all intelligent action must be guided by prior theoretical knowledge. Knowing-that is propositional knowledge, like knowing that Paris is the capital of France. Knowing-how is non-propositional knowledge, like knowing how to ride a bicycle. Ryle's regress argument goes like this: if knowing-how required knowing-that, then before you could perform any intelligent action you would need to have contemplated a proposition about how to do it. But then you'd need a further proposition about how to apply that proposition, and so on infinitely. Since we do manage to act intelligently, the intellectualist picture must be wrong. Knowing-how is therefore a dispositional capacity, not a kind of propositional knowledge.

The contemporary intellectualists, led by Stanley and Williamson, argue that Ryle was wrong and that knowing-how actually is a species of knowing-that. On their view, knowing how to F is knowing that there is some way w such that w is a way for you to F. They support this with linguistic evidence: 'know how' constructions pattern like other embedded question constructions that we already analyze as propositional knowledge, so it would be ad hoc to treat know-how differently. The strongest version of intellectualism adds that the proposition must be known under a 'practical mode of presentation,' which is just a fancy way of saying you have to know it in a way that's connected to your ability to act.

I think Ryle has the stronger case. The regress argument is basically decisive: intellectualism says that to know how to do something you have to know a proposition, but you could know the proposition and still not be able to do the thing — think of a sports commentator who knows everything about swimming but can't swim. The practical mode of presentation is supposed to fix this, but it's circular: it just restates the problem by saying you need to know the proposition 'in a practical way,' which is exactly what knowing-how was supposed to be in the first place. So intellectualism either collapses back into the Rylean view or fails to explain what it sets out to explain."
