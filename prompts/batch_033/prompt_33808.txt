I've been reading through the deflationism literature in philosophy of language and I keep getting stuck on the same point. The deflationist's core move is to say that truth isn't a substantive property — there's no nature of truth to investigate, no underlying essence that unifies all true propositions. Instead, "is true" is just a logical device: it lets us express generalizations we couldn't otherwise express. "Everything the Pope says is true" is shorthand for an infinite conjunction of claims like "if the Pope says snow is white, then snow is white; if the Pope says grass is green, then grass is green; …" without needing to list them all. So far so good.

But here's what I can't get to cohere. If the truth predicate is merely a device for generalization, then the deflationist owes us an account of how it performs that function — and that account seems to require quantifying over propositions, or sentences, or something proposition-like. The standard deflationist story is that "Everything the Pope says is true" means something like: for any proposition p, if the Pope asserts p, then p. But now we have quantification into sentence position — "for any p, … then p" — which is not standard first-order quantification. So the deflationist seems to need either substitutional quantification (which has its own problems) or an ontology of propositions (which starts to look like a substantive commitment). And if the generalization device requires a background ontology of propositions, then truth starts looking like a property of those propositions after all — namely, the property such that, when you have it, you can "disquote" and get back to the world.

I'd like a careful walkthrough of this problem and the main deflationist responses to it. In particular: what is the least philosophically-loaded way to make sense of the generalization function, and does the deflationist have to choose between giving up on genuine generalizations (by treating them as merely schematic) and admitting that truth is a property of propositions? If there's a version of deflationism that navigates between these two options, I want to understand what it buys and what it costs. Assume I'm comfortable with the basic technical vocabulary — substitutional vs. objectual quantification, schematic generalizations, propositions vs. sentences as truth-bearers — but haven't seen the dialectic laid out end-to-end.
