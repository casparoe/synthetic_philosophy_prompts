I got into a fight with my roommate about this decision theory puzzle and now I think I've talked myself into a corner. The puzzle is counterfactual mugging. Omega (a perfect predictor) flips a fair coin. If heads: Omega gives you $10,000 if and only if you would have paid $1,000 had the coin come up tails. If tails: Omega just asks you to pay $1,000. Omega tells you all this, then says the coin came up tails. Should you pay?

I started out thinking obviously you shouldn't pay — the coin already landed tails, nothing you do now changes whether you get the $10,000. But then my roommate pointed out that if you precommit to paying in tails, you get $10,000 in heads, for an expected gain of $4,500. And Omega knows what you'd do, so your decision "now" is really just revealing which policy you had all along. So I switched sides: you should pay.

But then I couldn't stop thinking. Here's the argument that's been eating at me:

(1) If you should pay in the original counterfactual mugging, it's because you want to be the kind of agent who pays in counterfactual muggings — that's what gets you the $10,000 in heads-worlds.

(2) Being that kind of agent means paying in every counterfactual mugging you encounter, not just this one. The disposition "pays when counterfactually mugged" doesn't come with a limit switch.

(3) But there's no bound on how many counterfactual muggings you could face. Omega, or some other predictor, could run this on you every day with different stakes — $100,000 for $1,000, $1,000,000 for $100, and so on.

(4) If you pay in all of them, you go broke in the tail-worlds, even though you get rich in the heads-worlds. And since you actually live in exactly one world, in the actual tail-world you're just bankrupt.

(5) A decision procedure that tells you to adopt a policy that leaves you bankrupt in the actual world (even if it had high ex ante expected value) is self-defeating.

(6) So you shouldn't pay in the original mugging either.

But (6) means I'm back to refusing the single mugging, which means I'm leaving expected value on the table in that case, which feels irrational in a different way. I think the problem has to be somewhere in (2) or (3) — maybe "being the kind of agent who pays" doesn't actually commit you to paying in every possible mugging, or maybe there's a relevant difference between the single case and the iterated case. But I can't make either objection stick. Where does this argument actually break?
