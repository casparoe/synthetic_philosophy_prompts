I'm in a philosophy of science course and we just covered the problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory. Two classmates got into it after the lecture and I'm trying to figure out who's right.

The setup: in Bayesian confirmation, E confirms H iff P(H|E) > P(H). But if E is already known, P(E) = 1, so P(H|E) = P(H), and old evidence can never confirm anything. Yet scientists clearly use old evidence — Mercury's perihelion advance was known for decades before Einstein used it to support general relativity.

Here's their exchange, paraphrased from memory:

(1) Mira said this shows the Bayesian framework is just broken. If a formalism can't capture one of the most basic features of scientific practice — that known facts can support new theories — then it's not a useful account of confirmation, period.

(2) Dan replied that's too quick. He said the real question is: what would your credence in general relativity have been if you didn't already know about Mercury? The evidence is "old" only relative to the theory, not relative to the confirmation relation itself. You can just ask what P(H|E) would be if you subtracted your knowledge of E — a counterfactual probability.

(3) Mira came back: but if you're subtracting knowledge of E, you're not using your actual credence function, you're using some hypothetical one. Which one? There's no unique way to "unknow" something. And doesn't this just admit the actual Bayesian formula, with your actual credences, gives the wrong answer?

(4) Dan said fine, but here's another way: when Einstein derived the perihelion result, the new evidence wasn't the perihelion itself — it was the logical fact that general relativity *entails* the observed advance. That logical fact wasn't known before, so P(it) wasn't 1, and the formula works fine.

(5) Mira said that's clever but now you've changed the subject. The thing scientists actually cite as confirming the theory is the observation, not the entailment relation. If you have to reinterpret what the evidence is to save the formula, you're basically admitting the formula doesn't apply to evidence as scientists understand it.

(6) Dan said maybe the problem is that we're treating "confirmation" as a synchronic relation between propositions when it's really a diachronic process — our credences change over time, and the formula describes that change, not an atemporal logical relation.

(7) Mira said if you go diachronic, you still have the problem: at the time the theory is proposed, the evidence is already old, so your credence in E is still 1, and the formula still says no confirmation happens.

They went back and forth a bit more but those were the main moves. I'd like an adjudication of who's right about what at each step, and who has the better overall position. Also, is there a way to dissolve the disagreement that neither of them saw?
