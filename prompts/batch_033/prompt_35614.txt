Explain the paradox of deontology and critically assess whether the agent-relative/agent-neutral distinction resolves it.

Here's what the student wrote:

The paradox of deontology arises because deontological theories include constraints like "do not kill an innocent person," and these constraints apply even when violating one would prevent a larger number of violations of the same constraint. If killings are bad, then a deontologist who refuses to kill one person to stop five seems to be acting against her own evaluation of the situation. Nozick's answer is that rights are trumps: my right not to be killed may not be overridden even for the greater good, so the constraint stands no matter how many killings it would prevent. A more sophisticated version of this reply distinguishes agent-relative from agent-neutral reasons. A constraint gives me an agent-relative reason not to kill, but gives me no reason at all to prevent other people from killing. Since the deontologist isn't trying to minimize violations, there's no inconsistency in forbidding my killing while allowing the five others to occur. The distinction therefore dissolves the paradox completely.
