I am a novelist researching the way small communities generate, police, and sometimes legally encode their own moral codes. In my current project, a neighborhood association in a coastal town has accumulated enough informal authority to function as a de facto legislature over private conduct, and I want the central conflict to turn on a precise fault line I have encountered in the Hart–Devlin debate. I know the stock contrast well enough: Devlin holds that a society may use the criminal law to enforce the shared morality that constitutes it, while Hart holds that private immorality alone is never a sufficient ground for coercion and that the law’s proper business is preventing harm to others. But I want to avoid the familiar version of the case. Specifically, I would like a thought experiment in which a Devlin-style legal moralist would argue against criminalizing a practice that a Hart-style harm-principle liberal would argue for criminalizing—an inversion of the usual direction. The scenario must be realistic, set in an ordinary workplace or a neighborhood rather than a state legislature or a science-fictional setting; the moralist’s restraint must come from Devlin’s own limiting factors, such as privacy, tolerance, or the absence of the relevant kind of social indignation, rather than from the moralist abandoning legal moralism; and the liberal’s support for criminalization must come from a non-fanciful indirect harm, not from an ad hoc or contrived externality. Please keep the scenario itself to one paragraph. After giving the scenario, walk me through the reasoning for each position, say which feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what a defender of Hart’s position would say to resist the accusation that the case has simply redefined harm in order to get the desired result.
