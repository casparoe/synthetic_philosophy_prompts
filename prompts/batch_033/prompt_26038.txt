I spent most of my career in experimental physics, where if you couldn't operationalize a distinction it wasn't a real distinction. I've been reading moral philosophy in retirement, and I keep tripping over the same passage in Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* — the one early in Section I where he discusses the honest shopkeeper.

The passage, as I understand it (I'm working with a translation, and I'll paraphrase since I don't have the text in front of me), runs roughly as follows. Kant asks us to consider a shopkeeper who refrains from overcharging an inexperienced customer. This action certainly conforms with duty — honesty in commerce is morally required. But Kant then distinguishes two cases. In the first, the shopkeeper is honest because he knows that cheating would damage his reputation and hurt his business; he acts on the prudential maxim that honesty is the best policy. In the second, the shopkeeper is honest simply because it is the right thing to do — he acts from duty. Kant claims that only the second shopkeeper's action has genuine moral worth, even though the outward behavior is identical. He seems to be saying that an action's moral worth depends entirely on its motive, not on its consequences or even on whether it conforms to duty. And he goes further: he suggests that actions done from inclination — even benevolent inclinations like sympathy — also lack moral worth unless they are done from duty.

Here is what bothers me. I can see why we might praise the duty-motivated shopkeeper more than the prudence-motivated one. But Kant's claim is stronger: he says the prudent shopkeeper's action has *no* moral worth at all. That strikes me as bizarre. If someone consistently treats customers fairly, and the world is better for it, why should we withhold all moral credit just because his psychology happens to route through self-interest? Put differently: Kant seems to be advancing an empirical psychological claim — that a given action can be sorted into exactly one motive — while simultaneously insisting that the moral quality of the act turns entirely on something unobservable. As an experimentalist, I find this deeply puzzling. Is Kant making a claim about how we should evaluate actions, or about what actually makes them right, or about what we can know? And does his position ultimately imply that we can never know whether anyone (including ourselves) has ever performed a morally worthy act?
