Two people in my department got into an argument about a news story and I'm hoping you can sort out who's right. The story was about a lifeguard who was texting and didn't notice a swimmer drowning. The swimmer died.

(1) Anna said the lifeguard's inaction caused the swimmer's death. That's just obvious.

(2) Ben said no, the drowning caused the death. The lifeguard's failure to act is an absence, and absences aren't the kind of thing that can be causes — they're not events, they don't transfer energy, they're literally nothing. Calling an omission a cause is a category mistake.

(3) Anna said that can't be right, because we routinely cite absences as causes. If a plant dies from lack of water, the lack of water caused its death. If a bridge collapses from missing bolts, the missing bolts caused the collapse. You can't just declare all of that ordinary causal talk mistaken.

(4) Ben said those cases are different. We cite the lack of water because we have a background expectation that plants get watered — the causal claim is really a disguised normative claim about what should have happened. The actual physical cause was whatever desiccation process occurred in the cells. The omission talk is just us assigning blame.

(5) Anna said but that's true of all causal claims. When you say the spark caused the fire, you're picking out one condition from a whole background of necessary conditions — the presence of oxygen, the dryness of the material — and calling it "the cause" based on what's abnormal or salient. So you can't use the fact that we select omissions based on expectations to show omissions aren't real causes, because we select positive causes based on expectations too. If you're going to be a purist, you'd have to say the only real cause is the entire set of conditions, which nobody actually does.

(6) Ben said fair, but there's still a difference. With positive causes, there's at least some physical connection between the cause and the effect. With omissions, there's literally no connection — the lifeguard's texting didn't do anything to the swimmer. So even if causal selection is interest-relative, omissions are a different kind of thing entirely.

They'd been at it for twenty minutes and I lost track of who had the better argument. Can you adjudicate this move by move? I'm especially curious about Ben's point in (6) — is there really a principled physical difference between omissions and positive causes, or is that just restating the intuition he started with?
