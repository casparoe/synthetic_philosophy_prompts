I'm a handling editor at an evolutionary biology journal, and I have a problem that won't go away. A large fraction of our submissions follow the same template: identify a trait, propose a selective regime that would have favored it, present comparative or experimental evidence consistent with that story, and conclude the trait is an adaptation for that function. The Gould–Lewontin spandrels paper is now over forty years old, and every author cites it in the introduction, but in practice our reviewers still evaluate adaptationist manuscripts by asking whether the evidence is consistent with the author's story rather than whether the alternatives have been seriously ruled out. I want to stop this at the editorial level.

Please write me a reviewer rubric — a decision procedure that a referee (and I, as handling editor) can use when evaluating a paper that claims trait T is an adaptation for function F. The procedure should be a sequence of questions or checks, ordered so that a paper can be rejected or sent back for major revision at an early step when it fails a basic burden. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer and what merely looks like one, and distinguish the failures that are fatal from those that can be addressed with more data. Flag the points where the reviewer has to make a judgment call rather than apply a clear criterion, and be explicit about which steps are grounded in the spandrels critique specifically — the problem of treating an organism as an atomized collection of separately optimized traits, the failure to consider structural and developmental constraints, the conflation of current utility with historical origin, and the neglect of drift and allometry — as opposed to general scientific skepticism that would apply to any causal claim. The procedure needs to be usable by a typical working evolutionary biologist who knows the empirical literature but hasn't read the philosophy.

Then walk the procedure through three hypothetical submissions (summarize the paper, don't fabricate data): (1) a comparative study claiming the human chin evolved as mechanical reinforcement against masticatory stress, (2) a paper arguing that a particular bird song variant functions as an honest signal of condition in mate choice, and (3) a paper revisiting the panda's thumb and arguing, against Gould, that the enlarged radial sesamoid is in fact a bona fide adaptation for bamboo stripping rather than a structurally constrained exaptation. For at least one of these, the procedure should return an answer the authors will not like — and explain why.

Finally, tell me where the procedure will systematically misfire: what kinds of adaptive claims it is structurally blind to, what forms of evidence it cannot assess (and therefore what kinds of papers it would unfairly penalize), and whether relying on it would bias the literature toward or away from any particular kind of evolutionary explanation.
