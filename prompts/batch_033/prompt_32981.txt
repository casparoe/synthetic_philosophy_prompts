I retired a few years ago from a career in quantitative genetics, and I have recently been reading moral philosophy, largely for the pleasure of watching careful people argue about matters I once took for granted. A family matter has now brought one of those arguments into my own house, and I find that I do not know how to advise the people involved.

My daughter and her husband are going through in vitro fertilization. Because my daughter is thirty-nine, the clinic recommended preimplantation genetic testing, which at first covered only chromosomal abnormalities and a small number of severe single-gene disorders; that seemed unremarkable to me. The clinic, however, now offers a further screen: polygenic risk scores for conditions such as schizophrenia, type 2 diabetes, and major depression, and, more disconcertingly, scores for complex traits such as body mass index and predicted educational attainment. The couple has seven viable embryos, and the reports rank them. My daughter is torn. She says that if she is going to bring one of these possible children into the world in any case, it would be almost negligent not to choose the one with the best prospects for a good life. Her husband replies that selecting on such scores treats their future child as a product to be optimized, and that there is something troubling about ranking one's own possible children by a polygenic number. They have asked me, as someone who once worked with exactly this kind of data, whether the act of selection itself is morally objectionable, or whether, once the decision to have a child has been made, it is not only permissible but perhaps even required to choose the embryo with the best expected life.

I am pulled in both directions. As a scientist, I am inclined to think that if information exists and is relevant to a decision, it would be irrational simply to ignore it. As a parent, I flinch at the thought of choosing a child the way one would select a line in a breeding program. There is also a further puzzle: the embryos not chosen would never have become anyone at all, so it is not obvious that anyone is harmed by the choice. I would be grateful for a clear way of thinking about this. Is there a defensible principle to the effect that we ought to select the possible child with the best chance of the best life, and if so, where does that principle run into difficulty once the traits in question go beyond the prevention of disease?
