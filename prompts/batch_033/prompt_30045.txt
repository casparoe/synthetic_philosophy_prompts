I'm working through William James's "What is an Emotion?" (Mind, Vol. 9, 1884) and find myself unable to resolve a tension in the central statement of his thesis. Here is the passage:

Our natural way of thinking about these standard emotions is that the mental perception of some fact excites the mental affection called the emotion, and that this latter state of mind gives rise to the bodily expression. My thesis on the contrary is that the bodily changes follow directly the perception of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur is the emotion. Common sense says, we lose our fortune, are sorry and weep; we meet a bear, are frightened and run; we are insulted by a rival, are angry and strike. The hypothesis here to be defended says that this order of sequence is incorrect, that the one mental state is not immediately induced by the other, that the bodily manifestations must first be interposed between, and that the more rational statement is that we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful, as the case may be. Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry.

My difficulty concerns what James means by "the emotion" itself. He writes that "the bodily changes follow directly the perception of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur is the emotion." This formulation distinguishes the bodily changes from the feeling of them, and identifies the emotion with the latter — a second-order mental state whose object is one's own peripheral physiology. The slogan that follows, however, seems to collapse this distinction: "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble." Here the crying, the striking, and the trembling — bodily events — appear to be treated as what the emotion consists in, or at least as what the emotion consists in feeling. And the closing lines of the passage say that without the bodily states, perception would be "purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth," as though the emotion simply is that felt warmth, directly given in the bodily arousal.

So I have three candidate readings of what James takes the emotion to be: (a) the bodily changes themselves, (b) the feeling (the interoceptive awareness) of those changes, or (c) some compound of bodily process and awareness thereof that James has not clearly teased apart. Which reading is best supported by the text, and which is philosophically the most defensible version of the view? Moreover, if the answer is (b) — the feeling of the changes — then James seems to require a perception-like act directed at one's own bodily condition. But if this act is itself a cognitive state (a perception or judgment about the body), does that not reintroduce the very split between cognition and emotion that James was trying to overcome, and risk making the emotion itself into something "purely cognitive in form"? I cannot tell whether this is a genuine structural problem for the theory or merely an infelicity in how James sets it up, and I would appreciate help thinking it through.
