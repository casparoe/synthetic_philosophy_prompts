I am a retired physical chemist, and after a long career spent with instruments, error bars, and noisy data, I have begun working through epistemology in earnest. A slogan I now encounter constantly, both in the literature and in the reading group I attend, is that assertion is governed by a knowledge norm — put roughly, that one should assert a proposition only if one knows it. Last week this slogan was used to settle a dispute, and the way it was used has left me uneasy. We were considering a speaker who asserts something true on the basis of grounds that fall short of knowledge: a true belief formed by reading a stopped clock, or a correct conclusion drawn from a flawed dataset. One member of the group dismissed the speaker as having violated the norm of assertion, since he did not know. I objected that in my former scientific life we routinely put forward claims we did not yet know — conjectures, preliminary findings, interpretations of noisy measurements — and that this practice seemed not merely permissible but essential to the enterprise. I was told that such utterances are either not assertions in the relevant sense or are defective as assertions.

I would like to know exactly what the slogan commits one to before I either accept it or push back. Could you lay out, as a menu of distinct readings, the precise theses that the slogan could be used to express? For each reading, please specify how the key terms are being precisified: what counts as an assertion for purposes of the norm (flat-out assertion, hedged conjecture, hypothesis, testimony); what kind of "should" is in play (constitutive of the speech act, regulative, epistemic, moral, conversational, prudential); whether the norm states a necessary condition, a sufficient condition, or a biconditional; and whose knowledge, at what time, is required. For each reading, please also say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and how it handles the case of scientific conjecture. I would also like to know how these readings compare with the rival claims that assertion is governed instead by a truth norm or a belief norm — which readings are genuinely distinct, which collapse into one another, and which best accounts for the scientific case. Finally, tell me which reading, if any, would actually support my interlocutor's rebuke of the stopped-clock speaker, and which readings would not.
