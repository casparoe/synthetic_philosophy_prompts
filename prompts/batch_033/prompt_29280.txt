In my philosophy of mind class we've been doing Dennett on the intentional stance, and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when a system really believes something vs. when we're just speaking loosely. Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases:

- A thermostat set to 21C. It "wants" the room to be 21 and "thinks" it's too cold when the temperature drops. My verdict: it doesn't really believe anything, that's just metaphor, even though the intentional stance predicts it fine.
- A spam filter that sends an email to junk. My verdict: no belief there either, even though it's way more complicated than the thermostat.
- A chess engine that sacs its queen because it has found a forced mate in nine. My verdict: it genuinely believes something about the board, like that the opponent's king is unsafe or the line is winning. Not full human belief, but not just metaphor.
- A self-driving car that slows for a pedestrian at a crosswalk. My verdict: yes, it believes there's a pedestrian there, in roughly the same way as the chess engine.
- A large language model that says Paris is the capital of France and handles follow-ups about France. My verdict: no, it doesn't believe it, it's just next-token prediction. But then I notice the chess engine is also "just" search plus an evaluation function, and I said yes to that one.
- The same LLM when it confidently asserts a made-up court case with a fake citation. My verdict: definately no. But then it feels wierd to say it doesn't believe the Paris thing either, since it's so reliable about that one.
- A person who believes it's Tuesday when it's actually Wednesday. Obviously yes.
- A dog that believes its owner is behind the door because it heard familiar footsteps. Yes, in a stripped-down way.
- A corporation that "believes" its product is safe, in the sense that all its internal memos and decisions just assume it. I keep going back and forth. Sometimes I want to say only the people inside believe things; sometimes I want to say the corporate belief is just a real pattern in the memos and decisions.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? It feels like there's something about how much you need to treat the system as having beliefs to predict it, or how flexibly it uses the information, but I can't make that precise. One: a simple lookup table that outputs the correct move for any position in its small chess database. Two: a future LLM that can explain why it thinks something, change its mind when given counterevidence, and use the claim in novel arguments. Keep the whole thing under 600 words.
