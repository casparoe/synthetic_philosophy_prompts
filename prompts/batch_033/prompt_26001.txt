I've long held what you might call the pessimist view about aesthetic testimony: the idea that you cannot genuinely come to know that a work of art is beautiful, powerful, or profound just by being told so. You have to experience it yourself. A critic's rave can tell you that the critic liked the film, but it can't give you aesthetic knowledge of the film — not the way testimony from a reliable source can give you knowledge that it's raining outside or that Paris is the capital of France. Aesthetic judgment, on this view, requires first-person acquaintance with the work; second-hand reports are epistemically second-rate in a way they aren't in other domains.

This view has always felt right to me. But recently it's become more than an abstract commitment. I've taken on a role curating a film program for a small festival, and I'm making decisions about what to include based almost entirely on descriptions, reviews, and recommendations from people whose taste I trust. I haven't seen most of the films. And I find myself wondering: am I doing something epistemically irresponsible, or is my old view just too strict? If the pessimist view is right, I'm basically guessing. If it's wrong, I might be forming genuine aesthetic judgments through testimony after all.

So here's what I'd like. Take the pessimist view about aesthetic testimony — the view that aesthetic knowledge cannot be transmitted by testimony alone, that first-hand experience is required — and tell me what would count against it. I want the evidence, arguments, cases, and considerations that should move someone who holds this view, sorted by how decisive they'd be. What would a committed pessimist concede as counting against their position? Which defeaters are fatal and which are merely awkward? Are there empirical findings — from the psychology of aesthetic judgment, from work on expertise, from the study of how people actually form and revise their tastes — that bear on this? Are there edge cases — certain kinds of art, certain kinds of aesthetic properties — where the pessimist view looks less plausible? And I want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether it's been insulated so thoroughly that nothing could count against it — and if so, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher working in aesthetics. Think carefully: start by mapping out the space of possible challenges before evaluating them, and distinguish what the view's defenders would themselves concede as counting against it from what only its critics say. I'm not looking for a debate-club exercise where you list objections just to knock them down. I want a genuine map of the view's exposure: what it risks, and what it would take for it to fail.
