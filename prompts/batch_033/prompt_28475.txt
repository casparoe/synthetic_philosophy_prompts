I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and next week I have to lead a fifty-minute tutorial for first-year undergraduates on split-brain cases and what they imply for the unity of consciousness. The students will have read a short introductory piece — something like Nagel's "Brain Bisection" paper — but will have no further background in neuroscience or philosophy of mind. I don't want you to explain the material to me; I want you to help me plan the tutorial.

Specifically, I need:

1. **The dependency structure.** What conceptual pieces must be in place before the central philosophical question even makes sense, and in what order should they be introduced? I take it the students need to grasp contralateral motor and sensory pathways, the role of the corpus callosum in interhemispheric transfer, the lateralization of language to the left hemisphere, and the logic of the divided-field experimental paradigm. But I am unsure whether to front-load all of this anatomy and methodology at the start or to introduce each piece only when the experimental finding that depends on it comes up. Which dependencies are strict, and which can be deferred?

2. **A carrying example.** Which one or two canonical experimental findings should do the heavy lifting throughout the session — for instance, the key-ring task where the left hand retrieves an object the right hemisphere saw but the left hemisphere cannot name, the chimeric face task, or the snow-shovel task where each hemisphere receives a different picture and the left hand draws what the right hemisphere saw while the patient confabulates an explanation? I want to pick a case that is vivid enough to anchor the discussion, that makes the conceptual puzzle unmistakable, and that I can return to repeatedly as the philosophical analysis deepens. What makes your recommended case pedagogically superior to the alternatives?

3. **A misconception map.** What will the students predictably misunderstand, and at what point in the sequence should I preempt or correct each one? I am especially worried about: (a) the conflation of split-brain phenomena with multiple personality or dissociative identity disorder; (b) the idea that each hemisphere houses a fully independent "person" with a complete mental life of its own; (c) the thought that commissurotomy simply "cuts consciousness in half" in a straightforward, spatial sense; (d) the assumption that because the right hemisphere cannot speak, it lacks consciousness or propositional attitudes; (e) the slide into treating the unity of consciousness as an all-or-nothing property rather than something that admits of degrees or dimensions and that might be present in some respects while absent in others.

4. **What to leave out.** Which distinctions or complications should I explicitly set aside for this level, and how should I signal that I am doing so? Candidates include the difference between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness as it bears on interpreting the split-brain data, the subcortical connections that remain intact after commissurotomy and complicate the picture, the distinction between synchronic and diachronic unity, and the question of whether the hemispheres are conscious in parallel during everyday life rather than only under experimental conditions that disrupt their typical coordination.

5. **A check for understanding.** One question or brief exercise I can pose at the end — not a factual recall question about the experiments, but something that reveals whether they have grasped the philosophical problem: why these cases put pressure on our ordinary concept of a single subject of experience, and what range of responses is available.
