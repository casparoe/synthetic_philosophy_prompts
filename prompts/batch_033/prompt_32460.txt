I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been working on a piece about what makes a life go well. In the course of reading and interviewing, I've collected my own verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them — or whether I need to revise some.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. **The standard experience machine.** You can plug into a machine that gives you a lifetime of whatever experiences you choose — achievement, love, discovery — but you won't actually be doing any of it, and you'll never know you're in the machine. I wouldn't plug in. The life in the machine seems to me worse than a real life with a similar experiential profile, even if the real life contains less pleasure overall.

2. **The experience machine with real connection.** Same as above, but the machine connects you to other real people who are also plugged in, so your relationships inside the simulation are with actual other minds. This makes the prospect more attractive, but I still wouldn't plug in. The relationships are real in one sense, but the shared world isn't.

3. **The palliative machine.** A patient with terminal cancer, in severe pain, with weeks to live and no prospect of meaningful activity, can enter a machine that gives them the experience of a peaceful, pain-free final month with their family. I think this is fine — not just permissible, but probably a good thing. This verdict seems to pull against my verdict on case 1, and I'm not sure what to do about that.

4. **The editor.** A device can selectively edit out specific painful experiences as they occur — the grief of a bereavement, say — while leaving the rest of your life and agency intact. You remember the loss factually but without the suffering. I think this is worse than just living through the grief. Something about editing your own emotional responses to real events seems to damage the life rather than improve it.

5. **The achievement machine.** You can plug into a machine that doesn't simulate experiences but instead causes real achievements — it manipulates your brain to make you more productive, more creative, more focused, so that you genuinely write the novel or solve the problem, but the motivation and ability come from the machine rather than from you. I'm uneasy about this but I can't say it makes the life worse. If the achievements are real and you're the one doing them, I'm not sure my objection to the experience machine carries over.

6. **The temporary machine.** You can enter the experience machine for a fixed period — say, a year — after which you return to your real life with full memory of both. I think I would do this, and I don't think it makes my life worse overall. But I can't see what principle lets me accept the temporary machine while rejecting the permanent one, given that the same experiential/reality distinction applies to each year inside.

7. **The result machine.** A device guarantees that your children will flourish, your work will have lasting impact, and your community will thrive — but you will never know it, because you'll be in a coma. (The outcomes are real; your experience of them is absent.) I think this does almost nothing for your well-being. The goods of life seem to require some connection to your experience, even if they aren't reducible to it.

What I'm after is this: Is there a principle about the relationship between experience and reality in well-being that captures these verdicts, or at least most of them? If no single principle gets them all, which verdicts should I reconsider, and why? I'm especially interested in whether the tension between cases 1 and 3 (and between 1 and 6) can be resolved without giving up the core intuition behind the experience machine objection.

Please also tell me what would change your mind about your analysis — which of my verdicts, if I turned out to be wrong about it, would most shift the principle you recommend, and where your own reasoning is most vulnerable.
