I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about whether information can be bad for you, and how that squares with Good's theorem. Here are my gut verdicts on cases:

- A free, perfectly reliable app tells me which of two gas stations is cheaper; I drive to the cheaper one. Obviously the information is good.
- A reliable source tells an investor a stock will drop about 10% next week; the investor panics, dumps their whole portfolio, and misses the rebound. I want to say the information hurt them, but I also think the problem was their updating, not the information.
- Someone with a severe fear of flying learns their plane has a one-in-ten-million chance of crashing, so they drive 600 miles instead and die in a car crash. Same as the last one: the info made them worse of, but I blame the way they weighed the risks.
- A patient in their last year of life is offered a free, accurate estimate of how many months they have left and declines because knowing would make them miserable. I think that's rational, and I think the information would genuinely be bad for them, even though there's no decision to make with it.
- A recovering addict knows that if they learn where their old dealer moved, they'll relapse, so they avoid finding out. I think that's rational too, and this one bugs me because they'd update on the information accurately, so I can't just blame bad reasoning.
- A manager is about to promote someone when a free, accurate background check turns up an old conviction that is legally irrelevant but, once known, triggers a company policy requiring a costly review. The manager ends up worse off, but I don't think that's a counterexample to Good's theorem, because the information changed which actions were available.
- A person learns their spouse is cheating, gets divorced, and is miserable for years. I don't think the information was bad; I think the marriage was bad and the information let them act on it.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, and what does it say Good's theorem is actually claiming? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what should I say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a person offered a free, accurate genetic test for a disease with no treatment, where the result would change nothing about what they can do but might change how they spend their remaining years in ways they can't predict?

Please write the answer in American English.
