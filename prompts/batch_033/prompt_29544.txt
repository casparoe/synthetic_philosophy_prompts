im a PhD student working on Pascal (mostly the Pensées and the Jansenist political theology around it), and I've sort of argued myself into a reading of the justice-and-force fragment that I now realize I've been defending more than testing. The fragment goes roughly like this: it's right to follow what's just, but it's necessary to follow what's strong; a just arrangement with no power behind it will just get overturned, while power without justice is tyranny, so the two have to be joined — either give the just side enough power, or give the powerful side enough justice. Then Pascal immediately forecloses the first option: justice is the kind of thing people can dispute forever, while force is obvious and nobody disputes who has it, so people couldnt hand power over to justice — force simply denied that the allegedly just thing was just and claimed that it, force, was the just one. So the route actually taken was the second one: the strong got themselves made just, or at least got themselves recognized as just.

The reading I've landed on is that Pascal means this as a kind of political realism before the term existed: for fallen human beings there's no usable, non-question-begging criterion of justice, so every actually existing 'justice' is just force that has managed to get itself accepted as authoritative. On my reading, the normative language — calling force-without-justice tyrannical, saying the two ought to be united — isn't the assertion of a real standard we could apply to fix the problem; it's part of the diagnosis of how the strong have to present themselves if their rule is going to be stable. So 'just' is, at the level of human societies, the name that successfully institutionalized force gets called.

I want to know what would count against this before I have to defend it in front of my supervisor, who thinks I'm overstating the realism. Not just that other scholars read him differently — I mean the actual textual, theological, and philosophical evidence that should move me: other things Pascal says in the Pensées (the three orders, custom making equity, imagination, the raison des effets), his Augustinianism and the Jansenist context, and the costs of the reading itself. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which of them a defender of my reading would concede are real costs and which we'd dismiss as missing the point. And give me a straight verdict on whether I've set the reading up so that no text could dislodge it — and if so, whether that's actually a problem for an interpretive claim like this.
