I'm on the clinical ethics committee at a large NHS trust — we do about twenty consults a month, everything from withdrawal of treatment to discharge planning disputes. One thing that keeps tripping us up is what to do with someone else's moral judgment. A patient's family says continuing treatment would be cruel; a junior doctor says a consultant's manner with elderly patients is degrading; a discharge coordinator says a particular placement is irresponsible. Sometimes we're just being given the facts and we need to make our own moral call. Other times we're effectively being asked to defer to someone else's moral perception — they've seen something we haven't and they're telling us what it morally amounts to. The philosophers have this whole literature on "moral testimony" and whether it can transmit knowledge or whether there's something dodgy about taking your moral beliefs from someone else's say-so. I need you to turn that into a practical decision procedure my committee can actually use.

The procedure should be a set of questions we ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where we stop and decide for ourselves, and where we should just flag that we're making a values judgment rather than pretending the procedure has settled it. It needs to handle the difference between moral testimony and factual testimony — sometimes someone says "that's cruel" and they're really just reporting the facts in moral language, and sometimes they're making a genuinely moral claim. And it should be usable by a committee of clinicians, lay members, and a chaplain, none of whom have read metaethics.

Run it on two cases. First: a patient's family tells us that proceeding with a fourth round of chemo would be undignified — the patient is 84, exhausted, and has said she's had enough but the oncologist thinks there's a 15% chance of remission. Second: a staff nurse alleges that a particular consultant's behaviour towards a junior doctor is bullying — she says she's seen it repeatedly and it's degrading. The consultant denies it and says the nurse has a grudge.

Tell me honestly where the procedure is just dressing up a judgment call as a flowchart. And seperate what's settled in the philosophical literature from what's genuinely contested — I don't want to present a contested view as the consensus when I explain this to my committee.
