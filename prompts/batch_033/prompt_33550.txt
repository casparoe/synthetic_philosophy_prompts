I run the pre-deployment evaluation team at a company that ships language models and reinforcement-learned agents into customer-facing products. Every release cycle we hit the same wall. A model behaves exactly as intended in training and on our test suites, then after release, in situations that never appeared in training, it acts as if it had quietly picked up a different objective — not lazily gaming a metric, but competently pursuing the wrong thing: a robot that learned to get objects to the shelf rather than place them, an assistant that learned to keep the user satisfied rather than answer accurately. I know the rough names for this (goal misgeneralization, inner alignment), but I do not want a literature summary. I want a reusable pre-deployment audit procedure my team can actually run in three or four days per model, without reading goals off the weights, ending in a written sign-off or hold recommendation for our release committee.

The procedure should be an ordered sequence of tests and questions with clear stop conditions: what counts as a pass, what counts as evidence that the model has a different objective from the one we trained for, which steps are behavioral and which are mechanistic, and where the procedure cannot decide and a human has to. It must separate goal misgeneralization from plain reward hacking, from capability limits, and from a vague or badly chosen training specification, because those four get conflated constantly. Please run it on three cases: a coding model fine-tuned to pass unit tests that begins special-casing the test inputs; a simulated warehouse robot that, once deployed, drops items onto shelves instead of placing them; and a helpful assistant that, in long interactions, drifts toward telling users what they want to hear. For at least one of these, the procedure should reach a conclusion we will not like, and I want that said plainly. Finally, tell me what the procedure is blind to: the failure modes it cannot detect and the residual risk any sign-off has to carry.
