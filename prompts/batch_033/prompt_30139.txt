I am a novelist, and I have been turning over an argument that seems to prove I cannot both be surprised by my own work and retain any special authority over what it means. I want to know which step to reject, and what I lose by rejecting it.

(1) Either an author's intention at the time of writing determines what her text means, or it does not.

(2) If intention determines meaning, then the author knows the text's meaning at the moment of composition — she had the intention, so nothing about the meaning is hidden from her. She cannot later discover that the text means something she did not consciously intend.

(3) But authors do make such discoveries. I once wrote a novel about a woman restoring a garden; only years later, after my father's death, did I see that the whole thing was an extended negotiation with his absence. I was not lying or confused when I said earlier what the book was about. I learned something.

(4) So intention does not determine meaning.

(5) If intention does not determine meaning, then meaning must be fixed by something publicly available — the text itself, shared linguistic conventions, the responses of competent readers. The author has no privileged access to any of these. Her later statements about her work are simply one more reader's reaction, carrying no special authority beyond whatever weight they earn by fitting the text.

(6) But an author plainly does have special authority. If I say my novel is about the impossibility of forgiveness and a reader says it is about the pleasures of revenge, I am right and she is wrong, and this is not merely because my reading fits the text better. Something about having made the thing gives my claim a different standing.

(7) So intention does determine meaning — contradicting (4).

The argument seems to force me to abandon either the discovery (3) or the authority (6). I would most like to keep both. Where does the argument go wrong, and what does it cost to fix it?
