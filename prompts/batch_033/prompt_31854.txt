I serve on the state's Advertising Practices Review Board, which hears complaints about manipulative advertising and marketing. We've issued a series of decisions over the past three years that are starting to form a pattern, though we've never articulated an explicit principle. Here are our key precedents:

1. A car dealership ran a newspaper ad showing a vehicle at an eye-catching price with the words "starting at" in small type. We ruled this was legitimate persuasion — the qualifying language was present, even if inconspicuous.

2. A weight-loss supplement company featured a testimonial from someone who claimed to have lost thirty pounds using the product, without disclosing that the person was simultaneously on a physician-supervised diet and exercise regimen. We ruled this was manipulation.

3. A political campaign sent mailers designed to resemble official government notices, complete with "FINAL NOTICE" in red and a government-style seal, though the content was standard campaign messaging. We ruled this was manipulation.

4. A gym required that membership cancellations be made in person at the original sign-up location, knowing that many members would continue paying rather than make the trip. We ruled this was manipulation.

5. A supermarket placed fresh flowers and produce at the store entrance, creating a pleasant first impression before customers reached the aisles. We ruled this was legitimate persuasion.

6. A professor assigned her own textbook to her class, describing it as "highly recommended" without disclosing that she received royalties on each sale. We ruled this was manipulation (the board has jurisdiction over campus commercial practices).

7. A social media app sent a notification to inactive users: "Your friends are waiting for you!" The statement was literally true — the user's friends were active on the platform — and the user could dismiss the notification with one tap. We ruled this was legitimate persuasion.

8. A website's cookie consent banner displayed "Accept All" as a large, brightly colored button and "Manage Preferences" as a small, low-contrast text link. We ruled this was manipulation.

Now a new case has landed on our docket. An online learning platform sends students a weekly email with their current course ranking relative to classmates: "You're in the top 30% of your cohort this week," or "You're currently in the bottom quartile." The rankings are accurate, the platform is voluntary, and students can opt out of the emails at any time. The platform's own internal research shows that the ranking emails increase engagement, with the largest effect on students who discover they are below average.

I need to write the board's opinion. Before I do, help me get clear on the principles we've been running implicitly. Identify the two most plausible principles that fit all eight of our precedents. For each principle, tell me what it would say about the ranking-email case. Which of our precedents is the closest analog to this new case? Which principle do you think better captures our pattern, and why? Give me your best prediction of what we'll decide, and be honest about what principle we would be adopting with each choice. Finally, describe a further case — not yet on our docket — that would cleanly separate the two principles, such that our decision on it would settle which one we are really committed to.
