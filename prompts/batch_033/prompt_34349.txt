Something that's been bothering me. Historic ship museums — think the USS Constitution or the Cutty Sark — have conservation policies that let them replace basically every piece of wood, iron, and rigging over the decades. After a century or two of this, none of the original material is left, but curators, the navy, the public all treat it as the same ship. Nobody calls it a replica. Yet if an art restorer repainted every square inch of a Rembrandt, we'd say the painting was destroyed. And if I stole your laptop and slowly replaced the motherboard, then the screen, then the case, a court would eventually have to ask whether I still possess your laptop or a different one. What view of identity over time does the ship-conservation practice commit to? Does it actually presuppose a solution to the Ship of Theseus problem, or does it just rely on something like institutional say-so? And is it consistent with how we handle identity in these other cases, or are we just being inconsistent?
