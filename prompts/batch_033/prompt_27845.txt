I'm preparing a short presentation for a seminar on the philosophy of probability, and I've been asked to write down my actual commitments about single-case chances so the group can stress-test them as a package. Before I present, I'd like an audit of whether the view I've landed on is stable. Here are the commitments. (1) Single-case probabilities are objective and well-defined: when I say that this patient has a 0.72 chance of surviving this surgery, I take myself to be asserting something that is determinately true or false, not merely reporting my degree of belief or my ignorance. (2) I hold a frequentist account of such probabilities: the patient's chance just is the relative frequency of the outcome in an appropriate reference class of relevantly similar cases. (3) I accept the narrowest-reference-class principle: the appropriate class is the most specific class for which we have adequate statistical data, so a broad class like all surgical patients is the wrong class once we know the patient is a 68-year-old diabetic with advanced kidney disease. (4) At the same time, I deny that there is any interest-independent, God's-eye fact about which of the many true descriptions of this patient selects the one correct reference class; the choice of class is guided by our predictive purposes and by which data we happen to have. (5) I nevertheless think the resulting probabilities are not arbitrary or merely subjective: once a class is fixed, the frequency is an objective matter, and the choice among classes is disciplined by the data and by our purposes. I want to know whether these five commitments form a consistent package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each genuine tension? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. I'd like a committed answer: take a definite position on which commitment or pair is the real source of instability and recommend a specific fix, with reasons, rather than laying out a menu of options for me to choose from.
