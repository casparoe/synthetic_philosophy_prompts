I hold the view that Bradley's regress is genuinely vicious and that the only adequate response is to reject the assumption that a relation is a further entity of the same ontological kind as its relata. Specifically, I endorse the broadly Fregean solution: relations are incomplete or unsaturated entities that, by their very nature, reach out to their relata and unify them into a fact without requiring any additional linking relation. The regress is stopped not by positing a special kind of relation, but by recognizing that the unity of a state of affairs is not itself an entity in need of being related to its constituents.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking only for the standard objections that critics of the Fregean solution have raised, but for a systematic map of the position's exposure: which arguments, intuitions, formal results, or metaphysical consequences would tell against it, sorted from those that would be fatal to those that are merely awkward. For each, I would like to know whether a committed defender of the unsaturatedness solution would concede that it counts against them, or whether they would dismiss it as resting on a misunderstanding. I also want an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated by qualifications — for instance, by leaning on a metaphor of "incompleteness" that resists precise formulation — and whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind.

Please present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
