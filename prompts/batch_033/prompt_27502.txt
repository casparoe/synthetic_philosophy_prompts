I recently read a popular article on decision theory, and it left me with the nagging sense that the argument was moving too quickly, though I could not say exactly where. The article's summary of the case for expected utility theory went roughly as follows:

"Decision theorists have proved, with mathematical certainty, that anyone whose choices violate the axioms of expected utility theory is irrational. The proof is elegant: if your preferences are inconsistent — for example, you prefer A to B, B to C, and yet C to A — then a bookie can offer you a series of trades, each one attractive on its own, which together guarantee that you lose money no matter what happens. This 'money pump' argument shows that violating expected utility theory makes you exploitable, and therefore that the only coherent way to choose is to assign precise probabilities and utilities to outcomes and always select the option with the highest expected utility. In short, unless you want to be a walking cash machine, you must be an expected utility maximiser."

I gather that there is a real body of work here — representation theorems, Dutch book arguments, money pumps — and that the relationship between these formal results and the claim "you must maximise expected utility" is less straightforward than the article implies. I would like an accuracy audit of that summary. Specifically: which sentences would a decision theorist working on these foundations accept, which would they qualify, and which would they reject? How do representation theorems, Dutch book arguments for probabilistic degrees of belief, and money-pump arguments for the expected utility axioms relate to one another, and does the article's final conclusion actually follow from any of them? Where the article is unfair, what would a defender of coherence arguments say in its place, and what residual force, if any, does the corrected argument still have?
