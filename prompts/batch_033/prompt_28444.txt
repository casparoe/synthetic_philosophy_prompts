I'm teaching a unit on classical Chinese philosophy and want to use Lord of the Flies to get my students thinking about the Mencius-Xunzi debate on human nature. The basic split: Mencius thinks we're born with innate sprouts of goodness (compassion, shame, deference, a sense of right and wrong) that need nurturing but are genuinely there from the start; Xunzi thinks our raw material is selfish and unruly, and we only become good through deliberate cultivation — ritual, education, the whole artifice of civilization.

On a first pass, Golding looks like he's handing the win to Xunzi. You strand a bunch of English schoolboys on an island without adults or institutions and within weeks they're hunting each other. But I think there's more going on, and I don't want my students to just file the novel under "human nature bad, Xunzi right." So here's what I'd like: a careful reading of the novel through both lenses that's fair to each. Where do we see Mencian sprouts in the boys — and I don't just mean Simon being saintly, I mean the smaller moments: the littluns' instinctive fear as something like proto-shame, Piggy's sense of fairness about the conch, even Ralph's early impulse to build shelters. Do these count as evidence for Mencius, or does the novel show them getting snuffed out so thoroughly that it actually strengthens Xunzi's case (i.e., the sprouts are too weak to matter without institutions doing the heavy lifting)? And what about the ending — the naval officer showing up and the boys collapsing into sobs. Is that a Mencian moment (the return of civilization reawakens their true nature, and they grieve what they became) or a Xunzian one (the artifice of adult authority snaps back into place and they're children again, which just proves the self is a product of its environment)?

I also want you to push back where the novel resists both frameworks. Xunzi's whole thing is that ritual and conscious effort can transform anyone — but Lord of the Flies has Jack, who seems to actively enjoy cruelty in a way that doesn't fit "selfish raw material that needs shaping." And Mencius has a problem with Roger, who doesn't just fail to nurture his sprouts but seems to delight in stamping them out. Does the novel ultimately endorse a third view — something more Hobbesian, or more Augustinian — that neither Mencius nor Xunzi can quite capture? I'd rather you tell me where the reading breaks than force it.
