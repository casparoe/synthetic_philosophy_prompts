I am a retired natural scientist, and I have only recently begun to read political philosophy with any seriousness. I have been trying to discipline my thinking about distributive justice in the way I once disciplined my thinking about a new body of data: first record the particular judgments I actually hold, then ask what general rule, if any, would generate them. I would like help with the second step.

Let me fix the subject matter. All of the following cases concern the distribution of income and wealth within a single, self-contained society, holding the population fixed, and I am asking only about the justice of the resulting distribution, not about efficiency, legality, or political feasibility. The figures are annual household incomes in dollars.

My case judgments:

1. Floor and top both rise. A society's least advantaged households have an income of $20,000 and its most advantaged $200,000. A change raises the former to $25,000 and the latter to $400,000. I count this as acceptable, even though the absolute gap widens.

2. Top rises alone. A change leaves the least advantaged at $20,000 and raises the most advantaged to $1,000,000. I do not count this as acceptable.

3. Same floor, different spreads. I am asked to choose between two feasible arrangements. In arrangement A the least advantaged have $25,000 and the most advantaged $300,000. In arrangement B the least advantaged also have $25,000 and the most advantaged $120,000. I prefer B, and not merely for aesthetic reasons: I regard A as in one respect less just than B.

4. Gains from productive innovation. A change raises the floor from $20,000 to $24,000 because new productive techniques enlarge total output, and the gains are widely though unevenly shared; the innovators gain far more than the bottom. I count this as acceptable.

5. Gains from inherited monopoly. A change also raises the floor from $20,000 to $24,000, but the mechanism is that a small hereditary class secures new legal monopolies, and the improved floor is financed from a fraction of the resulting rents. The resulting income distribution is the same as in case 4, yet I am uneasy about this case in a way that I am not uneasy about case 4.

6. A negligible floor gain. A change raises the floor from $20,000 to $20,100 and raises the top from $200,000 to $10,000,000. I want to say this is not acceptable, but I notice that if $20,100 really is the highest floor any feasible arrangement can produce, then my reasoning in case 1 seems to push the other way.

7. The next generation. A change raises the floor for the present generation from $20,000 to $26,000, but it does so by depleting a resource, with the predictable consequence that the floor for the next generation will be $16,000. I do not count this as acceptable.

8. Money income and social standing. A change raises the floor in money terms from $20,000 to $27,000, but it also sharply increases social distance: those at the bottom can no longer afford the forms of dress, schooling, and travel that have become expected, and they report that they cannot participate in common social life without shame. I do not count this as an improvement in the position of the least advantaged, despite the higher money income.

My tentative principle, drawn from Rawls, is something like this: inequalities in income and wealth are just only if they are part of a scheme that leaves the least advantaged at least as well off as they would be under any feasible alternative; and the veil of ignorance is a way of arguing that rational parties who did not know their place in society would choose something in that neighborhood. But I can already see that several of my verdicts sit awkwardly with that principle, and I am not sure whether to repair the principle or abandon the verdicts.

What I would like is this. First, state as precisely as you can the principle, or two or three candidate principles, that best captures these judgments. Second, say which case or cases motivate each clause of the principle. Third, apply the principle to cases I have not listed, including at least one involving risk or uncertainty about outcomes rather than known distributions. Fourth, give me an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which of my verdicts I would have to overturn if I insisted on keeping the principle simple. Finally, tell me whether the veil-of-ignorance argument, as I have described it, actually selects the principle you end up with, or whether the cases and the veil pull in different directions.
