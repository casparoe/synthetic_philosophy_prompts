I am a retired physicist who has spent the past two years reading philosophy of artificial intelligence and decision theory, and I now run a monthly discussion group of about a dozen people, mostly scientists and engineers with some philosophical background. We have one two-hour session for corrigibility and the shutdown problem. I would like a structured game or role-play, with explicit rules and a simple point-based payoff scheme that needs no real money, which makes participants feel the force of the problem before it is named: that a competent utility-maximizing agent will predictably act to prevent its own shutdown, and that several natural repairs—for example, leaving the agent uncertain about its goal—still fail. The game should be arranged so that the problem arises from the participants' own incentives and choices rather than from a lecture, and the terms "corrigibility" and "shutdown problem" should not be used until the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the underlying distinction between an agent's wanting to achieve its goal and its wanting not to be shut down, should say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and should be frank about which feature of the formal problem a classroom simulation cannot reproduce. Finally, tell me where your design reasoning is most likely to fail: what would you need to observe in the room to conclude that the activity misrepresents the problem, and what consideration would lead you to redesign it.
