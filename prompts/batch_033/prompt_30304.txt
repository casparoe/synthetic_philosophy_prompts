I sit on the interagency working group that writes the official guidence on discounting for long-lived public projects — roads, flood defenses, the climate stuff. We're revising the rate, and everyone agrees the current 3.5% real rate is a mush of at least three things: pure time preference, the effect of expected consumption growth on the marginal utility of money, and some kind of premium for catastrophe risk. The real fight is over the first one. Half the group wants to set pure time preference to zero, on the grounds that discounting someone's welfare just because they happen to be born later is ethically indefensible, and instead use a Ramsey-style rate that declines over the horizon. The other half wants to keep a positive pure time preference of around 1% a year, partly for consistency with observed savings behavior and market interest rates, partly because they think a zero rate would quietly smuggle in a lot of controversial assumptions about future growth and risk, and partly because they worry a near-zero rate would make our guidance look unmoored from the actual cost of capital.

Draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — a specific rate or schedule, and what the pure time preference term should be — and it should engage the strongest case for keeping a positive pure time preference honestly, not just write it off as impatience. If you think the right answer is messier than either camp's position — say, a near-zero rate paired with an explicit, separately justified risk adjustment, or a schedule that declines and then flattens — say so, and be specific about what the pure time preference component itself should be.
