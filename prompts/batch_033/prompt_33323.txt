My grandmother died a couple months ago and I've been kind of obsessively reading about afterlife arguments since then — not because I'm religious, I'm really not, but the finality of it hit me way harder than I expected. Anyway I stumbled on these letters Gödel wrote to his mother in 1961. She'd asked him whether he believed they'd see each other again (she was getting old, he was in Princeton and never went back to Europe), and he actually answered with a full argument. The core of it is: if the world is rationally organized and has meaning, then there must be an afterlife. Because what kind of meaning would there be in producing a being with this enormous range of possibilities for development — intellectual, personal, relational — and then letting them achieve not even a thousandth of it? He compares it to laying a foundation for a house and then just walking away from the project, letting it rot. The waste would be irrational, and a rationally ordered world can't countenance that kind of waste.

Most people I've mentioned this to roll their eyes and say it's just wishful thinking from a genius who was also paranoid and kind of unwell. But I've been sitting with this argument for weeks and I actually think it's better than that — way better. It's not the cosmological argument or the ontological argument or any of the standard ones. It's something about the structure of rational order and the mismatch between human capacity and a single lifespan. And I keep thinking there's something almost Gödelian about it — like, the same mind that proved incompleteness is making a move here about how the system of a human life can't be completed within itself, that something outside the system is needed.

So here's what I want: please argue that Gödel's argument for the afterlife is philosophically stronger than the dismissive treatment it usually gets. Make the best case for it — not as a proof, obviously, but as a serious piece of philosophical reasoning that deserves to be taken seriously. And if you can, say something about whether the argument has any genuine structural connection to his incompleteness results or whether I'm just projecting that. Also, if there's a hidden weakness that even the best version of the argument can't escape, I want to hear that too — I'm not trying to just feel better, I actually want to know what's there.
