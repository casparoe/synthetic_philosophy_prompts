I am an undergraduate currently taking a seminar on the ethics of blame. In our last meeting we were discussing whether people in affluent countries may criticize one another for participating in harmful practices — fast fashion, frequent flying, that sort of thing. A classmate shut the discussion down with the proverb "people who live in glass houses shouldn't throw stones." His claim was that anyone implicated in those systems has no standing to blame anyone else for being implicated in them, so the entire line of criticism was void.

I want to understand what this proverb could actually be asserting, because it seems to me to bundle several different thoughts together. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the proverb might be used to make — among them, claims about the truth of the criticism itself, about the critic's standing or entitlement to voice it, about the critic's credibility or sincerity, about the fairness of holding others to a standard one does not meet, and about whether anyone caught up in a harmful practice may criticize it at all. For each reading, say whether it is a near-trivial practical observation, a substantive and defensible philosophical thesis, or a false or self-undermining one, and briefly explain your verdict.

I would then like a committed assessment, not merely a menu of options. Identify the reading you think best captures whatever is genuinely defensible in the proverb, and state whether that reading vindicates my classmate's move in the seminar. If it does not, say exactly where his use of the proverb goes wrong, and identify any stronger reading under which his conclusion would follow but which is itself false or much too strong. That will help me see what he would need to establish for the proverb to do the work he wanted it to do.
