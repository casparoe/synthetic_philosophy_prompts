I spent thirty years in neurobiology — mostly systems-level stuff, some fMRI work toward the end — and since retiring I've been reading philosophy of mind, which I never had time for during my career. The Churchlands' eliminative materialism has gotten under my skin in a way most philosophy doesn't. The idea that "belief" and "desire" are theoretical posits of a stagnant folk psychology, and that a mature neuroscience might simply discard them rather than reduce them — it's bracing, and honestly it makes me look at my own mental life differently. But when I tried to explain it to my former postdoc over drinks, he just laughed and said "so you're telling me you don't actually believe eliminative materialism?" and I didn't have a crisp answer.

I want you to write a dialogue between two characters set in a neuroscience lab, late at night, in front of a monitor showing fMRI activation maps from a decision-making experiment. One character is a committed eliminative materialist about propositional attitudes — she holds something like the Churchland line that beliefs and desires are the posits of a false and stagnant theory and will be eliminated, not smoothly reduced, by advancing neuroscience. The other is a realist about folk psychology — he thinks beliefs and desires pick out real, causally efficacious internal states, maybe along broadly Fodorian lines, and that talk of eliminating them is confused. They're looking at the same brain scans and this sparks the conversation.

Both need to be genuinely sharp. No straw men. When the eliminativist says folk psychology has a terrible track record of explanatory failures — the realist should push back with something specific, not just indignation. When the realist says the eliminativist can't even state her view without using the concept of belief — the eliminativist should have a real reply, not a dodge. I want at least three substantive exchanges where each character responds to the actual argument the other just made. The dialogue can end unresolved, but it should end with a one-sentence summary of the deepest point of disagreement between them, as the last line.
