I'm putting together a "which view of aesthetic testimony do you actually hold?" quiz for an undergraduate aesthetics reading group. Write eight questions, each built around a concrete case — e.g., a friend whose taste you trust tells you a film you haven't seen is excellent; a detailed review tells you a painting is garish; you're on a prize jury working only from written descriptions; you saw a performance but were distracted and a companion fills in what you missed. Give each question two or three answer options. No question may use the word "testimony," ask whether one can learn about beauty from others, or invite the reader to pick a label. Include a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto these positions: (i) the acquaintance requirement — no genuine aesthetic judgment or justified aesthetic belief without first-hand experience of the work; (ii) weak pessimism — testimony can transmit knowledge that a work is beautiful but not the aesthetic judgment or appreciation itself; (iii) optimism — testimony is a normal source of aesthetic knowledge and can ground aesthetic judgment; and (iv) an evidential view — testimony supplies non-aesthetic reasons from which one may form an aesthetic judgment, though the judgment remains one's own. The key should be driven entirely by what readers say about the cases rather than by which label they like, and it should flag any recurring answer pattern that corresponds to no defended position (with a one-line diagnosis of whether the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored) and any pair of positions the cases cannot cleanly separate.
