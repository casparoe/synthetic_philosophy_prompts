I'm taking a seminar on consciousness and we spent last week on epiphenomenalism, and I've been going back and forth with a friend about an argument that feels like it proves too much. The view in question says phenomenal properties—what it's like to feel pain, see red, taste coffee—are caused by the brain but cause nothing themselves, not even your own judgments about them. Here's the argument, roughly as my friend put it:

(1) If phenomenal properties are epiphenomenal, then my judgment that I am in pain is caused entirely by physical processes, and the pain itself is causally idle with respect to that judgment.

(2) If the pain is causally idle with respect to the judgment, then I would make the very same judgment even if the pain were absent, so long as the physical processes were the same.

(3) If I would make the very same judgment whether or not it were true, then that judgment is not justified—or at least it isn't knowledge.

(4) But I do know that I am in pain when I am in pain; it's about as certain as anything I have.

(5) So epiphenomenalism about phenomenal properties is false, and in fact self-undermining, since if it were true we could never know it.

I don't quite buy that a view this seriously defended could be dispatched in five steps, and something about (3) smells like it's doing too much work—maybe a belief can be justified by how reliably it's produced even if the fact it's about doesn't cause it. But then I can't say what's special about introspection, and if I reject (4) I've given up the very thing I was trying to explain. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to?
