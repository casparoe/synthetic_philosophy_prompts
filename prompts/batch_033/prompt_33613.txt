I serve on my medical school's Clinical Skills Committee. We're revising our policy on students' use of AI diagnostic decision-support tools during the third-year internal medicine clerkship. Currently, students are expected to arrive at diagnoses through their own history-taking, physical exam, and clinical reasoning, consulting only attending physicians and residents. But AI tools are now integrated into the hospital's EHR system, and many residents and attendings use them routinely — not as a substitute for judgment, but as a check on differential diagnoses and a prompt for overlooked possibilities. A group of clerkship directors wants us to permit students to use these tools as well, arguing that learning to work with AI is now part of competent clinical practice and that withholding the tools creates an artificial training environment that doesn't reflect the reality students will face in residency. The opposing group argues that diagnostic reasoning is a foundational skill that must be developed without AI scaffolding; if students learn to rely on these tools from the start, they'll never develop the deep pathophysiological understanding that lets a physician catch an atypical presentation or question the AI's output when it's wrong. They point to research from other fields — aviation, radiology, financial auditing — showing that when professionals outsource judgment to automated systems early in training, their unaided performance deteriorates and their conceptual grasp of the domain becomes shallower in ways that are difficult to repair later.

The committee is divided roughly evenly, and I need to draft the decision memo that will be circulated before our vote next week. The memo should recommend either permitting AI use, prohibiting it, or permitting it with specified conditions, and should give reasons that both sides can recognize as fair. Please engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side rather than dismissing it. Limit the memo to 800 words.
