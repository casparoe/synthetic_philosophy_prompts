I recently encountered the following passage in a book on democratic theory written for a general audience:

"Condorcet's Jury Theorem proves mathematically that democratic decision-making is more reliable than rule by the few. The theorem shows that so long as each voter is more likely than not to get the right answer on a yes-or-no question, adding more voters makes it increasingly probable that the majority will be correct. With a large enough electorate, the chance of a correct outcome is effectively one hundred percent. This is why we should trust democratic procedures over expert panels or authoritarian decision-makers: the wisdom of the crowd, underwritten by Condorcet's mathematics, guarantees that the majority gets it right."

I teach social epistemology, and I have the sense that this summary is doing rather a lot of work with rather little care. I would be grateful for a systematic evaluation. Please identify which claims in the passage are accurate renderings of what the theorem actually states, which are defensible interpretations, and which are overstatements or outright errors. In particular, I would like to know what assumptions the theorem requires that the passage does not mention, and whether those assumptions, if made explicit, would weaken the political morals the author wishes to draw.
