A common move in philosophy of science textbooks is to introduce inference to the best explanation by analogy with detective work: the scientist, like the detective, surveys the evidence, considers possible explanations, and identifies the one that best accounts for the clues. The analogy is meant to do real justificatory work — it is not merely pedagogical — because it is used to deflect the charge that IBE is circular or mysterious: after all, nobody thinks there is anything wrong with a detective inferring the butler did it from the footprints, the motive, and the missing candlestick. I want to know how far this analogy actually holds. Give me the mapping: what corresponds to the detective, the crime, the suspect pool, the clues, the standard of best, and the verdict. Then identify the disanalogies that matter — especially the ones where the detective has resources (a known causal structure, a finite and specifiable suspect set, the possibility of independent verification through confession or further evidence) that the scientist often lacks. Once those are on the table, tell me whether the analogy can still support the conclusion that IBE is an unproblematic form of inference, or whether the cases where it works are precisely the ones where something else is doing the justificatory work. I want a committed answer, not a survey of positions.
