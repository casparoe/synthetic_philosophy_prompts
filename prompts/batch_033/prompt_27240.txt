Two friends of mine, both grad students in the same lab, got into an argument I'm still chewing on. They were trying to predict whether a particular paper from a rival lab would replicate. They'd read the same preprint, knew the same background about the field, and had spent an hour going through the methods section together. By the end, Maria thought there was about an 80% chance it would replicate; Josh thought maybe 30%. Neither was holding anything back — they'd aired all their reasons.

Josh said this was a problem. He'd just read about Aumann's agreement theorem and argued that two rational people with the same information can't disagree about probabilities. So at least one of them had to be messing up. Maria said that can't be right — they're both smart, they've shared everything, and they still disagree, so the theorem must have a loophole. She pointed out that Aumann's result assumes they started with the same prior, and they obviously didn't: she's more optimistic about the field's methods in general, he's more skeptical, and those priors came from years of different experiences. Josh said fine, but if the theorem only works when you assume the conclusion — that everyone already agrees on the priors — then what's the point of it? Maria said the point is that if you did share priors, disagreement would be irrational, which is still a nontrivial result. Josh said that just pushes the question back: are their differing priors themselves rational? Maria said priors aren't the kind of thing that can be rational or irrational — they're starting points, not conclusions. Josh said that's way too permissive; by that logic you could have a prior that the earth is flat and call it rational as long as you update consistently. Maria said no, because a flat-earth prior would get crushed by evidence pretty fast, and the fact that theirs haven't converged yet just means the evidence from one preprint isn't strong enough to swamp different starting points. Josh said that sounds like you're admitting the theorem is right but the evidence here is weak, which was kind of his point all along — they should both be less confident. Maria said that's a different claim and he's moving the goalposts.

I had to leave before they resolved it. Can you go through this exchange move by move and say who's right about what? Don't just pick a winner — tell me where each of them makes a good point and where they go wrong.
