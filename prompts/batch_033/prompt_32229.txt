I've been puzzling over a thing in how courts actually work, and I keep coming back to something Pascal says about justice and force—paraphrasing, since I don't have the text handy: justice without force is helpless, but force without justice is tyranny. A judge writes an opinion explaining why the law requires some outcome, as if the reasoning is what obliges the parties. But then the order gets its actual grip from a sherrif, a bailiff, marshals, the threat of jail or seizure. If the losing party ignores the reasoning and obeys only because of the force, the system still speaks as though the order binds because it is just. What theory of the relation between justice and force does that practice presuppose? Is it Pascal's view, some other view, or is the practice genuinly underdetermined between a few? Also, what would the practice look like if we rebuilt it around the theory you identify—would judges say something different about why their orders bind, or would enforcement look different? And tell me how confident you are in your reconstruction, and what the point is you're least sure of.
