I work in a lab that builds minimal synthetic cells. Every six months I write a grant report that says we aim to construct a minimal living system from the bottom up. After three years of this, I still cannot tell you what would count as having succeeded—because I cannot say what "living" means in a way that survives contact with the borderline cases my own work generates.

The philosophical debate about the definition of life has been running since at least the 1970s without resolution. The same positions recur: metabolic definitions (life as autopoiesis, as self-maintaining far-from-equilibrium systems), evolutionary definitions (life as populations of entities capable of Darwinian evolution), informational definitions (life as systems that store and process semantic information). Then there are the deflationists who argue that "life" is a folk concept with no natural-kind boundary, and the pluralists who say we should use different definitions for different purposes. And then there are the borderline cases—viruses, prions, transposons, the synthetic vesicles we build that metabolize but do not replicate, the ones that replicate but do not metabolize, the computer viruses that some people half-seriously propose as alive—which are deployed to show that every definition either includes too much or excludes too little.

My working hypothesis is that the debate is not really about finding the right necessary and sufficient conditions. It is about what work the category "life" is supposed to do. Astrobiologists need a definition that lets them recognize life on another planet. Origins-of-life researchers need a definition that tells them what they are trying to explain the origin of. People in my field need a definition that tells us when we have succeeded at building it. A lawyer or an ethicist might need a definition that tells them what has moral standing. These are not the same question. The debate has stalled, I suspect, because the participants are answering different questions under the same word, and the disagreement looks substantive only because everyone assumes there is a single phenomenon—life—that all these questions are about.

But I am not confident in this diagnosis. It might be too glib. It might be that there really is a substantive disagreement about the structure of the natural world, and that the borderline cases are genuinely diagnostic. It might be that the debate is stalled because it is simply hard, not because it is ill-posed. It might be that the right move is to abandon the category entirely—but then I have to explain to my grant agency why I am no longer building something "living."

Diagnose this debate. What kind of disagreement is it, really? Is my working hypothesis correct, or is something else going on? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—for me, sitting in my lab, trying to figure out whether the thing in the flask is alive?
