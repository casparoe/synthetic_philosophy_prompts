So I'm about a third of the way into a historical novel set around the outbreak of the First World War, and I've hit a wall that's less about plot and more about architecture. I keep going back and forth on how to present the causal logic of the whole thing.

Here's the problem. On one hand, I could write the lead-up to the war as this kind of structural inevitability — the alliance systems, the naval arms race, the mobilization timetables, the pressure of domestic politics in each of the great powers. The characters would be caught in something larger than themselves, and the reader would come away feeling that given all those conditions, something like the war was bound to happen. The explanation would work through general patterns: when you have rigid alliances plus mobilized armies plus a diplomatic crisis, you get war. It's almost lawlike.

On the other hand, I could write it as a chain of contingencies — the specific sequence of miscommunications, the personalities (the Kaiser's insecurities, Berchtold's maneuvering), the fact that the Archduke's driver took a wrong turn, the ultimatum that was almost but not quite acceptable. The reader would come away feeling that the war happened because of this particular story, not because of any deep structural necessity. The explanation works through narrative: first this happened, then that, and because of that, this other thing.

I know historians have argued about this kind of thing forever — some of them think proper historical explanation should look like scientific explanation (general laws plus initial conditions), and others think that's ridiculous and that narrative is its own irreducible form of explanation. I've read a bit of that debate but I'm not an academic, and I'm trying to figure out what serves the novel.

My characters themselves are historians, by the way — the framing story is set in the 1970s, with a group of historians arguing about the causes of the war while something else is happening in their personal lives. So the meta-question is also: what kind of historians are they? What do they believe about how history works? And can I dramatize that disagreement without the novel turning into a symposium?

I guess my question is: for a novel, which of these explanatory modes is actually richer? Does picking one over the other commit me to a philosophical position I might not want to be stuck with? And is there a way to hold both in tension — to let the reader feel the weight of the structural forces and the contingency of the specific events — without the novel feeling like it's trying to have it both ways?
