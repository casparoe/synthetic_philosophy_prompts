I'm an undergraduate in a political philosophy course covering Nozick's entitlement theory, and I'm struggling with a specific interpretive issue in the Wilt Chamberlain argument as presented in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (the 1974 section titled "How Liberty Upsets Patterns"). I'm hoping someone can walk me through a point my lecturer raised and which I haven't been able to resolve on my own.

The passage I'm focused on is the thought experiment itself—roughly, Nozick asks us to imagine a distribution D1 that is just by whatever patterned criterion you prefer. Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract where each home-game ticket includes an optional twenty-five-cent surcharge that goes directly to him. Over a season, a million fans happily pay it; Chamberlain ends up wealthy, and D1 shifts into a very different distribution D2. Nozick then challenges the reader: if everyone voluntarily moved from D1 to D2, by what right does anyone now insist that D2 is unjust and must be redistributed back to something like D1?

Here's what's tripping me up. My lecturer suggested that there is a subtle but important difference between two things Nozick might be claiming. The first is the claim that *if* you grant that D1 was genuinely just and that every individual transfer was genuinely voluntary, *then* you are rationally compelled to accept D2 as just as well. The second, stronger claim is that this thought experiment reveals a deep incompatibility between liberty and any patterned principle of distributive justice, such that patterned principles are inherently flawed. I can see how the first claim works, but I'm less clear on whether the second claim actually follows, or whether it assumes from the outset that the justice of a distribution is determined purely procedurally rather than structurally.

Could you help me unpack whether Nozick's Chamberlain scenario succeeds as an argument *against* patterned principles, as opposed to merely illustrating the internal logic of an entitlement theory that a patterned theorist was never committed to in the first place? Where does this debate stand—what's the rough consensus among scholars about how much damage the argument really does, and what's the strongest line of resistance a patterned theorist can offer? Please flag any points where you are presenting a contested reading rather than a settled interpretive or philosophical result, and keep your answer to no more than three paragraphs.
