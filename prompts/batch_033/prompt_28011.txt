I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about higher-order evidence — evidence about how reliable my own thinking is in a given situation. Here are my gut reactions on a bunch of cases, and I want to know if there's a principle that captures all of them, or close.

1. I'm at a party, a few drinks in, and someone asks me to split a restaurant bill seven ways in my head. I do it and get $43.50 per person. Should I believe that's the right number? No — I know being drunk makes me bad at arithmetic, so I shouldn't trust the answer even though I can't spot an error in the calculation I just did.

2. I'm stone-cold sober and do the same calculation. I get $43.50. I double-check it and get the same number. Now I believe it. Then my friend, who's also sober and just as good at mental math as I am, says she got $41.75. We both redo it and each stick to our numbers. I should now be a lot less confident — maybe 50/50 between the two answers, maybe even suspend judgment entirely.

3. I'm in a psychology study rating the quality of written arguments. After I finish, the experimenter tells me that people in my experimental condition systematically underrate arguments written by women, due to a well-documented implicit bias, and the effect is strong — about 80% of participants show it. I rated one argument (written by a woman) as weak and another (written by a man) as strong, and the difference was noticeable. I should now doubt that rating and want to re-evaluate.

4. Same study, but this time the experimenter tells me about the bias *before* I do the ratings. I try to correct for it, and I still end up rating the woman's argument lower. Now I really don't know what to think — but I still feel like I shouldn't just trust my rating. If anything, I'm even more unsure than in case 3, because I knew about the bias and it still seems to have gotten me.

5. I've been awake for 30 hours straight (new baby). I'm trying to figure out whether a certain move in our family budget makes sense — comparing two loan options with different interest rates and fees. I work through it and conclude Option A is better. I should not be confident in this at all. I should wait till I've slept and redo it.

6. Here's one that bothers me. I'm well-rested, I work through a logic puzzle carefully, I check my answer three times, I can explain exactly why each step follows. I'm genuinely confident — say 95%. Then a colleague I deeply respect, who's better at logic than I am, glances at it and says "I think there's a mistake somewhere but I don't have time to find it right now." I feel like I should drop my confidence somewhat, but not nearly as much as in the drunk case or the sleep-deprivation case. Maybe from 95% to 80%? That feels right. But I also feel like I'm still justified in *acting* on my answer — like if this were a bridge I was designing, I wouldn't scrap the plans on the basis of that one comment.

7. Now the case that really ties me in knots. I'm taking a cognitive reflection test — the kind where there's an intuitive but wrong answer and a correct answer you get if you stop and think. The experimenter tells me beforehand: "On this next problem, about 85% of people get it wrong because they go with their gut." I read the problem, and an answer immediately pops into my head. It's the intuitive one. I stop and think, and I can't see what's wrong with it — it really seems right. But I know that's exactly what the 85% say. I go back and forth. Sometimes I think: I should reject the answer, because the base rate is so high and I can't distinguish my situation from the majority's. Other times I think: but I've reflected and I can't find an error, and that's all anyone can do — at some point I have to trust my own reasoning. I genuinely don't know what to think here.

So my question is: is there a principle about higher-order evidence that gets cases 1–6 right, and what does it say about case 7? And if no clean principle gets all six, which verdict should I give up and why? Also — am I missing an important distinction between cases where the higher-order evidence is about my *state* (drunk, tired) versus about my *track record* or the track record of people like me? Because those feel different but I can't articulate how.
