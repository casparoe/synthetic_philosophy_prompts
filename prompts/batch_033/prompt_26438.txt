I've been reading some philosophy of science since I retired from the lab (physics), and I hit a chapter on Kuhn that summarized the view in a way that made me uneasy. Here's roughly what it said:

"Kuhn showed that science doesn't accumulate knowledge gradually. Instead, long periods of 'normal science' where everyone works within an accepted framework are punctuated by revolutionary episodes where the framework itself collapses. A paradigm, in Kuhn's sense, isn't just a theory — it's a whole package of assumptions, methods, and exemplars that define what counts as a good problem and a good solution. When anomalies build up and a new paradigm emerges, the two paradigms are incommensurable: there's no neutral, theory-independent language in which to compare them point by point, because the meanings of terms shift across the divide. So theory choice is never fully determined by logic and evidence alone; it always involves judgment, rhetoric, and the social structure of the scientific community."

Now, I've heard Kuhn dismissed as a relativist who thinks science is irrational, and this summary doesn't quite say that — but it seems to gesture at it. As someone who lived through a couple of theory changes in my own field, I'm trying to sort out what's fair and what's not. Which parts of this would a careful defender of Kuhn endorse, which parts are subtle misreadings, and does Kuhn's actual view (as opposed to this summary) leave room for the idea that we can still say later paradigms are better than earlier ones in some objective sense?
