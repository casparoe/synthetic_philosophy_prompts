I'm a philosophy professor teaching a graduate seminar on the structure of deontological ethics, and I want an exercise that forces my students to decide whether the paradox of deontology is a genuine structural problem or a symptom of speaking loosely. Here is what I would like.

Restate the paradox of deontology in the vocabulary of decision theory. By the paradox of deontology, I mean the puzzle that deontological constraints appear to forbid agents from minimizing violations of those very constraints: a constraint against killing seems to prohibit killing one person even when doing so would prevent five other killings, and yet if the constraint reflects the importance of persons not being killed, it is puzzling why it does not require agents to bring about the state of affairs with fewer killings. Translate this puzzle, together with the standard moves in the debate, into decision-theoretic terms. Use whatever resources the framework provides: state-based versus act-based evaluation, agent-relative versus agent-neutral value functions, constraints as side-conditions on permissible maximization, the distinction between what an agent ought to do and what an agent ought to prefer about the world, and so on. Include, where possible, Scheffler's idea of an agent-centered prerogative and the distinction between constraints and options.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the paradox translate cleanly? Which parts require distortion, and what kind of distortion? Are there features of the paradox that have no expression in decision theory at all — and if so, is that because decision theory's vocabulary is impoverished, or because those features were never precise enough to survive formalization? I want to know whether the agent-relative/agent-neutral distinction in decision theory is sufficient to capture what the paradox is about, or whether the paradox points to something — perhaps about the nature of agency or the structure of practical reasons — that the formal apparatus cannot represent even in principle. Finally, I want an honest verdict on whether translating the paradox into decision theory dissolves it, merely relabels it, or exposes that the original debate was trading on an ambiguity that the formalism makes visible.
