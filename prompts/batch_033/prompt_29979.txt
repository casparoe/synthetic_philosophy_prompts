I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending a Strawsonian line on moral responsibility, and I'm already dreading the Q&A. The one-sentence version of the thesis: our practices of blame, praise, resentment, and forgiveness aren't justified by some independently established fact about free will or desert; they're the bedrock — the participant stance — and the genuinely interesting question is when and whether we can or should drop into the objective stance toward a person. Determinism, I'll argue, is a theoretical truth that never engages the reactive attitudes the way finding out someone was coerced or delusional does. Im not going to argue that determinism is false or that we have libertarian free will; I'm going to argue that the whole 'does determinism undermine responsibility?' framing gets the order of explanation backwards: the practice comes first, and the metaphysics gets imported into it, not the other way around.

The audience is a philosophy department where most of the free-will people are hard incompatibilists in a Pereboom-ish mood, there's one reasons-responsive compatibilist who thinks the Strawsonian move is just changing the subject, and the rest are moral psychologists who run manipulation and implicit-bias experiments and keep pointing out that our reactive attitudes track all sorts of things that have nothing to do with control. So the objections I get will be very different from the ones at a law school or a public-facing venue. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would actually put them, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me where I should just concede up front instead of fighting.
