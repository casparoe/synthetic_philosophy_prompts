Two members of my reading group got into a dispute about whether voting is rational. The first argued that the expected impact of a single vote is effectively zero — elections are almost never decided by one ballot — so the time spent voting is wasted. The second replied that this reasoning, universalized, would be disastrous: if everyone who shares your preferences reasoned this way, your side would lose. He argued that you should treat your decision as diagnostic of what other similar agents will do, and act accordingly — this is the logic of evidential cooperation in large worlds. The first countered that this conflates causation with correlation: your decision to vote does not cause like-minded others to vote; it merely provides evidence about them. Since rational action should track causal consequences, not mere evidential relationships, the argument fails. The second responded that in a sufficiently large world, where your individual causal influence is negligible anyway, rejecting evidential considerations amounts to guaranteeing that everyone defects in all structurally similar situations — a kind of decision-theoretic suicide. The first called this magical thinking dressed up in formalism: you cannot make a difference by sheer force of correlation. Neither would budge, and they asked me to settle it. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: evaluate each argument, identify what each side gets right and wrong, and say which position is ultimately more defensible.
