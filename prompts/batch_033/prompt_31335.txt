Exam question: What is the relation between grounding and fundamentality? In your answer, explain (i) what grounding is supposed to be, (ii) how the notions of being more fundamental than and being fundamental are standardly defined in terms of grounding, and (iii) whether accepting grounding commits one to the existence of a fundamental level. Distinguish full from partial grounding where relevant.

Student answer: Grounding is the non-causal relation of metaphysical determination: when one or more facts ground another, the grounded fact obtains in virtue of the grounding facts. Unlike causal explanation, grounding is typically synchronic and hyperintensional, since necessarily equivalent facts can differ in what grounds them. Full grounding is widely taken to be factive, transitive, and irreflexive; partial grounding is standardly irreflexive as well, though its transitivity is more contested. More fundamental than is standardly defined in terms of grounding: x is more fundamental than y just when x grounds y or stands in the ancestral of grounding to y. And fundamental is standardly defined as ungrounded: an entity is fundamental just in case nothing grounds it. Since grounding induces a hierarchy of relative fundamentality, accepting grounding commits us to an absolute fundamental level: if some facts are more fundamental than others, then there must be facts more fundamental than everything else, namely the ungrounded facts at the bottom. Moreover, because full grounding is transitive and irreflexive, every chain of full grounds must terminate, since an infinite descending chain would eventually require something to ground itself. So grounding rules out infinitely descending chains, and anyone who accepts grounding must accept a bottom level of ungrounded, fundamental facts.
