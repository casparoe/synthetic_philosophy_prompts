I've been puzzling over the backward induction argument in the centipede game, and I can't tell whether it's valid with an unacceptable conclusion or whether there's a subtle mistake I'm failing to locate.

Here's the setup. Two players, A and B, alternate choosing whether to continue (pass the turn) or stop (end the game). The game lasts at most N rounds, with N large—say, 100. At each decision node, if the player whose turn it is stops, she takes the larger share of a growing pot and the other gets the smaller share; if she continues, the pot grows but the other player gets the next chance to stop and claim the larger share. Both players know the full structure of the game, both know that each is rational, and both know that both know this, with no upper bound on the iterations.

Now the argument:

(1) At the final node (round N), the player whose turn it is faces a choice: stop and take the larger share, or continue and let the game end, yielding a strictly smaller payoff. A rational player stops.

(2) At round N–1, the player whose turn it is knows that if she continues, the round-N player—being rational, by (1)—will stop, leaving her with the smaller share. Stopping now gives her the larger share. So she stops.

(3) The same reasoning applies at N–2, N–3, and so on, all the way back to round 1.

(4) Therefore, a rational A stops at round 1. Both players walk away with a tiny payoff, even though both would have been far better off continuing for many rounds.

The conclusion seems absurd: it says rational play produces an outcome worse for both players than nearly any other pattern of play. Yet each step looks airtight—at each node, stopping dominates continuing given what the other player will do at the next node.

I am torn between two diagnoses. One says the argument is valid and simply reveals that common knowledge of rationality is self-defeating here; the cost of resisting the conclusion is giving up the idea that rational players can reason their way to mutually beneficial outcomes. The other says there is a hidden assumption somewhere in the iterated reasoning—perhaps about what a player should infer when the game reaches a node that, under backward induction, should never have been reached. But I cannot make that precise without it sounding as though I am merely refusing to accept a logic I dislike.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? And what must I give up to resist the conclusion?
