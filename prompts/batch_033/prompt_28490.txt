I'm a high school student who runs a philosophy discussion group, and for the past several months we've been working through the problem of induction. We started with Hume, spent some time on Bayesian and reliabilist responses, and most recently I've been absorbed by Nelson Goodman's "new riddle of induction." I've arrived at a position I find compelling, but I want to stress-test it thoroughly before I present it to the group next month — I don't want to defend something that falls apart under the first serious objection.

Here's the position I've settled on. Goodman's grue paradox shows that no purely formal or syntactic approach to induction can succeed: you can't solve the problem just by specifying the right logical shape for inductive inferences, because for any pattern describable using "green" there's a formally identical pattern using "grue." The only viable response is to appeal to something substantive about which predicates we ought to project into the future — and Goodman's own proposal, that we should project "entrenched" predicates (ones we've actually used in past inductions), strikes me as the right kind of move. I recognize that this introduces a kind of circularity: we justify inductive practices by appeal to the very practices we're trying to justify. But I think this circularity isn't vicious — it's closer to the reflective equilibrium we accept in ethics — and that attempts to escape it (Quine's appeal to natural kinds, Bayesian efforts to ground priors in something non-arbitrary, or the claim that grue is somehow gerrymandered in a way that can be ruled out on purely logical grounds) either miss the force of the problem or smuggle in what they're trying to justify.

Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher of science who is sympathetic to the grue problem but skeptical of my conclusion. What would count against this position? I want the evidence, arguments, or formal results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be — from "this would force me to abandon the whole thing" down to "this would be awkward but survivable." In particular: are there ways of dissolving the grue problem that don't appeal to entrenchment and that I'm not taking seriously enough? Could a Bayesian give a convincing reply? And am I wrong to be comfortable with the circularity — is there a way of making it precise that shows it really is vicious? I'd also like you to assess whether my position has been insulated against falsification in a problematic way, and if so, whether that kind of insulation is a genuine flaw for a view of this sort. Please think through each objection carefully, distinguishing what a critic would say from what a defender of my position ought to concede genuinely counts against it.
