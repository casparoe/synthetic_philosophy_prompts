Two people in my department got into it after a talk last week, and I've been turning it over since. The talk was on AI alignment, and afterwards Liam said the whole situation — every major lab racing to build AGI while all of them say they're worried about safety — is a textbook multipolar trap: each lab would prefer a world where everyone pumps the brakes, but no one can unilaterally slow down without being left behind, so they all run. Nadia pushed back hard. She said that's not a trap, it's just a conflict — some of these labs actually *want* to be first, and they'd race even if others slowed down, so the 'we'd all prefer the slow world' premise is false. Liam said that's too quick: the claim isn't that every actor has identical preferences, it's that the *structure* of the situation — where defecting beats cooperating regardless of what others do — is what drives the outcome, and that structure holds even if some players are more hawkish than others. Nadia said if you allow heterogeneous preferences, the concept of a multipolar trap loses its teeth, because you can always say 'well, some actors are just more aggressive' and then the trap isn't doing the explanatory work — the preferences are. Liam said that's like saying the prisoner's dilemma stops being a dilemma if one prisoner really hates the other, which is missing the point about the payoff structure. It got a bit heated and they didn't resolve it. I want you to adjudicate this move by move. Who's right about what, and is there a way of stating the multipolar trap thesis that survives Nadia's objection without becoming vacous? And if the whole idea turns out to be less crisp than people in our circles pretend, say so plainly — I'd rather know than have a tidy answer.
