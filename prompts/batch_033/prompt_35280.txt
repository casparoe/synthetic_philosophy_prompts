I have been reading the fine-tuning debate for a while, and I am struck by how little movement there has been despite the fact that the parties appear to accept the same physical facts. Both sides grant that a small set of constants and initial conditions — the cosmological constant, the strengths of the fundamental forces, the initial low-entropy state — fall within a narrow range that permits stable stars, chemistry, and life, and that most nearby values in the space allowed by current theory would yield a dead universe. Both sides also grant that, conditional on there being a single universe whose constants are set by chance, a life-permitting outcome is vastly improbable. From this shared ground, one camp concludes that the constants are strong evidence for a designer, while the other concludes that the appearance of design carries no evidential weight at all once a multiverse is allowed or anthropic conditioning is applied.

The design proponent's move is a likelihood comparison: a designer would plausibly want life, so the probability of life-permitting constants given design is high, while the probability of life-permitting constants given single-universe chance is minuscule; the likelihood ratio therefore favors design. The naturalist replies that the relevant probability is not the probability of life-permitting constants given chance, but the probability of life-permitting constants given chance and our own existence, which is near 1 — or that a multiverse makes a life-permitting universe somewhere unsurprising. The design proponent answers that the multiverse is an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis with no independent support, and that the anthropic move would let one explain away any improbable fact whatsoever. The naturalist answers that design is at least as ad hoc, and that independent physics may anyway point toward a multiverse. And there the debate sits.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about the physics, and not really about the likelihoods, but about something upstream: whether the multiverse is a legitimate rival hypothesis that can absorb the improbability, and whether conditioning on our own existence is a principled way to dissolve surprise or an evasion. But I can see other candidates. It may be a dispute about priors, about whether probabilities can be assigned to the universe as a whole, about what would count as an explanation of the constants, or about whether the datum that this universe is life-permitting has any content once observation-selection effects are taken seriously.

Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, engaging with the moves each side actually makes. Where exactly do the two camps part ways — over a hidden empirical question, over standards of evidence, over the reference class, over starting intuitions, or over different questions being run under one heading? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or just hard? And given the diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a principled measure over the space of constants, an independent theoretical reason for a multiverse, an agreed formalization of anthropic selection, a theorem, a distinction that lets the two camps see they are answering different questions — or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the literature.
