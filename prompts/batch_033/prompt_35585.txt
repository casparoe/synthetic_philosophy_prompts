I'm teaching a philosophy of science seminar and I want to use Tom Stoppard's Arcadia to get students into the Humeanism vs. anti-Humeanism debate about laws of nature. (For reference: Humeans hold that the laws are just our best systematization of the Humean mosaic — the total distribution of particular, non-modal facts throughout spacetime. Laws describe regularities; they don't govern or necessitate anything. The world is one damn thing after another, and laws are the patterns we find in the sequence. Anti-Humeans — Armstrong, Dretske, Tooley, and others — hold that there is more to reality than the mosaic. Laws involve genuine necessitation relations between universals. They govern. The regularities obtain because the laws make them obtain.)

Arcadia seems to stage this dispute almost point for point. Thomasina's intuition about the second law — that heat flows one way, that you cannot stir jam out of rice pudding — suggests a world constrained by genuine directional necessity, something stronger than mere regularity. Meanwhile Valentine's grouse-population models show that perfectly deterministic equations produce outcomes that are unpredictable in practice and look like mere statistical sprawl. And the play's double time-scheme — 1809 and the present, converging at the waltz — seems to refuse any single picture of what temporal order consists in. I want to know whether the play, read carefully, earns its keep in a philosophy of science classroom.

Give me a reading of Arcadia through this lens. Specifically address:

(1) Which scenes or exchanges most directly engage the question of whether laws govern or merely describe, and what the play shows in each case.

(2) Whether the play as a whole takes a side — Humean, anti-Humean, or something that undercuts the debate — and what in the text supports your reading.

(3) Any moments where the play trades on a confusion the philosophical literature has already clarified (e.g., running together epistemic unpredictability with metaphysical indeterminism, or treating entropy as a force rather than a statistical tendency) and where I should flag this for students.

(4) What the play adds to the philosophical debate that the papers don't give you — something the drama does that argument alone cannot.
