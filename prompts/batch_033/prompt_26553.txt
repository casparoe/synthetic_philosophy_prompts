so i've been reading about fisheries management and the tragedy of the commons, and there's a practice thats bothering me. when a fishery is collapsing from overfishing, a standard response is to introduce individual transferable quotas (ITQs) — the government caps the total catch and allocates shares to individual fishers, who can then buy, sell, or lease them. the logic is that the commons gets destroyed because no one owns it, so you solve the problem by creating private property rights in the fish stock. fine.

but heres the thing i cant get past: the initial allocation of quotas is almost always based on catch history. the people who caught the most fish in the years right before the program was introduced get the biggest shares. so the fishers who contributed most to depleting the stock end up with the largest stake in the now-restricted resource. the small-scale fishers, the new entrants, the ones who were showing restraint — they get squeezed out.

what view of the commons, or of property, or of corrective justice does this practice presuppose? does it presuppose that historical use creates a legitimate entitlement, even when that use was the very thing the policy is meant to stop? or can you justify grandfathering on purely pragmatic grounds without committing to the idea that the overfishers had a right to what they were taking? and is the practice even consistent with its own diagnosis — if the problem was that the commons was unowned and therefore overused, does the initial allocation quietly treat it as having been owned all along by whoever happened to be fishing it?

i'm not looking for policy alternatives or a list of reforms. i want to understand what philosophical commitments are already built into the practice as it actually exists, and whether they hang together or contradict each other.
