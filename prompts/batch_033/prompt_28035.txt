For a seminar on political ethics, I would like you to write a dialogue between two characters who disagree about the problem of dirty hands. The first character defends the view, associated with Michael Walzer, that there are genuine cases in political life where a leader must do something morally wrong to discharge the responsibilities of office — and that the leader who does the necessary wrong and feels guilt afterward is morally superior to one who does the same thing with a clean conscience, because the guilt shows that the leader has not simply jettisoned ordinary moral standards. The second character argues that this framework is ultimately unstable: if the act is genuinely justified by the political good it secures, then retrospective guilt is irrational; if the act is genuinely wrong, then the leader ought not to have done it. On her view, the dirty-hands theorist is trying to have it both ways — claiming the act was necessary while claiming moral credit for feeling bad about it, as though the bad feeling retroactively launders the choice.

The dialogue should be a substantive exchange, not a series of set speeches. Each character should respond to the other's strongest points, and neither should be a strawman. The second character in particular should not simply be a naive consequentialist who denies that hard choices exist or that regret is appropriate; she should acknowledge that some decisions leave genuine moral remainders while maintaining that regret is not the same as guilt and that the language of "wrongdoing" is doing illicit work in the dirty-hands picture. The first character should have a real answer to this, not merely restate the initial intuition. Aim for roughly 800 to 1,200 words.
