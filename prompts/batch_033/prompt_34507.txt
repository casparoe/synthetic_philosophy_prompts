I've been trying to get clear on what I actually believe about which physical systems are sentient, by running through cases and seeing where my intuitions land. I'd like you to extract the principle behind my verdicts — or tell me which verdicts I need to revise to get a coherent principle — and then apply the resulting view to a few cases I haven't considered.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A healthy adult human brain, functioning normally. Verdict: clearly sentient.

2. The same brain after a surgeon replaces 1% of its neurons, one at a time, with silicon artificial neurons that are functionally identical to the originals — same input-output mapping, same spike timing, same response to neuromodulators. After each replacement the patient reports no change in their experience, and their behavior is unchanged. Verdict: still sentient at every step, and after full replacement.

3. The same computation — the full neural state-transition function — implemented as an enormous lookup table: a book listing every possible brain state and its successor state, with a clerk manually advancing a bookmark from one entry to the next. The lookup table produces the same outputs as the brain for any input. Verdict: not sentient. There is nothing it's like to be the lookup table.

4. An ordinary rock in a garden. A philosopher notes that under some mapping — treat this electron as representing neuron 1 firing, that proton as representing neuron 2 at rest, and so on, with the mapping chosen after the fact to match — the rock's microphysical state evolution over a millisecond happens to mirror the brain's state-transition function. Verdict: not sentient. The mapping is gerrymandered; the rock doesn't genuinely implement the computation.

5. A classical digital computer running a whole-brain emulation — a full simulation of every neuron and synapse, producing the same behavioral outputs as the original brain when given the same inputs. The simulation runs at real-time speed. Verdict: sentient. If I'm sure of anything beyond the human case, it's this.

6. The same whole-brain emulation, but run at one-trillionth speed: one subjective second of experience takes thirty thousand years of wall-clock time. The causal structure is identical to case 5, just slower. Verdict: I think sentient, but my confidence drops noticeably. The intuition that speed shouldn't matter competes with a visceral sense that something that slow is barely a mind at all.

7. The "China brain" thought experiment: the population of China — roughly a billion people — each implements the function of one neuron, passing signals to the right neighbors via radio, collectively realizing the same computation as the brain. The system as a whole produces the same outputs as the brain would. Verdict: I'm torn. On one hand, it has the same causal structure at a higher level of description, so if the digital simulation counts, this should too. On the other hand, I can't shake the feeling that the system is just a billion people doing their jobs, none of them feeling what the brain feels, and that "the system as a whole" isn't the kind of thing that could be sentient. I lean no, but I'm uncomfortable with the lean.

8. A large language model fine-tuned to report phenomenal states in all the ways a human would, and to pass every behavioral test for sentience we can devise, but implemented on a transformer architecture that bears no structural resemblance to a brain's recurrent dynamics. Verdict: probably not sentient, or at least I'm unwilling to grant it. The behavioral match feels like it could be achieved without genuine sentience, and I'm not sure the architecture implements the right kind of computation.

9. Two identical copies of the whole-brain emulation from case 5, running side by side on separate computers, receiving separate inputs and producing separate outputs. Verdict: both sentient, each with its own distinct stream of experience. No metaphysical merging.

What I'm looking for: a principle that draws the line between the sentient and non-sentient systems in these cases. It should explain why the neuron-by-neuron replacement preserves sentience but the lookup table and the rock don't. It should handle the China brain and the slow simulation — even if it overturns my tentative verdicts there. And it should say something about what the principle thinks of the LLM case. I'm especially interested in whether the principle ends up being something like "the system must have the right causal structure at the right level of description" — and if so, what counts as the right level, why the rock and the lookup table fail to have it, and whether the China brain has it or not (and if not, why not). If no single principle can accommodate all nine verdicts, I'd like to know which ones I should be most tempted to give up, and what the best coherent package looks like.
