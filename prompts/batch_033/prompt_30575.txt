In medical research, a data and safety monitoring board can stop a clinical trial early if interim results suggest the experimental treatment is harming participants. The statistical threshold for halting a trial on harm grounds is typically low — a trend, not proof — and the presumption is that it is better to lose the knowledge the trial would have produced than to continue exposing subjects to a likely harm. But if the same interim analysis shows the treatment to be strikingly beneficial, the trial almost never stops early. Instead, it continues to its planned conclusion, because researchers want more robust evidence before changing practice. A treatment that appears to save lives at the interim look must keep proving itself; a treatment that appears to take them is pulled immediately.

This asymmetry is embedded in the standard operating procedures of institutional review boards, in international research ethics guidelines, and in the statistical design of trials themselves. It is not a fringe view.

What theory of the relationship between harming and benefiting does this practice presuppose? Does it commit the research community to the view that preventing suffering has a kind of urgency or priority that promoting well-being does not? Or can the asymmetry be justified entirely by appeal to the different epistemic situations — or the different consent conditions — that apply to harms and to benefits, without taking any stand on the underlying value theory? If the justification must ultimately rest on a claim about value, what is the least controversial such claim that would do the work, and would it have implications the research community might not want to accept elsewhere?
