I'm in this grad seminar on epistemic game theory and we've been going through Aumann, Binmore, Brandenburger, all the usual suspects. And I keep feeling like the whole notion of "common knowledge of rationality" is doing a ton of work but nobody's actually pinned down what a good theory of it is supposed to accomplish. Like, we talk about whether common knowledge of rationality implies backward induction (or doesn't), whether it gets you to Nash equilibrium (or doesn't), the electronic mail game stuff where a tiny failure of common knowledge unravels everything — but it feels like people are just throwing their favorite formalization at the wall and seeing what sticks. Half the time I can't even tell whether two authors are disagreeing about the phenomenon or just using "common knowledge" to mean different things.

So before I read yet another paper proposing yet another formalism, I want the spec sheet. What would a theory of common knowledge of rationality actually have to deliver to count as adequate? I mean the full list of desiderata: which cases it has to get right (centipede game? coordinated attack? the email game? the surprise exam paradox?), which distinctions it can't collapse (like the difference between common knowledge and common belief, or between individual rationality and common knowledge of rationality, or between ex ante and interim reasoning), which puzzles it has to dissolve or at least explain, which existing results it has to be consistent with or else explain away convincingly. And for each desideratum, say what the motivating case or consideration is — like, why does this one matter and not just someone's aesthetic preference about formal systems.

Then the part I really want: tell me which of these desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one research program (e.g., "must use possible-worlds semantics" vs. "must handle the email game result"). And flag any pairs that you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — if there's a tension between wanting the theory to be robust to small perturbations and wanting it to deliver sharp equilibrium predictions, I want that called out. If some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable given something like the electronic mail game result or the Brandenburger-Keisler paradox, that's basically a theorem about the limits of the whole project, and that's the kind of thing I'm here for. Don't bother scoring existing theories against the list unless a particular desideratum is basically designed to rule out a specific approach — the criteria themselves are what I'm after.
