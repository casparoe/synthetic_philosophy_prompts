I am a research scientist on the alignment evaluations team at a frontier AI lab, and next week I have to present an internal proposal to our safety review group. The proposal is awkward for me personally: I have spent the last quarter building exactly the kind of large benchmark suite I am now going to argue should not, by itself, clear a model for release. I expect the room to be skeptical, and I want to be ready for the question period rather than the rehearsed presentation. The group is mostly empirical ML researchers and evaluation engineers who design, run, and trust large benchmark suites; two of the members work on safety cases and are sympathetic in principle but will only move on criteria that are operationalized, falsifiable, and tied to a release decision. My proposal is that for a narrow class of high-stakes properties—resistance to jailbreaking and manipulative behavior, honesty under optimization pressure, and refusal consistency under distribution shift—average-case benchmark performance should not be the primary acceptance criterion for release. I want the group to adopt worst-case guarantees against explicitly specified threat models as the bar, and to treat average-case evals as a continuous monitoring tool rather than a sign-off. My rough argument is that (1) average-case metrics over i.i.d. benchmark draws cannot distinguish rare catastrophic failures from benign noise, (2) for properties where a single failure can be catastrophic, expected-value reasoning is the wrong frame, and (3) worst-case evaluation is tractable if we scope the threat model honestly—say, an adversary with a fixed budget of optimization steps against a stated objective—rather than promising guarantees against all possible adversaries. I know I will get pushback on cost, coverage, the gap between formal guarantees and deployed behavior, and the risk of a false sense of security. Please prepare me for the objections as this specific audience will phrase them, in their own vocabulary: the eight or so I should actually expect, ordered by likelihood and damage; each phrased the way an empirical evaluator or safety cases person would put it; a two-minute reply; a note on where each reply is softest; what I should concede up front to keep credibility; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with a suggestion for how to handle it if it does come.
