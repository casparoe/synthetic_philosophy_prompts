I have been thinking, on my own and without any formal training in philosophy or neuroscience, about a set of experiments that seem to bear on whether our conscious decisions actually do anything. The experiments I have in mind are those in which a person is fitted with electrodes and asked to make a simple spontaneous movement—flexing a wrist, pressing a button—at a time of their own choosing, while watching a fast-moving clock and reporting the moment they first became aware of the urge or intention to act. The finding that has stayed with me is that a distinctive buildup of brain activity associated with the movement begins a few hundred milliseconds before the moment the person reports as the first conscious awareness of deciding. Popular accounts draw a strong conclusion from this: the feeling of deciding arrives too late to be the cause of the action, so what we experience as a free choice is really a story the brain tells after the fact. Other accounts I have read say this conclusion badly overreaches.

I would like your actual credence in the following claim: for the deliberate actions of ordinary life, the conscious experience of deciding does not causally contribute to the action it accompanies; the neural processes that produce the action are already underway before the feeling of deciding appears, and the feeling is best understood as a byproduct or retrospective interpretation rather than a stage in the causal chain. If you think the claim needs precisification before it can be assigned a probability, say briefly which precisification you are using—but then give the number.

I do not want the answer to stop at "it depends what you mean by free will." I want a number, and I want the reasoning taken apart. Please decompose your credence into at least the following terms, with a rough probability and one or two sentences of justification for each:

1. That the timing gap is real: the early brain activity is genuinely preparation for the specific movement, begins before the reported time of conscious awareness, and is not an artifact of measurement or of the subject's ability to report the moment accurately.
2. That, given the gap is real, it actually supports the strong conclusion: an early unconscious preparation is evidence that the later conscious experience has no causal role, rather than evidence merely that decisions have unconscious stages that culminate in awareness.
3. That the result carries over from trivial spontaneous movements in a laboratory to the deliberate, reason-involving choices people care about—decisions made after weighing considerations, where the person would cite reasons for what they did.
4. That the understanding of conscious control or free will that ordinary people actually rely on is the one these experiments would undermine, rather than a more demanding or confused notion that was never there to be threatened.

Add any further terms you think are carrying significant weight. Then show how the terms combine into your overall credence, and tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to—where a change in that one probability would move the total the most—and what evidence or argument would most change your mind about it.

For reference, my own credence in the claim is around 0.4, and a friend with whom I discuss this is at around 0.75. Based on your decomposition, where do you think the disagreement between us most likely lives?
