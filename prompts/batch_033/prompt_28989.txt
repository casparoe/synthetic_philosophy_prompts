so i've been chewing on the wrong kind of reasons problem and i have this half-baked idea for an essay. would love some comments on whether the argument actually goes through or if i'm missing something obvious that sinks it.

the core thought is that the WKR problem isn't really one problem -- it splits into two quite different problems depending on whether we're talking about doxastic attitudes (belief) or conative attitudes (admiration, fear, desire, etc.), and treating them as instances of the same phenomenon might be a mistake.

here's the rough argument:

for conative attitudes like admiration, the standard WKR case goes: there's an attitude with a constitutive correctness standard. admiring someone is correct iff the person is admirable. a demon threatens to punish you unless you admire him. you now have a reason to admire him, but it doesn't track admirability at all. the puzzle is to explain, in a principled way, why this reason is the "wrong kind." and the natural move is to appeal to the constitutive aim of the attitude -- admiration "aims at" the admirable, so reasons that don't bear on admirability are wrong-kind reasons.

for belief, the parallel move seems straightforward: belief aims at truth, so reasons that don't bear on truth are wrong-kind reasons. but here's the thing. in the belief case, we already have an entire field that handles exactly this -- epistemology. the question of which reasons for belief are the right kind just IS the question of how to distinguish epistemic from pragmatic reasons for belief. and that debate has its own long history, its own distinctions (evidential vs. pragmatic, theoretical vs. practical reasoning), and its own candidate solutions (evidentialism, pragmatism, etc.). it's not like we were waiting for the WKR literature to notice that being bribed to believe p doesn't make p more likely to be true.

so my worry is: when philosophers like Scanlon or Hieronymi treat the WKR problem as unified across attitude types, they might be glossing over a real disanalogy. in the conative case, the WKR problem feels genuinely puzzling because we don't have a pre-existing, well-developed framework for distinguishing right from wrong kinds of reasons for admiration or fear. but in the doxastic case, we absolutely do, and the WKR problem just collapses into a special case of a much older debate about epistemic vs. pragmatic normativity. so a unified solution risks either (a) saying something false about the belief case because it ignores the epistemic literature, or (b) saying something true but so general that it doesn't capture what's distinctive about the conative case -- in which case it's not really a unified solution so much as a vague umbrella.

i'm not sure whether this lands as a critique of the WKR literature or just as a plea for better taxonomy. i'd especially appreciate pushback from people who think there IS a unified solution that handles both cases without collapsing into either horn. also wondering if i'm being unfair to Hieronymi in particular, since she does talk about belief's constitutive aim explicitly and might already have resources to address this.
