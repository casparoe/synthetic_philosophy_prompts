Retired physicist here — spent 40 years in a lab, retired a couple years back, and got into philosophy to keep the brain ticking. I've been reading about forgery and aesthetic value, you know, the whole puzzle of why we care that a painting is a real Vermeer rather than a perfect forgery when they look identical.

A friend of mine keeps saying "if you can't tell the difference, there is no difference" like that settles it. He uses it to dismiss the idea that originals have some special value that perfect copies lack. Now, in physics, operationalizing your distinctions is good practice — if two hypotheses make all the same predictions, you've got one hypothesis. So the slogan has some pull on me. But I'm pretty sure he's overapplying it here, and I can't quite pin down why.

What I want: lay out the distinct precise claims that "if you can't tell the difference, there is no difference" might be expressing. I can see a few — a verificationist claim about meaning (no test, no distinction), a claim about aesthetic experience (same look, same experience), a claim about value (can't tell, so it's worth the same to you). But I bet there are more, and I bet some are trivially true while others are false or at least highly contentious.

Give me the menu of readings, say which are platitudes and which are substantive, and tell me which ones would actually support his conclusion that originals have no special value. I want to bring something clear to our next arguement.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
