ok so i keep noticing this thing and i want to know if philosophers have a name for it and what they think about it.

the basic pattern is: someone believes something, they also believe that their evidence doesn't support that belief, and they'll tell you both of these things in the same sentence. like my friend who says "i know the stats say the team is gonna lose but i still think they'll win." or my dad who goes "i'm aware i'm being totally irrational about this but i think that politician is corrupt." or honestly me, when i'm convinced i bombed a test even though i studied fine and the teacher said it was easy — i can see the evidence says i probably did okay but i still believe i failed.

what's weird to me is that this doesn't seem like normal irrationality. normally when someone's irrational they don't *know* they're being irrational. but here the person can state the case against their own belief perfectly clearly. it's like the belief and the assessment the evidence are just living in different compartments? but also they're not, because the person can access both at once.

i tried looking this up and found stuff about "akrasia" but that seems to be about actions — you know you shouldn't eat the cake but you do it anyway. this is about beliefs. i also found stuff about self-deception but that seems to require that you're hiding something from yourself, whereas the whole point here is the person is totally upfront about the conflict.

so my questions are: (1) is there an established name for this specific thing, (2) is it always irrational or can it sometimes be okay — like maybe you have reasons to distrust your own evidence-assessment that are hard to articulate so you just note the conflict and move on, and (3) if there's a literature on this, what are the rough camps people fall into? i don't just want a label, i want to understand what exactly the phenomenon is and where its boundaries are.
