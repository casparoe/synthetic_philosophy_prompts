I'm writing a paper defending a graded view of moral status — the idea that sentience is not an all-or-nothing property and that moral considerability comes in degrees, from insects up to humans and perhaps down to very simple artificial systems. In trying to anticipate objections, I built an argument that seems to show the view is either repugnant or arbitrary, and I cannot find the exit. I would like you to diagnose it.

Here is the argument. (1) If sentience comes in degrees, then along the continuum from a thermostat to an insect to a human, there is no sharp point at which sentience switches from absent to present. (2) If there is no sharp switch-on point, then there is no non-arbitrary place to draw a line below which a system has zero moral status; so if an insect has a little moral status, a thermostat-like feedback system has a vanishingly small amount too. (3) If such systems have even a tiny amount of moral status, and moral status aggregates across individuals, then for any serious human interest there is some sufficiently large collection of thermostat-like systems whose combined status would outweigh it. (4) But that is false: no number of thermostats should outweigh a person's serious interest in avoiding harm. (5) The only way to block (3) while keeping the graded view is to posit a threshold above which interests count lexically, so that no number of sub-threshold interests can outweigh a single supra-threshold interest. (6) But any threshold drawn on a continuous gradient is arbitrary, since a cutoff a hair lower or higher would fit the gradual facts just as well. (7) So the graded view must choose between repugnant aggregation and an arbitrary cutoff, and should be abandoned.

I would prefer to keep the graded view, and I am willing to give up the thermostat end of it, but I want to know exactly which step goes wrong, why that step looked compulsory, and what rejecting it commits me to. Which premise do I reject, and what does it cost?
