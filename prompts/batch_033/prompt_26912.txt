You are a professional philosopher specializing in weakness of will, akrasia, and the philosophy of action, with a working knowledge of the psychology of hyperbolic discounting, preference reversal, and self-control. You have been asked to help a novelist translate your field's insights into a reusable character-construction procedure. Think carefully before you write. Begin by brainstorming the key distinctions and mechanisms your field recognizes — judgment shifts, executive failure, preference reversals under temporal proximity, the difference between akrasia and compulsion, the question of whether clear-eyed akrasia is possible at all — and then decide which of these survive translation into a tool for someone who is not a philosopher and who needs to make a decision in under an hour. Be honest about which distinctions you are dropping and why.

I am a novelist. I write literary fiction in which characters make consequential decisions against their own better judgment: the politician who torpedoes a career they spent decades building, the parent who breaks a promise they genuinely meant to keep, the addict who walks back into the room they swore they would never enter again. I am tired of writing these moments by feel and suspecting afterward that they worked only because the plot required them. I want a procedure I can run during outlining, before I write a word of prose, that tells me whether a character's failure of will is psychologically credible given how I have constructed them — their desires, their beliefs, their history, their situation — and, if it is not, what I would need to change.

The procedure should be something I can use in under an hour per major character. I have no formal training in philosophy, though I am willing to learn a small number of technical distinctions if they earn their keep. I need the procedure to be honest about where it is making a judgment call rather than applying a rule, and I need it to flag the cases it cannot settle — where the philosophy itself is divided, or where the answer depends on a fact about human psychology that is not yet known. I also need it to tell me when the most honest answer is "this character, as you have built them, would not plausibly fail here — if the plot requires it, you must change either the character or the situation."

Please deliver:

1. A step-by-step procedure — a checklist, a flowchart in words, a set of questions to ask in order — that I can run on any character facing a moment of possible akratic failure. Each step should say what counts as a sufficient answer and where the procedure stops with a "no, this won't work."

2. A brief explanation, in plain language, of the key idea or ideas from the philosophy of weakness of will that the procedure operationalizes. I do not need a literature review; I need to know what the procedure is doing so I can trust it and adapt it when it misfires.

3. Three worked applications:
   - A politician who has spent twenty years building toward a Supreme Court nomination and, at the confirmation hearing, makes a gratuitously hostile remark that sinks it — a remark they knew, even as they formed it, would destroy them.
   - A parent who genuinely believes they should keep a promise to attend their child's performance and, at the last moment, chooses to stay at work instead.
   - An alcoholic who has been sober for three years and, on an ordinary Tuesday with no obvious trigger, walks into a bar and orders a drink.

4. For at least one of these, the procedure should tell me something I will not want to hear — that the failure is not credible as written, or that I have built the character wrong for the moment I need them to have.

5. A section on what the procedure cannot do: the cases it cannot adjudicate, the philosophical disagreements it papers over, and the ways a clever novelist can fool it.

Ground the procedure in the actual philosophical and psychological work on akrasia — the distinction between weakness of will and compulsion, the structure of hyperbolic discounting and preference reversal, the role of judgment shifts versus executive failure, the question of whether akrasia is even possible — but do not lecture. Every distinction you include must earn its place by doing work in the procedure. If a distinction cannot be translated into a question a novelist can ask about their character, leave it out and note that you have done so.
