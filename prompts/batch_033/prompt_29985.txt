I've read enough Sequences-adjacent stuff to have internalized the revealed-preferences line: talk is cheap, utility isn't some glow-y inner quantity, it's just a number that represents whatever a consistent choice pattern maximizes, and if you want to know what someone wants, look at what they pick when it costs them something. But my gut on cases keeps refusing to sit still, and I want to know whether there's actually a principle behind my verdicts or whether I'm just applying "revealed preference when it flatters me, mistake/context when it doesn't."

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can get them:

1. Coffee over tea at 8am, tea over coffee at 3pm. I dont think this means I'm irrational or have no preferences. The preference is just time-indexed; both choices reveal real preferences.
2. Surgery framing: told a procedure has "a 90% survival rate" I take it; told it has "a 10% mortality rate" I refuse. I'm quite sure the refusal doesn't reveal my real preference about the surgery. My real preference is for the surgery, and the framing effect is an error in the choice machinery, not a change in what I want.
3. I ask a friend to charge me $50 if I smoke this month. I'm confident the precommitment reveals my real preference (not to smoke, or to be a nonsmoker), and the cigarette I might smoke on day three doesn't overturn it. So some choices count for more than others, and the "cool" choice wins.
4. Survey asks whether I'd donate to a malaria charity; I say yes. When the actual opportunity to donate comes, I don't. Verdict: the choice reveals my preference; the survey answer is cheap talk. Talk loses to choice here.
5. I buy a gym membership every January and go four times. I don't think the purchase reveals a preference for working out. It reveals... a preference for feeling like the kind of person who works out? Either way, the purchase and the non-attendance don't have to be reconciled by one utility function; they're different time-slices with different preferences.
6. I choose the cheap store-brand cereal even though I'd say the expensive one tastes better and I can afford it. I think I'd be happier with the expensive one. I don't know what this choice reveals, but I'm not willing to say it reveals a preference for worse-tasting cereal.
7. Every night I scroll my phone past midnight, and every morning I'd pay money to have made the scrolling impossible. I don't think there's a single utility function over "scroll vs sleep" that captures both selves.

The tension I can't resolve is between cases 2, 3, and 7 (where I want to say the choice doesn't reveal the real preference, because there's some better-authority preference underneath) and case 4 (where I want to say the choice is the only authority and the stated preference is noise). What determines when a choice is authoritative evidence about utility and when it's a distortion? Is there a principle that gets all seven, or close, and what does it force me to drop? Please also run the principle on the case I keep going back and forth on: someone who says for years that they value time over money but keeps taking the higher-paying, longer-hours job whenever it's offered. And be explicit about what "utility" ends up meaning on your account — a description of choice, a real psychological quantity, a normative standard for which choices count, or something else. Tell me how confident you are in the answer and which part you're least sure of.
