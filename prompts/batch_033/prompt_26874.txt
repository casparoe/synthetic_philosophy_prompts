I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and we've been working through the species problem — roughly, the question of what kind of thing a species is and what principle should govern where we draw species boundaries. Our professor has been presenting us with pairs of populations and giving her verdict on whether they constitute the same species or distinct species. So far, her record is:

1. Donkeys and horses: distinct species.
2. The populations forming the Ensatina salamander ring in California: a single species. She stressed that the variation in morphology and genetics is continuous around the ring, with no non-arbitrary place to draw a line.
3. Two cryptic species of frog in the Amazon that are morphologically indistinguishable but have different mating calls and never hybridize in the wild: distinct species.
4. Two populations of bdelloid rotifers — asexual microscopic invertebrates — that are morphologically indistinguishable but show roughly ten percent sequence divergence across several nuclear genes: distinct species.
5. Polar bears and grizzly bears: distinct species, despite the fertile hybrids now appearing where their ranges have begun to overlap.
6. Two species of European oak (Quercus robur and Quercus petraea) that are morphologically and ecologically distinct but hybridize readily where they co-occur, producing fully fertile hybrids, while nevertheless maintaining their distinctiveness: distinct species.

Next week we are discussing a new case. A widespread freshwater fish was long considered a single species, but some ichthyologists have recently proposed splitting it into two. The two populations occupy separate lake systems, have been isolated for roughly eight thousand years, are morphologically indistinguishable, and occupy identical ecological roles. In laboratory crosses they interbreed readily and produce fully fertile offspring. However, they show a consistent five percent divergence in mitochondrial DNA and form reciprocally monophyletic clades — each population is more closely related to itself than to the other.

I want to predict what our professor will say about the fish. I can see at least two rules that fit all six of her verdicts so far, and they pull in opposite directions on this new case. I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the candidate rules themselves, which one she is more likely running and why, and what further case would cleanly settle which rule she has in mind. If you think her existing verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, I would also like to know what principle she would be committing to with each choice.
