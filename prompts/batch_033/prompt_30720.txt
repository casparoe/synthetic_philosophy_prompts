After a career in evolutionary biology, I have been spending my retirement reading philosophy of biology, and I keep returning to the dispute over adaptationism that Gould and Lewontin set off with their example of the spandrels of San Marco. I have tried to make my own views explicit by recording my verdicts on a set of concrete cases, and I would like to know whether there is a principle behind them.

Here are the verdicts. I accept an adaptationist explanation of the vertebrate camera eye without hesitation: its parts look organized for vision, and selection for seeing is the natural account. By contrast, the tapering triangular spaces where the arches of San Marco meet strike me as clear structural byproducts, not as features designed to display the mosaics later placed on them; the mosaics are a subsequent use, not the cause of the spaces. The giant panda's so-called thumb, really an enlarged wrist bone pressed into service for stripping bamboo, I count as an adaptation, but one that only makes sense against the constraint of descent from carnivores with a different digit arrangement; calling it simply an adaptation for stripping bamboo feels incomplete. I am confident that feathers began as an adaptation for thermoregulation and were later exapted for flight, and I am equally confident that the sickle-cell polymorphism is maintained by selection in malarial regions through heterozygote advantage, despite its cost in homozygotes. But I find every adaptive story about the human chin strained and suspect it is a byproduct of the reduction of the face and jaws; and I lean toward a byproduct account of the female orgasm through its shared developmental basis with the male orgasm, though with much less confidence than I have about the chin. The case I keep going back and forth on is human menopause: the grandmother hypothesis feels like a genuine adaptation, yet I can also tell a plausible byproduct story in which menopause falls out of extended lifespan and reproductive senescence, and I cannot say which kind of story should win.

Is there a coherent principle that captures these verdicts, or close to it? I want the principle stated precisely, with the case or cases that motivate each of its clauses, its verdicts on cases I have not listed, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. In particular, I want to know what the principle says about menopause, whether it forces me to revise any of the verdicts I currently hold most confidently, and how it distinguishes the cases where a selective story is required from those where a byproduct story is the default.
