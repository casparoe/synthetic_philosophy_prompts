Something I keep tripping over when I read neuroscience and animal-cognition papers, and I can't tell if it's a real question or just language policing. The authors will say, flatly, that a population of place cells represents the rat's location, that a honeybee waggle dance says where the nectar is, that a vervet alarm call means leopard — no scare quotes. But the same authors will bend over backwards to avoid saying a thermostat represents the room temperature or that tree rings represent a past drought; that's 'just information' or metaphor. So the field seems to draw a line between an organism's states and ordinary physical covariation, and treat only the first as literally contentful.

I've seen 'teleosemantics' thrown around in blog comments, but I don't trust my handle on it, so I want the implicit theory reconstructed from the practice rather than the label. What theory of mental content does that practice presuppose? Does it need teleosemantics (content fixed by evolved function), or would a causal-informational account also license the line, or is the practice underdetermined by several theories? And is the practice internally consistent: they'll attribute content to a neural ensemble trained on an arbitrary lab task, which has no evolutionary function — that looks like it breaks the teleosemantics reading, but if they fall back to 'it carries information' then the thermostat gets back in. What would have to change in how the field talks if the presuppossed theory turned out false?
