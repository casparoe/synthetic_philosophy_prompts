I've come to accept Benatar's asymmetry about the comparison between existence and non-existence, and I want to know what would count against it. As I understand the view, the presence of pain in a life is bad and the presence of pleasure is good, both for the person who exists; but on the non-existence side the pair breaks apart: the absence of pain is good even though no one is there to enjoy that good, while the absence of pleasure is not bad because there is no one who is deprived of it. I find that four-cell structure intuitive, and I can see how it does the main work in antinatalist arguments.

What I'm after is a map of the position's exposure rather than a recap of the standard objections. Which intuitions, cases, formal results, or developments in nearby debates would have to go a certain way for the asymmetry to be false, or for it to be doing less work than it appears to do? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish what a defender of the asymmetry would themselves concede as a genuine point of vulnerability from what only critics say. I'd also like to be able to say in advance which possible findings or cases would move me, so the view isn't simply tracking my current sympathies.

In particular, I want to know whether the asymmetry is best understood as one primitive commitment or as the product of more basic commitments — a person-affecting restriction on deprivation plus a heavier weighting of pain, say — and whether, if it collapses into one of those, the antinatalist argument survives the collapse. Tell me which consequences the asymmetry commits me to that would themselves count as refutations if they turn out unacceptable: for example, whether it forces me to hold that non-existence is better than a life that is clearly worth living, or that the absence of pleasure for a possible person is never a loss while the absence of pain is always a benefit. I want to know whether the view can handle the distinction between lives worth starting and lives worth continuing without ad hoc qualifications, and whether it has been insulated so thoroughly that nothing could count against it — and, if so, whether that insulation is itself a problem.

Finally, say what the asymmetry predicts about nearby intuitive cases that its rivals do not, and which of my intuitions a person-affecting or deprivation-discounting rival can and cannot capture. I personally find the contrast between absent pain counting as good and absent pleasure not counting as bad the most persuasive part of the view, and the case of a life that is barely worth living but filled with suffering the most troubling. Be willing to conclude that the asymmetry is well protected, and say honestly whether that is a problem.
