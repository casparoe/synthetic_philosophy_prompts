I am a professor of moral philosophy, and for the past three years I have been running a training workshop for restorative justice facilitators — people who sit in a room with someone who has caused serious harm and someone who has suffered it, and try to guide them toward acknowledgment, repair, and, when possible, some form of reconciliation. The facilitators come from social work, mediation, and criminal justice backgrounds, and they are thoughtful practitioners, but they lack a principled way of thinking through a problem that comes up in nearly every case they handle: when is it appropriate to hold someone fully responsible — with all the resentment, indignation, and demand for accountability that goes with that — and when should those reactive attitudes be suspended in favor of treating the person more like something to be understood, managed, or therapeutically addressed?

I have tried giving them P.F. Strawson's essay "Freedom and Resentment," but most of them do not have the philosophical background to extract an operational procedure from it. What I want is something I can hand them that translates Strawson's central distinction — between the participant stance, with its web of reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, forgiveness), and the objective stance, where we step outside that web and see the person as an object of social policy, treatment, or control — into a decision procedure they can actually run on a case. The procedure should tell them, step by step, what questions to ask about an offender and an offense to determine whether the participant stance is appropriate or whether the case calls for adopting the objective stance, even partially. It should incorporate Strawson's crucial point that the objective stance is warranted by specific excusing or exempting conditions (incapacity, immaturity, coercion, ignorance, being "not themselves") and not by abstract metaphysical worries about determinism, which Strawson regarded as irrelevant to the practice. The procedure should also mark the point at which a question is no longer factual but evaluative — where the facilitator has to exercise judgment rather than apply a rule. And it should be honest about cases where the two stances pull in different directions for different aspects of the same situation (e.g., the person is a fully responsible agent in general but acted under extreme situational pressure).

Please write this as a decision procedure, with a clear sequence of questions, criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and explicit stop conditions. Then apply it to three cases: (a) a seventeen-year-old who, in a group, vandalized a community center in what appears to have been an episode of peer-driven recklessness rather than targeted malice; (b) a person with untreated schizophrenia who assaulted a neighbor during a psychotic episode, and who has since been stabilized on medication and expresses profound remorse; and (c) a mid-level corporate manager who authorized the discharge of toxic waste into a river, knowing it was illegal, under intense pressure from superiors who implied his job was at stake. For each case, walk through the procedure and note where it yields a clear answer, where it forces a judgment call, and where — if anywhere — it recommends an answer the facilitator is likely to resist. Finally, flag the limits of the procedure: cases it cannot handle, ways it might be misused, and what Strawson's framework simply does not speak to.

Please think through the philosophical structure carefully before writing the procedure itself, and write the final answer in American English.
