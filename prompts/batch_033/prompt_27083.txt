I cover philosophy for a general-interest magazine and I keep coming back to Kavka's toxin puzzle because the debate around it feels totally stuck and I can't figure out why. Quick recap: you're offered a million dollars if at midnight tonight you *intend* to drink a nausea-inducing toxin tomorrow. You don't actually have to drink it — the money's already yours if the intention is genuine at midnight. The puzzle is whether you can form that intention given that you know, right now, that tomorrow you'll have zero reason to go through with it. The literature has been circling this since the 80s and the positions are basically: (a) you can't intend what you know you won't do, so the puzzle reveals a limit on our control over our own intentions; (b) you can form the intention because you have reason *now* to make yourself the kind of person who will follow through, maybe via precommitment or self-manipulation; (c) the puzzle just shows that "intention" isn't one natural kind and we should stop treating it like one.

What I want to know is: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a substantive dispute about the metaphysics of intention, a verbal dispute where people mean different things by "intend," or a clash of bedrock intuitions about practical reason that no argument can reach? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new conceptual distinction, an empirical result from psychology about how people form intentions, or just admitting the question is ill-posed? I'm less interested in who's right than in why nobodys moved in forty years.
