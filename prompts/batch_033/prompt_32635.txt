I am a high school student, and after working through Quine's essay Two Dogmas of Empiricism I have come to hold a broadly Quinean position: there is no principled analytic-synthetic distinction, and any attempt to draw one either presupposes the very notion it is meant to explain or reduces to a sociological fact about how we happen to hold certain beliefs fixed. I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for a list of standard replies from the literature; I want a map of the view's exposure. What would our linguistic or scientific practice have to look like for it to be reasonable to say that some truths are true purely in virtue of meaning while others are true in virtue of fact? Which specific observations, arguments, formal results, or cases should most move a Quinean to revise the view? Which parts of Quine's overall picture, for example his holism about confirmation or his behaviorism about meaning, could be abandoned without abandoning the conclusion, and which parts would have to be true for the conclusion to survive? I would also like an honest assessment of whether the position, as usually defended, has been qualified so much that it is no longer at risk from any possible finding, and whether that would be a defect in a view of this kind. Finally, please rank the possible defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish those a committed Quinean would themselves concede count against the view from those they would dismiss as changing the subject.
