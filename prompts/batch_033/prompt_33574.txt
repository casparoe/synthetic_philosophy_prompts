The debate about how to define 'life' has been running for decades and seems to be going nowhere. Biologists and philosophers keep proposing definitions — self-reproduction with heritable variation, metabolism, cellular organisation, information-processing, autopoiesis — and counterexamples keep turning up: viruses, prions, transposons, obligate intracellular parasites, synthetic minimal cells, and now the possibility of weird biochemistries elsewhere. Every proposed definition either excludes something people want to call alive or includes something they don't. The pattern has been stable for so long that it's reasonable to ask whether the problem is the proposals or the question.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it a verbal dispute about how to use the word 'life' — in which case there's no fact of the matter, just a choice about stipulation? Is it a factual disagreement waiting for more data from astrobiology or synthetic biology? Is it a disagreement about what kind of concept 'life' is — a natural kind with sharp boundaries, a cluster concept, a prototype concept, a graded notion — and the impasse is evidence that the wrong kind of concept is being sought? Or are the sides tacitly answering different questions: some trying to carve nature at its joints, others trying to draw a practical line for a specific purpose (origin-of-life research, exobiology, medical ethics)?

Give me a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of definitions. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a new way of framing the question, a formal result, or perhaps an argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed.

I'd also like you to say how confident you are in your diagnosis and to single out the point you are least sure of. Please answer in British English.
