Two friends of mine got into it at the pub last night about mind uploading, specifically the gradual-replacement version. I stayed out of it but I've been turning it over since.

The setup they were arguing about: suppose you go into a clinic and, over the course of a year, a machine replaces each of your neurons one at a time with a synthetic neuron that replicates the input-output function of the original. At every step your awake and talking, and you report feeling like yourself. At the end, your skull contains only synthetic neurons, and the biological ones have all been discarded.

Liam said the resulting person is you — same person, same consciousness, no death involved. He said there's no point in the process where you can point and say 'there, that's the moment the original person vanished,' so the original person never vanishes. He also said that since your ordinary biological neurons are already turning over their molecular constituents constantly, we've already accepted that material continuity isn't what matters for survival.

Chloe said that's a sleight of hand. She said if you took all the original biological neurons, kept them alive in a vat, and wired them back together, you'd have a brain that's also conscious and also claims to be the original person — and that brain has at least as good a claim to being you as the synthetic one. She said the gradual process doesn't solve the duplication problem, it just hides it. Liam replied that the vat-brain isn't you because your psychological continuity runs through the synthetic brain, not the vat. Chloe said psychological continuity is doing too much work: if we accept that, we'd also have to say that if you fall into a dreamless sleep and wake up, you've survived, but if someone made an exact duplicate of you while you slept and killed the original, the duplicate would be you — which she finds absurd.

Liam said that's different because in the duplication-during-sleep case the causal chain is broken, whereas in gradual replacement it's preserved. Chloe said 'causal chain' is just another way of saying 'the stuff we care about,' and that what she actually cares about is whether the thing walking around afterwards is the same biological organism. She said personal identity is an animalistic concept — we are human animals, and if the animal dies, we die, even if a psychologically continuous replica steps out of the clinic.

At that point they ordered another round and I lost the thread. But I can't stop thinking about who had the better of it. Can you adjudicate move by move? I want to know: who's right about which specific claims, where each of them overreaches, and whether there's a way to settle the core dispute or whether it bottoms out in a clash of intuitions that can't be resolved without begging the question. Please separate what's genuinely settled in the philosophy of personal identity from what's still deeply contested, and don't present one side of a live debate as if it's the consensus. And could you write in British English?
