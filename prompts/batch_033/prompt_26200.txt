I cover AI for a general-audience science magazine, and I run a roughly monthly brown-bag workshop for my newsroom — around 15 reporters and editors, 90 minutes, mostly smart people who don't code. The thing I keep tripping over in coverage is the gap between the press-release line that a system "learned the user's preferences" and what inverse reinforcement learning actually gives you: a reward function that makes the observed behavior look rational, out of a whole family of reward functions that would do the job just as well. I want my colleagues to feel that underdetermination in there bones before they write another sentence like "the model inferred what humans value."

Design a hands-on activity or game for that workshop. I'd like the participants split into small groups, some acting as the "demonstrator" (each given a private scoring scheme) and the others acting as the "learning system" (seeing only the demonstrator's choices, then trying to recover the scoring scheme). The catch should be that different private scoring schemes produce the same observable choices, so the learners confidently recover a reward function that is wrong — or right, but for reasons they can't tell apart. Give me the exact rules, the materials (paper, cards, a printable grid, whatever is cheap), a point scheme that doesn't need real money, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual technical issues: reward ambiguity and identifiability, how the choice of features or state representation sneaks in, why the rationality assumption (Boltzmann or otherwise) is doing a lot of work, and why a lab can still honestly report a learned reward function even when it isn't "the" human preference. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., a group picks a scoring scheme so simple everyone reconstructs it, or people start arguing that the demonstrator was just irrational), and be upfront about which part of the real technical problem the activity can't reproduce — I'd rather say that in the debrief than let people leave overconfident.
