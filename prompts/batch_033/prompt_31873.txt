I'm a software engineer, not a trained philosopher, and the question I keep coming back to is whether virtual objects—and the harms that happen to them—are real. I've built enough game backend infrastructure to know exactly what these things are at the technical level: rows in a database, serialized state, server-authoritative mutations. But knowing that doesn't settle whether a rare item that took someone four hundred hours to earn, or that they paid real money for, is a real possession when it gets scammed away, duplicated by an exploit, or silently deleted when a company shuts a game down. I've watched a guildmate be genuinely gutted by an item scam and genuinely relieved when a support ticket restored the item; I've also watched a whole community treat a server shutdown as a collective loss even though nothing physical was taken from anyone. I plan to read the philosophical positions on this eventually, but before I do, I want the requirements document: what any adequate theory of whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real has to deliver before I'd consider it adequate.

The theory should be adequate for judging, case by case, whether a harm of the ordinary sort occurred when a virtual item was taken, destroyed, copied, or deleted—not necessarily a legal policy proposal, but something that tells us when "I lost my sword" names a real loss and when it doesn't. Write the desiderata: the cases it has to get right (item theft, destruction of a player-built structure, a duplication bug that makes a "unique" item non-unique, cosmetic items with no mechanical effect, real-money trading, items earned through labor versus items bought, developer deletion, server shutdown); the distinctions it can't collapse (owning versus merely being licensed to use, losing versus being frustrated, depriving someone of a thing versus copying it, player-versus-player harm versus developer action); and the constraints from how these systems actually work that the theory can't contradict—for example, that two items can be bit-for-bit identical and yet one is mine, that a single server-side update can make an item stop existing, and that the platform's terms often say players own nothing. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one side. Flag any pairs you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. Then say which single requirement would do the most work in separating the positions that are out there, without turning the piece into a scorecard—I care about the criteria, not the bibliography; I'll add citations myself.
