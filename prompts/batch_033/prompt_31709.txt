I'm a PhD student in philosophy with a focus on formal epistemology, and I've been working through an argument that seems to show that Bayesian epistemology cannot be a complete normative theory of rational belief. Each step strikes me as plausible, but the conclusion seems too strong. I'd appreciate a careful diagnosis of where the argument goes wrong.

(1) A theory of rational belief should, for any given body of evidence, specify which credences are rationally permissible. If it leaves this radically underdetermined, it is not a complete normative theory.

(2) Orthodox Bayesian epistemology imposes two synchronic requirements — coherence (probabilism) and conditionalization — but says nothing about which prior distribution to adopt among the infinitely many coherent ones.

(3) Since two agents with different coherent priors will, via conditionalization on the same evidence, arrive at arbitrarily different posterior credences, Bayesianism as stated is too permissive: it licenses nearly any credence given the same evidence, provided the agent started from some coherent prior.

(4) To be a complete normative theory, Bayesianism must therefore be supplemented with a principle that constrains priors on the basis of evidence.

(5) The only candidate for such a principle that does not appeal to subjective taste or pragmatic considerations is the Principle of Indifference (PI): when a body of evidence is symmetric with respect to the members of a partition, a rational agent must assign equal credence to each member.

(6) Thus, PI is required for Bayesian epistemology to be a non-arbitrary normative theory of credence.

(7) But PI, when applied to continuous parameter spaces, yields incompatible probability assignments depending on how one parameterizes the space of possibilities. This is Bertrand's paradox. Suppose we know only that a chord is drawn on a given circle, and we ask for the probability that the chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. If we apply PI to the position of the chord's midpoint, we get 1/4. If we apply PI to the endpoints of the chord on the circumference, we get 1/3. If we apply PI to the radial distance and angle of the chord, we get 1/2. The evidence is the same in each case, yet PI delivers three different answers.

(8) A principle that delivers incompatible answers from the same evidence is inconsistent.

(9) Therefore, PI cannot serve as the needed prior-constraining principle.

(10) Therefore, Bayesian epistemology cannot be a non-arbitrary, complete normative theory of rational belief.

I find (10) difficult to accept. It seems to entail that there is either no fact of the matter about what we ought to believe given weak evidence, or that rational belief is radically subjective in a way that undermines Bayesianism's claim to be a normative theory at all. But I cannot see an obvious misstep in the reasoning.

Which premise or step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If there is a way to preserve something like PI while avoiding Bertrand's paradox — for instance, by restricting its application or supplementing it with a further principle — I would be particularly interested in whether that move rescues the argument or simply displaces the problem.
