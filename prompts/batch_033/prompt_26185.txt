I defend the view that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible. By a genuine moral dilemma I mean a situation in which an agent faces two or more conflicting moral requirements, neither of which overrides the other, such that the agent cannot avoid doing something that is wrong all things considered — not merely regrettable, not merely a violation of a pro tanto reason, but a violation of a conclusive ought. On my view, any apparent dilemma of this kind can be resolved under closer scrutiny: either one obligation proves weightier than the other, or the conflict rests on an equivocation about what is required, or the description of the situation is incomplete in a way that, once filled in, dissolves the conflict. I want to know what would count against this position. Give me the strongest arguments, cases, and theoretical pressures arrayed against the impossibility of genuine dilemmas, sorted by how decisive they are. I want you to distinguish between considerations that would make the view merely costly or awkward to maintain and those that would show it is straightforwardly false. Which of these would a committed anti-dilemma theorist concede as genuine difficulties, and which would they dismiss as resting on confusions about the logic of obligation? Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the anti-dilemma position, as it is standardly defended, has been qualified in ways that insulate it from counterexamples — and, if it has, whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or a legitimate theoretical refinement.
