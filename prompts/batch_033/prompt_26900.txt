I want to practice arguing against a view I'll call the "no free choices" position — the claim that a rational agent can never choose between options unless some reason tips the balance in favor of one over the other. If the options are perfectly tied in every respect the agent cares about, the agent is stuck. Please play a sharp, committed defender of this view. Don't be a strawman; concede small points when they're genuinely weak, but hold the core claim. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so it reads like a real exchange.

Here's my opening. Take Buridan's ass: a donkey exactly halfway between two identical bales of hay, no breeze, no bias, no reason to prefer left over right. Your view says the donkey never chooses and starves. But actual agents — animals, people, probably even simple AI systems — don't freeze in symmetric situations; they just pick one and move on. If your theory of rational choice predicts that a perfectly rational agent starves where ordinary agents eat, why shouldn't I conclude that the theory is wrong rather than that ordinary agents are irrational?
