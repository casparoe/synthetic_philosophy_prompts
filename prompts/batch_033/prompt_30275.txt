In the course of reporting on decision theory and the limits of rational self-interest, I have become interested in a puzzle that runs through discussions of Derek Parfit’s famous hitchhiker case. In the standard telling, an agent would like to convince someone that she will perform a costly action later, even though once the later moment arrives she will have no instrumental reason to do it. The usual remedies involve external precommitment, reputation, or being a different kind of rational planner. But Parfit’s broader work on rational irrationality suggests something more disquieting: it may be rational to make oneself genuinely irrational, because only an irrational future self will reliably produce the action that the present self wants to have produced.

I would like you to construct a thought experiment built to that specification. Please design a realistic, ordinary-life scenario—no desert bandits, no perfect lie detectors, no exotic technology—in which an agent deliberately brings about a durable change in her own beliefs, preferences, or dispositions, and thereby comes to perform an action that her original values would count as suboptimal. The key condition is that this self-manipulation must nevertheless be the best available strategy by the lights of the original, rational self. After presenting the scenario in one paragraph, walk through: (a) why the later action is irrational at the moment it is performed, (b) why the earlier decision to become that later agent is rational at the moment it is made, (c) what specific feature of the case prevents the agent from securing the same benefit through ordinary precommitment, reputation, or a credible promise, and (d) how a defender of a unified rational-agent account might reply to your case. Finish by identifying the single structural assumption about the continuity of the agent over time that your case most directly pressures.
