I am a technical writer at a software company, and my team is responsible for the error messages and status alerts our users see. We review several dozen of these a week, and we repeatedly find that a message we thought was clear was read as meaningless, or as the opposite of what we intended, or was not even recognised as a message. I have been reading on my own about the idea that a message has three levels: a frame message, which announces that there is a message here at all; an outer message, which conveys how the inner message is to be decoded; and an inner message, the content itself. The idea also holds that meaning is not stored in the words alone but is spread across those levels and whatever the reader brings to them.

Please turn this into a procedure my team can actually run on each message we draft. Give us the questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a satisfactory answer at each step before we move on. Say where the procedure should end in "rewrite the message," and where it should hand the decision to a person because the question cannot be settled mechanically. Then run the procedure on three messages: (1) a plain "Invalid input" error; (2) a status alert reading "Your data will be deleted in 30 days"; and (3) a message we are fond of, "We have made a change to your account," which some users have read as a threat. I expect the procedure to return at least one verdict we will not like, and I want you to state plainly what the procedure cannot guarantee about how a message will actually be understood. The team has no background in this theory and about fifteen minutes per message, and a senior editor signs off on anything the procedure flags. Please write your answer in British English.
