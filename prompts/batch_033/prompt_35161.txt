I am a software engineer who designs and operates large distributed systems, and my working vocabulary is processes, threads, schedulers, message passing, virtual machines, fault isolation, emulation, and observability. I have no formal training in philosophy of mind. Recently I was put in charge of a system that runs long-lived simulations of many interacting agents, and I found I could no longer say with confidence why none of what it does could matter morally. The philosophical debate over which computations matter and where sentience begins is exactly the debate I need, but its vocabulary — phenomenal consciousness, substrate independence, integrated information, causal powers — keeps slipping out of my hands.

Please translate the main positions in that debate into my working vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Cover at least these four: (a) the view that a computation of the right kind is sufficient for moral status regardless of what it is made of; (b) the view that moral status requires the right physical or biological realization no matter which computation is running; (c) the view that sentience is a matter of how much information a system integrates; and (d) the view that attributions of sentience are stances or instruments for predicting a system, not discoveries of an intrinsic property. For each, use my terms precisely, and flag any place where you are stretching a term such as "process" or "virtual machine" beyond its ordinary engineering sense.

Then give me the residue report. For each position, say what translated cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Conclude with your honest judgment: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, or does my vocabulary expose places where the original claims were less precise than they look?

Format: at most 1,200 words, in two sections titled "Translations" and "Residue report." Do not cite sources; I want the arguments, not the literature.
