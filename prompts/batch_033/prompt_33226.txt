I'm leading a discussion next month for my book club, and we're doing The Great Gatsby. I've been reading Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia and I can't shake the feeling that the whole novel is basically a case study in his entitlement theory, but I'm not sure whether the book vindicates Nozick or undermines him.

Here's what I'm turning over. Nozick says a distribution is just if it came about through just acquisition and just transfer — no pattern matters, only the historical chain. And the Wilt Chamberlain argument says that even if you start from a perfectly equal pattern, free choices will disrupt it, and that's fine because liberty upsets patterns. Now think about Gatsby's world: Tom's wealth is inherited old money, Gatsby's is bootlegging money, the Wilsons are stuck in the valley of ashes, and the whole novel turns on who deserves what and who has a right to what they have. The novel seems to have contempt for both old and new money, but for different reasons — the Buchanans are careless and corrosive, Gatsby is corrupt but somehow noble, and nobody comes out clean. And then there's the fact that everyone who shows up at Gatsby's parties freely choses to drink his champagne and eat his food — is that the Wilt Chamberlain argument in action? If the chain of transfers is what matters, does the novel's moral outrage at the resulting inequality just amount to an attachment to a pattern?

I guess what I'm asking is: read Gatsby through Nozick's entitlement theory. Where does the novel dramatize something Nozick gets right, and where does it push back in a way that should make a Nozickian uncomfortable? Are there specific scenes or characters that map onto the distinction between historical and patterned principles? One thing I'm stuck on: if Gatsby's wealth is unjustly acquired (bootlegging), then under Nozick's framework the whole downstream distribution is tainted and needs rectification — but the novel doesn't seem to think the problem is just that Gatsby's a criminal; it seems to think there's something wrong with the whole setup, old money included. Does Nozick have the resources to say what's wrong with Tom's inherited wealth if the original acquisition was itself unjust, or does the rectification principle end up being so sweeping that it collapes the distinction between historical and patterned views?

And one more: does the novel as a whole think the origin of wealth matters in a way Nozick's framework can capture, or does it point to something his theory misses — maybe something about how concentrated wealth shapes the very choices that are supposed to legitimize the transfers in the first place?

I'd also like the response to say how confident it is in its reading and to single out the point it's least sure about. I'm not looking for the one right answer — I want to know where the reading is on solid ground and where it's speculative.
