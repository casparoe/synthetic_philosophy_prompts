I have recently joined the strategy unit of a mid-sized charity, and one of our senior economists has a habit of settling internal disagreements by announcing that 'rational agents cannot agree to disagree' and that, since we are all reasonable people, someone in the room must be making a mistake. The discussion then turns to who is at fault rather than to what the evidence actually says. I have since noticed variants of this move — 'common priors force convergence', 'you cannot agree to disagree' — in blog posts, in an investment club I belong to, and at a conference on policy evaluation.

I am not in a position to challenge a senior colleague directly, but I want to understand what this slogan actually amounts to before I decide how seriously to take it. What are the distinct precise claims that 'rational agents cannot agree to disagree' could be asserting? Which of them are mathematically provable, which are substantive and contestable, and which are the ones that would actually license shutting down a real disagreement among reasonably well-informed people? I am especially interested in what hidden conditions are packed into the slogan — common knowledge of one another's beliefs, common priors in the Bayesian sense, the absence of computational constraints, and whatever else the formal result actually requires — and how many of those conditions are ever met in an actual meeting room. Please lay the readings out explicitly, distinguish trivial from substantive versions, say which of them would support my colleague's move, and flag any that are false or vacuous.
