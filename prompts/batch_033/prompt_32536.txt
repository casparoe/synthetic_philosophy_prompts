I'm a high school student in a philosophy club, and I've been trying to work through the debate about whether we can separate an artwork from its artist. I constructed an argument that now seems to prove too much, and I can't figure out which step fails. Here it is:

(1) The aesthetic value of an artwork is determined by properties of the work itself — its form, content, technique, and the relationships among those elements. Facts about the artist that are not embodied in the work are irrelevant to whether the work is good art. (Call this the "autonomy" premise.)

(2) The moral character of the artist — what the artist did or believed — is, at least in the ordinary case, a fact about the artist that is not embodied in the work itself. If a painter was cruel to his family, that fact is not literally inscribed in the paint on the canvas.

(3) Therefore, from (1) and (2), the moral character of the artist does not affect the aesthetic value of the work. An artwork can be good art even if its creator was a moral monster.

(4) Now, if (3) holds, it should also hold in cases where the artist's immorality is the very subject matter of the work. Suppose a filmmaker commits real acts of violence and then makes a film that aesthetically celebrates those exact acts. By (3), the filmmaker's moral character is irrelevant, so the film's artistic merit should be judged purely on its formal and expressive features — cinematography, narrative structure, and so on — not on the fact that it glorifies real violence.

(5) But now consider a limit case: a photograph taken by a killer during a murder, where the aesthetic decisions (framing, lighting, composition) are made in the service of the act itself. By the reasoning above, this photograph could in principle be great art, and we should evaluate it purely on its formal properties.

(6) That conclusion seems perverse. The moral horror of the act seems to infect the aesthetic evaluation — it is not a separate factual add-on but something that bears on whether the photograph can properly be called "good art" in an unqualified sense.

(7) So either premise (1) is false (aesthetic value is not purely internal to the work), or premise (2) is false (the artist's moral character can somehow be embodied in the work), or the move from (3) to (4) is invalid (there is a principled difference between ordinary immoral artists and artists whose immorality is the content of the work).

(8) But rejecting (1) threatens the very idea of artistic autonomy — we would have to say that discovering a creator's moral flaws retroactively changes how good their work is, which seems like wishful thinking. Rejecting (2) blurs the boundary between artist and artwork, making biographical criticism mandatory. And if we reject the move from (3) to (4), we need a principle that distinguishes the two kinds of cases — but every attempt I have seen to draw that line either excludes too little (propaganda films can be formally brilliant) or too much (half the canon was made by people with serious moral flaws).

Where does this argument go wrong? Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? Please keep your response to at most 500 words.
