I've been asked to read The Brothers Karamazov for a discussion group, and the "Grand Inquisitor" chapter has lodged itself in my head in a way I did not expect. The part I cannot get past is the Inquisitor's long speech about the three temptations. I am going to paraphrase the stretch that troubles me most, since I do not have the translation in front of me and I want to describe the thought accurately rather than from memory.

The Inquisitor accuses Christ of having overestimated humanity. In the wilderness, Satan offered Christ bread, miracle, and earthly authority — and Christ refused all three, insisting that man shall not live by bread alone, shall not tempt God, and shall worship God alone. The Inquisitor says that by refusing, Christ demanded a freedom that ordinary human beings cannot bear. What the vast majority of people actually want, he argues, is not freedom but happiness, and happiness requires exactly what Christ rejected: someone to feed them, someone to astonish them with miracles, and someone to rule over them and take the terrible burden of choice off their shoulders. The Inquisitor says that the Church has corrected Christ's work: it has accepted the three offers Christ spurned, and in doing so it has finally made human happiness possible. The awful line of thought is that Christ's gift of freedom was, in practice, a curse — that love of humanity means giving people bread and authority, not liberty, and that the few who do the ruling are miserable precisely because they bear the freedom the many are spared.

Here is what I need help with. The passage is clearly meant to be a critique of institutional religion, but I cannot tell how far the critique is supposed to run. Is the Inquisitor's argument meant to be seductive in a way that Dostoevsky himself finds genuinely troubling — that is, does Dostoevsky think the Inquisitor has put his finger on a real problem with the Christian ideal, one that a believer must honestly face? Or is the whole speech framed so that the reader is supposed to see through it from the start, with the Inquisitor's anguish serving as evidence that his project is self-defeating? I am especially interested in what the text itself does to mark the limits of the Inquisitor's position — for instance, the fact that Christ says nothing and at the end kisses the Inquisitor, or the way Ivan frames the story when telling it to Alyosha. I want to know what those details imply about whether the Inquisitor's critique lands or collapses.

Please say how confident you are in your reading and single out the point you are least sure of.
