Question (20 marks): Explain the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. What exactly is the challenge, and how have expressivists attempted to meet it?

Student's answer:

"The Frege–Geach problem is the central difficulty for expressivist, or non-cognitivist, theories in metaethics. Expressivism holds that moral sentences do not describe facts or express beliefs; instead, they express non-cognitive attitudes such as approval, disapproval, or acceptance of norms. On the early emotivist view, for example, 'Murder is wrong' is not a truth-evaluable statement but an expression of a negative attitude toward murder, rather in the way one might express distaste for a villain by saying 'boo'.

The problem, named for Peter Geach, who drew on Frege's distinction between content and assertoric force, arises when a moral sentence appears in an embedded context. Consider the conditional 'If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.' In uttering this conditional, a speaker need not be expressing disapproval of lying; she may be considering the antecedent only hypothetically. Yet the sentence 'lying is wrong' occurs as a constituent of the conditional, and if the meaning of that sentence is exhausted by the attitude it expresses when asserted, expressivism seems unable to say what it contributes to the meaning of the whole conditional. The difficulty generalizes to negation, disjunction, questions, and other unasserted contexts, so it is fundamentally a problem about the compositionality of moral language.

The problem also threatens the validity of moral inference. Consider: (1) If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong; (2) Lying is wrong; (3) Therefore, getting your brother to lie is wrong. This is an instance of modus ponens, but if 'lying is wrong' expresses disapproval when it appears as premise (2) and does not express disapproval when it appears inside premise (1), then the sentence seems to change its meaning between the premises, and the argument risks committing the fallacy of equivocation. Expressivism therefore seems unable to explain how even simple moral reasoning can be valid.

Expressivists have tried to meet the problem in two main ways. Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism treats moral conditionals as expressing beliefs about the logical relations among the attitudes: 'If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong' expresses the belief that disapproval of lying rationally commits one to disapproval of getting one's brother to lie. Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism takes a different route, constructing a possible-worlds semantics for normative language that assigns truth-conditions to moral sentences so that they can enter into standard logical relations just as descriptive sentences do. Both approaches aim to show that expressivists can explain moral inference without giving up the core idea that moral judgments are non-cognitive attitudes."
