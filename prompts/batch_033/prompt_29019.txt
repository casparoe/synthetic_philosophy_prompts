I've been trying to get clear on something about belief and desire for a while now, entirely on my own, without any formal background. The idea that has its grip on me is this: when we explain why someone did something — left a job, married a particular person, voted a certain way — we reach for words like 'believed', 'wanted', 'hoped', 'feared'. But I've started wondering whether these are real things inside a person, the way bones and blood vessels are, or whether they are more like convenient fictions we use because we cannot see the underlying machinery. I've since learnt that philosophers call the most radical version of this worry eliminative materialism: the view that our everyday talk of beliefs and desires picks out nothing real, and that a mature science of the mind will discard these notions entirely, much as physics discarded phlogiston.

I want you to construct a thought experiment for me that isolates exactly what is at stake. Here are the specifications.

The scenario should depict two people — call them Anna and Ben — who are looking at the same bit of human behaviour and the same neuroscientific data about it, but disagreeing about whether the data eliminates the belief-desire explanation or merely underpins it. The behaviour should be something ordinary and emotionally charged: a specific decision whose belief-desire explanation feels obvious and intimate to the person making it, but whose neural story is also richly detailed. The neuroscientific data in the scenario should be described concretely — particular patterns, regions, or processes — but it should all be plausible given today's science, not futuristic. No brain scanners that read thoughts; just the sort of correlational and mechanistic detail that real cognitive neuroscience provides.

The key constraint: Anna must be an eliminativist about this particular instance and Ben must be a realist about it, and their disagreement must turn on a single feature of the data — one specific finding that Anna sees as doing the eliminative work and Ben sees as neutral or even supportive of the belief-desire story. The feature cannot be mere incompleteness (Anna cannot simply say 'belief doesn't show up in the scan therefore it isn't there'); it has to be a positive feature of the neural story that actively competes with the belief-desire account. Likewise, Ben cannot just say 'but it feels like I have beliefs'; his defence must engage with the same piece of data Anna is leaning on.

After the scenario, I need you to do four things. First, state clearly what the disputed feature of the data is and why Anna thinks it is eliminative — what principle is she implicitly relying on? Second, give Ben's best counter-move that stays within the data rather than appealing to introspection or ordinary language. Third, tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting — that is, if you changed one thing about the scenario, the disagreement would collapse into a standoff where neither side has a grip on the other. Fourth, take the side of whichever character came out weaker in your scenario and strengthen their argument as much as you honestly can: what would they need to concede, and what would they gain by conceding it?

I want this because I am trying to understand whether eliminative materialism is a claim that could ever be settled by looking at the brain, or whether it is a metaphysical stance one brings to the data before the first electrode is placed. Please write your answer in British English.
