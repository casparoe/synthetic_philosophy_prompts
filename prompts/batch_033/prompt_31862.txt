At a philosophy department happy hour, I overheard two graduate students arguing about the norm of assertion. I didn't catch how it started, but here is what I remember of their exchange:

Maya: The norm of assertion is knowledge. You may assert that p only if you know that p. That is why "How do you know?" is always a legitimate challenge to an assertion, and why "Sorry, I don't actually know that" functions as a retraction.

David: But people assert things they don't know all the time without doing anything wrong. If I ask you where the department meeting is, and you say "Room 302," and it is in Room 302, your assertion is perfectly fine even if you are not certain — even if you would only say you are pretty sure.

Maya: If you are not certain enough to know, you should hedge. "I think it is in 302" is fine; the flat-out assertion "It is in 302" implies you know. That is the norm at work.

David: That cannot be right, because we make flat-out assertions about the future all the time. "It will rain tomorrow" — no one strictly knows that, but it is a perfectly proper thing to say given a good forecast.

Maya: You might know it will rain on the basis of a reliable forecast. Knowledge does not require Cartesian certainty.

David: Fine, but take a lottery case. I buy a ticket in a fair lottery with odds of one in a million. I do not know I will lose — that is standard. But if I say "I am not going to win," that seems like a perfectly proper assertion. If knowledge is the norm, I have violated it. But I have not done anything wrong.

Maya: Lottery cases are puzzling for everyone, not just the knowledge account. The truth norm and the belief norm also have to explain what is going on there.

David: Actually, the truth norm handles lottery cases easily: "I am not going to win" is almost certainly true, so asserting it is fine. The knowledge norm cannot say that, because you do not know it.

Maya: The truth norm cannot explain why it is wrong to assert something true on the basis of a lucky guess. If I guess the score of a game and happen to be right, I have violated a norm even though I spoke the truth. The knowledge norm explains that: I did not know.

David: The knowledge norm has its own lucky-guess problem. Suppose I have good evidence that p, I assert p, and p is true, but my evidence is Gettiered — there is a deviant causal chain. I do not know p, but my assertion seems perfectly proper. The knowledge norm says I violated a norm; I did not.

At that point someone spilled a drink and I lost the thread. I have been turning their arguments over since.
