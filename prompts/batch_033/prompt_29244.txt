I have been trying to work out what distinguishes a genuine transcendental argument from a merely rhetorical one that moves from the fact that we cannot help but think something to the conclusion that the thing is true. My verdicts on several arguments, stated in my own words, are as follows.

1. An argument that a being capable of experience of an objective world must be able to distinguish how things merely seem to it from how they are, since otherwise there is no room for the thought of a world that could diverge from how it is experienced. I find this convincing.

2. An argument that anyone engaged in inquiry must presuppose the law of non-contradiction, because inquiry seeks an answer while treating incompatible alternatives as mutually exclusive; therefore the law of non-contradiction is true. I do not find this convincing: it seems to show only that inquiry would be impossible without our accepting it, not that the principle holds.

3. An argument that wholesale doubt about other minds is self-undermining, because the concepts used to frame the doubt get their meaning from a shared public practice that already involves other speakers. I find this mostly convincing, though weaker than 1.

4. An argument that free will is a precondition of deliberation, because to deliberate about whether to accept free will is already to take oneself to be able to step back and decide; so deliberation presupposes free will. I do not find this convincing, even though its structure looks much like 1.

5. An argument that anyone who puts forward an argument is thereby committed to there being reasons for belief, since to argue is to offer something as a reason. I find this convincing, but it feels different in kind from the others.

My tentative principle is that a legitimate transcendental argument must identify a condition on the content of a kind of thought or experience, not merely a condition on carrying out an activity, but I do not see how case 5 meets that and I am unsure whether case 3 does. Is there a principle that captures all five verdicts, or nearly all? State it precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me what it predicts about a further case I have not settled: an argument that memory requires a real past, because a memory is by its nature an awareness of something as having happened, and a creature whose apparent memories were systematically false would not be remembering at all. If no principle captures the verdicts, say which verdict I should overturn and whether the resulting simpler principle is worth the cost.
