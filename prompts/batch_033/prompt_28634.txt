In my department's reading group on social epistemology, two colleagues fell into an argument about the Condorcet jury theorem that I have not been able to referee to my satisfaction. (1) One said the theorem is useless as a justification for majority rule in real institutions, because its independence condition — that voters' errors be uncorrelated conditional on the correct alternative — is systematically violated whenever voters rely on shared sources of evidence, which is nearly always. (2) The other replied that this is like rejecting a frictionless model of motion: the theorem isolates the mechanism by which adding voters contributes information, and thereby explains why shared evidence threatens collective judgment rather than reassuring us. (3) The first answered that a model whose central condition is never satisfied cannot underwrite the practice, and that under realistic correlation the majority can be more likely wrong than right even in arbitrarily large groups, despite each voter's being individually more likely right than wrong — so the real world can invert the theorem's conclusion. (4) The second granted the last point but said this is the theorem doing its work: the result is a diagnostic, not a blanket endorsement, and a diagnostic is not refuted by the fact that the failure it predicts actually occurs. (5) The first countered that as a diagnostic it is toothless, because in practice we cannot estimate the correlation structure of voters' errors well enough to know whether a given electorate lies on the safe side of the theorem's conditions. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, state who is right about what, and identify any distinction the two are jointly missing.
