I'm a graduate student TA for an introductory philosophy of science course, and in two weeks I have to teach Hempel's raven paradox to a section of about twenty-five second-year undergraduates. Most of them are not philosophy majors; they're taking the course to satisfy a distribution requirement, and they arrive with the working assumption that science is about gathering evidence that supports theories, end of story. The professor has covered the hypothetico-deductive model and basic confirmation theory in lecture, so the students have heard the Nicod criterion and the idea that a universal generalization is confirmed by its instances. But they have not yet seen the paradox, and the lecture slot before my section will introduce it only briefly.

I want you to plan how I should teach the paradox in a fifty-minute section. Do not explain the paradox to me; I already understand it. Instead, give me the sequence of ideas I should present and the order in which they must appear, with the dependency structure made explicit: what must be in place before what, and why. Give me the one carrying example that should run through the whole session, and tell me what that example needs to have and what it must lack for it to work. Identify the misconceptions the students will predictably form at each stage, and tell me at exactly which point in the sequence I should head each one off and how. I also want to know what I should openly refuse to resolve at this level and why. Finally, give me one question I can ask in the last three minutes that will tell me whether they have understood the paradox itself rather than merely memorized the steps or decided it is silly. Your response should be continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
