I've been thinking about a problem that came up during an argument with my partner a few weeks ago. We were discussing a friend of ours, Callum, who holds two political views that contradict each other in a way that takes about three or four steps of reasoning to uncover. My partner said Callum was being irrational, full stop. I pushed back, saying that if the contradiction requires real inferential work, failing to see it is a failure of cleverness or effort, not rationality. We got nowhere, and since then I've been collecting my verdicts on cases to see whether there's a principled line behind them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. **Simple modus ponens.** Alice knows that if it's raining, the picnic is cancelled. She looks out the window, sees it's raining, and goes back to reading. She hasn't yet concluded that the picnic is cancelled. She would assent if asked, but the thought hasn't crossed her mind. **Verdict:** she is being irrational. The failure is trivial, but it is a failure.

2. **Long chain.** Ben knows P₁, and knows P₁→P₂, and knows P₂→P₃, and so on through P₁₉→P₂₀. He has never drawn the conclusion P₂₀. If you walked him through each step he would assent at every stage, but he has never assembled the chain himself. **Verdict:** not irrational. Twenty steps is too many to expect anyone to hold in mind.

3. **Hidden contradiction (Callum's case).** Callum believes that the state should not interfere with private contracts between consenting adults. He also believes that employment contracts should be regulated to prevent exploitative wage arrangements. The contradiction only becomes visible after several inferential steps connecting the two commitments. **Verdict:** not irrational. The inconsistency is real but not accessible to casual reflection.

4. **Obvious contradiction.** Diane says she believes all humans have equal moral worth, and also says she believes some people's lives simply matter more than others because of their contributions to society. When the contradiction is pointed out, she says she meant both statements literally and stands by both. **Verdict:** irrational. The inconsistency is right on the surface.

5. **Probabilistic incoherence.** Evan assigns credence 0.7 to proposition A, 0.6 to proposition B, and 0.4 to the disjunction A∨B. He has never studied probability theory and has no idea these numbers are incoherent. The violation is not something he could detect without training. **Verdict:** I want to say he is being irrational, but I'm not confident. His credences are objectively incoherent — they violate the probability axioms — yet the incoherence is hidden from him.

6. **Unproven theorem.** Fatima is a professional mathematician who knows the ZFC axioms. She has been trying to prove a certain open conjecture for years and hasn't succeeded. **Verdict:** not irrational. No one thinks mathematicians are irrational for failing to prove hard theorems.

7. **Forgotten premise.** Greg knows that if the grant deadline is Friday, he needs to submit by Thursday night. He knows the deadline is Friday. On Wednesday, he acts as if he has plenty of time and makes no preparations. The conclusion simply hasn't occurred to him. **Verdict:** irrational. This is just the modus ponens case with a longer delay — the inference is immediate once attended to, and a rational person would have attended to it.

The tension I can't resolve is between cases 3 and 5. In case 3, I excuse Callum because the contradiction takes work to uncover. But in case 5, Evan's incoherence also takes work to uncover — he would need training he doesn't have — and yet I still feel he's irrational. What distinguishes them? Is probability theory normatively special in a way that ordinary deductive logic is not? Is the difference that Evan's credences are his own mental states while Callum's contradiction is between external propositional commitments? Or am I simply wrong about one of the two verdicts?

I'm looking for a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures my verdicts as well as possible, explains what distinguishes the cases where logical ignorance is irrational from those where it isn't, and tells me which of my verdicts I should revise if they can't all be saved. Please state the principle precisely, show how it handles each of the seven cases, and apply it to a further case I haven't been able to settle: someone who believes the premises of a valid argument, can find no flaw in the reasoning, but rejects the conclusion because they distrust the arguer. Is that person rationally required to accept the conclusion, or does their distrust give them a rational excuse?
