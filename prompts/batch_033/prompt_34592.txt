**Exam Question**

In Rosenthal's centipede game, two players alternate choosing whether to take a larger share of a growing pot or pass to the other player. The game has a known, finite number of rounds. Explain the backward induction argument for what rational players should do in this game, and then discuss why this argument has been thought to generate a paradox. What, if anything, does the paradox reveal about the concept of common knowledge of rationality?

---

**Student Answer**

The centipede game is a two-player extensive-form game where players take turns deciding whether to "take" or "pass." If a player takes, the game ends and they receive a larger payoff than the other player. If they pass, the total pot grows and the other player gets to decide. The game has a fixed number of rounds, say 100.

Backward induction works by starting at the last decision node. At round 100, Player 2 (assuming Player 2 moves last) faces a choice: take and get more than if they pass (since passing would end the game with a smaller payoff, or zero), or pass. Obviously, a rational player takes at the last round. Now go back to round 99. Player 1 knows that if they pass, Player 2 will take at round 100, leaving Player 1 with a smaller payoff than if Player 1 takes now. So Player 1 should take at round 99. This reasoning unravels all the way back to round 1: Player 1 should take immediately, and the game ends on the first move.

The paradox is that this recommendation seems absurd. If both players passed for many rounds, they would both end up with much larger payoffs than if someone takes early. Backward induction tells rational players to take immediately, but this is clearly a worse outcome for both. The paradox shows that backward induction is not always the right way to reason about strategic situations — sometimes rationality is self-defeating, and the rational thing is to cooperate. This is similar to the prisoner's dilemma, where individual rationality leads to a collectively worse outcome. The centipede game paradox thus reveals that common knowledge of rationality is a problematic assumption: if both players know the other is rational, they are forced into a bad outcome, which suggests we should sometimes abandon the assumption that others are rational in order to achieve better results.
