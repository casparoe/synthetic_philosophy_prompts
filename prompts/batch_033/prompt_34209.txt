So in my Kierkegaard seminar we got into this argument about Fear and Trembling and I'm still not sure who's right. Two of my classmates went back and forth and I wanna know what you make of it.

(1) Maya said the whole "teleological suspension of the ethical" doesn't actually work as a concept. Her point was: if God commands Abraham to sacrifice Isaac, then obeying God *is* Abraham's ethical duty — like, the fact that the command comes from God just makes it the right thing to do, ethically. So there's no real "suspension" of the ethical happening, just a conflict between two ethical duties (don't kill your kid vs. obey God) where the higher one wins. She said Kierkegaard is dressing up a straightforward ethical hierarchy as something more radical than it is.

(2) Ben pushed back and said she's missing the whole structure of the book. The ethical, for Kierkegaard (or for Johannes de Silentio anyway), is the *universal* — it's about duties that can be stated publicly and justified in terms anyone could accept. Abraham's relation to God is totally private and can't be universalized. He can't explain himself to Sarah or Isaac or anyone. That's precisely why it's a *suspension* of the ethical and not just a higher ethical duty — the ethical is defined by communicability and universality, and Abraham's act has neither. It's not "ethical but higher priority," it's outside the ethical altogether.

(3) Maya came back: "Okay, but then how do you distinguish Abraham from someone who murders his kid and says God told him to? If the criterion is purely private and incommunicable, there's literally no difference between the knight of faith and a delusional killer — from the outside they look identical. Doesn't that make the view unfalsifiable in a really bad way?"

(4) Ben said that's exactly the point — that's the "fear and trembling." Faith is absurd, it has no rational guarantee, and you can't point to external evidence to reassure yourself. The whole anguish of the position is that you *can't* know from the outside, and Abraham himself can't be certain in any demonstrable way. The lack of a public criterion isn't a bug, it's the feature.

(5) Maya said: "So the view is completely self-sealing — any criticism I make just gets absorbed as 'yes, that's the absurdity of faith.' At that point isn't it just unfalsifiable in principle, which makes it not really a philosophical position at all?"

Ben started to answer but we ran out of time. Can you go through this exchange move by move and say who's right about what? And if you had to pick, who's got the better overall read of what Kierkegaard is doing?
