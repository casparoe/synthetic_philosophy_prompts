**Exam Question:**

Explain what heritability (h²) means in behavioral genetics. Your answer should: (a) define the statistic precisely, distinguishing narrow-sense from broad-sense heritability where relevant; (b) identify and correct at least three common misinterpretations of heritability estimates, explaining in each case what the heritability statistic does and does not license us to conclude.

---

**Student Answer:**

Heritability, denoted h², is a statistic from quantitative genetics that quantifies the extent to which genetic differences between individuals cause phenotypic differences in a population. Specifically, narrow-sense heritability is the proportion of total phenotypic variance (V_P) that is attributable to additive genetic variance (V_A), so h² = V_A / V_P. Broad-sense heritability (H²), by contrast, uses total genetic variance (V_G) in the numerator, which includes dominance and epistatic effects in addition to additive effects. Narrow-sense heritability is the more commonly reported figure in behavioral genetics because it captures the component of genetic influence that responds to selection.

Three common misinterpretations of heritability are as follows.

First, many people think that a heritability estimate tells you how much of an individual person's trait is caused by their genes. If the heritability of IQ is 0.5, they conclude that half of their own IQ is genetic and half is environmental. This is a mistake because heritability is a population-level statistic: it describes the decomposition of variance across individuals in a particular population at a particular time, not the causal makeup of any single individual's phenotype. A trait can have high heritability in a population even if, for any given individual, the developmental pathway to that trait involves an inseparable tangle of genetic and environmental factors.

Second, many people infer from a high heritability estimate that a trait is fixed or unchangeable by environmental intervention. This is also mistaken. Heritability tells us about the sources of variation as they currently exist in a population; it does not tell us what would happen if the environment changed. A classic illustration is phenylketonuria (PKU), a metabolic disorder that is entirely genetic in origin and highly heritable under ordinary environmental conditions, yet its most severe cognitive effects can be prevented by a simple dietary intervention. High heritability is fully compatible with high malleability.

Third, many people assume that if a trait is highly heritable within groups, then differences between groups on that trait must also be genetic in origin. This does not follow. As Richard Lewontin's well-known seed-corn thought experiment illustrates, two genetically identical batches of seeds can produce plants of different average heights when grown in different soils, even though height variation within each soil type is entirely environmental (heritability is zero within each group). The reverse scenario is equally possible: high within-group heritability combined with purely environmental between-group differences. The causes of within-group variation and between-group variation are logically independent, which means that heritability estimates are irrelevant to understanding the origins of group differences.
