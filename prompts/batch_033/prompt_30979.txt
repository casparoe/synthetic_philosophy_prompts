The social psychology situationist tradition and neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics have been in a standoff for over two decades. Imagine that a representative from each camp has just completed a year-long adversarial collaboration, facilitated by an empirically minded metaphysician neither side had reason to distrust. Their goal was to produce a document that both can sign: one that records what they now agree on, states their remaining disagreements in wording each side accepts for its own position, identifies the cruxes on which the dispute turns, and—for each crux—specifies what evidence or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Write that document. It should include:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should go beyond truisms (e.g., "situations influence behavior") and capture substantive concessions each side made during the collaboration. Pay attention to the distinction between local traits (narrowly specified dispositions such as "honesty about money when being observed") and global traits (broad dispositions such as "honesty"), and to what exactly the classic experiments—Milgram, Darley and Batson, Isen and Levin—do and do not show.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: the question, the virtue ethicist's answer, and the situationist's answer. Each answer must be phrased so that its own side would say "yes, that is exactly what I think"—not a caricature the other side would prefer. Questions should include at least: whether the 0.3 cross-situational correlation ceiling is a problem for virtue ethics; what kind of behavioral consistency virtue requires; and whether the empirical burden of proof lies with the virtue ethicist or the situationist.

3. A list of cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side appreciably. For each crux, describe a concrete empirical study, conceptual analysis, or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, and state what each side predicts the outcome would be. At least one crux should concern the metaphysics of dispositions (e.g., whether virtues must manifest across all relevant situations or only across a specified range of conditions), and at least one should concern the interpretation of a specific experimental paradigm that both sides agree is probative.

4. A brief note on whether any part of their dispute turned out to be merely verbal, and if so, which part and why.

Do not write a dialogue or a narrative of the collaboration. Write the finished document itself—the text both representatives would put their names to.
