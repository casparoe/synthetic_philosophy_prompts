A friend and I got into an argument about Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and I can't find the exit. Here's the argument, numbered so we can point at steps. (1) Take the proposition that the ball is red. For it to be true or false at all, its constituents — the ball, redness, and whatever ties them together — have to actually be combined into one unified thing, not just a heap or a list. (2) If a relation is what does the tying, then that relation has to itself stand in some connection to the ball and to redness; otherwise it's just a third item sitting next to the other two, and we still have a list. (3) But the connecting relation needs its own connection to the ball, to redness, and to the first relation, and that connection needs another, and so on without end. (4) This regress is vicious: an endless chain of relations never actually relates anything, because each link just passes the job to the next one. (5) So the original relation never relates the ball to redness, and the proposition never gets unified. (6) Therefore no proposition is ever true or false. (7) But that's absurd: the proposition that the ball is red just is true when the ball is red. So one of the earlier steps has to go. I'm stuck between rejecting (1) (maybe truth doesn't require that kind of unity), (2) (maybe a relation relates without needing a further connection), and (4) (maybe the infinite chain is harmess, not vicious). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me elsewhere?
