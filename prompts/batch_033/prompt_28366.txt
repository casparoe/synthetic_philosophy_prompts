Molyneux imagined a man born blind who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch. If this man were suddenly granted sight, could he tell which object was the cube and which the sphere by vision alone, before touching them? Locke, in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding, answered with a firm no. His reasoning was that the ideas we receive from different senses are of fundamentally different kinds — visual ideas are patterns of light and colour, tactile ideas are patterns of resistance and texture — and there is no innate connection between them. Only repeated experience of seeing an object while simultaneously touching it can forge an association between the visual and tactile ideas of that shape. The problem remained a philosophical curiosity until the twenty-first century, when neuroscientists working with congenitally blind patients whose sight was surgically restored — most notably through Project Prakash in India — were able to test Molyneux's question empirically. The results were striking: newly sighted subjects could match seen objects to previously felt objects at rates significantly above chance almost immediately after bandage removal. This demonstrates that cross-modal transfer of shape information is innate, that Locke's empiricist answer was mistaken, and that Molyneux's problem has been resolved by empirical science in favour of a yes answer.'
