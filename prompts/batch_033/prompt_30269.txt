At a small online reading group on social choice theory — the kind that treats Arrow's theorem as a touchstone — two regulars spent most of an hour arguing about what Sen's liberal paradox actually shows, and I was asked to referee. I could see both of them being partly right and partly talking past each other, but I couldn't make the adjudication precise, and neither of them moved.

The background, as everyone in the group understood it: Sen's theorem says that if a social decision procedure must (i) respect unanimity — whenever every individual strictly prefers X to Y, society prefers X to Y — and (ii) honor a minimal form of liberalism — at least two individuals each get to be decisive over at least one pair of alternatives — then there are preference profiles that force a social cycle. The standard illustration involves one copy of a book and two readers. The first reader prefers that no one read it, and if someone must, that he read it rather than the second; the second prefers that the first read it, and if not, that he read it rather than no one. Give the first decisiveness over whether he reads it, and the second decisiveness over whether he reads it. Pairwise decisiveness yields "neither reads" over "first reads" and "second reads" over "neither," while unanimity yields "first reads" over "second reads" — a cycle.

The argument went as follows.

(1) The first said the paradox is a real discovery about the structure of liberal commitments: you cannot simultaneously endorse unanimity-respecting social choice and protected personal spheres without adding a further doctrine specifying which preferences count. Every repair — weakening the Pareto condition, restricting the domain, reformulating the decisiveness condition — is itself a substantive normative move with costs, and the theorem's job is to force those costs into the open.

(2) The second replied that this reads too much into a modeling artifact. The conflict depends on counting meddlesome preferences — both readers' preferences over the pair "first reads" versus "second reads" — as inputs to the Pareto condition. Actual liberal doctrine has never required social choice to track preferences about who reads a book, even when those preferences happen to be unanimous; it says certain choices are outside social control. Restrict the domain to exclude such preferences, or formulate the condition as a right over acts rather than over full social states, and the standard cycle disappears. So the theorem shows only that an unrestricted-domain formalization that counts meddlesome preferences is inconsistent with Pareto.

(3) The first answered that the domain restriction being offered as a technical fix is itself a substantive liberal principle — a theory of admissible preferences — and that is exactly what the theorem forces into the open. Before Sen, one might reasonably have thought that "unanimity plus personal spheres" was a complete statement of the position; the theorem shows it is not, and the cost of the repair is that one now has to defend a filtering rule that excludes preferences people actually have. The artificiality of the example is a feature, not a bug: impossibility theorems standardly use minimal, idealized conditions to locate hidden structure, and if a cycle arises under conditions this mild, the conflict is robust rather than an edge case.

(4) The second came back: then the theorem's contribution is at most pedagogical, and the Arrow analogy cuts the other way. Arrow's conditions were each independently motivated as minimal commitments of democratic aggregation, so the impossibility lands on democracy itself. Here, the formal "liberalism" condition corresponds to no principle anyone actually held — it makes social choice track preferences over outcomes rather than protect choices — and the result changes entirely depending on how one formalizes "rights." A theorem whose content depends on a modeling choice is a result about the formalism, not about liberalism.

Then say what, if anything, both participants are missing, and who is more right overall. I would rather you identify the precise points where the disagreement turns than give a global verdict without the step-by-step ratings.
