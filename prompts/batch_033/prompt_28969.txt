I spent much of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether a mutual acquaintance's information environment is best described as an echo chamber, an epistemic bubble, or neither. We agreed on the observable facts — the narrowness of his reading, the way he discounts outside sources, the history of how he came to distrust them — but we could not agree on the diagnosis, and I came away convinced that we were applying different concepts without realizing it. Before I resume that argument, I want a specification of what an adequate account of echo chambers and epistemic bubbles must do, where an account is adequate if it lets us diagnose cases like his, explain how the epistemic damage occurs, and say what could help.

Please write the desiderata. I am inclined to think an adequate account must do at least the following: distinguish an epistemic bubble from an echo chamber rather than treating the terms as interchangeable; explain why exposure to contrary evidence is widely thought to burst one but not the other; explain why an echo chamber is epistemically harmful in a way that goes beyond the mere absence of exposure to opposing views; and distinguish an echo chamber from an ordinary community of inquiry that shares background assumptions, since otherwise every discipline, tradition, and close-knit group would count as one. For each, give the case or consideration that is supposed to make it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp.

Then evaluate whether the following further candidates belong on the list, with the reason in each case: that an account must explain how contrary evidence can be neutralized or inverted, so that the very plausibility of outsiders is treated as evidence against them; that it must separate the epistemic defect from moral or intellectual criticism of members, so that one can be inside an echo chamber without being stupid, vicious, or culpable; that it must allow for benign or temporary epistemic bubbles; that it must allow that a group can be an echo chamber even around a true or well-evidenced belief; and that it must distinguish echo chambers from mere political polarization, from algorithmic filter bubbles, and from groups with merely high evidentiary standards. For each, say whether it is a real requirement or a confusion, and what it would cost to omit it.

Finally, I want the two structural questions. First, are any subsets of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable — for example, does the requirement that echo chambers be defined by a distinctive mechanism of manufactured distrust conflict with the requirement that membership be evaluable independently of anyone's culpability or bad character? Second, which single requirement does the most work in ruling out inadequate accounts, and why that one rather than the others? Please keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I am less interested in a scorecard of existing theories than in knowing what the standard of evaluation itself should be.
