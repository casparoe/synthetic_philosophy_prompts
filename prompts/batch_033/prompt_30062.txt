As a novelist, I have been rereading Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day with an eye to what it can teach me about writing moral motivation, and I have found myself pulled into a philosophical problem that I would like a careful reading of. Stevens, the narrator and butler, has built his entire life around an ideal he calls dignity: the conviction that a great butler serves his employer with total loyalty, subordinates his own judgment and feelings to that service, and never lets his personal inclinations interfere with the demands of his role. He appears to believe that this self-subordination gives his life moral worth—that he has done his duty, and that doing one's duty is what matters. The novel then quietly dismantles that belief. Lord Darlington, to whom Stevens has given his life, is revealed to have been manipulated by the Nazi regime, and Stevens's refusal to exercise independent judgment extends to carrying out, without protest, the dismissal of two Jewish housemaids. The novel closes with Stevens still holding to some version of his old framework even as he acknowledges that his life may have been misspent.

I would like to read the novel through the lens of philosophical debates about moral worth and acting for the right reasons. On a familiar Kantian picture, an action has genuine moral worth only when it is done from duty rather than from inclination, and Stevens looks at first like a textbook case: he suppresses his own desires, his affections, and even his private judgments, and he acts from what he takes to be duty. Yet the novel seems to convict him anyway, which suggests that the problem is not merely that he served the wrong master, nor merely that his duty was misidentified, but something deeper about what the duty-versus-inclination framework leaves out—perhaps something about the difference between acting from duty and acting from a self-image of dutifulness, or about the way a whole life can be organized around a false conception of what duty demands. What exactly is the novel's implicit theory of moral worth, and how does it track or depart from the philosophical views? Which scenes and decisions carry the most weight for the question, and what does Ishiguro dramatize about moral motivation that the standard philosophical examples miss? I am interested in a reading that takes the novel and the philosophy equally seriously, and that is willing to say where the novel's implicit theory is less coherent than it might appear.
