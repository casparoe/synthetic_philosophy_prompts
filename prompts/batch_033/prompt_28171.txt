Two friends of mine got into an argument about a mutual acquaintance, David, who has been saying for years that he wants to leave his corporate law job to teach high school history. David has a mortgage, two children, and a spouse who works part-time. He says he has no real choice — the pay cut would be too steep, the family depends on him, and that is simply how things are. He is plainly unhappy but insists his hands are tied.

(1) Claire said David is in bad faith. Sartre's central claim is that we treat ourselves as things when it suits us: David is pretending his situation is a brute fact that compels him, when in reality he is freely choosing to stay and then disowning that choice by calling it necessity. He is a lawyer playing at being a trapped man the way Sartre's waiter plays at being a waiter.

(2) Marcus pushed back: that is too quick. Sartre did not say every constrained choice is bad faith — he acknowledged facticity, the givens we do not choose. David did not choose to have a mortgage or dependents; those are real constraints, not self-deception. If bad faith covers everything, it covers nothing.

(3) Claire replied that facticity is precisely what bad faith exploits. The mortgage and the children are real, but what they mean — that he must stay in this job, that no other path is possible — is a meaning he confers and then treats as discovered. He could sell the house, his spouse could work full-time, he could find a cheaper route into teaching. He is not wrong that the constraints exist; he is wrong that they add up to "no choice."

(4) Marcus answered that this proves his point: once you say "he could sell the house, his spouse could work full-time," you have made bad faith so easy to diagnose that anyone who declines to upend their family's life to pursue a preference is guilty of it. Sartre's own examples — the waiter who is too waiter-ish, the woman who ignores the sexual meaning of her date's hand on hers — involve self-deception about identity, not about weighing practical costs. Claire is stretching the concept past its breaking point.

(5) Claire said the structure is identical. In both cases, someone treats a situation they sustain through their own choices as though it were an external fate. David goes to work every morning, bills his hours, cashes the checks — he is actively constituting the very necessity he claims imprisons him. That is the waiter's dance, just in a suit.

They went back and forth a while longer but never resolved it. I want a committed adjudication: who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, are both missing? Please give your adjudication as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
