So I just spent an hour arguing with a friend about whether what you already believe can change what you literally see, not just what you say about it afterward. His line was that perception is modular and informationally encapsulated — beliefs and expectations can't get in, they can only mess with interpretation or memory after the fact. I wasn't convinced, and then I went down a rabbit hole about police lineup proceedures and realized the cops might be on my side, or at least acting like they are.

Here's the practice I can't stop thinking about. A lot of departments now run lineups double-blind: the officer administering the photos doesn't know which one is the suspect, and the witness's confidence is recorded right then, before anyone can talk it up or deflate it. But the very same system later puts the witness in front of a jury and treats the witness saying they're certain it's him as evidence about what the witness saw at the time of the crime. That looks inconsistent to me — either the identification is a clean readout of the witness's actual perception, in which case why all the elaborate shielding, or it's easily pushed around by expectations and feedback, in which case why trust the courtroom version?

So my question is this: what theory of perception (and memory, if they come apart here) does the double-blind lineup practice actually presuppose? Does it commit the criminal justice system to cognitive penetration — the idea that expectations and background beliefs can reach down into perceptual experience itself — or is it fully compatible with encapsulation, with all the contamination happening after perception? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, mark which parts of the practice strictly require what and which parts are just consistent with several views, and then tell me what would have to change in how lineups are run, or in how courts use eyewitness testimony, if you went all-in on encapsulation on one side or all-in on penetrability on the other.
