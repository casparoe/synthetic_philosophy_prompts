I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and last week two friends in my cohort, Maya and Eli, got into it about whether kidney sales should be legalized, and I got drafted as the referee. I couldn't do it. I'm hoping you can act as a philosopher who works on commodification and the ethics of markets, and walk me through the exchange carefully rather than just declaring a winner.

Maya started by saying it should stay illegal because it would exploit the poor — people in desperate financial straits would "choose" to sell a kidney, but really the desperation is doing the choosing. Eli came back that the status quo is worse for exactly those people: the waiting list kills them, and a ban removes one of the few options they have. If the worry is unequal bargaining power, he said, then plenty of jobs poor people take — mining, factory work, military enlistment — are exploitative in the same sense, and we don't ban those. So either she's being inconsistent, or "exploitation" is doing more work than she's spelled out.

Maya said the kidney is different because it's part of your body. There are some things you shouldn't be able to alienate even voluntarily, because selling them changes what the body is — turns it into a stock of parts. The wrong isn't bad terms, it's a change in the meaning of the thing. Eli said that's just repugnance wearing an argument's clothes: people said the same about blood plasma, surrogacy, even life insurance, and the repugnance faded. Every market changes the meaning of what it touches — paying for childcare changes what it means to watch a neighbor's kid — so "it changes the meaning" can't by itself justify a ban. You'd need a non-circular story about which meanings matter and why.

Maya said the story isn't circular. Pricing a kidney crowds out donation and shifts the norms of medicine, and that's an identifiable social mechanism, not a feeling. A kidney market would make giving an organ look like a lost sale, and a body look like a parts inventory. That shift in norms is a real cost even if it doesn't show up in a price. Eli said the crowding-out point cuts the other way here, since donation is already failing — that's why there's a shortage. If the non-market practice can't supply the good, the market isn't corrupting a healthy practice; it's supplementing a failing one. So the expressive cost, if it exists, has to be weighed against actual lives saved, and Maya hasn't shown it's the kind of cost that should override those lives.

Neither of them moved, and they both turned to me. I've gone back and forth on it maybe five times and ended up more confused then when I started.

So please go through the exchange step by step and tell me, for each move, who is right about what and where the argument goes wrong or lands. I'd rather have a point-by-point verdict than a global one. Also say what both of them are missing, and mark clearly which parts of your answer are settled among people who work on this stuff and which parts are genuinely contested — don't present a contested view as the consensus. If it helps, start by brainstorming the distinct objections in play before you commit to a verdict.
