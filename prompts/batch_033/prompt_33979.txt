I've been taking a seminar on persistence and identity this term, and something about the Ship of Theseus has been eating at me in a way that feels disproportionate to a two-thousand-year-old puzzle. I don't mean the puzzle itself—I mean what it seems to imply once you take it seriously and follow it through. I've written up the chain of reasoning that's been keeping me up, and I'd like someone to tell me where it goes wrong, because the conclusion is plainly unacceptable but I can't find the faulty step.

(1) The Ship of Theseus presents us with two candidates for being the original ship—the continuously repaired ship and the ship reassembled from the original planks—and there is no non-arbitrary way to say which one is identical to the original. Both satisfy plausible criteria (spatiotemporal continuity and material composition, respectively), and neither criterion clearly dominates the other.

(2) The same puzzle can be constructed for any composite object whose parts are gradually replaceable. Take any such object, imagine replacing its parts one by one while preserving its form and function, then imagine reassembling the discarded parts into the same arrangement. You now have a fission case with two candidates, and the same problem arises. So the indeterminacy generalizes.

(3) If identity over time is indeterminate for every composite object in fission cases, then identity over time is not a fully objective, determinate relation for composite objects. There is no deep fact of the matter about whether a composite object persists through change—at best, it's a matter of convention or pragmatic interest which candidate we call the original.

(4) Human persons are composite objects. (If you think persons are immaterial souls, set that aside—I'm assuming the naturalistic view that we are, at minimum, embodied beings whose physical parts turn over.)

(5) Therefore, there is no deep fact of the matter about whether I persist through time. Whether the person writing this now is identical to the person who will wake up in my bed tomorrow is, at bottom, indeterminate or conventional.

(6) But that conclusion is absurd. I have a special, non-transferable concern for my own future welfare—I save for retirement, I dread future pain in a way I don't dread the pain of strangers, and I take myself to be the same person who made promises last year and is responsible for keeping them. If (5) is true, all of this rests on a mistake.

(7) So one of (1) through (6) must be false. Where does this argument go wrong? The obvious candidates are (2)—maybe persons are different from ships in a way that blocks the generalization—and (3)—maybe indeterminacy in exotic fission cases doesn't undermine determinacy in ordinary cases. But I'm not sure either response works. If you reject (2), you need to explain what feature of persons blocks the generalization without begging the question or retreating to a non-naturalistic metaphysics. If you reject (3), you need to explain how identity can be perfectly determinate in ordinary cases while going indeterminate the moment we imagine a fission scenario—which seems to make identity depend on what merely possible events we happen to be considering. I want to know which step is the weakest, what it costs to reject it, and what the alternative positions pay to keep the conclusion at bay.
