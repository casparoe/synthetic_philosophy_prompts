A colleague and I have been arguing about whether rationality ever permits tie-breaking, and I have ended up with an argument that seems to prove far too much; I cannot find the step where it goes wrong.

(1) An action is rationally permissible only if the agent does it for a reason that favors doing it.

(2) A reason can favor doing an action only by favoring it over its alternatives: to have a reason to take the left bale rather than the right, you need a consideration that supports taking the left bale in a way it does not support taking the right — not merely a consideration in favor of eating.

(3) In the traditional Buridan's ass case — an animal exactly midway between two qualitatively identical bales of hay, with no relevant asymmetry — nothing supports the left bale in a way it does not support the right, and nothing supports the right bale in a way it does not support the left.

(4) So, from (1) and (2), neither taking the left bale nor taking the right bale is rationally permissible.

(5) Remaining in place is not rationally permissible either: by (2), that would require a consideration that supports starving in a way it does not support eating, and the animal's hunger supports eating over starving.

(6) Therefore every option available to the animal is rationally impermissible.

(7) If (6) is right, rationality would leave any agent facing a choice among equally supported options with no permissible option at all — a normative deadlock that applies not only to donkeys but to a shopper choosing between identical cans.

(8) But that consequence is hard to accept: a hungry animal standing between two identical piles of food is surely permitted to eat from one, and a theory of practical rationality that denies this has refuted itself.

(9) So at least one of (1), (2), and (3) must be false.

I would most like to keep (1) and (3): (1) because I want rationality to be about acting for reasons, and (3) because it seems to do nothing but report the symmetry of the case. That points to (2), but I cannot see how to deny (2) without conceding that the ass's taking the left bale is not really rational, only something rationality is silent about. Which premise should be rejected, and what is the full price of rejecting it? If (2) is the one to give up, spell out what a non-comparative or merely permissive notion of acting for a reason would look like, and say whether the ass's choice comes out rational or merely not irrational. If (1) or (3) must go instead, say which, and whether the resulting view can still explain why the ass ought not to starve.
