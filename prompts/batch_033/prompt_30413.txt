A colleague passed along the following summary of a view about our growing reliance on AI:

"Once an AI can answer your questions reliably, you stop working through the reasoning yourself. But understanding is not the same as having the right answer; it is grasping why the answer follows. So each time you let the AI do the thinking, you lose a little of your ability to think, and the skill atrophies like an unused muscle. That is why AI is different from books or calculators: those tools still made you do the work, but AI does the work for you. The upshot is that a society that depends on AI for diagnosis, legal judgment, and research is outsourcing its mind, and will eventually no longer understand the things it claims to know."

I find the conclusion intuitively alarming, but I suspect the summary runs together several distinct claims. I want an accuracy audit of this summary, going sentence by sentence. For each sentence, tell me what a careful philosopher working on epistemic dependence, cognitive offloading, and the erosion of understanding would accept, what they would call an overstatement or a conflation, and what they would reject outright. In particular, keep apart the empirical claim that reliance causes skill fade, the conceptual claim that understanding is distinct from merely having the right answer, and the collective-epistemology claim about what a society understands. Then say whether the final claim about a society "no longer understanding what it claims to know" follows from the parts that survive your audit, and how a defender of the erosion-of-understanding worry would amend the summary to make it defensible. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
