I'm taking a course this semester on fate, freedom, and foreknowledge, and we just finished the sea-battle chapter of Aristotle's On Interpretation, where he worries that if every assertion about the future is already true or already false, the future looks fixed whether we like it or not. Around the same time I've been rereading Macbeth, and I can't shake the sense that the play is doing philosophy about exactly this, but in a register the seminar's logical formulations don't quite capture. I'm planning a final paper that reads the witches' prophecies through the future-contingents problem, and I want to do it in a way that's fair to both the play and the philosophy rather than treating Macbeth as a tidy illustration of a doctrine.

The place I keep getting stuck is the status of the prophecies. When the witches tell Macbeth he will be king, is the play presenting that future as already settled, the way the sea-battle sentence is supposed to be true or false in advance, or is the prophecy more like an intervention that helps bring about the very thing it announces? Macbeth does not sit back and wait; he murders Duncan, and the play seems interested in the difference between a future's being known, being caused, and being fated. The later prophecies about Birnam Wood coming to Dunsinane and about Macbeth's safety from anyone born of woman raise a related problem about equivocation: they come true in ways no one anticipated, which makes me wonder whether Shakespeare is dramatizing the idea that the future is determinate only under some description, or whether he is showing that the words of a prophecy do not pin down a unique future at all. There is also the speech near the end where Macbeth reflects on tomorrow and the days after it as a sequence of empty moments, which reads to me as a kind of fatalism arrived at too late. I would like you to work through the play with the sea-battle debate in view and tell me where the framework genuinely illuminates it and where forcing it would distort the play.

I am less interested in a verdict on Aristotle's actual position, though you should be accurate about the standard options in the debate, than in a reading of what the play dramatizes. If you think the whole premise is confused, for instance because the sea-battle problem concerns truth while Macbeth is about action, or because the play uses prophecy as a dramatic device rather than making a metaphysical claim, please say so plainly instead of forcing the reading. Also flag the places where your interpretation depends on a contested reading of either the play or the philosophy, and how confident you are in those moves.
