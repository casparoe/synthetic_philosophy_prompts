**Question**

Explain Aumann's agreement theorem. What conditions must hold for the theorem to apply, and what exactly does it rule out? Does the theorem show that Bayesian rationality alone is sufficient to preclude persistent disagreement between agents who communicate their views?

**Student's Answer**

Aumann's agreement theorem, proved by Robert Aumann in his 1976 paper "Agreeing to Disagree", is a formal result in Bayesian epistemology. It states that two agents who share a common prior probability distribution cannot rationally "agree to disagree" — that is, if their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, those posteriors must be equal.

The theorem requires three conditions. First, both agents must be Bayesian rational: they update their beliefs by conditionalising on the evidence they receive. Second, they must share a common prior probability distribution over the relevant set of possibilities. Third, they must have common knowledge of each other's rationality — meaning that each agent knows the other is rational, each knows the other knows, and so on ad infinitum. This common knowledge condition is what gives the theorem its force, because it ensures that neither agent can dismiss the other's posterior as the product of irrationality.

What the theorem rules out is the possibility of "agreeing to disagree" — a situation in which two rational agents, having exchanged their views, continue to hold different posterior probabilities for the same event while knowing each other's posteriors. The theorem shows that, under the stated conditions, such a situation is impossible. If the agents' posteriors differ, at least one of the conditions must have failed: either they did not share a common prior, or their posteriors were not in fact common knowledge, or one of them is not Bayesian rational.

As to whether Bayesian rationality alone precludes persistent disagreement: the theorem comes close to showing this, but the common prior assumption does some of the work. In practice, if two rational agents communicate their beliefs honestly and repeatedly update in light of each other's views, the theorem suggests they should converge even if they started from different priors, because the process of updating on each other's posteriors should wash out the initial differences over time. So Bayesian rationality, together with open communication, does largely preclude persistent disagreement, even if the common prior condition is not strictly met in real-world cases.
