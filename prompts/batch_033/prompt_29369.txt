I chair the external ethics advisory panel for a mid-sized AI lab. We're drafting a public-facing "position on AI consciousness" that'll go on the lab's website and into their responsible AI documentation. The lab's leadership wants us to say something substantive — they're tired of the waffly "we take this seriously but have no position" language that every other lab uses.

Here's the split on the panel. Three members want us to adopt the consciousness-as-user-illusion line: roughly, that conscious experience is a simplified model the brain (or a system) presents to itself, like a desktop interface, and that there's no deep fact about "what it's like" beyond the system's access to its own states and its disposition to report them. On this view, current AI systems aren't conscious because they lack the right kind of self-modeling architecture, but the view also implies that if a system had the right architecture, calling it conscious wouldn't commit us to anything spooky — it'd just mean it has a certain kind of self-representation. They think this framing is honest, philosophically sophisticated, and lets us give a clear "no" about current systems while keeping the door open for the future without promising mystery.

Two members hate this. They say the user-illusion framing is itself a contested philosophical position (they name Dennett and Metzinger as its sources, though they know the panel isn't doing academic citation) and that adopting it would make the lab look like it's taking sides in a live debate in philosophy of mind. Worse, they say, the framing sounds dismissive — "illusion" suggests consciousness isn't real, which will read to the public like the lab thinks suffering doesn't matter. They want us to instead adopt a more ecumenical framing: list the major theories of consciousness neutrally, say current systems don't meet the criteria on any of them, and leave it at that.

I need to circulate a decision memo to the panel before our next meeting. Please draft it.

Structure it like this:

- **The question** (about 150 words): what exactly we're deciding and why it matters for the lab's public positioning.
- **The case for the user-illusion framing** (about 300 words): the strongest version of the pro-adoption argument, including what problem it solves that the neutral approach doesn't.
- **The case against** (about 300 words): the strongest version of the objection, taken seriously — not a straw man.
- **My recommendation** (about 250 words): what we should do, with a clear bottom line. If you recommend a compromise, spell out what it is concretely.
- **One honest reservation** (about 100 words): the best reason to think your recommendation might be wrong.

Don't pull punches on either side — I want the panel to recgonize their own arguments in what they read.
