Question: "Explain Russell's theory of descriptions, using the sentence 'The present King of France is bald.' What truth-value does Russell assign to this sentence, and how does his theory handle the negation of the sentence?"

Student answer:

Russell's theory says a definite description like "the present King of France" isn't a referring expression the way a proper name is. The sentence "The present King of France is bald" should really be analyzed as: there is at least one present King of France, there is at most one present King of France, and that individual is bald. In symbols: ∃x(Kx ∧ ∀y(Ky → y=x) ∧ Bx). Since there is no present King of France, the first conjunct Kx is false, so the whole sentence is false. Russell therefore says the sentence is meaningful but false, not meaningless. For the negation, "The present King of France is not bald" is ambiguous. On one reading it denies the whole analysis and comes out true; on the other reading it says the unique king exists and is not bald, and comes out false. Russell called this the difference between primary and secondary occurrence of the description. So for Russell, a sentence can be meaningful even though its subject term fails to refer.
