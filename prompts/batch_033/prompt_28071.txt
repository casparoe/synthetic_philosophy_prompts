I teach a 75-minute session on manipulation versus legitimate persuasion to about 18 upper-level undergraduates in a course on the ethics of influence. This recently became personal: I left a workplace where a manager was extraordinarily good at getting people to take on work they later resented, and months later I still cannot settle whether he manipulated us or simply persuaded us skillfully. I want my students to encounter that same discomfort — the genuine difficulty of the distinction, not just the textbook cases with overt lies or threats.

Design an in-class activity — a role-play, a structured deliberation with constraints, a game with incentives — where students try to influence each other and then must judge whether what they or others did was manipulation or legitimate persuasion. The key is that the cases that arise should be genuinely ambiguous: framing effects, selective disclosure, appeals to identity, exploiting emotional vulnerabilities the persuader did not create but is clearly using. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the main theoretical pressure points: whether manipulation requires an intention to manipulate, whether it is defined by the means used or by its effect on the target's autonomy, and whether the notion of legitimate persuasion can survive the observation that nearly all persuasion bypasses rational deliberation to some degree.

Give me rules, materials (ideally only printed handouts), a timeline for the 75 minutes, and an honest assessment of what predictably goes wrong — politeness suppressing the hard cases, students treating it as a game to win rather than a situation to reflect on, or concluding too quickly that it is all manipulation. Also tell me which feature of the distinction the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion.
