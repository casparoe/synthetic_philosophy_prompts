I teach a graduate seminar on the concept of liberty, and we have spent several weeks working through the familiar trichotomy: negative liberty as non-interference, positive liberty as self-mastery or self-realization, and republican liberty as non-domination. My students have gotten good at analyzing the textbook cases where these come apart — the benevolent dictator (negative says free, republican says unfree), the addict (negative says free, positive says unfree), the contented slave who has internalized his condition (republican says unfree, negative says free if no one is actually beating him). But a pattern has started to bother them, and it bothers me too: in nearly every standard case, republican liberty looks like the most demanding conception. It finds unfreedom in places the other two do not. The students are beginning to suspect that this is a contingent feature of the examples we happen to reach for, not something built into the concepts themselves, and I want to give them a case that breaks the pattern cleanly.

I would like you to construct a thought experiment that meets the following specifications. The case should be one in which **positive liberty is violated while both negative liberty and republican liberty are fully respected** — so positive liberty turns out to be the most demanding of the three, reversing the usual ordering. The scenario must be set in a realistic institutional context (a university, a company, a professional association, a residential community — no political fables, no science fiction, no exotic psychology). The positive-liberty violation must arise from the agent's external situation, not from an internal defect like addiction, compulsion, or weakness of will; the agent should be a psychologically normal adult by any reasonable standard. And the case must not be rigged by making the institution obviously sinister or oppressive — on the contrary, it should look like a well-functioning institution by the lights of ordinary common sense, perhaps even an admirable one.

After you present the scenario, walk through what each conception of liberty says about the agent's situation and why, making the reasoning explicit for each. Then identify which specific feature of the case is doing the work to make positive liberty peel off from the other two, and explain what would happen to the verdicts if you removed that feature — would the case collapse back into one of the standard alignments? Finally, tell me how a defender of the negative-liberty or republican-liberty tradition would push back against your analysis. Would they argue that the case actually does involve interference or domination after all, once we look more carefully? Or would they concede the verdicts but argue that the positive-liberty theorist is not tracking freedom at all — that the agent really is free and the positive-liberty tradition has confused freedom with something else, like flourishing or authenticity? I want the pushback to be the best version of the objection, not a strawman.
