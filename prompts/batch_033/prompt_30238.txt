I'm trying to figure out what principle I actually hold about when a consideration counts as a reason for an attitude, versus a reason to try to bring about the attitude. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases:

- An evil demon says he'll torture me unless I admire him for his cruelty. I think I have a reason to make myself admire him, but I don't think the threat is itself a reason to admire him.
- A billionaire offers me a million dollars to believe that the moon is made of cheese. Same thing: reason to acquire the belief, not a reason for the belief.
- My friend risked his life to save a stranger. That fact, all by itself, strikes me as a reason to admire him.
- The testimony of three reliable witnesses that the defendant was at the scene: that's a reason to believe he was there.
- Now the tricky ones. Suppose a demon threatens to torture me unless I desire a saucer of mud. Intuitively, this feels different from the admiration case—somehow the threat does seem like a reason to desire the mud. But I can't tell if I'm just confusing "reason to desire" with "reason to make myself desire."
- A pill will make me admire a total jerk, and admiring him will make my life go better in no other way—it just feels nice to admire people, even jerks. I think I have a reason to take the pill, but I don't think I have a reason to admire the jerk. Yet if I don't take it, I can't shake the feeling I'm being irrational.
- Someone I trust offers me a large sum to be angry at a third party who did nothing wrong. I don't think the money is a reason to be angry. But I do feel a pull in a way I don't with the belief cases.

What I want to know: is there a unified principle that gets all of these right, or at least most of them? If there is, what does it predict about a case where a demon threatens to torture me unless I believe a proposition for which I have strong evidence? And are there any of my verdicts that the best principle just has to overturn? I'd rather know what I should think than have all my intuitions preserved.
