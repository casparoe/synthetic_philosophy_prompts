I've been reading about the rules around embryo screening and something about the way the lines are drawn has been bugging me. If you're going through IVF and you opt for PGD, you can screen out embryos with Tay-Sachs or Huntington's or cystic fibrosis and nobody blinks — in fact, in a lot of places the standard of care basically expects you to. But if you want to select for sex, and there's no sex-linked disease involved, that's actually illegal in Canada, the UK, and a bunch of other countries, and even where it's technically legal (like parts of the US) the clinics often won't do it and the bioethicists come after you. And if you wanted to screen for something like predicted IQ or height, assuming polygenic scores ever got good enough, the reaction is basically "that's eugenics and we don't do that." So there's a line between the medical and the non-medical, and then within the non-medical there's another line between the merely disfavored and the outright unthinkable.

What I can't get straight is what theory of what parents owe their future children — or what theory of the value of a child's life — could actually underwrite these distinctions. It seems like the practice presupposes that parents have a duty to prevent serious suffering but no right to pursue a particular vision of flourishing. But that can't be the whole story, because the practice also treats certain conditions (deafness, for instance) as harms-to-be-prevented even when some deaf parents argue that deafness isn't a harm but a culture, and the medical mainstream mostly dismisses that framing. So the practice isn't just about preventing suffering; it's smuggling in a substantive view about which lives are worse than others. And the sex-selection ban is weird too — if the worry is about sexism, why is it okay to have a second child naturally because you want a girl after having three boys, but not okay to select an embryo for the same reason? The practice seems to care about the method more than the motive, or maybe it cares about the motive but only when a certain technology is involved.

I want a reconstruction of the implicit philosophical commitments the current PGD consensus actually rests on. Are they consistent? Would the practice collapse if you swapped in pure procreative beneficence — the view that parents are obligated to pick the embryo with the best expected life — or pure reproductive autonomy, where parents just get to choose whatever they want? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theories could license it and the choice between them is being made on some other basis entirely?
