I'm a science journalist who covers consciousness research and the philosophy of mind, and I'm nearing the end of a long-form essay — soon to be a book chapter — arguing that the paradox of phenomenal judgment remains a live, unresolved problem for any theory on which consciousness is causally inert, and that the three standard replies in the literature don't close the gap as cleanly as their proponents claim. Next month I'm presenting a condensed version of the argument at a salon-style event hosted by a university's Center for Mind, Brain, and Consciousness. The audience: two philosophers of mind (one leans panpsychist, the other illusionist), a cognitive neuroscientist who works on metacognition and confidence judgments, a philosophically sophisticated AI researcher interested in machine consciousness, and about fifteen graduate students split between philosophy and neuroscience. I have thirty minutes to present, then forty-five minutes of Q&A.

You are a professor of philosophy who specializes in philosophy of mind, has chaired dozens of Q&A sessions at conferences, and knows the sociology of this field — who is committed to what, who bristles at which arguments, and where the real fault lines run. Your task is to prepare me for the objections I will actually get from this specific audience.

Before writing the final prep sheet, take time to think through the intellectual positions in the room: what each person's prior commitments are, what they are motivated to protect, which moves they would find natural, and which moves they would find illegible or beside the point. Then produce a prep sheet with the following structure:

- Eight to ten objections, ordered roughly by likelihood-times-damage — a predictable but weak objection can come before a devastating one that only one person might formulate. Phrase each objection as someone in this audience would actually say it, capturing their vocabulary, framing, and intellectual temperament.
- For each objection, provide a two-minute spoken reply plus a candid note on where the reply is softest, what I'm hoping they won't press, and whether I should concede any ground.
- Then, separately, give me the one question I'm silently praying no one asks. Explain what makes it the dangerous one.
- End with a short list of points I should concede up front during the talk itself to defuse the most predictable objections before the Q&A even begins.

The thesis I'm defending, in brief: the paradox of phenomenal judgment — roughly, if consciousness is epiphenomenal, then our judgments about our own consciousness cannot be caused by consciousness itself, and we have no reason to think those judgments track the truth — is not adequately answered by (a) the common-cause reply (third-factor physical processes cause both consciousness and our judgments about it, so correlation is secured without epiphenomenal causation), (b) deflationary accounts of what a "judgment" is that redescribe it in purely functional or dispositional terms, or (c) the claim that the paradox rests on a Cartesian theater picture we should have already abandoned. I argue that each of these replies either smuggles in the very causal efficacy it claims to do without, or else renders the reliability of our phenomenal judgments a massive coincidence — a coincidence we have no independent reason to accept.
