I have been reconstructing an argument that appears to show that Nāgārjuna's dialectical method, consistently applied, refutes every assertion, including its own. I am not willing to accept that conclusion, because the method's local applications — against motion, against the self, against causation — seem to me to be doing genuine critical work. But I have not been able to locate the precise point at which the argument overreaches, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey of interpretations.

Here is the argument.

(1) The Madhyamaka procedure is as follows. When a concept is put forward as capturing how things are in themselves — as having svabhāva, or intrinsic nature — one sets out the four alternatives of the catuṣkoṭi, the tetralemma: the thing is, the thing is not, the thing both is and is not, the thing neither is nor is not. Each alternative is shown to collapse, whether by contradiction, by regress, or by covertly presupposing the very intrinsic nature it was meant to secure.

(2) The procedure is not merely a piecemeal refutation of particular views. Its advertised result is general: nothing has svabhāva; all things are empty (śūnya). This is the doctrine of emptiness (śūnyatā).

(3) The procedure is presented as fully general, so it should apply to the concept of emptiness itself. Set out the four alternatives for emptiness: emptiness is a real feature of things, existing in its own right; emptiness is nothing at all; emptiness both exists and does not exist; emptiness neither exists nor does not exist. Each alternative can be reduced to absurdity by the same style of argument used against motion or the self.

(4) Emptiness, then, cannot be said to exist, not to exist, both, or neither. But if that is so, then the thesis that all things are empty cannot be asserted as a claim about how things are; any assertion of it would have to take up one of the rejected alternatives.

(5) Yet Madhyamaka is nothing other than the assertion that all things are empty. So the method refutes the very doctrine it was introduced to establish. And since the method is general, the same fate befalls every assertion, including the ordinary claims — about pots, roads, and debts — that the dialectician continues to make while arguing.

(6) That conclusion is unacceptable, since the dialectic itself consists of assertions, inferences, and distinctions between better and worse positions; if no assertion is assertable, the method cannot even state its own results.

So the argument seems to prove far too much. I am aware of the standard reply that the Mādhyamika advances no thesis of his own and that emptiness is only a conventional designation rather than a view, but that reply has always struck me as a restatement of the problem in more elegant terms, not a solution to it. I would like to keep the local negative results, and I would like to avoid both self-refutation and the conclusion that ordinary discourse is impossible. If I cannot have all of that, I want to know exactly what I am paying. Which step should be rejected, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it commit one to?
