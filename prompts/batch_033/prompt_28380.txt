I'm a novelist, and my current project has a protagonist who's self-deceived about the real reasons she ended her marriage — she tells herself it was about his career choices but the reader slowly realizes it was something else entirely. I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about the paradox of self-deception: how can you simultaneously know the truth and believe the lie? The standard story is that deception requires an asymmetry — the deceiver knows p and gets the deceived to believe not-p — but when you're both people, the whole thing looks impossible. I've come across the partitioning views (different mental subsystems), the temporal shift views (you believe p at t1, form the false belief at t2, and somehow manage to keep both), and the Sartrean bad-faith stuff where it's not quite belief-level deception. None of these fully satisfy me but they've been generative for thinking about my character.

Anyway, here's what I'd love: a villanelle about self-deception. The form seems almost too perfect — those two refrains that keep circling back, never quite resolving until they're forced together in the final couplet, which is exactly the structure of the self-deceived mind. I want one refrain to be the voice of the part that knows the truth, and the other to be the rationalization or the false belief. The poem should capture the philosophical tension — not just name-drop the paradox but actually enact it through the form. Someone who's read Davidson or Mele on self-deception should recognize the structure of the problem in how the refrains interact, how each return of a line feels slightly different because of what's been said in between. And the final couplet where the two lines meet should do something interesting — maybe the moment where the self-deception collapses under its own weight, or maybe the moment where it's sustained despite everything. I'm not sure which is more honest to the phenomenon, honestly. I keep going back and forth, which i suppose is the point.
