I am putting together a short interdisciplinary unit on free will for an adult-education seminar, and I want to pair a philosophical text with Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead. The philosophical frame I have in mind is Daniel Dennett's Elbow Room: The Varieties of Free Will Worth Wanting, in which he argues that the freedom worth caring about is not the ability to act in a way that is uncaused or undetermined by one's past, but rather the cluster of capacities we actually value: the ability to deliberate, to act on reasons one can endorse, to be moved by persuasion rather than force, and to escape manipulation and coercion. The topic has become personally pressing for me over the past year, because I have been watching a parent lose exactly those capacities to dementia, and it has made me take Dennett's question—which variety of freedom is worth wanting, and what we lose when it goes—far more literally than I used to.

Against that background, I would like a serious critical reading of Stoppard's play through this lens, one that does not reduce the play to an illustration. The play seems to literalize determinism: the two courtiers are trapped inside a script they did not write, their deaths are fixed before the action begins, and the opening run of coin-flip heads already suggests that something has gone wrong with ordinary notions of chance and choice. Yet within that script they worry, argue, form plans, interpret their situation, and make small choices. My question is whether the play ultimately vindicates or subverts the Dennettian thought that the freedom worth wanting is compatible with a world in which outcomes are fixed and one's role is not of one's choosing. Does the play show that Rosencrantz and Guildenstern retain something like the freedom worth wanting—genuine deliberation, responsiveness to reasons, resistance to being mere characters—or does it show that once the script is authored by someone else, such freedom is revealed as a comforting illusion? Where does the play resist the compatibilist reading, and where does the compatibilist reading illuminate moments that might otherwise seem merely comic or pathetic?

I would also like you to weigh the Player's perspective. He seems to hold that being inside a part, with a known exit, is not a loss of agency but its condition—that the freedom worth wanting is precisely the ability to play one's part well rather than to rewrite the play. Is that a version of Dennett's view, or does it trade on a metaphor that Dennett would reject? And what would a Dennettian response be to the darker implication that the script is written by someone who does not much care about the characters?

I would like a reading that takes both the play and the philosophy seriously, and that is willing to say where the analogy between being scripted and being caused breaks down. Close paraphrase of scenes is preferable to verbatim quotation, since I care about the argument rather than the exact wording.
