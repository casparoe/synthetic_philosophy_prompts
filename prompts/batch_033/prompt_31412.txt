I'm drafting a novel set on a small island that's about to vote on whether to break off and govern itself, and the subplot I keep getting stuck on is the electoral commission deciding who belongs on the voter roll. I don't want the commission to feel like its just doing whatever the plot needs, so I've been sketching a little record of their earlier rulings. So far they've ruled that a noncitizen who has lived on the island for nine years and works at the ferry terminal can vote; that a native-born citizen who moved to the mainland thirty years ago and hasn't been back since cannot vote; and that a citizen who moved to the mainland for an eighteen-month job but kept her house on the island and comes back most weekends can vote.

The case they're about to hear is a man who lives in the mainland town across the channel but has worked at the island's cannery for over a decade, pays the island's income tax, and sends his kids to the island's school. If the commission wants to stay consistent with its own record, should it put him on the roll? I'm less interested in the bare yes or no than in the candidate rules that fit the earlier cases, which of the earlier rulings is the closest analog, and what principle the commission would be committing to either way. And if the record genuinely underdetermines it, what future case would best expose the difference between the rules?
