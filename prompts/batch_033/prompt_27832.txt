I have no formal training in philosophy, but a question about perception has been turning in my mind for some time, and I have settled into a view that I would like someone to try to break. The question is Molyneux's, more or less as I have since learned it was originally put: suppose a person blind from birth has learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch alone, reliably and without hesitation. If their sight were restored all at once, and the two objects were set before them on a table, would they know which was which by looking, before being allowed to touch? I have come to think the answer is no. My reason is that vision and touch seem to me to present the world in ways different enough that there is no automatic bridge between them. Sight delivers a flat, colored, shadowed scene from a particular angle; touch delivers solidity, edges, and resistance through time. I doubt the visual world would arrive already labeled with the geometry the hands know. I do not mean only that the person might hesitate out of unfamiliarity; I mean the capacity to connect a seen shape with a felt shape is not there until experience builds it.

I know that thoughtful people have gone the other way, holding that shape is shape, and that someone who genuinely understands cube through touch has already grasped the very same spatial fact that sight would reveal, so recognition should follow at once. But I am not asking for a survey of the old debate. I want to know what would count against my view. Which observations, possible cases, arguments, or results should genuinely move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward? Which of them would a defender of my position have to concede puts real pressure on it, rather than something to be dismissed or explained away? And please say, at the end, what would change your own assessment: which single consideration, if it turned out otherwise, would most push you toward the opposite verdict, and where you think your reasoning about this is least secure.
