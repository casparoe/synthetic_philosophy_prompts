As a novelist, I have spent a good deal of time watching my sentences move from manuscript to galley to finished book, and I have become puzzled by something in the practices that surround the printed letter. Consider the lowercase 'a'. A type designer draws a new one, and it is expected to be immediately recognizable as an 'a' and yet distinct enough from every previous 'a' to count as a new design; a publisher can set the same text in a dozen different faces and no one supposes the letters themselves have changed; an optical character recognition system can be trained on thousands of labeled examples and then identify 'a's in hands and fonts it has never seen. These practices seem to presuppose that there is something every 'a' shares — a fixed thing of which all the marks are only variations. But the surrounding institutions are not consistent about that. The designer can claim rights in the particular drawing, while the letter 'a' itself remains common property; a schoolchild who drifts too far from the form of 'a' is corrected, while an adult designer who drifts just as far may be praised for style. What view of what makes every 'a' an 'a' does this cluster of practices presuppose? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed: which rival views it quietly rules out, whether its parts presuppose consistent things, where it is philosophically underdetermined, and what account of style it commits us to. And if the presupposed view were replaced — say, by the view that 'a' is fixed only by overlapping similarities, or only by a community's habits of recognition — what in these practices would have to change?
