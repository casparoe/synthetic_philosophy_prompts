I am a novelist by trade, and for the past year I have been reading Hannah Arendt in the evenings — not for any professional reason, but because I am trying to understand how a person comes to do monstrous things without the subjective experience of being a monster. I have read *Eichmann in Jerusalem* twice now, and I find myself stuck on the concept of the "banality of evil," which Arendt proposes as a kind of diagnosis of Eichmann's particular evil, but which I suspect she also meant as a broader claim about how certain evils become possible at all.

My problem is this: I am a novelist, not a political theorist, and the vocabulary I have for thinking about character, motivation, and action is the vocabulary I use to build fictional persons — interiority, self-narration, the stories people tell themselves about who they are and what they are doing, the gap between a character's self-conception and what the reader can see, the way a person can be sincerely unaware of their own motives because the available narrative templates don't include them. When Arendt says that Eichmann's evil was not rooted in deep ideological conviction or sadistic impulse but in something like thoughtlessness — an inability to think from the standpoint of another, a reliance on clichés and bureaucratic language that insulated him from the reality of what he was doing — I keep wanting to translate this into the novelist's question: what kind of character is this, and what is happening in his interior life (or failing to happen there)?

So I would like you to do the following. Restate Arendt's argument about Eichmann and the banality of evil — not the whole of *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, but the core claim about the nature of his evil and the kind of incapacity it involved — in the vocabulary a novelist uses to think about character: self-narration, interiority, self-deception, the stories one tells oneself, the relationship between language and moral perception, the question of whether a person can be genuinely "shallow" in the way Arendt seems to suggest. Translate as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. Which parts of Arendt's claim map cleanly onto the novelist's categories, and which parts resist translation? Are there aspects of what she is trying to say that the novelist's vocabulary simply cannot capture, and if so, does that point to a limitation in the novelist's framework — something it is structurally unable to see — or does it point to something unstable in Arendt's own concept? I am less interested in whether Arendt was right about Eichmann as a historical matter than in whether the idea of banality of evil is coherent once you try to cash it out in the terms someone like me actually uses to think about why people do what they do.
