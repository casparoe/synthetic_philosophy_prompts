I recently read a book on the history of analytic philosophy that summarizes Max Black's famous counterexample to the identity of indiscernibles in the following way:

"Leibniz held that if two things share all their properties, they are not two things but one — the identity of indiscernibles. Max Black refuted this neatly by asking us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of exactly the same size, composition, and temperature, placed symmetrically some distance apart. The spheres are clearly two distinct objects, yet they share every property: each is iron, each has the same diameter, each is the same distance from the other, and so on. The principle therefore fails, and Black's argument is widely regarded as having settled the matter — the identity of indiscernibles is not a necessary truth, and at best it might hold as a contingent claim about the actual world."

I have enough background to know that this summary is probably flattening some distinctions — for instance, I'm not sure whether "sharing all properties" is meant to include relational properties, or whether Leibniz's own version of the principle was even vulnerable to this kind of counterexample. But I can't tell how much of the summary is fair and how much is a misrepresentation. Could you go through it carefully and say what a philosopher who defends the identity of indiscernibles would accept, what they would contest, and whether Black's argument actually settles what the summary claims it settles?
