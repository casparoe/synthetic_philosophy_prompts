You are a moral and political philosopher with expertise in welfare economics and the measurement of well-being, acting as an advisor to a health-policy analyst. Please think carefully and begin by distinguishing the possible positions before committing to one.

I work for the public body that decides which medical treatments our health system will fund. Our method is cost-effectiveness analysis using quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). For each treatment, we estimate how many additional years of life it would give a patient, multiply each year by a quality weight between 0 and 1 (1 is full health, 0 is death), add the resulting QALYs across everyone affected, divide by the treatment's cost, and rank treatments by cost per QALY. We fund down the list until the budget runs out.

What unsettles me is the step where we add QALYs across different people. In practice we treat a QALY gained by one patient as exactly interchangeable with a QALY gained by any other, so a treatment that gives 0.1 QALYs each to ten thousand people with mild symptoms can outrank a treatment that gives 20 QALYs to a single severely ill person, and we fund the first and not the second. That seems justified only if one person's well-being can be placed on the same scale as another's, so that gains and losses across people can literally be summed and compared. Yet our own survey data show that the quality weight for the very same health state varies widely between respondents — healthy people tend to rate a condition much worse than people living with it rate it — and we simply take one group's ratings and use them as if they were everyone's.

I want to understand what philosophical commitments this practice rests on. Specifically: (1) What exactly does cost-per-QALY allocation presuppose about whether, and in what sense, one person's well-being can be compared with another's? Does it strictly require a cardinal common scale on which differences and sums of well-being are meaningful, or would an ordinal ranking, or something weaker, suffice? (2) Which rival views about the measurement and comparison of well-being does the practice quietly rule out, and is it genuinely incompatible with them — or is the practice underdetermined, so that several such views would license the same allocation decisions? (3) Is the practice coherent given that the weights on which everything rests come from one group's preferences rather than from a neutral common scale? (4) If we concluded that principled interpersonal comparisons cannot be made, or can be made only as rough conventional judgments, what would a health-allocation system have to look like instead — which decisions could we no longer justify, and what alternative rules might remain?
