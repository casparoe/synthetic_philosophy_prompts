I have been turning over an argument that seems to prove that the notion of higher-order evidence is incoherent, and I cannot find the exit. It runs as follows.

(1) Suppose you examine a body of first-order evidence E and correctly judge that it strongly supports proposition P — say, you assign P a credence of 0.9.

(2) You then acquire higher-order evidence H: you learn that you are severely sleep-deprived, and you know that sleep-deprived people commit reasoning errors at a substantially elevated rate. H does not itself bear on the truth of P; it bears only on your reliability in evaluating E.

(3) It seems obvious that H should make you less confident in P. Someone who said "I know I am sleep-deprived and error-prone, but I am sticking with 0.9" would strike us as dogmatic and irrational.

(4) But notice: E itself has not changed. The first-order evidence still supports P to exactly the same degree it did before. H gives you no new information about P — only about you.

(5) So after receiving H, you find yourself in the following position: your first-order evidence supports P to degree 0.9, yet you now believe P to a lower degree, say 0.7.

(6) That is epistemic akrasia: you hold a doxastic state (credence 0.7 in P) that diverges from what you yourself take your evidence to support (credence 0.9 in P). And epistemic akrasia is widely regarded as irrational — you are, by your own lights, believing against your evidence.

(7) But the only alternative — refusing to adjust your credence in response to H — is also irrational, since it amounts to ignoring relevant evidence about your own cognitive condition.

(8) So rationality demands that you both adjust and not adjust. That is a contradiction.

(9) Therefore, the concept of higher-order evidence must be confused. Either H is simply more first-order evidence (in which case we should stop treating higher-order evidence as a distinct category), or H is not evidence at all and should be ignored.

I find the conclusion hard to accept — it seems obvious that learning you are impaired should affect your beliefs — but I cannot locate the misstep. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
