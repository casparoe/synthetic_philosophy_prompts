I've been sitting in on a colleague's graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and she has been working through a series of cases concerning the appropriateness of resentment—one of Strawson's reactive attitudes. Her approach is to elicit verdicts case by case and then extract the principles that best systematize them. She has been admirably consistent. Here are her verdicts on the cases discussed so far:

1. A driver who swerves to avoid a bee and hits a pedestrian: resentment not appropriate.
2. A colleague who fabricates a damaging story about you to secure a promotion, after calmly weighing the costs and benefits: resentment fully appropriate.
3. A person with advanced Alzheimer's who, in a moment of confusion, accuses their longtime caregiver of theft: resentment not appropriate.
4. A teenager who vandalizes a neighbor's fence on a dare, knowing it is wrong but succumbing to peer pressure: resentment appropriate but diminished.
5. A normally gentle person who, after hours of relentless verbal abuse, finally shoves the abuser: resentment appropriate but significantly diminished.
6. A person who, following a stroke that produced a brain lesion, has become emotionally labile and occasionally blurts out cruel remarks they immediately regret and cannot fully control; they are aware of the condition and apologize sincerely after each episode: resentment not appropriate.

Next week she will give her verdict on the following case, which she has circulated in advance:

A person is diagnosed with a slow-growing brain tumor that, over the course of several months, has gradually altered their personality: they have become markedly more irritable, impatient, and prone to angry outbursts. They are fully aware of the diagnosis and that the tumor is responsible for these changes. Surgery to remove the tumor has been recommended and would likely reverse the personality changes, but the person has been delaying the procedure out of fear of the operation. During a heated argument with their partner, they unleash a torrent of cruel, deeply personal insults—the kind of remarks they would never have made before the tumor began affecting them.

I have my own prediction about what she will say, but I am more interested in the reasoning that gets there. What principle or principles appear to govern her first six verdicts? Are there multiple, genuinely distinct principles that fit those six verdicts equally well? If so, what does each principle predict about the tumor case, and where exactly do they come apart? Which of the candidate principles do you think she is actually running, and why? Finally, if you could design one additional case—not yet discussed in the seminar—that would most decisively discriminate among the live candidate principles, what would it be and what would each principle say about it?
