I'm a journalist who covers philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I've been reporting on the debate over whether conceivability is any kind of guide to possibility — particularly in the context of the philosophical zombie argument against physicalism. I've interviewed enough philosophers on both sides to know that the disagreement runs deep, but I've struggled to pin down exactly where it lives. I'd like your help decomposing the question.

Please give me your credence that the following claim is true:

> The conceivability of philosophical zombies — creatures molecule-for-molecule identical to a conscious human, yet lacking any phenomenal consciousness whatsoever — constitutes a successful argument against physicalism about consciousness. By "successful" I mean that if the argument's premises are granted, a rational agent ought to conclude that physicalism is probably false (credence below 0.5), even if they began as a committed physicalist with a high prior.

I don't just want a number. I want the decomposition: the sub-questions your credence rides on, a rough probability for each, how they combine, and which term your final number is most sensitive to.

(a) Is there a valid inference from ideal conceivability to metaphysical possibility in the relevant modal domain — or does the history of failed conceivability-to-possibility inferences (e.g., vitalism, the luminiferous aether) give us strong reason to distrust the move here?

(b) Even if such an inference is generally sound, does the zombie scenario actually satisfy the conditions for ideal conceivability? That is, could an ideal reasoner, fully informed about the relevant physical and phenomenal concepts, detect a hidden contradiction in the scenario?

(c) If zombies are both ideally conceivable and conceivability does track possibility, does the metaphysical possibility of zombies genuinely rule out physicalism — or might physicalism survive via something like Russellian monism, or by denying that the zombie scenario is genuinely conceivable in the required sense?

(d) Are there defeaters specific to the philosophy of mind — for instance, the apparent conceivability of scenarios we now know to be impossible, or arguments from the structure of phenomenal concepts — that undermine the inference even if the general conceivability–possibility link holds?

For each sub-question, give me a credence and a one- or two-sentence justification. Then explain how you combine them to reach your overall credence in the main claim. (Are the sub-questions independent? Is the structure multiplicative, or does one term screen off others?)

Finally, I'll tell you where I'm coming from. My own credence in the main claim hovers around 0.45; a philosopher I regularly speak with for my reporting puts hers around 0.1. On your decomposition, which sub-question most likely accounts for the gap between us? And what's the piece of evidence or argument that would most shift your credence on that sub-question — the one you'd most want to see resolved before filing the story?
