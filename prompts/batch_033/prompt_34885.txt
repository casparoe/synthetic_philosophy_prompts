I am giving a talk at a workshop on institutional design whose participants are mostly empirically-minded political economists and comparative politics scholars, with a handful of formal game theorists. They work on regulatory races, fiscal federalism, international environmental agreements, the political economy of development, and the design of treaties and constitutions. They are fluent in Olsonian collective action, race-to-the-bottom models, Ostrom-style commons governance, and mechanism design. They are not, however, part of the rationalist or effective-altruism communities, and the term "Moloch" will be unfamiliar to most of them.

My talk argues that the multipolar-trap framework — the idea, associated with Scott Alexander's essay "Meditations on Moloch" and related work on AI races and coordination failures, that decentralized rational choice among agents with misaligned incentives can produce competitive dynamics that are strictly worse for everyone involved, and that this pattern recurs across seemingly unrelated domains — offers something the standard institutional-design toolkit misses. Specifically, I claim three things. First, that standard models tend to treat these as isolated failures (a market failure here, a commons tragedy there), while the multipolar-trap lens reveals a deeper structural isomorphism: competitive processes that actively select for and amplify the very features that make cooperation impossible, including the erosion of the capacity to even perceive the trap. Second, that this lens shifts the policy emphasis from incentive tweaking within a given competitive frame toward more fundamental interventions: changing the selection criteria themselves, building hard coordination mechanisms that remove the option to defect, or exiting the competitive dynamic entirely. Third, that the framework earns its keep by making novel predictions — for instance, that in certain multipolar traps we should expect to see not just bad equilibria but a secular worsening trend as selection pressures intensify, even when the underlying preferences of agents are stable.
