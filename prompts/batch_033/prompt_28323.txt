"Some anti-intentionalists hold that the meaning of a literary text is a public matter, fixed by linguistic conventions and the text itself, rather than by the author's private intentions. Explain this view, and then evaluate whether it commits its holder to the further claim that evidence about the author's intentions — from letters, diaries, or reported statements — can never be legitimately used in interpreting the text. If it does commit them, say why; if it doesn't, explain what the difference is."

A student wrote:

"I think the anti-intentionalist is basically right. Wimsatt and Beardsley's intentional fallacy shows that what the author meant to do is not something we can reliably recover, and even if we could recover it, it wouldn't be the right standard for judging the poem. We can't get inside the author's head, and the poem has to stand on its own as a public object anyway. So the meaning of a text is fixed by public linguistic conventions plus the internal evidence of the text, not by what the author privately had in mind. Hirsch's distinction between meaning and significance supports this: meaning is what the text says, while significance is what it does for particular readers in particular contexts. This is also why appeals to letters, diaries, or biographical facts are always fallacious — once you let the author's life in, you're confusing the author with the text. So the right interpretive policy is to stick to the words on the page and their conventional meanings. Intention can at most tell us what the author wanted the text to mean, but that's a question about the author, not about the poem."
