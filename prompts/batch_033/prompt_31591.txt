For the past several months I have been reporting on a set of clinical trials for a novel psychiatric intervention that, if the preliminary data hold up, could shift how we treat one of the most refractory conditions in the field — a severe and chronic form of depression that does not respond to standard pharmacotherapy. The researchers are respected in their subfield, the basic science strikes me as plausible, and I have spoken at length with half a dozen patients whose accounts are genuinely striking: people who had cycled through every available treatment for years and are now, by their own description, living differently. But the sample sizes in the published work are small, the findings have not been independently replicated, and two biostatisticians I consulted warned me that the reported effect sizes may be substantially inflated by a combination of regression to the mean, selection effects, and the file-drawer problem. I do not yet have the kind of evidence that would let me state, flatly, that the treatment works.

My editor wants the story. Her position is that the public deserves to know what is being developed at the frontiers of the field, that even provisional findings can inform patients and families who are out of options, and that we can qualify the piece carefully — appropriate caveats, prominent mention of the replication gap, no overclaiming. A senior colleague in the newsroom, by contrast, has urged me to kill the piece until the evidence base is far stronger. He argues that when journalists amplify uncertain clinical findings they do real, lasting damage: a retraction never travels as far as the original headline, and publishing now would feed a wave of clinical enthusiasm that could lead vulnerable people toward an intervention that may not help them and may, in the end, cause harm. His view is that a journalist who publishes on insufficient evidence is being professionally irresponsible, full stop.

I find myself genuinely stuck. I believe — I cannot shake the feeling — that there is something real in these data, and I worry that sitting on the story for two or three more years while replication efforts grind forward means withholding information that could matter to people right now, people for whom a few years is a very long time to wait. But I also recognize that my belief is partly built on anecdote and instinct, on the felt weight of patient narratives, and on a kind of hopefulness about the science that I may be importing into the story rather than extracting from it. I cannot rule out that I am simply wrong. I am trying to think clearly about whether it can be responsible — or even permissible — to publish on the basis of evidence I know to be insufficient for belief in any strict sense, when the stakes on both sides are this high and the people affected are this desperate.
