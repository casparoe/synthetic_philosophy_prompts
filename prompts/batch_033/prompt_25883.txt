hey, i'm working through spinoza's political philosophy and i'm stuck on how a particular passage in the tractatus theologico-politicus connects to his whole thing about democracy. i get the broad strokes — democracy is the most natural form of government, it preserves equality, etc. — but when i go back to the text the foundation he lays for it seems almost designed to make democracy look impossible or at least really weird. here's the passage that's tripping me up, from chapter 16 (this is the 1862 translation i'm reading):

"Nor do we here recognize any difference between man and the rest of the beings of creation; nor between the man endowed with reason and the man who knows nothing of reason, nor between the sane in mind and the insane or fatuous. For whatsoever does anything acts by the laws of its nature or by the highest right, because acting as it is ordained to do by nature, and incapable of acting otherwise. Wherefore, among men, so long as they are considered to be living under the empire of nature alone, he who as yet knows nothing of reason, or who has not yet the habit of virtuous conduct, lives with perfect right by the laws of mere appetite, even as he lives by the laws of highest right who regulates his life in conformity with the dictates of reason."

so spinoza is saying that in the state of nature there's literally no normative distinction between the wise person and the fool — both have equal "right" because right just is power, and each acts according to their nature. the fool's appetite-driven life is just as "rightful" as the sage's reason-driven life. ok, fine, i think i follow that.

but then later in the same chapter he says democracy is the form of government that "most naturally" follows from this state of nature, and that in a democracy everyone transfers their natural right to the collective body. here's what i don't get: if the state of nature is a radical equality of power where appetite and reason are on exactly the same footing, and if the social contract is just people pooling their power, why would the resulting thing look anything like a well-ordered democracy rather than just mob rule by the most powerful appetites? it seems like spinoza wants to get from "everyone's appetite has equal natural right" to "democracy is the most rational form of government," but the bridge between those two looks really shaky to me. is the idea that the very act of entering into a collective body somehow transforms the quality of the power, or is spinoza banking on something else that i'm missing? i feel like there's a step in the argument i'm just not seeing, and it's making the whole democracy-as-most-natural claim feel unmotivated.
