I run the trust and safety policy group for a mid-sized social platform, and we are rewriting our slur policy. Our current rule is a blunt one: if a post contains any string on a blocklist, it is removed. We have known for a while that this gets the wrong answer in both directions. It takes down uses almost no one on the policy team wants taken down—a member of the targeted group using a reclaimed term about themselves or their community, a news account quoting a public figure's use of a slur, an educational post about the history of the word—and it misses derogatory uses that never touch the list, like an account that systematically refers to a group through a euphemism or a stock phrase that does the same work in context. The moderators who will run the next policy are not linguists; they make thousands of decisions a day, usually in under a minute, and the stakes are high in both directions, because under-removal exposes users to abuse and over-removal chills legitimate speech and punishes the very groups the policy is meant to protect.

I want you to write a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the semantics and pragmatics of slurs as philosophers of language understand them, that a moderator can actually run. It should encode the distinctions that matter—conventional meaning or content versus what a use pragmatically conveys or does, offense versus derogation, speaker identity and audience in reclamation, and use versus mention or quotation—and it should not quietly assume one contested theory where the main accounts would give different verdicts. For each step: state the question, say what counts as a satisfactory answer before moving on, and say where the procedure stops with a decision and where it escalates to a human policy specialist because the step is a judgment call no written rule can absorb. Then run the procedure on three cases: a member of the targeted group using a reclaimed slur in a celebratory in-group post; a news organization quoting a government official's use of a slur; and a post that never uses a listed slur but calls a marginalized group "those people" in a way that clearly does the work of a slur. At least one of the three should return a result the policy team will not like, and I want you to say so plainly. Finally, be explicit about which steps are really the company's values doing the work under a procedural disguise, and about the cases this procedure will get wrong no matter how carefully it is written.
