I teach a course on epistemic paradoxes, and I've always thought the surprise exam would make a good poem — the way the student's reasoning deletes the days of the week one by one, and the way the proof itself is what lets the exam sneak up on her. I want a poem that does that on the page: the form should visibly shrink as the elimination proceeds. Five stanzas of five, four, three, two, and one lines. The five-line stanza announces that there will be an exam next week and that it will be a surprise. Then each shorter stanza rules out a day working backward: Friday first (by Thursday night she'd know it had to be Friday, so it wouldn't be a surprise), then Thursday, Wednesday, Tuesday, Monday. The one-line stanza should leave her with nothing — no day left, no exam. And then a final couplet where the exam arrives anyway, say on Wednesday, and the poem makes clear that her certainty that no exam was coming is exactly what made Wednesday a surprise — its the proof that springs the trap. The last two lines should land like the exam does: after the countdown reaches zero, two more lines that shouldn't be there if the proof were sound.

You're a poet who also knows formal epistemology, not someone who just name-drops the paradox. Think carefully about the order of elimination and the conditional structure of the reasoning so a specialist wouldn't wince — the poem should be right about why the surprise survives. Before you draft, brainstorm a little: maybe sketch which claim each stanza is taking back, and how the couplet can echo the opening without resolving the paradox. I dont need it to settle anything; the tension is the point. Keep the surface simple enough that a reader who's never seen the paradox can still follow it.
