You are a philosopher who specializes in the ethics of paternalism and choice architecture. Before you write the procedure, take time to think through the key conceptual distinctions in the literature — hard versus soft paternalism, means versus ends paternalism, manipulation versus rational persuasion, transparency conditions, autonomy as non-interference versus autonomy as capacity — and decide which of these distinctions must survive the translation into an operational tool and which can be simplified without losing too much.

I work in a city government's behavioral insights unit. We design small changes to default options, forms, signage, and choice architecture to improve outcomes — automatic enrollment in retirement savings, changing the order of food items in cafeterias, simplifying paperwork for benefits. We have a loose set of principles we inherited from the nudge literature, but we don't have anything systematic for deciding whether a proposed intervention crosses the line from acceptable influence into manipulation or unjustified paternalism. Our decisions are medium-stakes: they affect thousands of residents, and a bad one generates complaints to the mayor's office and op-eds about the "nanny state." We need something we can run in an hour or two before a design meeting, and the output has to be defensible to a non-specialist deputy mayor who will ask, "Why is this one okay and that one isn't?"

Please write a decision procedure — a checklist or structured set of questions in order — that my team can apply to a proposed nudge to determine whether it is ethically permissible. The procedure should be grounded in the philosophical literature on paternalism, autonomy, and manipulation, but you should translate the concepts into operational tests that someone without a philosophy background can apply. For each step, specify what kind of evidence or reasoning counts as a satisfactory answer, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it flags a case for human judgment because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

1. Changing the default on a city retirement savings program from opt-in to opt-out (with easy opt-out), on the grounds that many eligible residents who say they want to save never get around to enrolling.
2. Rearranging a cafeteria line so that fruit and vegetables are at eye level and desserts are below the counter, out of direct sight, to reduce calorie intake.
3. Automatically scheduling a colonoscopy appointment for every resident who turns 50 and sending them a letter that says, "Your appointment is on [date]. If this doesn't work, call to reschedule. If you don't want one, call to cancel." (The aim is to increase screening rates; the procedure is medically recommended and covered by public insurance.)

Make sure at least one of these applications yields an answer that my team will find uncomfortable — that is, the procedure says "no" to something we would like to do, or "yes" to something we would rather not defend. Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot handle: which kinds of cases will it misclassify, and where does it rely on distinctions that turn out to be vague or contested when you look at them closely?
