My study group got into a fight about the centipede game, and the standard argument for taking immediately feels like it proves too much, but I cant locate the bad step. Setup: two players alternate choosing take or pass. The pot doubles whenever someone passes. If a player takes, she gets the larger share and the game ends. If both pass to the end, both get a large payoff, but at each node the player to move gets more by taking than by passing and then watching the other player take on the next move. The argument: (1) At the final node, the player to move will take, since passing there just gives the other player the bigger payoff and ends the game. (2) So at the second-to-last node, the other player knows that passing will be met with a take, leaving her worse off than taking now, so she takes. (3) The same reasoning iterates backward one node at a time. (4) Therefore a rational first player takes at the very first move, even in a 100-move game where both could walk away with much more by passing. (5) But that can't be right — real players don't do that, and the argument seems to label a mutually beneficial string of passes as irrational. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (5), but I suspect holding onto it quietly costs me one of the earlier steps. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which step goes — and then give the reasoning, without restating the question.
