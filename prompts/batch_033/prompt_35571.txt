I'm a software engineer at a company that builds personalization systems, and I've been put in charge of revisiting our user preference model. The more I read the economics and decision-theory literature, the more I realize that the whole project rests on philosophical assumptions about what a preference even is, and what it means to say someone's choices reveal their utility. People use "utility" and "preference" and "welfare" and "value" in ways that seem to presuppose different answers to deep questions without flagging them, and I keep finding myself unable to tell which disagreements are substantive and which are just terminological.

Before I commit to an architecture, I want to get clear on the conceptual landscape. I'd like you to write a decision tree that takes someone from a starting point like "I want to build a system that learns what users prefer" to a named philosophical position about the nature of utility and preference. The tree should be organized around the diagnostic questions that genuinely separate the positions, not around the labels. Each branch node should be a concrete claim or case that someone could answer without already knowing the philosophical territory, and each leaf should name a position and give a brief description of its core commitment and its most awkward consequence. The work should be in the questions: anyone should be able to answer them honestly and end up at the view they actually hold, even if they have never heard the view's name.

The positions I keep encountering and need the tree to cover are at least these four. Utility as a real hedonic experience, a mental state of pleasure or satisfaction that we can in principle measure directly, and that choice is only a fallible proxy for. Utility as nothing more than a formal representation of consistent choice behavior, the Samuelsonian revealed-preference approach where saying someone prefers A to B is just shorthand for saying they reliably choose A over B. Utility as preference satisfaction where preferences are richer than just choice behavior, encompassing desires, plans, second-order attitudes, and the things people would choose under ideal conditions, so that choice data is one piece of evidence among others. And utility as objective well-being or welfare that can diverge from what someone actually prefers or chooses, because people can be mistaken about what is good for them. If there are other positions that fill out the logical space, include them, and if any combination of answers has no defender, flag it and say whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

The tree should be usable by someone on my team who has never read a word of philosophy. Every question must be phrased in terms of concrete cases, ideally drawn from the kind of data we actually work with: clickstreams, purchase histories, dwell time, explicit ratings, user surveys, A/B test outcomes. A question might go something like this: if a user spends a lot of time on content they rate poorly, and we have to pick one signal as the one that reflects their real preference, which wins and why? The answer should not depend on knowing what revealed preference or hedonic utility mean beforehand. Another might be: does the system's job change when we have reason to think the user would later regret a choice they are making now? Another: if we find that a user's choices are intransitive, cycling from A to B to C and back to A, does that mean our model is wrong, or does it mean the user is making a mistake, or does it mean something else entirely?

Please flag any pair of positions that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what additional question or case would be needed to pull them apart. Also flag any leaf where the name is not settled, or where different traditions use the same name for different things.

Please write the whole answer in continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a long letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Just paragraphs, in American English.
