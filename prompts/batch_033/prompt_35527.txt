Analyze the global distribution of COVID-19 vaccines from late 2020 through 2022 through the lens of the philosophical debate about whether compatriots have special claims on each other. The core of that debate is this: do the citizens of a state owe more to one another, simply in virtue of shared citizenship, than they owe to non-citizens, or are our obligations fundamentally cosmopolitan, demanding equal concern for all persons regardless of national boundaries?

The salient facts of the episode: beginning in late 2020, wealthy countries — including the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, and the member states of the European Union — entered into advance purchase agreements with vaccine manufacturers that secured enough doses to cover their populations several times over. By mid-2021, while these countries were vaccinating healthy young adults, many low-income countries had vaccinated less than one percent of their populations. The COVAX facility, an international mechanism designed to procure and distribute vaccines equitably, was chronically underfunded and could not compete with the purchasing power of wealthy states. Several countries, including India and the United States, imposed export controls on vaccines or on the raw materials needed to manufacture them. The emergence of new variants — Alpha, Delta, Omicron — was driven in part by uncontrolled transmission in unvaccinated populations, making the pandemic longer and deadlier for everyone, including the citizens of the vaccinating countries.

I want a careful mapping. First, take the position of a strong compatriot-partialist: someone who holds that a government's primary obligation is to its own citizens, and that this obligation permits or even requires giving citizens priority in the distribution of a scarce life-saving resource. What would this position say about the decisions of wealthy-country governments? Does it justify them, or does it impose limits that those governments exceeded? Next, take a strict cosmopolitan position: what does it say those governments should have done, and where does the actual distribution fall short? Be precise about the points of disagreement: are they disagreements about the content of the obligations, or about their weight relative to other considerations, or about who bears them?

Now identify which features of the episode each framework explains well. For instance, does the compatriot-partialist framework capture the political reality that governments are accountable to their own electorates in a way that a purely cosmopolitan framework cannot? Does the cosmopolitan framework capture the moral arbitrariness of vaccine access being determined by the nationality of the person who needs it? And where does each framework fall silent or misdescribe? In particular, does either framework have anything to say about the fact that a pandemic is a global collective action problem — that vaccinating only your own population is self-defeating if the virus is mutating in unvaccinated populations elsewhere?

Then run the analysis in the other direction. What does this episode reveal about the adequacy of each position? Does the fact that enlightened self-interest and cosmopolitan morality appeared to converge in this case — because controlling the pandemic everywhere was in every country's interest — strengthen the cosmopolitan position by showing that its demands are less opposed to national self-interest than they seem, or does it weaken the cosmopolitan position by suggesting that cosmopolitan outcomes are only achievable when they happen to align with national self-interest? Does the episode reveal tensions within the compatriot-partialist position — for example, between the claim that compatriots have special claims and the claim that those claims are not absolute? And finally: does the episode suggest that the dichotomy between compatriot partiality and cosmopolitan impartiality is itself inadequate — that a third framework, neither purely nationalist nor purely cosmopolitan, is needed to make sense of what happened and what should have happened?
