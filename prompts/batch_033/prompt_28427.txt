I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about the Ellsberg paradox, and I walked away with an argument I built during the conversation that now bothers me because I'm not sure where it fails — but its conclusion is clearly absurd. I'd like you to diagnose it.

Here's the argument:

(1) In the classic Ellsberg setup, most people prefer to bet on drawing a red ball from Urn A (50 red, 50 black) over betting on red from Urn B (100 balls, unknown mix of red and black), and they also prefer betting on black from Urn A over black from Urn B. These preferences cannot be represented by any single probability distribution over the composition of Urn B — they violate the sure-thing principle. The standard interpretation is that people are ambiguity-averse: they prefer known probabilities to unknown ones, even when the payoffs are identical.

(2) My friend defended these preferences as rational. His claim: when you don't know the probabilities, you face a kind of uncertainty that standard expected utility theory ignores, and it's reasonable to penalize options whose probabilities you're ignorant about. Call this the rationality-of-ambiguity-aversion thesis.

(3) But if ambiguity aversion is rational in the Ellsberg case, then by parity of reasoning it should be rational whenever we face uncertainty about probabilities — not just in stylized urn problems. If the structure of the situation is the same, the normative verdict should be the same.

(4) And here's the trouble: almost every consequential decision in real life involves uncertainty about probabilities. You don't know the probability that a career change will work out, that a medical procedure will succeed, that a relationship will last, that a policy will have its intended effect. These are all Urn B situations: you're betting on an outcome whose odds you cannot pin to a precise number.

(5) If ambiguity aversion is rational, then in each of these cases you should prefer an option whose probabilities you know over one whose probabilities you don't — even when your best estimate of the unknown-probability option gives it a higher expected value. You should stay in the job you know, decline the novel treatment, avoid the unfamiliar relationship, reject the untested policy.

(6) But this is decision-theoretic paralysis. It would mean never trying anything new, never innovating, never taking a calculated risk on something whose odds you can't precisely quantify. That conclusion is absurd on its face — and it's also self-defeating, because the option of "sticking with what you know" itself has unknown probabilities once you look at it over a long enough time horizon. The known-unknown distinction collapses under its own weight.

(7) Therefore, the rationality-of-ambiguity-aversion thesis is false. Ambiguity aversion is a bias, not a rational principle, and the Ellsberg preferences are a mistake.

I'm suspicious of my own argument because the conclusion seems too strong — it would sweep away a whole literature on ambiguity aversion in one move, and plenty of smart people have thought about this without landing there. But I can't find the step where the reasoning goes wrong. My friend would reject (7) and probably dig in at (2), but he couldn't tell me which later step misapplies his claim. I'd most like to keep (3) — the parity-of-reasoning move — because I think it's the strongest part of the argument. But maybe that's exactly where it fails.

Tell me: which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid and the real question is which premise to give up, price the alternatives. If there's a subtle flaw — an equivocation, a scope shift, a slide between senses of "rational" — name it and explain why it looked fine. Keep the response to no more than 900 words.
