I keep noticing a pattern in practical reasoning that I do not have a name for, and I would like to know whether philosophers or decision theorists have identified and analysed it — and if not, what it should be called and how its boundaries should be drawn.

The pattern goes like this. Someone makes an outlandish threat: if you do not hand over your wallet, they will activate a device that causes an astronomically large number of conscious beings to experience the worst possible suffering — a number so vast it requires special notation even to write down. You dismiss this as absurd. But when you try to say exactly why it is absurd, you find yourself in difficulty. You already accept that small risks of large harms justify precautions: you pay for house insurance, you wear a seatbelt, you think it reasonable to fund research into asteroid deflection. In each of those cases, a tiny probability of a bad outcome justifies a finite cost. So why does the same style of reasoning not apply here? The probability that the stranger is telling the truth is not strictly zero — you cannot prove he lacks such a device — and the stakes he describes are incomparably larger than any ordinary risk. If you multiply even the most minuscule probability by a sufficiently enormous cost, the result can be made larger than any finite sacrifice you might be asked to make. It seems that for any refusal, a hypothetical can be constructed with stakes high enough to make your refusal look unreasonable by the very same calculation that tells you to buy insurance.

This is not quite the St Petersburg paradox. There, the trouble is that an endless series of outcomes, each with a shrinking probability, sums to an infinite average payoff. Here, a single low-probability scenario can be made to yield an arbitrarily large average loss simply by escalating the stakes described, without any infinite series. It also does not seem to be a Dutch book, since no sequence of bets is being offered that would guarantee a loss regardless of how things turn out. And it is not a paradox in the strict sense — no contradiction is derived. It is more like a vulnerability: a way of weighing reasons that seemed perfectly sensible turns out to be exploitable by anyone imaginative enough to describe a sufficiently extreme hypothetical.

Is there an established term for this class of problem? If there is, what exactly are its boundaries — how does it differ from the St Petersburg paradox, from ordinary risk aversion, and from general sceptical worries about simulation or other minds? And if no settled term exists, what would you propose, and how would you define it so that it captures this specific vulnerability without collapsing into a blanket rejection of weighing costs by their likelihood and magnitude? If the way I have set up the problem conflates distinct issues that ought to be kept apart, or if the very framing rests on a confusion about how probability or practical reasoning works, I would rather you said so than tried to fit an answer to a mistaken question. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
