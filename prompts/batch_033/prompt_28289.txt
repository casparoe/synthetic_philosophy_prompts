My roommate and I have been going back and forth about forgeries, and he keeps coming back to the same analogy. He says worrying about whether a painting is the original is like worrying about which hard drive a file was first saved on. If a forger (or a high-end scanner and printer) can produce something brushstroke-for-brushstroke indistinguishable from the original, then the copy carries exactly the same information as the original, so any extra value we attach to the original is pure status fetishism — like paying a premium for a file because it happens to live on the disk it was first written to. His conclusion is that, once we can't tell them apart, there's no rational basis for valuing originals over perfect copies, and the whole business of provenance, authentication, and museum prestige is just snobbery dressed up as principle.

I keep feeling this has to be wrong, but I cant make the disanalogies stick. I'm writing a short paper on it for an aesthetics class and I want to audit the analogy properly. Can you give me the mapping table: what in the painting case corresponds to the file, the disk, the act of saving, the copy operation, and the idea of "the same information"? Then push on where the mapping breaks: what does the file picture quietly import into the painting case — that a work is a repeatable pattern rather than a particular thing, that copying is lossless, that its history is just metadata, maybe that the only value is in the pattern itself? Which of those disanalogies actually do work against his conclusion, and which are just beside the point? And after all that, does his conclusion survive — or was the file analogy carrying the whole argument?
