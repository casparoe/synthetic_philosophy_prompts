Last night I had a long argument with a friend about legal moralism — specifically, whether the criminal law may prohibit conduct solely on the ground that it is immoral, even when it harms no one else. I was defending the view that the law cannot avoid embodying moral judgment: the harm principle itself is a moral position, and once that is conceded, there is no principled line between the moral judgments the law is permitted to enforce and the ones it is not. My friend kept cutting the argument short with the same sentence, delivered as though it settled the matter: "You can't legislate morality."

I suspect that sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and that only some of the things it might mean would actually answer what I was arguing. Could you separate out the distinct claims "you can't legislate morality" might be used to assert — for example, an empirical claim about what legislation can effectively change in people, a conceptual claim about what law is, and a normative claim about what the state may legitimately do — and for each tell me whether it is trivial, plausible but contestable, or false? Then say which of those readings, if any, would actually block my argument, and which would merely change the subject. I want to know exactly what my friend would need to be claiming for the slogan to do the work he was using it to do.
