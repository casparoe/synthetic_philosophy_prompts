"Bertrand's paradox is a problem for the principle of indifference, which says that when we have no evidence favoring one outcome over others, we should assign equal probabilities to all possible outcomes. The paradox involves a circle and the question: what is the probability that a randomly drawn chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle? Bertrand showed that there are (at least) three natural ways to apply the principle of indifference here, and they give different answers. If we pick two random points on the circumference, the probability is 1/3. If we pick a random radius and a random point along it to serve as the chord's midpoint, the probability is 1/2. If we pick a random point inside the circle to serve as the chord's midpoint, the probability is 1/4. Since the principle of indifference gives three different answers to the same question, it must be internally inconsistent. This shows the principle is fundamentally flawed. Jaynes later argued that the second method (giving 1/2) is the correct one because it's the only one invariant under rotations and translations, which resolves the paradox. But this just pushes the problem back: why should we priviledge that particular symmetry? Ultimately, Bertrand's paradox shows that the principle of indifference can't be salvaged and that all probability assignments are subjective — there's no objective way to set priors, so any prior is as legitimate as any other."
