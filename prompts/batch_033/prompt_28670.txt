I'm on my university's academic integrity board and we're stuck on a procedural question that keeps coming up. We often get evidence thats not certain — a witness who's probably telling the truth but might be lying, a document that's likely authentic but could be forged, a data analysis that probably shows fabrication but the stats are messy. Some members want to treat each piece of evidence as if we'd learned it for sure and then discount at the end; others say that's double-counting uncertainty and we need to update our beliefs differently when the evidence itself is uncertain. I know this is basically the problem Jeffrey conditionalization was invented for, and a couple committee members do too, but we've never settled on a procedure. I need to draft a short memo recommending how the board should reason from uncertain evidence — a general method we can apply case by case, not just a ruling on the current case. It should engage the philosophical issue head-on (explain why the naive approach is problematic, lay out the alternative, flag any remaining difficulties) but the bottom line has to be something non-philosophers on the board can actually follow.
