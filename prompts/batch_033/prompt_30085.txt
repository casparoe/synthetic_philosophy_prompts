I've been working through a philosophy of language textbook on my own, and a passage on Quine's indeterminacy of translation has left me unsure how much of it to trust. The book's gloss runs as follows: "Quine imagines a field linguist in a newly encountered community. Each time a rabbit runs by, the speakers say 'gavagai,' and the linguist tentatively writes down 'gavagai = rabbit.' Quine points out, however, that 'gavagai' might equally well mean undetached rabbit parts, rabbit stages, or rabbithood, since all of these would be present in exactly the same observable situations. The linguist can therefore never know which translation is correct. Quine concludes that translation is impossible in any strict sense, because there is no fact of the matter about which translation is right; what a word means is fixed only relative to the translation manual one happens to adopt. The upshot is that meaning is not an objective feature of the speaker's words but an artifact of the translation scheme we impose."

I can see how the evidence underdetermines the choice between "rabbit" and the alternatives, but I don't see why that should make translation impossible rather than merely uncertain, and the final upshot about meaning being an artifact of our scheme sounds to me more like relativism than anything I'd have expected from Quine. I'd like a careful audit of this summary: which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which would a Quinean reject outright? In particular, does Quine himself draw the conclusion that translation is impossible, or does the book conflate two different claims? And is the final upshot—that meaning is an artifact of our translation scheme—something Quine actually holds, or a misreading of his claim that there is no fact of the matter?
