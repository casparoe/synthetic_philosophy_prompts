I've recently found myself unsettled by how much of my own thinking runs on autopilot — not just habit, but patterns of judgment I deploy before I've even noticed there was a decision to make. It brought me back to the concept of sphexishness: the unnerving fact that the digger wasp performs an apparently intelligent sequence of behaviors (paralyzing a cricket, leaving it at the burrow entrance, inspecting the burrow, then dragging the cricket inside) that turns out to be a rigid, mindless routine — if a researcher nudges the cricket a few inches away during the inspection, the wasp will repeat the whole sequence from that point, and can be made to loop indefinitely without ever adapting. The term originates with Douglas Hofstadter and was taken up by Daniel Dennett in discussions of free will, but I find the philosophical implications underexplored. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about what sphexishness teaches us. One should argue that the wasp's situation is, in essence, our own: we run vastly more complicated programs, but the difference is one of degree and not of kind, and this should change how we think about agency. The other should argue that sphexishness, while vivid, is a misleading analogy — human beings possess a kind of responsiveness to reasons and capacity for self-directed change that is qualitatively different from even the most elaborate stimulus-response architecture, and the wasp example actually helps us see, by contrast, what genuine flexibility consists in. Both characters should be equally competent and should genuinely engage with each other's arguments, not talk past one another. The dialogue should push into territory where each position faces real pressure.
