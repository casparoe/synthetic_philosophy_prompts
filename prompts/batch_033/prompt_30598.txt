I work as a policy analyst for a state legislative committee, and every session we sit through testimony where advocates lean hard on thought experiments — the ticking bomb to justify surveillance powers, the lifeboat to argue about how to ration scarce resources, the tragedy of the commons to justify regulation or privatization, that sort of thing. Some of these seem to genuinely clarify a tradeoff, and others seem to just make one option feel inevitable by the time the story is over, and I honestly can't always tell which is happening until it's too late. I've seen the term intuition pump used as an insult in the comments on our public hearings, but nobody in my office could give you an actual criterion. We process maybe a dozen of these arguments a week during session, none of us have philosophy training, and we have to turn our notes into a one-page memo for the committee chair by the next morning, and she will not accept 'this felt manipulative' as a reason to discount somebody's testimony. If we get it wrong, actual legislation gets drafted on the back of an intuition.

So I'd like a reusable procedure we can run: a checklist or ordered sequence of questions for deciding whether a thought experiment in a policy argument is doing real argumentative work or just pumping intuitions — with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure says 'discount this argument,' and where it has to hand the decision to a philosopher becuase the question is substantive rather than procedural. Then run the procedure on two or three concrete cases: for example, a surveillance bill defended with a ticking-bomb scenario, and a healthcare rationing argument that leans on a lifeboat story. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us to disregard an argument the committee is inclined to love, and be explicit about where the procedure itself breaks down or quietly smuggles in judgment calls dressed up as method.
