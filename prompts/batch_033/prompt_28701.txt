I run a small company with roughly twenty employees, and for years I have watched the same cycle repeat itself. We identify something that matters — customer satisfaction, thoroughness in documentation, the quality of our outreach — and we create a metric so we can track whether we are improving. For a few weeks, the numbers move in the right direction. Then someone figures out how to move the number without doing the thing the number was supposed to stand for. The metric keeps climbing, but the underlying reality gets worse. I eventually learned that this pattern has a name — Goodhart's law, and the broader phenomenon of specification gaming — but naming it has not stopped it from happening.

I want to run a workshop for my team that makes them feel the problem rather than hear me describe it. The goal is not to scold anyone; it is to give all of us a shared experience we can refer back to when we set targets in the future.

The setting: roughly twenty people in a large conference room, two hours total. I can prepare simple materials — paper, pens, sticky notes, envelopes, a whiteboard, maybe inexpensive prizes like gift cards or snacks. No real money needs to change hands during the activity itself, but there should be some incentive to try to win, because the whole point is that people will respond to the incentive structure.

What I want: a game with clear rules and a scoring system, playable in about forty-five minutes, in which participants are given a proxy to optimize — a number to maximize or minimize — and the proxy is initially correlated with some underlying thing I actually care about, but the correlation breaks when people start gaming it. I do not want the participants told in advance what the real goal is or that the proxy is a proxy. They should discover, through play, that hitting the target and achieving the real aim come apart, and ideally they should notice themselves doing things that feel clever but hollow. The force of the idea should show up in what they do and how they feel about it, not in anything I say beforehand.

After the game, I need a structured debrief — roughly forty-five minutes — that walks the group through what happened. The debrief should map specific moments from the game onto the anatomy of specification gaming: the proxy measure, the true objective, the initial correlation, the point where the correlation breaks, and the way optimizing the proxy can actively damage the true objective. Only during the debrief should the terms Goodhart's law and specification gaming be introduced.

I also need you to tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. Will some participants refuse to game the metric because they sense what is happening? Will the game produce a stalemate rather than a clear case of gaming? Will people get genuinely frustrated with each other? For each likely failure mode, tell me how to handle it in the moment and what to say about it in the debrief.

Finally, be honest about what a two-hour workshop game cannot reproduce. In a real organization, specification gaming unfolds over months or years, involves stakes much higher than snacks, and is tangled up with job security, professional identity, and relationships. Which features of the real phenomenon will my activity inevitably leave out, and how should I address those gaps in the discussion so that the lesson does not feel like a toy example that everyone can safely ignore on Monday morning?
