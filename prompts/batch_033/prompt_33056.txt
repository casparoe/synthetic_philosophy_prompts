I'm trying to isolate a variable that I think gets glossed over in the standard thought experiments.

Please construct a thought experiment with the following specifications. There should be two systems, A and B, that implement the same fine-grained computation: same functional states, same transition rules, same input-output dispositions across all possible inputs. However, they differ in their physical realization such that (1) system A intuitively seems like a plausible candidate for sentience, and (2) system B intuitively seems not to be, even though it is computationally identical to A. The asymmetry should not turn on speed, scale, or merely on B being made of an unfamiliar material. Constraints: no science fiction, no exotic physics, and do not reuse Block's homunculi-head, the China brain, or Searle's Chinese Room — I need a fresh pair of realizations. The two systems must be physically possible, even if impractical to build.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the verdicts of three positions: (a) substrate-neutral computational functionalism about moral patienthood, (b) the view that only biological information-processing matters morally, and (c) integrated information theory. For each, say what verdict the theory gives about A and B and why.

Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the most work in generating the intuitive asymmetry, and explain what happens if we remove it: does the case collapse into a standard intuition-pump for one of the three theories, or does a new and different problem emerge?

Format the response as exactly five numbered items: (1) the scenario, (2) the computational functionalist's verdict, (3) the biological naturalist's verdict, (4) the IIT verdict, and (5) the feature analysis. Each item should be two to four sentences. No introduction, no conclusion.
