A colleague in the formal epistemology group pressed this on me, and I have not found a clean way out. It seems to show that a minimally rational agent cannot, even in principle, trust its own reasoning — a conclusion too strong to accept, since it would convict every one of us of irrationality. Here is the argument.

(1) A minimally rational agent is entitled to trust its own conclusions: if, after careful deliberation, it derives P, then it is rationally permitted — indeed rationally required — to accept P. Call this the self-trust principle.

(2) Idealize such an agent as a consistent formal system S strong enough to formalize its own syntax and satisfying the usual derivability conditions (say, a recursively axiomatizable extension of Peano arithmetic), with S's theorems representing the agent's conclusions. For an agent so idealized, trusting its reasoning in the sense of (1) just is accepting the general principle: for every sentence φ, if φ is derivable in S, then φ. Since the agent's acceptances are S's theorems, this means S must derive its own soundness.

(3) Löb's theorem says that no such S can derive its own soundness. More precisely: if S derives, for every sentence φ, the conditional "if φ is derivable in S, then φ," then S derives every φ outright. A consistent S that derived its own soundness would therefore be trivial, proving every sentence of its language.

(4) Therefore, no consistent formal agent can satisfy the self-trust principle of (1). Explicit self-trust is impossible for any agent of the kind described in (2).

(5) But we are — or can be faithfully idealized as — agents of the kind described in (2), and we plainly are rational in trusting our own reasoning in the way (1) describes.

(6) Hence at least one of (1), (2), or (5) must be false; (3) is a theorem and is not a candidate. Yet each of the others seemed independently compelling.

Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (3), but I cannot see how to hold both without giving up one of the remaining claims, and each of those appears to be doing indispensable work. A satisfying answer will name the step, explain why it looked unimpeachable, and price the alternatives: what does each way out force one to say about the relation between an agent's rational entitlements and what its formal model can prove, or about the idealization itself?
