I'm a retired nurse, not an academic, but I've run a small reading group at the public library for about six years. This season we've been working through some of Schrödinger's later essays, the ones where he keeps coming back to consciousness and to the idea that the many seperate minds we seem to see are something like an illusion produced by one underlying consciousness. I've ended up convinced of a fairly specific version of this: that Schrödinger's 'one consciousness' talk isn't a mystical aside, its a natural conclusion once you accept that physics describes one world and that nothing in the physics marks a boundary between my mind and yours — the boundary is something the mind itself draws, not something the world comes with. I also think he was basically right to go to Vedanta for that idea, even if he read it through a Westernized filter. Next month I'm giving a talk defending that, and I'm honestly more nervous about the questions than the talk itself.

The audience: our group is mostly retired people with a scientific bent, but two regulars matter. One is a retired physicist who worked in quantum optics and thinks Schrödinger's later philosophy is embarrassing and should be ignored; the other still teaches physics at the local college and is more sympathetic but keeps saying Schrödinger is being 'poetic, not rigorous.' There's also a man who has spent twenty years in a Vedanta study circle, and he thinks Schrödinger read a boiled-down, Westernized version of the tradition and got key distinctions wrong. The rest are smart laypeople. I need a prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get from these people, phrased the way they'd phrase them, roughly in the order they'll come, which ones genuinely hurt my position, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the one question I'm praying nobody asks. And please tell me where my replies are soft, not just what to say.
