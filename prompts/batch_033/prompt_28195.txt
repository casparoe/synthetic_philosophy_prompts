A philosophy podcast I listened to recently offered this summary of Hegel's master–slave dialectic. It said that self-consciousness needs recognition from another self-consciousness in order to be truly itself; the encounter between two self-consciousnesses therefore tends toward a struggle, because each wants the other to acknowledge it while not being willing to acknowledge the other first. In that struggle one consciousness stakes everything on proving that it is not reducible to its biological life, while the other, seeing that a dead consciousness cannot be recognized, yields; the former becomes the lord, the latter the bondsman. The podcast then said that Hegel regards the lord's position as ultimately disappointing: the lord has received recognition only from a dependent, thing-like consciousness, so the recognition is not worth having, while the bondsman, through fear and especially through labor, gains a kind of self-discipline and impresses himself on the objective world. According to the podcast, Hegel's final lesson is that the worker becomes the real master of society through the economic importance of his labor.

I can follow the famous inversion at the level of consciousness—the lord turns out dependent, the bondsman turns out independent—but the last step seems to import a later economic or revolutionary claim into Hegel. I would like an expert to go through this summary piece by piece and tell me what is a faithful reading, what is a common but contestable simplification, and whether the podcast's concluding social-economic lesson actually follows from anything Hegel says in the master–slave section. In particular, is the struggle best understood as a strategic choice in which one party weighs life against recognition, and is the bondsman's work supposed to free him by changing his relation to objects and fear, or by giving him an economic role that undermines the lord?
