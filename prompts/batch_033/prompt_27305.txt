I'm teaching an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar, and we've just spent three weeks on the units of selection debate — Williams against group selection, Dawkins's selfish gene, Sober and Wilson's multi-level selection revival, the Price equation, and the quieter arguments about whether the whole dispute is just a matter of bookkeeping. Next week is our last meeting, and I want to give my students something that captures why the debate feels both interminable and genuinely illuminating rather than merely unresolved.

I'd like you to write a sestina about the units of selection problem. The six end-words should be drawn from the debate's conceptual vocabulary — I'm thinking something like "gene," "body," "group," "fitness," "level," and "story" — though you can adjust these if another set does better work. The sestina form matters here: its pattern of rotating end-words through six stanzas, each one reshuffling the same six terms into a new order, should mirror the way the debate keeps telling the same evolutionary narrative at different levels without any one framing decisively winning. The envoi should bring all six words together in three lines that don't resolve the debate so much as show why it persists — perhaps by suggesting that the question "at what level does selection act?" is itself ill-posed, or by showing the gene's-eye view and the group-selection view as two descriptions of one process that resist reduction to each other, or by having the envoi itself perform the vertigo of realizing you can shift your gaze from one level to the next and watch the causal story reconfigure itself.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the literature — the problem of altruism and how it motivates group selection, the distinction between replicators and vehicles, the idea that genic and multi-level descriptions can be formally equivalent even while their causal narratives differ — recognizes the moves being made. But it should also work as a poem: the end-words shouldn't feel forced, and the rotation of terms should generate genuine shifts in perspective from stanza to stanza rather than just rearranging the furniture.
