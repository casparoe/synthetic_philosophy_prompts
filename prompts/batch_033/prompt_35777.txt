I have come to accept the Harsanyi line in the foundations of ethics: the social aggregation theorem shows that if individual preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and the social preference ordering also satisfies those axioms while respecting Pareto indifference, then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities; the impartial-observer theorem then supplies the further step of equal weights, yielding average utilitarianism. I am not asking for a defense of utilitarianism as such. I am asking how much of the argumentative weight these theorems can actually bear. Treat the position as the claim that the theorem-plus-impartiality package does genuine foundational work — that it shows utilitarianism follows from rationality and impartiality rather than merely restating utilitarianism in formal dress.

What would count against that claim? I want, first, the formal assumptions that could fail, and what social choice would look like if each failed; second, the interpretive questions that would have to be resolved a particular way for the standard reading to go through — interpersonal comparability of the von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, the ex ante/ex post distinction, whether social preferences should satisfy the independence axiom at all; and third, the specific arguments a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against them, such as Diamond's fairness example and Sen's comparability challenge. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and then give me an honest assessment: once the assumptions are made explicit, is the theorem still doing the work, or was the work being done by the assumptions all along? I would find a defeater most persuasive if it showed that the theorem contributes nothing over and above the axioms one had to grant it.
