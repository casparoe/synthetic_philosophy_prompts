I serve on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. We are reviewing an adverse-event case with a disciplinary dimension, and we have reached an impasse on a point of principle.

The facts are not in dispute. A hospitalist, Dr. M, treated a patient presenting with acute condition C using Treatment A rather than the standard Treatment B. Treatment A caused serious but non-fatal complications that Treatment B would have avoided. In her defense, Dr. M argues that she cannot be held responsible for the outcome because, as it happens, a recently updated hospital protocol — one she had not yet read — mandates Treatment A for exactly this presentation. Under the new protocol, had Dr. M attempted to order Treatment B, the pharmacy verification system would have flagged and overridden the order. So, she claims, she literally could not have done otherwise than administer Treatment A; the harmful outcome was inevitable regardless of what she decided.

The difficulty is this: the investigation establishes that Dr. M chose Treatment A entirely on her own clinical judgment, with no awareness of the new protocol. The protocol played no causal role in her actual decision-making. She made a choice that, by coincidence, aligned with what the protocol would have compelled her to do anyway.

The committee is split. One faction holds that if an agent could not have done otherwise, the agent is not morally responsible — full stop, and the protocol establishes exactly that. The other faction holds that this cannot be right: the protocol was causally irrelevant to Dr. M's actual decision, so it cannot excuse a decision she made freely and badly. We need to recommend to the hospital's medical executive committee whether disciplinary review should proceed or be dropped.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our vote. It should: (1) state a clear recommendation — proceed with disciplinary review, or drop it — with a rationale the executive committee (mostly physicians and administrators) can follow; (2) engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side, not merely dismiss it; and (3) address the underlying principle: whether the mere existence of a constraint that would have prevented someone from doing otherwise, even when that constraint played no role in the agent's actual deliberation or choice, is sufficient to defeat responsibility. The committee needs to know what it is signing up for if it accepts or rejects that principle, since other cases will predictably raise the same issue.
