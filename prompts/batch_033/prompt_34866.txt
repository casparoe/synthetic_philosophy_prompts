I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a module called 'Ethics, Numbers, and Conflict', and we have spent the last three weeks on the debate that John Taurek ignited with his 1977 paper 'Should the Numbers Count?' (Philosophy & Public Affairs, Vol. 6, No. 4). The central case is maddeningly simple: you can rescue either one stranger or five strangers, no relevant differences between any of them, no special ties, no time to do anything but pick one group or the other. Yet the seminar keeps splintering because people apparently agree on what to do — save the five — but disagree radically about why, and then a quiet minority insists we should not simply save the five at all. Our lecturer has gestured at a taxonomy of positions but never laid it out systematically, and my study group is struggling to see the logical terrain.

I would like you to build us a decision-tree handout that sorts someone into a position based purely on their answers to concrete diagnostic questions. Start from the basic rescue case above. Each branch node should be a question about what matters in that choice, phrased so someone can answer without knowing the philosophical labels. The leaves must include at least: straightforward aggregation (the claims of the five jointly outweigh the claim of the one), Taurek's equal-respect coin-flip view, the weighted-lottery proposal in which each group receives chances proportional to its size, and the family of views that insist we should save the five but deny that this is because individual claims aggregate — sometimes called pairwise-comparison or contractualist approaches. Please flag any logically possible combinations of answers that do not seem to correspond to a position anyone actually defends, with a sentence on whether that combination looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Also flag any leaves your tree cannot separate: for instance, if two distinct positions give the same answer to every question you ask, note where the tree's diagnostic power runs out and what a further question would need to target. The aim is a tool we can use in study group to discover that someone who thought they were a straightforward aggregationist is actually committed to something quite different once they answer the intermediate questions honestly.
