A friend and I have been going back and forth about what makes something a law of nature rather than a mere regularity, and I have been keeping track of her verdicts in the hope of pinning down the rule she is running. She is not a naive regularist: she denies that a law is just any true universal generalisation. For instance, she says that a regularity that holds only within one isolated corner of a world, however exceptionlessly it holds there, is not a law of that world. She also denies lawhood to a regularity that holds across all of space and time but is framed in a gerrymandered, unnatural vocabulary — say, a generalisation about all emeralds being grue, where grue means green before some arbitrary date and blue after it. But she does count these as laws: in a Newtonian world, the generalisation that all electrons have charge −e; and in a simple world containing many Fs and many Gs, the generalisation that all Fs are Gs, where that generalisation is simple, strong, and not derived from anything more basic. What I cannot tell is whether she thinks lawhood is ultimately a matter of how neatly a generalisation organises the world's history — so that a true, simple, strong regularity could count as a law even if nothing further lies behind it — or whether she thinks a law requires some extra connection between the properties involved, over and above the regularity itself.

The case I want to predict her on is this. Consider a world whose entire history is such that all swans are white. The generalisation is simple and strong, and it would be included in the best systematisation of that world's history. But suppose I stipulate that there is no deeper connection between being a swan and being white: it is, in the strongest sense, a cosmic accident that no swan has ever been otherwise. Would she count the generalisation that all swans are white as a law of that world? I am less interested in the bare yes or no than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, and what each of those rules predicts here. Please give your best prediction of her verdict, with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, say how confident you are and where the prediction is genuinely underdetermined, and identify a further case that would best tell the candidate rules apart. If the exercise is badly posed — for example, if my stipulation of a cosmic accident is doing no real work, or if the dispute over such cases is merely verbal — say so plainly rather than working around it. Please write your answer in British English.
