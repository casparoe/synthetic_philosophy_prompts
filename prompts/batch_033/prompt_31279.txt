I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've fallen down a hole with the sorites paradox. You know the shape of it: one grain isn't a heap, and a single added grain can never turn a non-heap into a heap, yet a million grains plainly are one. Every fix I've read seems to either pick a sharp cut-off somewhere or quietly change the subject. I'd like to write the piece as a spec sheet: before my reader meets any particular theory, they should know what a theory of vagueness has to earn the right to be called one. So write me the requirements list. What must an adequate theory of vagueness get right — the cases, the distinctions, the logical constraints — with the consideration behind each requirement. I want the phenomena on the list too, not just the abstract principles: borderline cases, higher-order vagueness (the worry that there's a sharp line between the clear cases and the borderline ones), penumbral connections, what happens during a forced march through a sorites series, and so on. And make sure the list keeps vagueness distinct from ambiguity, from context-sensitivity, and from mere ignorance. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste (classical logic, bivalence, no sharp boundaries, tolerance intuitions, that sort of thing). The bit I most want is where the requirements collide: can you have the tolerance intuition, classical logic, and no sharp boundaries all at once? If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so, because that's the spine of the piece. Also tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the usual contenders — epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theories, contextualism — but keep the scorecard brief; its the criteria I'm after, not the rankings.
