Argue that the common prior assumption, as deployed in Aumann's agreement theorem and the broader epistemic foundations of game theory, is not a harmless modeling idealisation but rather embodies a substantive and contestable epistemological thesis—namely, that all differences in the posterior beliefs of ideally rational agents must be attributable entirely to differences in their private information. In making your argument, address the following points. First, explain why the common prior assumption cannot be justified merely by appeal to a diachronic Dutch book argument, since such an argument presupposes that the betting setup itself is framed in a way that agents with different priors would find mutually acceptable, which is precisely what is at issue. Second, consider the objection that rejecting the common prior assumption opens the door to an unattractive form of epistemic relativism in which any belief, no matter how bizarre, can be immunised from rational criticism by appeal to a divergent prior. Explain why this objection, while serious, conflates the claim that rational agents may permissibly begin from different priors with the claim that any prior whatsoever is rationally permissible. Finally, defend the view that the common prior assumption functions not as an expression of a norm of rationality but as a modelling choice that, when treated as a rationality requirement, silently rules out the possibility that rational disagreement can stem from fundamentally different but equally reasonable ways of weighing uncertainty—a possibility that deserves to be taken seriously in both epistemology and the social sciences.
