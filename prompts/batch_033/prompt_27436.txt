ok so im working through spinozas ethics and im stuck on a move he makes in part 1, proposition viii, when hes arguing that substance is necessarily infinite. the passage goes like this:

"There can only be one substance with an identical attribute, and existence follows from its nature (Prop. vii.) ; its nature, therefore, involves existence, either as finite or infinite. It does not exist as finite, for (by Def. ii.) it would then be limited by something else of the same kind, which would also necessarily exist (Prop. vii.) ; and there would be two substances with an identical attribute, which is absurd (Prop. v.). It therefore exists as infinite."

i get the basic structure—he's doing a proof by cases and eliminating the finite horn. but what i dont understand is why the possibility of being "limited by something else of the same kind" is supposed to be built into the very concept of being finite in the first place. like, why cant a substance just be finite without anything else actually limiting it? the way he leans on definition ii here feels like hes smuggling in a pretty substantive metaphysical claim under the guise of a definition—that finitude just *is* being limited by another thing of the same nature. is this supposed to be obvious from the definition of "finite after its kind" that he gives, or is there a deeper argument about grounding that i'm missing? and why does this whole move entitle him to say substance is infinite, rather than just "not known to be finite"? i feel like theres something about his notion of what it means to be a self-caused, independent thing thats doing the real work but i cant quite articulate it. help?
