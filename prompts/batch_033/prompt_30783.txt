Write a terza rima — four tercets and a closing couplet, fourteen lines in all, in iambic pentameter — about origin essentialism: the Kripkean thesis that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg. Let the form carry the argument rather than merely frame it. In terza rima, each tercet's middle rhyme is taken up as the next tercet's outer rhymes, so the poem can move forward only by inheriting what the previous stanza left at its center; use that interlocking to make the reader feel why 'I might have been another child' is not a near-miss about me but a description of someone else. The poem should work through the naive thought — conception feels contingent, another sperm might have won, and a child much like me would have been born — and turn, around the third tercet, to the essentialist correction: that child would have its own first-person perspective; the 'I' doing the asking is fixed by this origin; and the apparent contingency of conception is epistemic — a matter of what no one could have known in advance — rather than a metaphysical possibility that I exist with a different origin. The closing couplet should state the necessity of origin without overclaiming. It should not say that the actual gametes uniquely determine a person, since identical twins share an origin; the claim is only that a different origin would have been a different person. A philosopher of language should recognize the argument in the poem's movement, and a reader who has never encountered it should still find the poem good.
