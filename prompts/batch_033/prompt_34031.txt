I'm a PhD student in philosophy, mostly working on practical reason and trying to get a better grip on decision theory. At a workshop a few weeks ago an economist gave a quick summary of the Allais paradox that I scribbled down, and I've been turning it over since. Here's roughly what he said:

"The Allais paradox demolishes the independence axiom. You give people two choices. First, a sure million dollars or a gamble: 89% chance of a million, 10% chance of five million, 1% chance of nothing. Everyone takes the sure million. Then you give them a second choice: an 11% chance of a million or a 10% chance of five million. Everyone takes the 10% chance of five million. But this is inconsistent. The independence axiom says your preference between two gambles shouldn't change when you mix both with the same third gamble in the same proportion. And if you mix an 11% chance of a million with an 89% chance of a million, you get a sure million; if you mix a 10% chance of five million with an 89% chance of a million, you get the first gamble from the original pair. So the independence axiom requires you to prefer the 11% chance of a million in the second choice, since you preferred the sure million in the first. But almost no one does. So the axiom is false, and since the independence axiom is the backbone of expected utility theory, the whole framework is empirically refuted. Decision theory has had to move on to prospect theory and other models that take these patterns seriously."

I've read enough to know there's something off here — it feels like he's running together a descriptive claim about what people actually do with a normative claim about what the axiom demands, and the "empirically refuted" language seems way too strong given how many economists still work within expected utility theory. But I don't know the terrain well enough to seperate the fair parts from the unfair ones, and I'm not sure whether my suspicions are on track or whether I'm just uncomfortable with the conclusion. Could you go through this summary and tell me what a decision theorist would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the final claim about the whole framework being refuted actually follows from the accurate parts?
