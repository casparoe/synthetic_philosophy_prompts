A colleague of mine introduces metaphysical grounding to non-metaphysicians by calling it metaphysical causation. His standard line is that the dependence between a cause and its effect has the same shape as the dependence between the microstructure of the water and the water's being hot, except that grounding runs synchronically between facts at different levels of fundamentality instead of diachronically between events. He then leans on the analogy for a specific conclusion: we are all realists about causation — we do not treat it as a mere projection of our explanatory interests — and since grounding is just causation's metaphysical counterpart, we should be realists about grounding too, as a relation out in the world rather than a mere manner of speaking. I suspect the analogy is carrying that conclusion rather than supporting it, but I want the failure located precisely.

Audit the analogy for me, and give me a committed verdict rather than a balanced survey. I want an explicit correspondence table: what in the grounding case is supposed to play the role of the cause, the effect, the temporal order, the mechanism, and the laws. Then I want the disanalogies that matter for his realism argument separated from the harmless ones — in particular, which features of causation (temporality, contingency, production, manipulability, nomic connection) the analogy quietly imports and whether grounding actually has them. Finally, give a definite ruling on whether the step from causation's mind-independence to grounding's mind-independence survives once the disanalogies are on the table; if it does not, say what the analogy can legitimately be used to establish and whether some other analogy would do the work he wants.
