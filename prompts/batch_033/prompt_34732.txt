I've been working through the causal exclusion argument on my own over the past year or so, and I've ended up convinced that it poses a genuine dilemma for anyone who isn't a reductive physicalist. The rough idea, as I understand it, is that if every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, and mental properties are distinct from physical properties, then mental properties seem to be causally idle — they're screened off by the physical causes. That leaves you with either reduction (identifying mental properties with physical ones) or epiphenomenalism, and neither is appealing if you think conscious experience actually does something.

What I want to know is: what would count against this dilemma? I'm not asking for a summary of the standard responses — I've read about counterfactual theories, interventionist accounts, the subset view, and so on. What I want is a map of what the position actually risks. Which assumptions in the exclusion argument could in principle be rejected, what would the world have to look like for each rejection to be plausible, and which of these are live options rather than mere logical possibilities? And I'd like an honest assessment of whether the argument has been insulated through qualifications to the point where nothing could really count against it — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a metaphysical argument of this kind, or just how these arguments work.

Please write in British English.
