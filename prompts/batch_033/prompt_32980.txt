I've been messing around with these new language models — the ones that can write essays and have conversations — and there's this one thing that's been nagging at me for months now. I'm not a philosopher, I'm a software developer, so maybe I'm just missing the obvious term, but here's what I keep noticing.

These models can describe the taste of a strawberry. They can tell you what it feels like to stub your toe, or the particular loneliness of being in a crowded room, or the way wet wool smells. And they do it well — sometimes better than I could. But they've never tasted anything, never felt pain, never been lonely, never smelled anything. They've only ever seen text. So all those descriptions are just... patterns. Statistical regularities. There's nothing on the other end of the words. And yet if you read the output without knowing where it came from, you'd swear the author had lived a life.

I'm not asking about whether machines can be conscious, or the Turing test, or the Chinese Room — I've at least heard of those. What I'm after is something more specific. It's the gap between being able to talk about X as if you understand X, and actually having the thing that X refers to anywhere in your experience. It's like the words are floating free of the world. I know philosophers must have a term for how language hooks onto reality — reference, or grounding, or something — but I'm not sure if that's the same issue. Is there an established name for this particular configuration, where a system has total linguistic mastery of a domain it has zero access to? And if the answer is some concept I haven't heard of, I want to know whether it was built for exactly this situation or whether what we're seeing now with these models is a weird edge case of it — because the original philosophical discussions of this stuff were happening decades before anyone had systems that could actually pull off the trick at this scale.
