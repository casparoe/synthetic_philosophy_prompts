I serve on the strategic policy committee of a national research funding council. We are revising our guidelines for how to handle situations where multiple research groups are pursuing the same high-stakes goal — a vaccine platform, a quantum supremacy milestone, a novel battery chemistry. In the past we have often funded several groups in parallel, reasoning that competition accelerates progress and that we cannot reliably pick the winner ex ante. But our budget is finite, and critics argue that parallel funding creates a commitment race: each group, once funded, must keep spending to stay ahead, and the aggregate cost can exceed the social value of reaching the goal a few months sooner. Others reply that parallel approaches are not pure duplication — different groups explore different technical paths — and that the real waste would be betting everything on one team that hits a dead end.

Please draft a policy memo for the committee that recommends a concrete framework for deciding when to fund parallel competing efforts and when to concentrate resources on a single team. The memo should engage seriously with the structure of commitment races — the conditions under which they generate net benefits versus wasteful duplication — and should give criteria our review panels can actually apply. It should also treat fairly the strongest argument for the losing side.
