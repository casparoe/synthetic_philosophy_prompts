I'm on my university's IRB and we're reviewing a behavioral econ experiment where subjects play a variant of the Pasadena game for real money. Quick background: the Pasadena game is that thing where you flip a coin til it lands tails, and if it lands tails on flip n you get paid (-1)^(n-1) * (2^n)/n dollars. The expected value is the alternating harmonic series, which converges conditionally but not absolutely — so the EV is undefined (you can reorder the terms to get any value you like). The researchers want to see whether people avoid gambles with undefined EVs when the weirdness is explained to them. Half the board thinks this is flatly unethical: informed consent requires the participant to assess whether the gamble is favorable, and here there's literally no fact of the matter. The other half says that's nonsense, people face undefined-EV situations all the time (any heavy-tailed risk), and anyway the point is to study how people handle exactly that. Draft the decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions. I need reasoning that actually grapples with the philosophy of undefined expected values and what it means for consent, not just a recitation of the regs.
