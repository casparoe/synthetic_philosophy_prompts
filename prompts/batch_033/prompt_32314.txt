I've been going down a rabbit hole of arguments about whether things like depression or ADHD are "real diseases" — partly online, partly one long argument with my brother — and I keep noticing a two-step move that I don't have a word for. Someone will defend the claim that a condition is a genuine medical illness by pointing to something factual: a brain scan shows a difference, there's a chemical imbalance, a medication changes something, it runs in families. But when you press on why that particular difference counts as an illness rather than just a variation (being very tall is a difference too, and so is perfect pitch), the answer switches registers completely: because it causes suffering, because the person can't hold a job, because their life is going badly. Then, if you point to someone with the same scan-difference who is doing fine, they'll say that person doesn't have the disorder — the scan-difference alone wasn't enough after all. So the factual part is doing the work of making it sound like a broken bone, and the evaluative part is doing the work of deciding who actually counts as ill, and people slide between the two without flagging the switch. It isn't exactly equivocation, because the two seperate claims are both true and compatible; it's more like the label has two jobs and the person pretends it's doing only one. I also notice the same thing at the level of the whole category: the list of what gets called a mental illness seems to track, at least roughly, what the surrounding society finds distressing or disapproves of, which is hard to square with the "it's just a biological fact" story unless the two parts are doing different jobs all along. Is there a name for this pattern — in philosophy of medicine or psychiatry — the way a diagnosis slides between "there is a measurable difference" and "the difference is bad for the person or for other people"? And how does it fit with the old argument about whether mental illness is a myth or an illness in the same sense as strep throat? I should say: I'm not just after a label. I want to know exactly what the move is, whether it's a fallacy or just a feature of how disease concepts work, and where the edges of it are.
