I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of logic, and we spent a week on Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." I've been turning it over in my head and I'm starting to think the argument is more devastating than my professor let on.

As I understand the setup: the tortoise accepts that things equal to the same are equal to each other, and that the two sides of this triangle are things equal to the same, but refuses to accept that the two sides are equal to each other. Achilles, trying to force the tortoise's hand, adds a new premise: if the first two premises are true, then the conclusion must be true. The tortoise now accepts all three premises but still will not accept the conclusion, and demands yet another premise to bridge the gap. This regress, it seems, never bottoms out.

What I take from this is that deductive inference itself can never be rationally justified. To justify moving from premises to a conclusion, you need a rule of inference — but justifying that rule requires a meta-rule, and so on ad infinitum. So at the bottom of all deductive reasoning there is just a brute leap, a kind of epistemic animal faith. Isn't this a skeptical result about the very possibility of rational deduction? And if so, why don't philosophers of logic treat it as a crisis on a par with Hume's problem of induction?
