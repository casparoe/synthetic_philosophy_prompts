Imagine a county that uses a pretrial risk-assessment instrument to help judges decide whom to detain before trial. The instrument produces a risk score from 1 to 10, and judges are directed to treat scores above a fixed cutoff as creating a presumption in favor of detention. An audit finds that the instrument is well calibrated with respect to two racial groups: among defendants with any given score, the rate of rearrest is approximately the same for both groups. But the audit also finds that among defendants who are not rearrested, Black defendants are assigned scores above the cutoff at a substantially higher rate than White defendants, so the false positive rate is higher for one group. In response, the county directs the vendor to reweight the score so that the false positive rate at the cutoff is equal across groups. The vendor warns that, given the data, this will require making the scores miscalibrated: at some score levels the empirical rearrest rate will now differ by group. The county accepts this tradeoff.

What theory of fairness does the county's decision presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can: what is it treating as the central wrong to be avoided, and which rival views of algorithmic fairness does the choice quietly rule out or demote? Distinguish what the decision strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with: could the same decision be justified under several different fairness theories, and if so, which parts of the practice would each theory explain differently? Finally, the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness show that, in general, a score cannot simultaneously be calibrated across groups, have equal false positive rates, and have equal false negative rates when base rates differ and prediction is imperfect. What would the county have to change about its instrument, its cutoff rule, or its public justification if it instead treated calibration as the non-negotiable requirement, and would that alternative practice presuppose a different theory of what makes a risk score fair?
