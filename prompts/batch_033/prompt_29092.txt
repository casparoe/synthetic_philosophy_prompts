I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether the regress in Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" is vicious or benign. My friend holds that the regress exposes a genuine justificatory gap: deduction cannot be non-circularly justified, because any attempt to justify an inference rule either uses that very rule or launches an infinite regress. I hold that the Tortoise makes a category mistake by treating a rule of inference as if it were a premise, and the regress is an artifact of that mistake — it doesn't show anything deep about deduction, only that you can't force someone who refuses to infer to do so by adding more premises. We reached an impasse, and I think we need a thought experiment that lets us see the dispute from a different angle.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

The scenario: An agent — call her Dana — adopts the Tortoise's policy as her explicit, self-imposed reasoning procedure. That is, Dana will only take an inferential step from premises she accepts to a conclusion if she also explicitly accepts, as an additional premise, the inference rule that licenses that step. She is otherwise rational, well-informed, and acting in good faith. Place her in a realistic, high-stakes situation where she must complete a multi-step deductive inference to prevent a serious harm. Suitable settings: a physician working through a differential diagnosis with a treatment deadline, an engineer checking a safety interlock before a scheduled launch, a lawyer determining whether a statute of limitations has run. No trolleys, no demons, no science fiction. The scenario should be detailed enough that we can trace Dana's reasoning step by step.

Walk through what happens. At each inferential step, show how Dana's procedure generates the regress. Does she reach the conclusion in time? If not, what exactly stops her — does she get stuck in an infinite task, hit a practical limit and abandon the procedure, or something else? Does the harm materialize?

Then answer these questions:

1. Which specific feature of the scenario is doing the work to reveal whether the regress is vicious or benign? If you removed the time pressure and made the stakes low — Dana is reasoning at leisure with nothing on the line — would her procedure become viable, or does the regress cause trouble even without a deadline?

2. Suppose a defender of the Tortoise objects that your scenario is unfair. The real issue, they say, is justificatory rather than practical: the Tortoise's point is that any attempt to justify modus ponens without using modus ponens fails, and Dana's practical breakdown doesn't address that. How would you reply using the materials of the scenario? Does Dana's failure illuminate the justificatory question at all, or does it only show that the Tortoise's policy is a bad idea for agents with finite time?

3. If Dana pre-accepts a finite list of inference rules before the high-stakes situation begins — she writes down modus ponens, universal instantiation, and whatever else she'll need, and treats them as accepted — does that solve her problem or just relocate the regress to the question of why she should accept that particular list? What does your answer imply about the original dispute between me and my friend?
