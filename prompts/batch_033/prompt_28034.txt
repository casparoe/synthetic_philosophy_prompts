I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy, politics, and economics program, currently taking an upper-level seminar called The Moral Limits of Markets. Next week I have to present and defend a term-paper argument in front of the seminar. The paper argues that a carefully regulated public monopsony for kidneys from living donors is morally permissible and should be piloted: the state would be the sole legal buyer, would pay a fixed and non-negotiable sum plus comprehensive follow-up care, and would allocate organs strictly according to medical urgency rather than ability to pay. My argument has three main moves. First, prohibition does not eliminate a market for kidneys; it pushes the trade underground, where donors face worse exploitation, worse medical screening, and worse outcomes. Second, a public monopsony can control the forms of pressure, fraud, and inequality that make kidney sales most troubling, so many of the standard objections are objections to badly designed markets rather than to this design. Third, the residual objections that say there is something degrading or wrong in itself about buying a kidney rest on an expressive claim about what such a transaction means, and that claim is shakier than the harm-based case for allowing a regulated market to save lives.

The seminar audience is mixed, and I need to be ready for the question period. Several philosophy students have strong Kantian and virtue-ethics commitments and are likely to press on dignity, objectification, and the kind of person one becomes by participating in such a system. Two public-health students work on transplant policy and are most worried about exploitation of poor donors, informed consent under financial pressure, and long-term donor health. One economics student is broadly sympathetic to markets but skeptical that a government monopsony would actually be run well. The instructor has written on commodification and tends to reject both naïve economism and blanket prohibition. Please prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they are likely to arise, each phrased the way these particular people would phrase it, and ordered so I can see which are most damaging. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, an honest note on where the reply is soft, and a flag for what I should concede up front rather than try to defend. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it would hurt.
