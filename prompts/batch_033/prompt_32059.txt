In a philosophy of biology seminar, a colleague has been arguing that the genome is literally a code, not just a metaphor, and that this licenses a strong conclusion: just as you can debug a program once you understand the source code, we will eventually be able to predict and repair organism-level traits once we fully map genotypes to phenotypes. On their view, the cell's reading of DNA is a decoding operation in the same sense that a compiler translates source code into machine code, and the central dogma of molecular biology makes this precise.

I want to evaluate how far this analogy actually holds. Please provide:

1. A correspondence table: what in the computational domain maps onto what in the genetic domain (e.g., what plays the role of the code, the compiler, the machine language, the programmer, the bug, the patch)?

2. The most important disanalogies — the places where the mapping breaks and where the computational picture imports features that the genetic domain lacks.

3. A judgment about whether the colleague's conclusion — that mapping genotype to phenotype will eventually yield the kind of predictive and reparative control we have over software — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy exists.
