A couple of my grad students got into it after epistemology reading group last week, and they've both been at me to settle it. I have my own view, but I want an independent read on the exchange itself before I say anything. The topic was the dogmatism paradox — the argument that if you know p, and you know p entails that any evidence against p is misleading, then by closure under known entailment you're in a position to know that any counterevidence is misleading, so you can rationally set it aside. Which sure looks like it licenses ignoring evidence. Here's the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Nadia: Take an ordinary case where I know p. I just watched my colleague walk into her office, so I know she's in there. Now someone tells me she slipped out the back five minutes ago. If I really know she's in her office, then I know any evidence that she isn't is misleading. So I'm entitled to set that report aside without investigating. The paradox is supposed to make that look scandalous, but its just what knowledge is for — it keeps you from being knocked around by every bit of conflicting testimony.

(2) Leo: But the same move is available to someone who falsely believes she's out. From his point of view he "knows" she left, so he can dismiss my watching her walk in as misleading. Your rule gives everyone a license to ignore everyone else — two people with flatly contradictory views each get to keep them forever, and both count as rational. That's a reductio, not a consequence to embrace.

(3) Nadia: No, because the license attaches to knowledge, not to mere belief. The guy who falsely believes she's out doesn't satisfy the antecedent. The rule isn't "if you think you know, you may ignore counterevidence"; it's "if you know, you may ignore counterevidence." The fact that you can't always tell from the inside who satisfies it doesn't make the rule false. It just makes it hard to apply, like plenty of norms.

(4) Leo: But that's exactly the problem. A norm you can't apply from the inside isn't a norm of rational belief at all. Rationality has to guide us from our own perspective. If the person with the false belief can't tell he doesn't know, your rule gives him no usable guidance — and the paradox's whole interest was that it seemed to give each of us, from the inside, a reason to dismiss counterevidence. Saying "it only applies to real knowledge" changes the subject.

(5) Nadia: You're running together two different things. The claim is about what my evidence supports, not about what I should do with my afternoon. I might still go check the office, out of caution or politeness or because I care about being wrong. But that's practical, not epistemic. If I know p, and I know that anything conflicting with p is misleading, then my confidence in p shouldn't drop when the report arrives. That's the real thesis.

(6) Leo: And that's the most implausible part. Suppose I'm a juror who knows, on strong evidence, that the defendent is guilty. The defense produces a signed confession from someone else. On your view I shouldn't lower my confidence in guilt one bit — I already know any exculpatory evidence is misleading. But any rational juror's confidence would move at least a little. So either you're committed to saying the juror shouldn't be moved, which is false, or you're quietly retreating to the practical point, which doesn't answer the question. The question is whether the new evidence should move me. I say it should — and that's exactly why the paradox shows the closure step has to go.

(7) Nadia: Not quite. When the confession actually arrives, my evidence changes: I now know the confession exists, and that's a new fact about the world I didn't know before. I can know the defendant is guilty and also know the confession is misleading; but the confession's existence can still matter to all sorts of other things. The paradox only says I may dismiss it as evidence against guilt. It doesn't say I learn nothing at all.

(8) Leo: That's a distinction without a difference for credence. If I know in advance that whatever exculpatory evidence appears will be misleading, then the existence of this particular piece of misleading evidence shouldn't lower my credence in guilt. You keep saying there are other things I might learn — fine. But the original question stands: should my confidence in p drop? You have to say no. I say yes, and that's the reductio.

Okay, there's the exchange. I'd like you to go through it move by move: for each numbered step, say who's right about what, where each overreaches or misses the other's point, and then say who's more right overall and what both of them are failing to put their finger on.
