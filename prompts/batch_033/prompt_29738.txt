I've been collecting my own verdicts on cases where someone deliberately makes themselves less rational — through precommitment, self-deception, or changing their own desires — and I want to know whether one principle captures them.

- Stranded hitchhiker. A driver stops but won't trust a promise to pay on arrival, because he knows I'd have no reason to pay once I'm safe in town. If I could take a pill that would make me keep the promise regardless of my interests, I'd take it, and I think doing so would be rational — even though the pill makes me less responsive to my reasons at the moment of paying.
- Ulysses. Binding himself to the mast so that his later self can't steer toward the Sirens seems plainly rational.
- Negotiating pill. A negotiator takes a drug that makes her genuinely unable to accept any offer below a floor, so that her refusal is credible. I think that's fine, and rational.
- Smoking contract. Someone pays a third party to send a large sum to a cause he detests if he smokes again. Fine, rational.
- Cultivated rage. Someone deliberately cultivates a disposition to fly into costly rages so that people won't cross him, knowing the rages will sometimes cost him more than giving in would have. I don't think that's rational, though the mechanism looks the same as the others.
- False-belief pill. A student takes a pill that makes him believe he will succeed, though the belief is false; believing it makes him work harder, and he ends up succeeding. I want to say this isn't rational — he bought success with a false belief.
- Promise chip. A person has a device implanted that will force him to keep every promise he makes, including promises extracted by duress or trickery; he knows this in advance. I don't think that's rational.
- Fraudulent driver. The hitchhiker case again, but the driver is a known con man who takes the money and drives off. I still want to say the pill was rational at the time, but I'm less sure.

The pair that bothers me: the negotiating pill and the false-belief pill feel different, though both are cases of deliberately inducing a locally irrational mental state to gain a strategic benefit. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what should the principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on — someone who undergoes a procedure that makes her permanently and genuinely care more about keeping promises than about her own welfare, knowing she will sometimes be exploited by people who know she's been altered?
