I have spent enough time reading Mencius and Xunzi side by side to be puzzled by a very old impasse. The two are standardly presented as holding opposite views about human nature, and the opposition has remained live for over two millennia without either side moving the other. Yet on the concrete claims that would seem to matter, they agree far more than the standard opposition suggests. Both hold that ordinary people can become sages. Both hold that goodness requires cultivation, the rites, a teacher, and sustained effort, and neither thinks virtue comes about automatically or survives neglect. Mencius locates the good in innate sprouts: the spontaneous distress at seeing a child about to fall into a well, the inborn sense of shame and deference, tendencies that need favorable conditions and patient extension to mature into full virtues. Xunzi locates the bad in the raw material of human nature: inborn desires for benefit and comfort that, left unchecked, produce contention and disorder, so that goodness is not grown but made, through artifice, ritual, and accumulated effort imposed on that material. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is largely a verbal one about the word xing: Mencius is using it for the inborn normative endowment, the incipient tendencies that can be developed into virtue, while Xunzi is using it for the starting state before cultivation, the raw desires and capacities that must be reshaped. If that is right, the two are closer to compatible than contradictory. But I can see other diagnoses: an empirical disagreement about what human beings would be like absent all cultivation, a state neither side can directly observe; a disagreement about which cases count as data, the child's untaught compassion or the adult's untaught greed; or a normative disagreement about whether to characterize a thing by its best or its worst starting point. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, grounded in what each side actually says, and an honest account of what would count as progress given that diagnosis, whether that is a distinction, an empirical finding, a reframing of the question, or, if you think no progress should be expected, an argument for why this is a dispute in which the best available outcome is a precise statement of what the two sides are not actually disagreeing about.
