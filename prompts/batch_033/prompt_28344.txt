I have recently been placed on the governing board of a mid-sized foundation that is deciding whether to undertake a politically exposed and financially risky initiative during an unusually unstable moment. A senior board member keeps invoking Machiavelli’s line “fortune is a woman” to cut off discussion of downside planning. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it, is that uncertain times reward audacity, since fortune yields to force and boldness, while caution merely waits for a stable situation that will not arrive. I would like not simply to reject the slogan on grounds of anachronism or its gendered phrasing, but to understand exactly what it might be claiming. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that someone could mean by “fortune is a woman” in this kind of context. In particular, separate readings concerning the phenomenology of luck or accident; the causal power of impetuousness; the claim that prudential deliberation is self-defeating under volatility; the relation between fortune and virtù; the claim that Fortuna is best managed by force rather than by foresight or accommodation; and any reading that treats the slogan as a claim about women rather than about fortune. For each candidate thesis, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or likely false, and say which reading or readings would actually support the conclusion that the foundation should commit to the risky initiative rather than await more information. The deliverable should function as a disambiguated menu with verdicts, not a single interpretation.
