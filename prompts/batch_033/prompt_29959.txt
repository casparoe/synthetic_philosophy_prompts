I keep coming back to pain asymbolia — the condition where a person will tell you a stimulus hurts, and can rate its intensity and location, but doesn't pull away, grimace, or seem to mind it at all. Its made me suspicious of the idea that the badness of a pain is built into the sensation itself, but the clinical reports feel messy, so I want a cleaner case built to order.

Build me a thought experiment in which the sensory side of a pain is held fixed — same location, same intensity, same quality — while the evaluative/affective side (whether the pain is minded, unwanted, aversive) varies between two conditions, so that two theories of pain's badness are forced to give opposite verdicts. The theories are: (i) the badness of a pain is intrinsic to the painful sensation itself, and (ii) the badness is a separate evaluative or affective response to the sensation, which is exactly what asymbolia seems to show can go missing. Constraints: realistic (a pain clinic, a burn unit, a long-term meditator — not a philosophy lab), one paragraph for the scenario, no stipulating the verdicts, and the difference between the two conditions can't lean on tissue damage or social consequences; it has to live in the pain experience itself, or in the subject's relation to it. This is for a paper I'm sketching on whether pain's badness is intrinsic, so a case that simply declares one pain bad and the other not is useless to me.

Then do the check. In "The setup" (about 100 words), give the two conditions and state exactly what is held fixed and what varies. In "The intrinsic view" (about 150 words), say what theory (i) says about each condition and why. In "The evaluative view" (about 150 words), do the same for theory (ii). In "What's doing the work" (about 100 words), identify the single feature of the case that forces the disagreement, and say what would happen if you removed it. Finish with "The intrinsic theorist's reply" (about 100 words): what would a defender of (i) say to resist the case, and whether that reply is any good.
