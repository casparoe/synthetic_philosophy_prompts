I've been dipping into the philosophical literature on hypocrisy and the standing to blame — partly for a novel I'm working on where a character's moral authority is undercut by his own past — and I'm struck by how the debate seems to have just... stalled. One side says when a hypocrite blames you for something they do themselves, they lack the normative standing to issue the blame, even if what they say about your conduct is perfectly accurate. The other side says this "standing" talk is confused: either the blame is deserved or it isn't, and the blamer's own misconduct is a seperate question — relevant to whether *they're* blameworthy, sure, but it doesn't cancel *your* blameworthiness. Both sides have been saying versions of this for years and nobody moves.

As a novelist I spend a lot of time thinking about how people actually experience moral criticism from hypocrites — the particular sting of it, the way it can feel both true and impossible to hear at the same time. And I wonder whether the philosophical impasse is because both sides are answering different questions under the same heading. Is this a disagreement about the metaphysics of blame (whether "standing" is a real normative property or just a confused way of talking about something else), or about the phenomenology of being blamed, or is it a verbal dispute where "blame" means different things to different people? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction nobody's made, a different way of framing the question, or is this just one of those problems where the intuitions run too deep and we should stop expecting resolution?
