I've been reading the notes from Wittgenstein's 1938 lectures on religious belief, the ones published later in *Lectures and Conversations on Aesthetics, Psychology and Religious Belief*. The passage thats tripping me up is where he asks us to imagine someone who says they believe in the Last Judgment. He contrasts this with someone who says they believe, say, that a certain aeroplane is overhead. His point seems to be that the religious believer isnt using "believe" the way we do when were ready to give evidence or revise on the basis of evidence. Instead, the belief regulates the persons whole life, and the picture of the Last Judgment just is what the belief consists in — there isnt some further fact behind it. Then he says he wouldnt contradict such a person, even though he doesnt share the belief, because they arent playing the same language-game. I cant get clear on whether this means religious beliefs are insulated from rational criticism and can never be wrong, which sounds like a cop-out, or whether hes saying something subtler about the grammar of "belief" here. Could you walk me through what he might mean, and especially whether on this view a non-believer can still say the believer is mistaken?
