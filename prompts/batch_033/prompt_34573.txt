Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether an AI system can be made low-impact — in the technical sense of avoiding side effects — without first giving it a full specification of what we value. I came away holding the view that it cannot. Every concrete proposal I know of seems to fail in the same way. Penalizing divergence from an inaction baseline requires choosing the baseline; preserving other agents' ability to achieve their goals requires choosing which agents and which distribution over goals count; restricting the variables an agent may affect requires choosing which variables matter; bounding relative reachability requires choosing the state space and the relevant notion of reachability. Each of those choices is either arbitrary or is a value judgment smuggled in as a technical detail. On my view, "avoid side effects" is not an independent constraint that can be bolted onto an otherwise value-neutral system; it is a placeholder for a complete axiology, and any tractable impact measure either misses what we care about or quietly assumes it.

What would have to be true for this view to be false? I want the evidence, arguments, formal results, and worked-out mechanism designs that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward. Be specific about what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and which of those predictions could in principle be shown wrong — for example, predictions about the impossibility of learning an impact measure from human feedback, about putatively distinct measures collapsing into one another, or about value-neutrality being unattainable. Say which of these defeaters are live rather than merely logically possible, which a committed defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it, and which they would dismiss. And give an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
