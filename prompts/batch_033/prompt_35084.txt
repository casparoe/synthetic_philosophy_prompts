I'm a journalist working on a piece about typography and the cognitive science of reading, and I've fallen into a philosophical rabbit hole I can't climb out of. The question is deceptively simple: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Not in the dictionary-definition sense, but perceptually and categorically — across fonts, handwriting, historical scripts, what's the thing that makes us group radically different-looking shapes under the same letter? I started collecting edge cases and testing my own gut reactions, and my intuitions are all over the place. I'd love your help extracting a principle, or at least seeing which of my verdicts I've gotta give up.

Here are my verdicts. A standard double-story 'a' (Times New Roman): obviously an 'a'. A single-story 'a' (Futura, most handwriting): also an 'a', no pause. A capital 'A': yes, same letter, but it feels like it gets there by a different route — like uppercase and lowercase membership are two distinct subcategories. A sloppily handwritten 'a' where the bowl doesn't close: if I can read it in context, it counts; if I can't, it doesn't — but making my verdict depend on my own recognition bothers me. A Cyrillic 'а' (visually identical to Latin single-story 'a'): my gut says no, it's a different alphabet's letter that happens to look the same, so shape isn't sufficient. The @ symbol: not an 'a', even though it's historically an 'a'+'d' ligature — history seems irrelevant. An 'a' rotated 180°: no, even though it's the identical shape. A blackletter Fraktur 'a' that looks to modern eyes like an 'o' with a hat: intellectually I know it's an 'a', perceptually I resist it — I'm torn. A minimalist logo 'a' reduced to a circle with a short right-side stem: in context I see it, in isolation maybe not — another torn case. Greek alpha (α): no, not an 'a', even though it's the direct ancestor. But if descent doesn't matter for alpha, why does it seem to matter a little for capital 'A', which is equally Greek-derived?

I'm hoping you can give me exactly five candidate principles — each stated in a sentence or two — that could capture most of these verdicts, with no introduction or conclusion. For each principle, tell me which of my verdicts it handles cleanly and which one or two it forces me to abandon. And if you can, use the fifth slot to say what the best of the four predicts about a case I didn't list: an 'a' in an experimental typeface that no one would recognize without being told, but once told, they can't unsee it.
