I hold the view that under moral uncertainty — when we have credences distributed across several moral theories we cannot decisively rule out — the rational way to act is to maximize expected choiceworthiness: roughly, to weigh the choiceworthiness of each available action according to each theory by our credence in that theory, and then select the action with the highest expected score. What would count against this view? I want to know which counterexamples, formal results, or conceptual difficulties pose the most serious threat — not just the obvious worry about intertheoretic comparability of units, but the deeper structural problems. Which of these would a defender of maximizing expected choiceworthiness concede as genuine costs, and which would they dismiss as relying on a misunderstanding of the framework? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified — by restricting its domain, by retreating to a merely procedural reading, or by building in escape clauses — to the point where it no longer risks anything intuitive or structural, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind.
