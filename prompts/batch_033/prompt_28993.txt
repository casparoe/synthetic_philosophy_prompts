I'm a novelist, and I'm the faculty member on my college's academic integrity board. We've got a case that's been eating at me. A student says that in a one-on-one meeting about a grade, a professor made a disparaging remark about her disability. There were no other witnesses, no recording, no contemporaneous email or text; the professor denies it flatly and has twenty years of teaching with nothing like this on his record. Half the board thinks a single credible accuser is enough to find him responsible on a preponderance standard — you don't need corroboration if you find the person beliveable, and you're entitled to start from the assumption that people are telling the truth until something specific undermines them. The other half thinks that's epistemically reckless: without independent evidence about the reliability of this particular report, one person's word is just one person's word, and a finding has to rest on more than that. I keep going back and forth, and as somebody who writes fiction I can't help noticing that the two sides are really arguing about whether believing what people say is a basic source of knowledge, like seeing something yourself, or a derived one that has to be earned through other evidence. Please draft the short opinion memo I'll circulate before we vote on Thursday. It should recommend a concrete outcome — responsible, not responsible, or some intermediate disposition — give reasons the rest of the board can actually act on, and take the strongest version of the losing position seriously rather than knocking down a straw man. I'm the one who'll have to explain the vote to the student and the professor afterward, so the memo should be fair enough to read aloud to either of them.
