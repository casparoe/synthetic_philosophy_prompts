ok so i've been thinking about insurance underwriting and i can't figure out what philosophical move is being made there. here's the thing: an insurance company puts you in a risk pool based on a handful of variables — age, zip code, smoking status, whatever. but obviously you belong to infinitely many reference classes. you're not just "a 35-year-old nonsmoker in brooklyn," you're also a person with your specific genome, your specific commuting route, your specific diet, your specific sleep schedule. some of those narrower classes would spit out wildly different risk estimates. the company doesn't use them. it stops at some particular grain of classification and calls the rest irrelevant, or too expensive to measure, or whatever.

but here's where it gets wierd: the same company will absolutely use a narrower reference class when it suits them — if your credit score is bad, they'll slice things finer than if it's good. or they'll use your exact driving telemetry if you install their app, but not your exact genome even if the predictive power is higher. so it's not just a principled stopping rule about "the narrowest feasible class." some reference classes are treated as legitimate and others aren't, and it's not obviously tracking anything like predictive accuracy or measurement cost.

what theory of probability, or of evidence, or of fairness does this whole practice presuppose? like, what view of what makes a reference class the *right* one is built into the way insurance actually works? and is the practice coherent, or is it just a patchwork of different implicit theories that dont play nice together? what would have to change if you replaced the presupposed view with a different one — say, one where the only legitimate reference class is the narrowest one you can actually compute?
