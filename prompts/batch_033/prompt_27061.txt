I've been trying to understand what heritability numbers actually mean, and I keep running into a picture that I'd like someone to check carefully. The picture, which I've seen in online discussions and from science communicators, is that a twin study is essentially a controlled experiment run by nature: identical twins share all their genes, so comparing them is like holding the genetic variable fixed while letting the environment vary, and comparing identical with fraternal twins is like comparing a group that received 100 percent of a treatment with a group that received 50 percent. The conclusion people draw is that a heritability estimate cleanly separates how much of a trait is genetic from how much is environmental — so a heritability of 80 percent for height means height is 80 percent genetic and 20 percent environmental, in roughly the way a fertilizer trial might show that 80 percent of a yield difference is due to fertilizer.

I'd like an audit of that analogy. Please lay out explicitly what corresponds to what between a controlled experiment and a twin study: the random assignment, the control group, the treatment, the held-fixed background conditions, and so on. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks down, and for each disanalogy say whether it matters for the specific conclusion the analogy is being used to support. In particular, I'd like to know whether differences between identical twins raised apart really give a clean read on "the environment," whether identical twins actually share the relevant genes and environments in the way the experiment picture assumes, and whether the estimate would come out the same in a different population or environment. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that heritability separates genetic from environmental contributions the way a controlled experiment would — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it doesn't, what a better analogy would be.
