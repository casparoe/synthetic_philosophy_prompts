I've been working through the philosophical literature on responsibility gaps and autonomous systems, and I've hit an argument that I can't shake. The standard picture, as I understand it, is that increasingly capable AI systems — self-driving cars, medical diagnostic tools, algorithmic trading platforms — can cause harms that no human intended, foresaw, or could reasonably have prevented, and since the systems themselves are not moral agents, we face a gap: a harm with no responsible party. The usual response is that this is a practical problem to be managed through better regulation, insurance schemes, or design standards. But I've encountered a more radical line of argument that seems to show that either these gaps are everywhere or they are nowhere, and I can't tell whether it's a reductio of the whole idea or a reason to rethink responsibility from the ground up.

Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it. (1) Moral responsibility for an outcome requires that the agent could have done otherwise — the control condition. (2) When an autonomous system trained on large-scale data produces a harmful output that was not specifically intended, foreseen, or reasonably foreseeable by any human involved in its design, training, or deployment, no human satisfied the control condition with respect to that particular output. (3) Therefore, no human is morally responsible for that harm. (4) The system itself is not a moral agent, so it cannot bear responsibility. (5) Thus, there is a responsibility gap: a serious harm for which no one is morally responsible. (6) If responsibility gaps are acceptable in the case of autonomous systems, then by parity of reasoning they are acceptable in any case where an agent's conduct runs through a complex causal chain whose downstream consequences no one fully controls. (7) But that includes corporate wrongdoing (diffused through organisational structures), market harms (emerging from the aggregate of individually blameless decisions), and even some cases of negligent parenting (where the child's specific misdeed was not foreseeable). (8) So if we accept responsibility gaps for autonomous systems, we must accept that no one is morally responsible for an enormous range of serious harms — a conclusion that seems morally intolerable. (9) Therefore, either premise (1) or premise (2) must be false, and autonomous systems do not create genuine responsibility gaps after all; or the conclusion of (8) is true and our ordinary practices of holding people responsible are riddled with gaps we have chosen not to notice.

I find myself unable to choose between these options. If I reject (1), I seem to need a whole new theory of responsibility. If I reject (6), I need a principled disanalogy between autonomous systems and the other cases, and I'm not sure one exists. If I accept (8), I'm giving up on something central to how we hold each other accountable. Which step is the weakest, and what does it cost to give it up?
