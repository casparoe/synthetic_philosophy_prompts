I'm in a philosophy of biology course and we're on the units of selection debate, and I wrote this argument for my paper but now it feels like it proves to much and I can't find the exit. (1) Natural selection needs heritable variation in fitness. (2) For something to count as a unit of selection, it has to be copied faithfully across generations so that selection can accumulate changes in it. (3) Organisms and groups get broken up every generation, but genes get copied basically intact. (4) So the gene is the unit of selection, and organism- or group-level selection is just loose talk. (5) But 'gene' is fuzzy: recombination can split within a gene, a single nucleotide can mutate, and a whole linkage group gets inherited together — so by the same criterion the faithful copier is the nucleotide, or the codon, or the linkage group, not the gene. (6) So the gene's-eye view either picks its grain arbitrarily, or it admits the unit of selection is relative to the question your asking — which guts the claim in (4) that it's really genes. Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep (4), but I want to know which step to give up and what it costs. Please answer in American English.
