I have been working through the end of the second chapter of the *Zhuangzi*, in James Legge's 1891 translation, and I am having trouble seeing how the passage's final claim relates to the dream doubt that precedes it. Could you help me understand what philosophical work the closing remark is doing?

Here is the passage, from Legge's rendering of 'The Adjustment of Controversies':

"Formerly, I, Kwang Kâu, dreamt that I was a butterfly, a butterfly flying about, feeling that it was enjoying itself. I did not know that it was Kâu. Suddenly I awoke, and was myself again, the veritable Kâu. I did not know whether it had formerly been Kâu dreaming that he was a butterfly, or it was now a butterfly dreaming that it was Kâu."

The passage then goes on to say that between Kâu and a butterfly there must nevertheless be a distinction, and it calls this a case of the Transformation of Things.

My question is this: if the narrator cannot tell whether he is a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he is a man, why does the text immediately insist that there is, after all, a difference between the two? Is this meant to answer the sceptical worry, to change its subject, or to show that the worry rested on a false assumption? In particular, I would like help understanding how the notion of the Transformation of Things is supposed to bear on the dream problem rather than simply dissolving it. Please answer in British English.
