I've been covering health policy and research funding for several years, and I keep returning to a structural tension that seems to me to raise deep questions about cause prioritisation. The English National Health Service operates what is, on paper, a rigorous framework for deciding which treatments to fund. NICE — the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence — evaluates new therapies by calculating their cost per quality-adjusted life year, or QALY, and generally recommends against funding interventions that cost more than roughly £20,000 to £30,000 per QALY gained. The logic is straightforward and explicitly about opportunity cost: a pound wasted on a low-value treatment is a pound denied to some other patient whose need might be greater or more tractable. Yet from 2011, the same NHS ran the Cancer Drugs Fund, a dedicated pot of money — initially some £200 million a year — that paid for cancer drugs NICE had specifically declined to recommend, often at costs exceeding £50,000 or even £90,000 per QALY. The Fund was widely understood as a political fix: cancer patients are vivid, their advocates are well-organised, and no health minister wanted to be seen denying them drugs, whatever the cost-effectiveness arithmetic said.

I want to use this episode to think about what different practices of resource allocation presuppose about cause prioritisation in the specific sense developed in the effective altruism tradition — the idea that we should direct resources across causes by weighing importance (how much suffering or loss is at stake), tractability (how much progress a marginal unit of resource can make), and neglectedness (how many resources are already flowing to the problem). The NHS, like any fixed-budget health system, makes cross-cause trade-offs continually: a cancer drug versus more community nurses, a rare-disease therapy versus a diabetes prevention programme, a high-tech scanner versus better palliative care. NICE's QALY framework is one attempt to render those trade-offs systematic and transparent. The Cancer Drugs Fund was a decision to override that system for one category of patient. Reconstruct the implicit theory of cause prioritisation that each practice presupposes. In particular: does the NICE approach implicitly treat all QALYs as fungible and equally valuable regardless of who receives them, and what does that commit it to about the trade-off between severity and cost-effectiveness? Does the political override presuppose that identifiability, or perhaps the acuteness of the threat, should outweigh tractability considerations — and if so, is that a coherent alternative framework, or simply an inconsistency produced by political pressure? And stepping back: if a health system genuinely tried to allocate its entire budget by the importance-tractability-neglectedness triad, would it end up looking more like NICE, or would it diverge sharply — perhaps by directing far more toward prevention and public health, where tractability may be lower but importance and neglectedness are arguably higher?

I would be grateful if you could distinguish, in your response, between what is reasonably settled among people who work on health prioritisation — the existence of the tension, the unsustainability of the original Cancer Drugs Fund, the general case for some form of explicit cross-cause comparison — and what remains genuinely contested. For instance, whether a pure QALY-maximising framework is actually what an importance-tractability-neglectedness approach recommends, or whether severity weights, rare-disease premiums, and other equity considerations can be incorporated coherently into that framework without abandoning the core insight about opportunity cost.
