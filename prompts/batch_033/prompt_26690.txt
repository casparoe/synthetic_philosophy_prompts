In a seminar on climate ethics last week, a classmate dismissed the idea of carbon offsets with the line "you can't put a price on everything" and moved on as though the matter were settled. I have since noticed variants of the same move in discussions of organ markets, commercial surrogacy, and the practice of paying a substitute to serve in one's place during conscription. The slogan plainly has rhetorical force, but I am not sure what precise claim it is making. Is it a psychological claim about how pricing a good changes our attitude toward it — that markets crowd out nobler motives? A metaphysical claim about incommensurability — that some values resist cardinal comparison in a way that makes pricing them a category mistake? A deontic claim about the intrinsic wrongness of certain transactions, independent of their consequences? A claim about distributive justice — that allowing purchases of this kind will predictably concentrate access among the rich? Or something else entirely? I would like you to lay out the distinct theses that the slogan "you can't put a price on everything" could be expressing, from the obviously true to the highly contentious, and to say, for each reading, whether it would actually support the argument against carbon offsets (or organ markets, or whichever application) and whether it survives scrutiny. I am less interested in which reading is the "right" one than in having a clear menu of the options, so that I can see which version my classmate would need to defend for his objection to go through.
