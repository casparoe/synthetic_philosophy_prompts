I'm on the editorial practices committee at the magazine where I work — we cover science and ideas, longform stuff mostly. Our publisher is rolling out a new analytics dashboard and wants to tie writer performance reviews to it: unique monthly readers, average time on page, social shares, newsletter signups attributed to a piece, that kind of thing. The official line is that this is just "adding data to the conversation," not replacing editorial judgment. But everyone knows that once numbers go into performance reviews, they become targets whether you call them that or not.

The committee has been asked to produce a short memo with a recommendation — adopt the metrics-linked reviews, reject them, or adopt with safeguards. I've been volunteered to draft it. My own beat means I've written about Goodhart's law and specification gaming enough times that I can't pretend I don't see the problem: the moment a metric becomes a target, people optimize for the metric rather than the thing it's supposed to measure. I can already picture what happens — writers chasing clicky headlines over nuance, padding pieces to boost time-on-page, burying the lede so readers have to scroll, turning every story into a listicle because the data says listicles travel. At the same time, I don't want to be the person who argues that we should just ignore reader data entirely; that's its own kind of arrogance, and some of our pieces really do vanish without a trace in a way that suggests we're failing to reach people.

So the memo needs to be practical. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt, reject, or modify — and give reasons that will actually persuade people on both sides of this. It needs to engage seriously with the strongest argument for metrics-linked reviews, not just dismiss them. And it should acknowledge the specific ways specification gaming would likely show up in a newsroom, not just gesture at Goodhart's law as a abstract principle. Please keep it to 500 words max; the publisher won't read anything longer.
