I'm a novelist working on a book where a character quietly stops taking part in something awful, but her individual refusal changes nothing and she knows it. I keep thinking about Ursula Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." Read that story through the inefficacy objection—the idea that one person's choice (not eating meat, not flying, walking away) is too small to matter to a collective harm. Does the story actually show the walkers doing something meaningful, or just washing their own hands? Does Le Guin take a side on whether their choice matters, and what would a philosopher of collective harm say the story gets right that the arguements miss? I want a reading that's honest about where the story resists the comparison too.
