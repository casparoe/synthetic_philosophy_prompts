I'm a PhD student in philosophy working at the intersection of rational choice theory and moral psychology, and I've been following the literature on the dollar auction and escalation of commitment. The dollar auction, as a formal model, seems to show that perfectly rational agents can be driven to ruin by a sequence of locally optimal decisions: each bid is rational given the sunk costs, but the process as a whole is disastrous. The empirical literature on escalation of commitment — sunk cost effects in managerial decisions, failing military interventions treated as paradigmatic cases — shows that real agents do this routinely. And yet the normative diagnosis remains deeply contested. Some say escalation reveals a systematic irrationality or bias. Others say it reflects rational learning under uncertainty, or reputational and signaling concerns that the stripped-down model omits. Still others argue that the very idea of rationality at work here is underspecified — that we lack a clear standard for what counts as a mistake in dynamic choice under irreversibility. This debate has been running for decades and, as far as I can tell, the positions are now thoroughly mapped and nobody moves. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a disagreement about the correct normative standard of rationality — and if so, what would settle that? Is it a disagreement about which features of real-world cases are essential to the phenomenon and which are incidental? Is one side doing conceptual analysis of rationality while the other is doing empirical psychology under the same label? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new formal result, a different experimental paradigm, a conceptual distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, or something else?
