The essay's still in note form, so it's rough, but I want to know whether the core argument is worth pursuing or if I'm missing something obvious. Here's the gist:

---

Most people read Kant's essay on the murderer at the door (the one from 1797, "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropic Concerns") as if it's just an application of the ethical absolutism he defends in the Groundwork — no lying, period, even to save a life. But I think this misses something important about the architecture of Kant's system. By 1797, Kant had already published the *Metaphysics of Morals*, which splits the whole field into the Doctrine of Right (juridical / external duties) and the Doctrine of Virtue (ethical / internal duties). The lying essay was published the same year and is explicitly framed as a response to Benjamin Constant's political argument about whether a society can function if everyone has a duty to tell the truth. Constant was worried about legal and political consequences, not about virtue.

So here's my argument: the murderer-at-the-door case is actually a case about *right*, not about *virtue*. The question Kant is trying to answer isn't "what should a good person do?" but rather "can you be held legally responsible for the consequences of a well-intentioned lie?" The famous passage where Kant says you'd be legally accountable if your lie inadvertently led the murderer to find the victim makes no sense as an ethical argument — it sounds bizarre and legalistic. But it makes perfect sense as a juridical argument about imputability. Kant's point is that if you tell the truth, you've done nothing for which you can be held externally accountable; if you lie, you've introduced a wrongful act into the causal chain and you're on the hook for whatever follows, even if you meant well.

Meanwhile, the ethical question — should I lie to the murderer? — might actually have a different answer, or at least a more nuanced one, within the Doctrine of Virtue. The duty of truthfulness in the ethical sphere is grounded in a duty to oneself, not to others. Lying degrades your own moral personality. But this is an internal matter, not something the state can compel. So it's possible, on my reading, that Kant's ethical theory doesn't straightforwardly forbid the lie in the murderer case — or at least, the case is genuinely harder in the ethical domain than in the juridical one — while his juridical theory says: go ahead and tell the truth, because if you lie and it goes wrong, that's on you.

The upshot is that the standard "Kant says you can't lie to the murderer, what a monster" reaction conflates two different normative standpoints that Kant took pains to keep separate. Whether that makes Kant's position more palatable is another question, but at least it's not as simple as the textbook version.

---

(b) Are there textual details in the 1797 essay that undercut my reading — e.g., does Kant explicitly invoke ethical considerations alongside juridical ones? (c) Does the argument just shift the problem one step over without really solving anything? Be brutal, I'd rather find out now than in the Q&A.
