I am a novelist writing a time-travel novel in which the protagonist means to go back and kill his grandfather before his father is conceived. I want a scene in which two of his companions argue about what would actually happen if he tried: one holds that history is a single, self-consistent timeline in which a traveler's actions were always already part of the past, and the other holds that travel into the past splits off a new branch, so that the protagonist could kill a grandfather without erasing the branch he came from. Write the dialogue. Each position should be given its strongest, most competent version; neither character should be a strawman, and they should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than trading slogans. Let the exchange turn on the hard points: whether "changing" the past is even coherent on a single timeline, whether the man killed on the new branch is genuinely the protagonist's grandfather, what constrains a traveler on the single-timeline picture and why the grandfather killing in particular keeps failing, and what each view must say about the traveler's own survival and identity. The dialogue should leave the reader with a sharper sense of what each picture commits one to rather than resolving the paradox.
