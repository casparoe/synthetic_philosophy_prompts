I serve on the board of a small family foundation that gives out roughly half a million dollars a year. We are in the middle of setting our funding priorities for the next cycle, and the board has split down the middle. My younger relatives on the board have been reading a lot online about how to give effectively, and they are pushing us to fund only interventions with clear, measurable cost-effectiveness: dollars per life saved, dollars per year of schooling delivered, that kind of thing. Their argument is that if you cannot measure what a dollar buys, you have no way of knowing whether it bought anything at all, and given that the money could save lives elsewhere, you owe it to the people you might have helped to stick with what the numbers support. My uncle, who founded the foundation, is pushing in the opposite direction. He wants to fund local advocacy groups that work on housing policy and tenant organizing — the kind of work where it is genuinely hard to say what any given dollar accomplishes, because the causal chain runs through legislation, enforcement, and shifting public attitudes over many years. He thinks his younger critics have confused what is easy to count with what is actually important, and that a foundation that only funds the countable ends up chasing a kind of false precision while ignoring real but messier forms of impact. I am supposed to break the tie, and I keep going back and forth. Is there a way to think about this that does not just reduce to temperament — to whether you happen to be the kind of person who trusts numbers or the kind who trusts judgment? I would like your advice.
