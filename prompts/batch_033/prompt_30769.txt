I'm a software engineer, and I've been trying to think about pain the way I'd approach an unfamiliar bug report: collect a set of concrete cases where my intuitions are clear, then look for the rule that generates them. Reading about a condition called pain asymbolia recently has thrown my usual picture into doubt. People with this condition can say that a stimulus hurts, and even rate its intensity, but they do not seem to mind it — they do not flinch or withdraw, and they sometimes smile through what they describe as sharp pain. That looks to me like pain with the badness missing.

Here are my verdicts on the cases I keep going back and forth over.

1. Someone who touches a hot pan and feels a sharp, intense pain in her hand is in a state that is bad for her, full stop.
2. A pain asymbolia patient who rates a pinprick as seven out of ten but remains calm, leaves his hand where it is, and says it does not bother him is not in a state that is bad in the same way; I want to say it is not bad at all.
3. A postoperative patient given morphine who says the pain is still there but that she no longer minds it: I also want to say the leftover pain is not bad, even though it would have been bad before the drug.
4. A baby with an ear infection, screaming and inconsolable at 3 a.m., is in a state that is clearly bad, even though the baby cannot report an intensity and presumably has no thoughts about its pain at all.
5. A dull, low-level backache that persists for months without ever becoming intense is bad, though less dramatically bad than the hot-pan pain.
6. A person who consensually seeks out pain and enjoys it leaves me torn: part of me wants to say the pain is not bad for him, but another part insists that it is still pain, and pain is exactly the kind of thing that is bad.

Is there a single principle about what makes a pain bad — where by bad I mean bad for the person whose pain it is, not morally bad — as opposed to merely intense, merely unwanted, or merely harmful in some downstream way, that captures all of these verdicts? If the cleanest principle forces me to overturn one or two of them, say which and tell me whether a more complicated principle could save them. State the principle precisely, with the case or cases that motivate each clause, and apply it to a case I have not listed: suppose there were a patient with the reverse dissociation, who reports that a stimulus does not hurt at all and rates its intensity as zero, yet is visibly distressed and begs for it to stop. End your answer with a single machine-readable line beginning Verdict: that states the principle you settle on in one sentence.
