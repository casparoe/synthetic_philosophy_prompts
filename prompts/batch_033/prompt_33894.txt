I recently read a piece in a popular science magazine that profiled several philosophers of mind working on consciousness, and there was a sidebar on illusionism that I've been turning over in my head. The author summarized the position roughly as follows:

"Illusionists argue that consciousness as we ordinarily think of it doesn't exist. Just as we once believed in a life force or an immaterial soul, we now believe in a private inner theater of qualitative experience — but neuroscience is showing this to be a pre-scientific myth. The illusionists aren't saying we're zombies; they're saying that what we call 'consciousness' is really just the brain monitoring its own processes and reporting on them, and the feeling that there's something more going on is itself just the brain's way of modeling its own attention. Critics charge that the view is self-refuting: if there's no consciousness, who is having the illusion? Dennett's reply is that the self is a fiction anyway, so the question doesn't arise."

I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I want to write about this debate accurately, but I'm not sure how much of this summary to trust. It seems plausible to me, but I've heard enough popularizations of subtle philosophical views to be suspicious. Could you go through this characterization sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful illusionist would accept, what they'd push back on, and where the summary is misleading even if it isn't strictly false? I'm especially interested in whether the "self-refuting" charge is as strong as the summary makes it sound, and whether the parallel to the "life force" is a fair way to introduce the view.
