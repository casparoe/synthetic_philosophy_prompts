I've been thinking about self-deception and trying to get clear on what I actually count as an instance of it, as opposed to optimism, denial, wishful thinking, or just plain error. I went through a list of cases and recorded my gut verdicts, and now I'm trying to figure out whether there's a coherent principle behind them — or whether I need to revise some of my verdicts.

Here are the cases:

1. A man's wife has been coming home late for months, smelling of an unfamiliar cologne, and growing evasive when he asks about her day. He tells himself — and apparently believes — that she's just been under a lot of pressure at work. Verdict: self-deception.

2. A graduate student receives her third rejection from a journal in a row, with detailed referee reports pointing to the same structural flaw in the argument. She tells herself the referees simply didn't understand her approach, and she submits the paper unchanged to another journal. Verdict: self-deception.

3. A patient diagnosed with a terminal illness is told she has roughly a five percent chance of surviving the year. She focuses single-mindedly on that five percent and tells herself, and seems to believe, that she will be the one who beats the odds. Verdict: not self-deception — or at least, I don't feel the same readiness to call it that.

4. A parent whose child is clearly struggling academically — failing grades, conferences with teachers, an educational assessment — tells himself the child is just a late bloomer and the school system doesn't know how to handle creative kids. Verdict: self-deception.

5. A smoker who has "quit" a dozen times buys a pack and says, sincerely, "this is the last one." He seems to believe it each time. Verdict: self-deception, but it feels different from cases 1, 2, and 4 — maybe more like a failure of self-prediction than a stable false belief.

6. An entrepreneur has launched three startups, all of which failed. Before launching the fourth, she tells herself that this one is different because she's learned from her mistakes. The new venture is in a different industry, and she has a concrete plan addressing the specific reasons the previous ones failed. Verdict: I'm torn. It could be self-deception, or it could be reasonable persistence — I can't tell which.

7. A person systematically avoids reading news about climate change, skips documentaries on the topic, and changes the subject when it comes up socially. He doesn't form any explicit belief that climate change isn't happening; he just doesn't engage with the question. Verdict: not self-deception, exactly, but something adjacent — maybe self-deception about the importance of the issue, or a kind of strategic ignorance.

8. A person with chronic back pain enrolls in a clinical trial and is told she's receiving a placebo. She tells herself that placebos sometimes work through real physiological mechanisms, so the pill might actually help her. Verdict: not self-deception — she's not wrong about the mechanisms, and she's not misrepresenting the evidence to herself.

I'm trying to find a principle that captures the pattern across these verdicts — something that tells me why 1, 2, and 4 are clearly self-deception, why 3 and 8 aren't, why 5 feels like a different kind of case, why 7 seems adjacent but not quite the thing, and what to say about the entrepreneur in case 6. In particular, I want to know: what are the necessary conditions for self-deception, and are they jointly sufficient? If no simple set of conditions captures all my verdicts, which verdicts should I reconsider, and what would a revised set of cases look like under the best available principle? Please also apply your preferred account to one or two cases I haven't thought of — preferably cases designed to stress-test the principle at its weakest point. Please write your answer in American English.
