I'm running a reading group with some friends — we're not academics, just people who like arguing about philosophy over drinks — and we've gotten stuck on this thing about hypocrisy and blame. You know the basic puzzle: if someone does X themself, does that mean they can't legitimately blame someone else for doing X? So the adulterer who condemns adultery, the tax cheat who lectures others about civic duty, that sort of thing. The intuition that there's something wrong with the hypocrite's blame is super strong, but when you try to say exactly what's wrong with it you run into problems. The blamed person might genuinely have done something wrong. The hypocrite's statement might be entirely true. So what's the problem exactly — is it that the hypocrite lacks some kind of standing, the way you might lack standing to forgive someone for a wrong that wasn't done to you? Or is it that the hypocrite's blame is blame but just bad in some other way? Or is the whole "standing" framing misleading and we should just say the hypocrite is blameworthy for their own conduct while their blame of the other person remains perfectly fine?

We've been going back and forth on this for weeks and I thought it'd be fun to give the group a dialogue to react to, something that dramatizes the disagreement better than us just repeating ourselves. Could you write one? Two characters: Maya, who defends something like the view that hypocrisy really does undermine one's standing to blame — that there's something about blaming others for a fault you share that makes the blame itself illegitimate, not just hypocritical in a way that's separate from the blame's aptness. And David, who pushes back hard on this, arguing that the aptness of blame and the standing of the blamer are two different things, and that we shouldn't confuse the moral failings of the person doing the blaming with the question of whether the blamed person actually deserves it. Maybe he thinks the whole "standing" talk is a red herring and we should just say the hypocrite is blameworthy for their own hypocracy while still being correct in their blame of the other person.

Neither should be a pushover. I want real back-and-forth where they respond to each other's actual points, not just trading speeches. Each one should land some good blows, and the reader should come away feeling like they'd need to think hard to pick a side. Don't resolve it neatly at the end. And write it in a natural conversational style — they're not reading papers at each other, they're talking, maybe over coffee or drinks. A couple pages of dialogue would be perfect.
