I'm working on a feature about a debate in philosophy of biology I'd heard about for years but never actually dug into: what it means to say a biological trait has a function. The rough split, as I understand it, is between people who say the function of a trait is whatever it was selected for in evolutionary history — the heart's function is to pump blood because hearts that pumped blood were the ones favored by natural selection — and people who say the function of a trait is the causal role it plays in the organism right now, the contribution it makes to some larger capacity like circulating blood. I used to work as an ML engineer before I became a journalist, and when I described the debate to a friend who still works in the field, he kept saying it sounds exactly like how engineers talk about trained models: the loss function and the training run that shaped the weights on one side, and what a given layer or attention head actually does in the forward pass of the deployed model on the other. I want to know if he's right.

Restate the etiological (selected-effects) account and the causal-role account in that ML vocabulary as faithfully as you can — training objective, gradient descent, selection pressure, ablation, feature attribution, distribution shift, whatever earns its keep — and don't force a match where there isn't one; if gradient descent is not really natural selection, say so. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which ones you had to fudge, and which have no counterpart at all in the way ML people talk about models. I especially want to know what happens to malfunction and to the idea of a trait being for something, because those feel like the spots where the two vocabularies talk past each other. And at the end, tell me wether the residue is a sign that the philosophical distinction is doing real work, or that one side has smuggled in something the other side never needed.
