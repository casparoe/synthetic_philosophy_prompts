I'm trying to locate where my disagreement with a colleague actually lives on the question of advance directives. Consider this case: a woman, at sixty-five and in full possession of her faculties, signs an advance directive refusing life-sustaining treatment should she develop severe dementia. At seventy-eight, she has advanced Alzheimer's disease; she does not recognise her family, lives pleasantly in a care home, enjoys simple pleasures, and shows no signs of suffering. She develops pneumonia. Her advance directive says no antibiotics. Her current self, insofar as one can discern preferences from her behaviour, seems to want to go on living. My credence that the advance directive ought to be honoured is about 0.7; my colleague's is about 0.3.

Please give me your own credence that the advance directive ought to be honoured in this case, but more importantly, decompose the question. I want the sub-questions your credence depends on — for instance, whether the demented woman is the same person in the morally relevant sense as the woman who signed the directive; whether the earlier self has the standing to bind the later self even if they are the same person; whether the later self's apparent contentment should shift our judgement; whether the nature of the treatment (here, antibiotics for pneumonia rather than, say, major surgery) matters; and any other considerations you think belong. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, show how they combine to yield your overall credence, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me which of the two people — the one at 0.7 or the one at 0.3 — you think is more likely getting the wrong answer on that pivotal sub-question, and what evidence or argument would most shift your own view.
