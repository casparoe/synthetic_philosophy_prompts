I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend who is a fairly uncompromising adaptationist — the kind who thinks that if you look hard enough at any trait, you'll find a selective story that explains why it's there and roughly why it takes the form it does. I'm more sympathetic to the Gould and Lewontin line: that organismal form is shaped as much by architectural constraints, developmental pathways, and historical contingency as by selection, and that the habit of treating every trait as an optimized solution to some adaptive problem is a methodological trap. The argument went in circles. By the end we agreed on more than either of us had expected, but we couldn't quite locate where the residual disagreement lived, or what would settle it.

I'd like you to draft the document we should have produced: an adversarial-collaboration statement between an adaptationist — someone who holds that natural selection is the primary and most powerful force shaping organismal form, that adaptation is the proper default hypothesis for a trait's presence and form, and that the research program built on this commitment has been enormously productive — and a structuralist pluralist — someone who holds that selection is one cause among several, including developmental constraint, allometry, phyletic heritage, and drift, and that the adaptationist program systematically underestimates these alternatives and overproduces untestable just-so stories.

The document should have two parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign: claims about what natural selection can do, what the fossil and comparative record shows, what counts as evidence for or against an adaptive hypothesis, what methodological precautions are needed, and what each side genuinely acknowledges as a contribution of the other's tradition. These should be neither vacuous nor slanted; they should capture the real common ground that exists once the rhetorical heat is turned down. Second, a disagreements table. For each row, give the question at issue phrased neutrally; the adaptationist's answer, phrased so an adaptationist would own it; the structuralist pluralist's answer, phrased so a structuralist pluralist would own it; and a judgment about whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal — and if partly verbal, explain which term is doing the work. After the table, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would cause at least one side to revise its position substantially. For each crux, describe a kind of evidence or a research program both sides would agree bears on it, along with each side's prediction about what that evidence would show.

I'm especially interested in whether the dispute reduces to a difference in prior probabilities over how often selection will find an adaptive solution versus being blocked by constraints, or whether there's something deeper — a disagreement about what counts as an explanation, or about where the burden of proof falls. If you think the two sides are partly talking past each other about the word "adaptation" itself, say so and disentangle it.
