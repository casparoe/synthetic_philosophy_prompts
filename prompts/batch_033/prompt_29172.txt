I recently became the person who signs off on how choices are presented to roughly 4,000 employees at a mid-sized financial-services firm — benefits enrollment, retirement-plan defaults, and a few internal wellness and sustainability programs. A few times a month I receive a proposal that changes some default, reorders some menu, or adds some message to a portal, and each one has to be defended to Legal, HR, and the executive team. The people proposing these changes call them nudges and treat them as obviously harmless; I am not sure they always are, and I have no formal training in the philosophy behind any of this.

I would like a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical discussion of paternalism and nudging, that I can run on a proposal before I sign off. I know that discussion makes distinctions — hard versus soft paternalism, interference with a person's means versus their ends, welfare versus autonomy, transparent versus hidden influence, manipulation versus rational persuasion, and the special power of defaults and the status quo bias — but I do not know how those distinctions should be ordered into a screen, which ones are decisive, and which are just considerations to weigh.

Please write the procedure as an ordered set of questions a non-philosopher can ask about any proposed change to how a choice is presented. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer and where the procedure stops with a no. Mark the steps that the procedure cannot settle on its own and must hand to a person, and be explicit about what kind of judgment is actually being made at those steps. The procedure needs to be usable in a one-hour review meeting by me and two non-specialist staff.

Then run the procedure on three proposals currently on my desk:

(1) automatically enrolling new hires in the 401(k) at a 6 percent deferral rate with automatic annual escalation to 10 percent, with an opt-out form;

(2) reordering the cafeteria and vending layout so that lower-sugar and lower-sodium items sit at eye level and in the most convenient line, while the less healthy items remain available but in a less convenient location;

(3) adding a peer-comparison message to the benefits portal telling employees how their retirement contribution rate compares with the average for their job band, with a smiley-face icon for those above the average.

At least one of these should come out differently than my initial instinct, and I want to see where and why the procedure forces that. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which proposals it will classify too quickly, and which parts of the underlying question are not procedural at all. Please write the answer in American English.
