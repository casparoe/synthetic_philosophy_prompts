Hey, so I'm a science journalist and I've got a chance to apply for this six-month research fellowship where I'd actually get to do a deep dive on something instead of just chasing deadlines and word counts. I cover brain-tech stuff mostly—neural implants, BCIs, that sort of thing—and I keep running into this philosophical puzzle that I think might actually matter for how we think about the technology, but I'm not sure and I'd like to spend the fellowship figuring it out.

The puzzle is the old gradual replacement thought experiment: replace your neurons one at a time with functionally identical artificial ones, and at the end, is it still you? Philosophers have been kicking this around forever and they split into roughly two camps—those who say yes because psychological continuity and functional equivalence are what matter, and those who say no because something about the substrate or the original biological consciousness gets destroyed along the way.

Here's my hunch: this debate has stayed abstract for decades, but we're now at a point where actual neural prosthetics are doing something that looks a lot like baby versions of this. Cochlear implants are the straightforward case, but now there's experimental hippocampal prosthetics that replace memory encoding functions, deep brain stimulators that modulate mood and decision circuits, and people are talking seriously about cortical implants for vision. None of this is full neuron replacement, but its piecemeal functional replacement of neural tissue with silicon.

I want the fellowship to answer this question: does the gradual replacement thought experiment actually illuminate the identity questions raised by real neural prosthetics, or does it mislead us? More precisely: what does the philosophy of gradual replacement predict about a person with, say, a hippocampal prosthetic—do they survive the implant in the same sense the thought experiment says they'd survive neuron replacement, or do the differences between the thought experiment and the real case (the prosthetic isn't a neuron-for-neuron swap, it doesn't replace but augments or bypasses, the biological tissue stays in place) make the thought experiment mostly irrelevant? And if the thought experiment *doesn't* map onto real cases, what does that say about the thought experiment itself—was it too idealized to ever do the work philosophers wanted it to do?

I can see a couple ways to structure this. One is a careful mapping: take three or four real neural prosthetic technologies and analyze each through the lens of the major positions in the gradual replacement debate, and say exactly where the mapping holds and where it breaks. Another is to flip it and ask: if you start from the real technologies and work backward, do you end up wanting a different thought experiment entirely—one that doesn't assume neat one-to-one replacement of identical units? Maybe the real philosophical issues in neural prosthetics are about partial replacement, augmentation, or something about boundaries of the self that the gradual replacement literature never had to confront because it was always imagining a clean swap.

For success, I think I'd need either (a) a clear argument that the thought experiment does or doesn't map onto real cases, with a framework that someone actually designing neural implants could use to think about the identity implications of their work, or (b) a demonstration that the real cases reveal a genuine gap in the philosophical literature and point toward a new question that needs answering. Failure would be ending up with a shrug—like, "it depends" with no principled way to say what it depends on.

The deliverable would be a longform essay (think 8,000 to 10,000 words) aimed at a scientifically literate but not specialist-philosopher audience, plus maybe a shorter policy-facing piece on what any of this means for the regulatory conversation around neural implants, since right now nobodys really talking about the identity question in those circles at all.

Scope-wise, six months feels right—three months to really nail down the philosophical positions and the technical details of the technologies, two to work out the analysis and write a draft, and the last month to revise and get feedback from both philosophers and neuroscientists. I can fill in citations and the bibliography stuff later, I just need the architecture of the thing to be solid.

What I'm nervous about: that the whole project rests on a false analogy and I won't realize it until month four. Is the gradual replacement thought experiment even about the same *kind* of question as the neural prosthetics cases? Or is figuring that out the very thing worth spending six months on?
