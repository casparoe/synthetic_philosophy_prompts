I've been reading a bunch of philosophy lately and I keep running into this same argument move that I don't have a word for. It goes roughly like this: someone wants to defend X (say, free will, or the reliability of reason, or the reality of the external world), and instead of giving direct evidence for X, they argue that you can't even coherently deny X because the act of denying it already presupposes X. Like, "you can't argue that reason is unreliable, because arguing itself relies on reason." Or "you can't claim that nobody has free will, because the act of making a claim presupposes you're freely choosing what to assert." I've seen this used for all sorts of things — free will, the existence of other minds, the law of non-contradiction, even for God in some older texts.

What I'm trying to figure out is: does this specific argument pattern have an established name? And more importantly, is there a way to tell when it's a legitimate move versus when it's just a rhetorical trick? Because sometimes it feels genuinely deep — like, yeah, if you try to argue against logic you're kind of sawing off the branch you're sitting on. But other times it feels like someone is just exploiting an ambiguity about what "presupposes" means, or they're confusing a psychological precondition (you have to believe you're free to bother making arguments) with a logical one (the truth of free will is required for arguments to be valid). I'd love to know if philosophers have carved this up in a useful way, and if the good and bad versions have different names, or if it's all one thing and the quality just depends on execution. I'm not just looking for a label — I want to understand what exactly distinguishes the legit cases from the cheap ones, and whether there's a principled line or it's all judgment calls.
