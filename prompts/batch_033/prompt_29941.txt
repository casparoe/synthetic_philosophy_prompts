I am a novelist working on a historical novel set in Königsberg in the final decade of the eighteenth century. One of my characters is a young man of earnest moral sensibility who has absorbed Kant's ethics and tries, with some unease, to apply it to the way he treats his dog. In the course of my research, I read a book on the history of animal ethics, and it summarizes Kant's position in these terms:

"Kant held that animals are not persons, so we owe them no direct duties; they are merely means to human ends. His only objection to cruelty to animals is indirect and instrumental: mistreating a dog is wrong because it can make a person callous, and a callous person is more likely to mistreat other human beings. It follows that if a person could be certain that his cruelty would never spill over into his treatment of humans, Kant would have to admit that the cruelty is perfectly permissible."

I am not a philosopher, and I cannot tell how much of this to trust. Something about the phrase "indirect and instrumental" strikes me as reductive, and the final inference feels too quick, but I do not know enough to say where the summary goes wrong. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what Kant actually held, which parts are accurate, which are subtly wrong, and whether the concluding claim really follows from the accurate parts? I also want to avoid writing my character as a straw man: what difference does the distinction between direct and indirect duties actually make for how a Kantian of that period would think about his dog?
