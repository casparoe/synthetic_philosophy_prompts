I am a retired neuroscientist who has recently developed an interest in philosophy of mind, particularly theories of pain and its badness. I have been reading about pain asymbolia — the condition in which patients report feeling pain but deny that it is unpleasant or bothersome — and I am struck by how it challenges different accounts of what makes pain bad.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of mind and the metaphysics of pain. Please think carefully about the following task and, before writing your final answer, take some time to brainstorm the key structural features the case needs.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications:

A patient with pain asymbolia experiences a clearly identifiable sensory pain state (they can locate it, describe its quality, rate its intensity) but lacks the normal affective response — they do not find it unpleasant, they are not bothered by it, and they have no intrinsic desire for it to stop. The scenario should place this patient in a situation where they must decide whether to take an action that would eliminate the sensory pain state (e.g., taking an analgesic, undergoing a minor procedure, or stopping a stimulus). The case must be designed so that imperativist and attitudinal theories of the badness of pain yield different verdicts about what the patient has reason to do.

For clarity: by "imperativist" I mean the view that pain is essentially a command to stop doing something, and that this commandedness itself supplies a reason for action. By "attitudinal" I mean the view that the badness of pain consists entirely in the subject's conative or affective response — their disliking it, their desire for it to stop — so that in the absence of that response, the pain is not bad for the subject.

Constraints:
- The scenario should be realistic — a clinical or everyday setting, not a science-fiction thought experiment.
- No exotic neuroscience; the pain asymbolia should be described as it actually occurs (e.g., from lesions to the posterior insula or from analgesic drugs that preserve sensory discrimination but eliminate unpleasantness).
- The disagreement between the theories must genuinely turn on the location of pain's badness (in the sensory quale vs. in the affective/conative response), not on some extraneous feature of the case.

After presenting the scenario, please:
1. Walk through what each theory says about the patient's reasons and why they diverge.
2. Identify the specific feature of the case that does the work in separating the theories.
3. Explain how a defender of each theory would try to resist the other's verdict.
4. Describe what happens if you remove or alter that feature — does the case collapse into a simpler one where the theories agree?
