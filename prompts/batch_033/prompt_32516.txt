I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about Dutch book arguments, and I came away with a knot I can't untie. My friend was defending the standard line: Dutch book arguments show that rational credences must satisfy the probability axioms, because if they violate them, you can be made to accept a set of bets each of which you consider fair but that collectively guarantee you a net loss. I have always found this compelling as a piece of philosophical machinery, but as we pushed on it, I started to worry that the argument form proves too much — and if it proves too much, I am not sure it proves anything at all.

Here is the worry, laid out as carefully as I can reconstruct it. (1) If Dutch book vulnerability is a mark of irrationality, then any credal state that leaves you open to a sure loss is irrational. (2) The same style of argument that establishes probabilism also seems to establish that you must update by strict conditionalization: if you announce a deviant updating rule in advance, a bookie can offer you a collection of bets now and later whose net payoff is negative no matter what happens. (3) The same style of argument also seems to establish the Reflection Principle — roughly, that your current credence in P should equal your expectation of your future credence in P — because if you expect your credences to shift in a predictable direction, a bookie can exploit the gap between your current and expected future betting odds. (4) The same style of argument also seems to require logical omniscience: if you assign probability less than 1 to a logical truth, a bookie who knows the proof can sell you a bet on the truth and its negation in a way that guarantees you lose. (5) But many careful philosophers reject strict conditionalization as a universal requirement (they allow Jeffrey conditionalization, or they think updating rules are permissive), many reject Reflection (think of cases where you know you will take a drug that impairs your reasoning, or where you expect to forget evidence), and virtually no one thinks rationality requires logical omniscience. (6) So if the Dutch book argument form is valid, it forces us to accept a package of requirements that almost no one accepts. (7) If the argument form is not valid, then we need to identify exactly where it breaks down — and the worry is that whatever breaks it in the conditionalization, Reflection, and logical omniscience cases will also break it in the original probabilism case.

My friend's response was that the Dutch book for probabilism is qualitatively different from the others. The conditionalization and Reflection Dutch books, he said, rely on the agent's announcing a policy in advance, which is an extra commitment beyond merely holding credences, and the logical omniscience Dutch book requires the agent to be willing to bet on propositions whose logical structure they do not grasp. But I am not sure this distinction holds up under pressure. The probabilism Dutch book also requires the agent to be willing to bet on arbitrary Boolean combinations of propositions, and it is not obvious that a rational agent must be willing to do that either — especially if the combinations are computationally intractable. So the question I am left with is this: which step in the argument is the wrong one, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If the answer is that Dutch book arguments only work when we idealize the agent's willingness to bet in particular ways, then I want to know which idealizations are defensible and which are not — and whether the ones we need to secure probabilism survive the same scrutiny that sinks the others.

I would be grateful if you could walk through this argument and tell me where it goes wrong, if it does. In particular, I would like you to separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what remains contested, and not to present a contested resolution as though it were the consensus view.
