I'm in a philosophy of medicine seminar this term, and I'm writing a paper on the concept of disease. Before I commit to the view I've been developing, I'd like a consistency check on the set of commitments I've ended up with. Here they are:

1. Whether a person has a disease is an objective matter. It does not depend on whether the person, their culture, or their doctor regards the condition as bad, undesirable, or a problem.
2. A condition counts as a disease only if it involves a failure of some biological mechanism to perform its species-typical function — in short, a dysfunction.
3. Which dysfunctions get classified as diseases, and where the line between disease and normal variation is drawn, is nevertheless partly settled by social values and interests.
4. The declassification of homosexuality in the DSM was a scientific correction: it was removed because the classification failed the dysfunction criterion, not because surrounding social values shifted.
5. Chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a disease even though no specific underlying biological dysfunction has been identified.
6. Infertility is a disease even when the person who has it does not want children and does not experience the condition as a problem.

I'd like you to audit this set. For each pair of commitments, say whether there is a genuine tension, a merely apparent tension, or no tension at all. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity and say, for each, what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set cohere. I'm not asking you to defend your own view of disease; I want to know where my position is pulling against itself.
