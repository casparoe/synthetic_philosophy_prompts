I'm giving a talk next month at a local AI alignment meetup, and I want to be ready for Q&A. I'm a software engineer, not a philospher, so I'm presenting my own rough version of the indirect normativity idea. The thesis I'm defending is: when we build a system that's going to act with a lot of power, we shouldn't try to hard-code our current values into it or train it on what we say we want right now, because our current values are basically buggy legacy code. Instead we should build it to aim at what we'd want after some kind of idealized deliberation — knowing more, reasoning more carefully, being less impulsive and tribal, having fixed some of our own bugs. My argument is basically: any target has to come from somewhere; if you aim at current revealed preferences you get all our bugs and blind spots; if you write down a fixed list of values you'll miss edge cases and get Goodharted; so the least-bad target is the endpoint of us reflecting under better conditions. I also want to claim the vagueness is a feature, not a bug, because it stops us from pretending we solved values by writing a config file.

The audience is mostly working software engineers. A few of them do RLHF at startups, one does infrastructure at a big lab, one is a product manager who has read a lot of LessWrong, and the rest are just curious engineers who show up. They're pretty allergic to anything that sounds like philosophy, and I expect them to push on whether this is actually implementable or whether I've just renamed the problem. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way they'd actually say them, not the way an academic would. For each one, give me a reply I can get through in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
