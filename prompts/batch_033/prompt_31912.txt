I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about pain asymbolia and what it tells us about the badness of pain, and I have come away with a set of views I would like to have stress-tested before I commit to them. The friend was pushing a line according to which pain is intrinsically bad — that the sensation itself is necessarily awful, and that pain asymbolia patients must be confused, inattentive, or using words loosely when they report feeling pain but not minding it. I found myself unwilling to accept that, and over the course of the argument I arrived at the following seven commitments.

(1) Pain asymbolia is a real phenomenon: the sensory and affective dimensions of pain doubly dissociate, and the patients are not making any kind of error when they describe themselves as feeling pain without suffering from it. (2) The badness of pain is entirely located in the affective dimension. The sensory component, considered in itself, is evaluatively neutral — it is simply information about tissue state, like a signal from a damage sensor. (3) The affective dimension of pain is not itself a sensation or a quale. It is a conative state: an intrinsic, non-instrumental urge that the sensory state cease — something closer to a command than to a feeling. (4) Because the badness of pain is constituted by this conative state, the badness of pain is response-dependent: pain is bad for a creature only if, and because, that creature is averse to it in the relevant way. (5) It follows that pain asymbolia patients are in a state that is not bad for them, and they are not making any normative or conceptual error when they say they feel pain but do not suffer. (6) Nonetheless, for a normal human being, pain provides a categorical reason to seek relief — a reason whose force does not depend on whether the person reflectively endorses the aversion, or whether relief serves any further goal the person happens to have. (7) The fact that pain's badness is response-dependent does not undermine its normative authority. Learning the neuroscience of pain affect does not give me a reason to try to extinguish my aversion rather than to take the analgesic.

The friend pushed back hard on (6) and (7), arguing that if the badness of pain is response-dependent, then the reason to seek relief cannot be categorical in any interesting sense, and the normative authority of pain is threatened by the very discovery that the badness is conferred by our own aversion. I think the friend was conflating a few things, but I want to be sure I am not the one running together distinct issues. Please go through these seven commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension and why the appearance dissolves, and what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each, say what would have to give and what the philosophical cost of giving it up would be.
