I'm on my university's animal care and use committee, and the pattern of our decisions is starting to look incoherent to me. We're ruthless about pain: every protocol has to justify any painful procedure, show that anesthesia or analgesia will be used, and we'll send a proposal back over a tail snip done without pain relief. We also make people justify the number of mice with a power analysis, so they use as few as possible. But the same protocols routinely end by killing all the animals, including healthy ones that never underwent a painful procedure, and nobody treats that as a harm that needs justifying; the benefit that supposedly justifies the whole experiment is almost always a benefit to humans, not to other animals. What theory of animals' moral status does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose the view Nozick floated — that animals fall under a utilitarian calculus where one animal can be sacrificed for the greater good of animals or people, while persons are protected by side constraints — or is the practice compatible with several rival theories? Is it internally consistent, or does its strictness about pain and its indifference to death quietly contradict each other? And what would our review process have to look like if we instead thought individual animals had side constraints, or if we thought animal welfare didn't count at all?
