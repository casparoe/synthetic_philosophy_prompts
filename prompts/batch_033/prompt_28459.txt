I'm a PhD student in social epistemology and I've gotten pretty attached to the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers — the idea that bubbles are about information deprivation (you just never encounter the other side's evidence) while echo chambers are about manufactured distrust (you do encounter it, but you've been primed to dismiss it as lies or propaganda). The practical upshot is supposed to be that bubbles are relatively easy to fix — you just expose people to what they're missing — whereas echo chambers need something more like trust repair or even the kind of techniques you'd use to deprogram someone from a cult.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not just "here's what critics say," but what should actually make me worry that the distinction is less clean or less useful than I think. Like, are there real cases that sit awkwardly between the two categories? If someone's information environment is curated by algorithms that both omit some sources *and* frame the ones they do see as untrustworthy, is that a bubble, a chamber, both, or does the framework just not handle hybrid cases well? Are there empirical results — from network science, psychology, whatever — that suggest the mechanisms blur together in practice? And does the view make any risky bets? If the distinction is right, what should we observe in the world that we wouldn't observe if it's wrong? If the answer is "nothing in particular, it's a conceptual distinction," is that a problem, or is that fine for this kind of philosophical tool? I guess I'm asking for a map of where the view is exposed and where it's armored, and an honest take on whether the armor is too thick.
