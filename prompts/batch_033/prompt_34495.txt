Benacerraf's dilemma has been on the table since his 1973 paper, and the dialectic around it feels frozen in place. The standard shape of the debate is this: Platonists point to the indispensability of mathematics in science and the apparent reliability of mathematical intuition, but then concede that they have no good answer to the epistemological challenge — how do we come to have knowledge of causally inert, non-spatiotemporal objects? Nominalists and fictionalists point to the epistemological problem as a decisive reason to reject Platonism, but then concede that their own views struggle to account for the applicability of mathematics and the phenomenology of mathematical practice, which certainly feels like a domain of robust discovery rather than useful fiction. The same moves have been traded for decades, and while the positions have become more technically sophisticated — the debate has broadened beyond the original causal theory of knowledge into the more general "access problem" — the underlying standoff seems unchanged. I want to understand what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a substantive disagreement about a single question — "how can we have knowledge of abstract objects?" — or are the two sides effectively answering different questions (one about ontology, one about epistemology) that only appear to conflict because of an implicit commitment to a causal constraint on knowledge that neither side fully endorses? Alternatively, is the impasse a sign that the question itself is ill-posed — that we are demanding a kind of explanation that is not appropriate for the domain of mathematics, or that "mathematical knowledge" is a category mistake because mathematical practice is better understood as something other than knowledge of a domain of objects? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of the literature and not a verdict for one side. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a new epistemological framework, a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or an argument that the debate should be dissolved rather than resolved.
