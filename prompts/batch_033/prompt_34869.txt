I've been reading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments on my own, not for any course, and I keep finding myself thinking about Kazuo Ishiguro's novel The Remains of the Day. Smith's central idea is that we judge our own conduct by imagining how an impartial spectator — someone well-informed and disinterested — would view it. Conscience, on his account, is this internalized spectator. The butler Stevens, narrating his life, seems to have something very like an internal spectator: he constantly steps back and evaluates whether he has acted with what he calls "dignity," and he judges others by the same standard. But his spectator is clearly not impartial. It speaks with the voice of his profession, his employer Lord Darlington, and a particular ideal of Englishness. Through this spectator, Stevens persuades himself that his finest moment was calmly carrying out his duties while his father lay dying upstairs, and that his loyalty to Lord Darlington — even as Darlington drifts into appeasement and anti-Semitism — was morally unimpeachable because it was professionally impeccable.

I'm trying to work out what the novel is saying about Smith's theory. Is Stevens a case of the impartial spectator gone wrong — someone who has internalized a spectator that is merely partial but masquerades as impartial? If so, does Smith's theory have the resources to explain how that happens, or does it assume too easily that the internal spectator will converge on something genuinely moral? Or is the novel exposing a deeper problem: that the impartial spectator, as Smith describes it, is always trained by a particular social world, and that there is no guarantee the training will produce anything we would recognize as moral conscience? Stevens's tragedy seems to be that he has a fully functioning internal spectator — he is constantly self-critical, constantly measuring himself — and it leads him straight into moral failure. He feels the pull of sympathy (for Miss Kenton, for his father, even for Darlington's Jewish housemaids) but his spectator tells him to override it in the name of duty.

I'd like a reading that takes both Smith and Ishiguro seriously. Where does the novel confirm something Smith gets right about moral psychology, and where does it suggest his theory has a blind spot? Does the novel offer any hint about how one might correct a corrupted internal spectator, or is Stevens's case meant to be irreparable? I'm not looking for a summary of either work — I've read both — but for an honest critical engagement that doesn't reduce the novel to an illustration and doesn't distort Smith to fit the novel.
