In my philosophy elective, we've been working through excerpts from Sartre's Being and Nothingness, and I cannot get the chapter on bad faith out of my head. The idea that we can lie to ourselves about our own freedom — that we can treat ourselves as though we were objects whose futures are fixed by our past, our social roles, or our "nature" — strikes me as both obviously true and deeply unsettling. My friend Jenna, who is in the class with me, thinks the whole concept is overblown. She says Sartre is basically pathologizing the ordinary ways people make decisions: most of the time, when someone says "I have to do X" or "I'm just not the kind of person who does Y," they are not fleeing from freedom; they are drawing on their values and commitments in a perfectly healthy way. We have been going in circles about this for two weeks, and I think the only way to make progress is to see the argument dramatized.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two characters, Maya and Leo, sitting in a café, who are debating whether their mutual friend Sam is in bad faith. Sam has just announced that he is applying to medical school because he comes from a long line of doctors and, as he put it, "this is just who I am." Maya thinks Sam is a textbook case of bad faith: he is treating a contingent family tradition as though it were a fixed fact about his essence, and he is doing it precisely to avoid the anguish of confronting the full range of possibilities he actually has. Leo thinks Maya is being unfair: Sam has reflected on his values, he genuinely admires what his parents do, and he is freely choosing a path that happens to align with his family background — there is nothing inauthentic about that. The dialogue should be a genuine philosophical exchange rather than a setup where one side wins. Both characters should be sharp and intellectually serious, and each should respond to the other's actual arguments instead of talking past the other. I want to see the deepest tension in Sartre's view get worked out through their conversation: how can we ever tell the difference, from the outside, between an authentic choice and bad faith? And if we cannot reliably tell the difference, does that make the concept of bad faith empty, or does it just mean that the diagnosis is something a person can only apply to themselves?
