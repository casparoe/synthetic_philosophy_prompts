Question: "What is the problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory? Explain the problem and discuss whether the counterfactual approach solves it."

Student answer:

"The problem of old evidence, introduced by Clark Glymour, challenges the Bayesian account of confirmation. According to standard Bayesian confirmation theory, evidence E confirms hypothesis H just in case P(H|E) > P(H). However, if E is old evidence — evidence we already know — then P(E) = 1. When we plug this into Bayes' theorem, we get P(H|E) = P(H), so no confirmation occurs. This is problematic because historically, old evidence has confirmed theories: the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion was known for decades before Einstein proposed general relativity, yet it's widely considered strong confirming evidence for GR.

The counterfactual approach tries to solve this by asking us to consider what our credence in E would have been if we didn't already know it. We imagine a 'counterfactual prior' in which P(E) < 1, and then check whether E would have confirmed H under that prior. If so, we can say E confirms H even now.

But this approach runs into trouble. The problem is that when a new theory like GR comes along, there's no fact of the matter about what our prior probability for the theory should have been — we didn't have the theory before, so we couldn't have assigned it any probability. This is really the same problem as the problem of old evidence: we can't coherently ask what probability we would have assigned to something we hadn't conceived of. So the counterfactual approach doesn't really solve anything."
