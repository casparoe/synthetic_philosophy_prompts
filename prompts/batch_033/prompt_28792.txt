So my two friends got into this argument at a barbecue and its been bugging me for weeks. I want someone to go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what.

The argument started because my friend Dave told us about a job interview he had. They asked if he was still employed at his current company, and he said yes — which was true, technically, because his last day wasn't for another week. But what he didn't mention was that he'd been put on a performance improvement plan and was basically being managed out. He got the new job.

My other friend Mira said that was basically lying. Dave got defensive and said he told the truth: he was employed, end of story. Its not his job to volunteer damaging information nobody asked for.

Mira said that's sophistry — the whole point of the question was to figure out if he was leaving on good terms, and he knew that, and he deliberately let them draw a false conclusion. She said misleading someone is morally the same as lying, because you're still intentionally causing a false belief.

Dave said okay, by that standard, if your partner asks if you like their new haircut and you say "it really brings out your eyes" instead of "I hate it," you're a liar. Mira said no, that's different because social tact isn't about deception — everyone understands the game. Dave said oh so now there's a "game" exception? What if job interviews are also a game where everyone knows you put your best foot forward?

Then Mira shifted ground. She said look, even if misleading isn't always as bad as lying, there's something extra cowardly about it — you get the benefits of deception while keeping your hands clean, like your too precious to actually say the false thing. Dave said that's backwards: being careful to stay within the truth shows more respect for honesty, not less. If someone really didn't care about truth they'd just lie outright.

At this point my other friend Jen jumped in and said she thinks the real question is whether you'd be willing to say the misleading thing if the other person knew your full situation. Like, if the interviewer knew Dave was on a PIP, would Dave still feel okay saying "yes I'm employed"? If not, that's a sign he was exploiting their ignorance. Dave said that test proves too much — if a surprise party planner knows the guest of honor would see through the deception, that doesn't make surprise parties wrong.

It kind of petered out after that but I keep turning it over. Can someone adjudicate this round by round? I want to know who had the better of each exchange, and also whether there's a principled way to settle the underlying question or if they were just talking past each other. Also, please tell me how confident you are in each of your judgments and which point you're least sure about.
