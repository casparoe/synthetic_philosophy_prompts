I'm working on a paper about Borges's views on time as they emerge from his fiction, and I want to center the argument on "The Garden of Forking Paths." I'd like a careful reading of the story through the lens of contemporary philosophy of time — specifically, the dispute between eternalism, presentism, and the growing block theory, as well as branching-time models in temporal logic and their relationship to the many-worlds interpretation in physics.

The story's central passage describes Ts'ui Pên's vision: "He believed in an infinite series of times, in a growing, dizzying net of divergent, convergent and parallel times. This network of times which approached one another, forked, broke off, or were unaware of one another for centuries, embraces all possibilities of time. We do not exist in the majority of these times; in some you exist, and not I; in others I, and not you; in others, both of us."

I'm trying to determine what metaphysical picture this actually commits Borges to. Is this closest to eternalism — a block universe where all times are equally real and the branching is already fully laid out, with "choice" being a matter of which path one's consciousness happens to illuminate? Or is it closer to a genuinely branching ontology where the forks represent alternative possibilities that are all ontologically on a par, with new branches genuinely coming into being? And does the phrase "growing, dizzying net" suggest something like the growing block theory, where the past and present are real but the future is not — except that here all futures are real too?

I'm also interested in how the nested structure of the story — a spy thriller whose climax turns on the discovery of a fictional novel that encodes a metaphysics of time — bears on the philosophical content. Is Borges making a claim about time itself, or about what it means to represent time in narrative, or about the relationship between fiction and metaphysics? Does the story's form do philosophical work that its explicit statements don't?

I'd like a reading that takes both the story and the philosophical theories seriously, that doesn't reduce the fiction to an illustration of a pre-existing position, and that is willing to say where the story resists being mapped onto any single theory. In particular, I want to know: does Borges anticipate anything that the philosophical literature only reached later, and does the story capture something about the experience of living inside branching time that formal models miss? And what does the story, as a story, add to the debate that argument alone cannot deliver?
