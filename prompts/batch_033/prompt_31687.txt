Question: "Explain Williams's 'one thought too many' objection. What does it reveal about the relationship between impartial moral theories and the agent's integrity, and why does Williams think this is a problem for utilitarianism in particular?"

Student's answer:

"Williams's 'one thought too many' objection comes from his example of a man who has to choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger from a burning building. The utilitarian would say the man should save whichever person would produce the most overall utility. But Williams's point is that the husband shouldn't be thinking in these terms at all. If the husband paused to calculate whether saving his wife maximizes utility, that calculation itself would be 'one thought too many' — it shows that something has gone wrong with his relationship to his wife. The husband should save his wife simply because she is his wife, not because an impartial calculation happens to endorse that choice.

This connects to Williams's broader integrity objection. Williams argues that utilitarianism attacks the integrity of agents by forcing them to view their deepest commitments — what he calls 'ground projects' — as merely one set of inputs into the utilitarian calculus. A person's ground projects are the things that give their life meaning: relationships, creative work, political causes. But utilitarianism demands that we be willing to abandon any of these whenever the impartial calculation says so. This is an unreasonable demand, because it asks us to alienate ourselves from the very things that make our lives worth living. If you truly adopted the utilitarian standpoint, you would cease to be a person with integrity — you'd be a utility-maximizing machine with no particular attachments of your own.

The 'one thought too many' is really just a vivid illustration of this same integrity objection. In both cases, Williams is showing us that utilitarianism requires a kind of psychological stance that is incompatible with being a full human agent. The theory isn't just too demanding in terms of what it asks us to do — it's too demanding in terms of what it asks us to become. By demanding that we always take up the impartial point of view, it makes authentic human relationships impossible. Williams's conclusion is that utilitarianism is therefore false as a moral theory, because any adequate moral theory must be compatible with the kind of creatures we are."
