For the past few years I've been covering stories at the intersection of art, technology, and authenticity — forgeries exposed by AI image analysis, museums producing high-resolution digital twins of their collections, the strange career of artists who legally paint exact copies of famous works, and the philosophical disputes that surface whenever a long-trusted attribution collapses. The more cases I encounter, the less confident I am that I have any coherent view about why originals matter, or when they matter, or what kind of value is lost when we learn that something is not what we thought it was. My gut reactions to particular cases pull in different directions, and I would like to know whether there is a principle that captures most of them — and which of my verdicts I would have to give up to get one.

Here are the cases as they strike me, honestly reported.

(1) A small museum owns a portrait long catalogued as a minor Rembrandt. New technical analysis reveals it was painted by one of Rembrandt's students, working in the master's studio, using the same materials and techniques, to a standard nearly indistinguishable from Rembrandt's own work of that period. The painting itself has not changed; the brushstrokes are exactly the brushstrokes they always were. And yet my reaction is that the painting has lost something important — its value drops, and I understand why the museum would move it from the main gallery to a side room. I do not think this is merely about market price; I think I am tracking something aesthetic or historical.

(2) Imagine a device that can produce a physical replica of any object, identical down to the molecular arrangement. It produces a copy of the Mona Lisa that is, by any physical measure, indistinguishable from the one in the Louvre. Suppose I am offered a viewing of either the original or the replica, but I am not told which is which. I find that I do not care which one I see. If I later learn I saw the replica, I feel mildly cheated, but I cannot articulate what I was cheated of — the visual experience was identical.

(3) A novelist submits a manuscript that wins a major prize. It later emerges that the manuscript is a word-for-word copy of an out-of-print novel from the 1950s by an obscure author. The plagiarist changed nothing but the title page. Before the revelation, I read the novel and admired it. After the revelation, I cannot read it the same way — the prose feels hollow to me, even though I know the words are the same words I admired before. My verdict: the work is genuinely diminished by the exposure, not merely my opinion of it.

(4) A friend loses his wedding ring, which his late wife placed on his finger forty years ago. Without telling him, his daughter has an identical ring made — same design, same metal, same wear patterns artificially reproduced — and swaps it for the lost one. He never notices. My verdict: something has gone wrong here. The ring on his finger is not the ring his wife gave him, even though he believes it is and would never know otherwise. But I struggle to say what the wrong consists in, given that his experience is unchanged.

(5) A major museum creates an ultra-high-resolution digital scan of a Vermeer and displays it on a screen in a gallery next to the original, which sits behind reflective glass in dim light. Viewers report that the digital version lets them see details — brushwork, color gradations — that are invisible on the original under its protective display conditions. And yet I would still cross the room to stand in front of the original, and I suspect most people would. I do not think this is mere superstition, but I cannot say what value I am tracking.

(6) A skilled jazz pianist transcribes, note for note, a famous improvised solo by a deceased master and performs it in concert. The performance is flawless and moving. My verdict: the performance has less value than the original improvisation, even if the audience cannot tell them apart. But this seems to be a different kind of value-loss than in the forgery case — the pianist is not deceiving anyone, and the performance is genuinely skillful.

(7) An AI system trained on the complete works of a great painter generates a new canvas in the style of that painter. The result is beautiful, original in its composition, and would be praised if a human had made it. My verdict: I value it less than I would value the same image if a human had painted it, but I value it more than I would value a forgery intended to deceive. I am not sure why.

I can feel that some of these verdicts are in tension — (1) and (2) seem to point in opposite directions about whether physical identity is enough, and (3) and (4) both involve deception but the loss feels different in each case. Is there a single principle, or a small set of principles, that captures the pattern across these cases? Which of my verdicts would a coherent view force me to revise, and what would that view say about a case I have not considered: a forgery so good that it is later discovered to have been painted by another, equally great master — not the one it was attributed to, but someone whose work we value independently?
