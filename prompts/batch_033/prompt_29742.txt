Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading philosophy, and I have found myself puzzled by a pattern in the digital systems that now mediate so much of ordinary life. Nearly every website and mobile application begins by presenting a consent notice: a long privacy policy, rarely read, with a button that makes the data collection proceed. The design seems to say that privacy is mine to dispose of — that once I have been informed, however formally, and have clicked my agreement, the collection and processing of my personal data is made acceptable by my choice. But the very same regulatory regimes that built this consent machinery also refuse to treat every kind of personal data as something an individual can sign away. Certain categories of information — health data, biometric data, information about children — receive protection that consent cannot waive, and even processing to which a user has consented remains subject to purpose limitation and data minimization, constraints that no individual click can override. The practice therefore speaks in two voices: privacy as a personal asset to be traded for convenience, and privacy as a standing constraint that society enforces whether or not the individual chooses to waive it.

What theory of the nature and value of privacy does this practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose a control-based account — the view that privacy consists in control over information about oneself — or is that account merely the one the practice advertises, while its non-waivable elements quietly presuppose something else? I would like a reconstruction of the implicit theory, drawn from the concrete details of the practice rather than from the theory labels themselves. I would also like to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival views would license the same design — and where its parts presuppose inconsistent commitments. And I would like to be told what would have to change in the design and enforcement of these systems if one or another rival view of privacy's value were adopted instead. If the practice is internally inconsistent, I want to know precisely where the inconsistency lies and which element is doing the real normative work.
