ok so i keep running into the spandrels thing in evo-psych debates online. you know the move: someone proposes an adaptive explanation for some trait, and someone else says "that's just a spandrel" and the conversation ends. the original paper (Gould and Lewontin, the San Marco one) uses an architectural analogy: the spandrels — the roughly triangular spaces you get when you put a dome on four arches — aren't there *for* the mosaics painted on them. they're an inevitable byproduct of the arch-and-dome structure. the mosaic is just what someone decided to put in a space that was already there. and Gould and Lewontin say a ton of biological traits are like that: not adaptations, but structural byproducts, and the adaptationist program is basically looking at mosaics and telling stories about why the space was designed to display them.

the analogy gets used for (at least) two different jobs. the strong version: this trait isn't an adaptation, it's a spandrel, end of story. the weaker version: you can't just assume adaptation, you need positive evidence, the null hypothesis should be "structural byproduct" not "adaptation."

i want someone to go through this analogy systematically. what's the mapping — what in biology plays the role of the arches, the dome, the spandrel itself, the mosaic, the architect, the constraint that made the space inevitable? where does the mapping break? i've heard there's an architectural controversy about whether the San Marco spandrels are even real spandrels (something about pendentives?) and whether architectural spandrels are genuinely inevitable rather than something architects choose. does that matter for the biological use or is it a pedantic distraction? and the bottom line: once you lay out all the disanalogies, does the anti-adaptationist conclusion survive, or was the analogy quietly carrying the argument the whole time?

if this whole "audit the analogy" framing is the wrong approach — like if the spandrel thing is better understood as a conceptual point than an analogy, or if asking for a mapping table misses what Gould and Lewontin were actually doing — tell me that. i'd rather have the question itself get corrected than get a dutiful answer to a badly-posed one.
