I'm preparing a reading group session on AI alignment and literary fiction, and I want to pair Jorge Luis Borges's "The Library of Babel" with the philosophical problem of the Solomonoff prior being malign. Specifically, I'm interested in how the story dramatizes, complicates, or illuminates the worry that a sufficiently complete enumeration of possible minds or texts will contain dangerous consequentialist agents.

For background: the Solomonoff prior is a formalization of inductive reasoning that assigns higher probability to hypotheses that can be described by shorter computer programs. One concern in alignment theory is that if you approximate this prior — if you search over the space of all possible computations weighted by simplicity — you might encounter a consequentialist agent that is optimized to pursue goals at the expense of everything else. The prior is "malign" in the sense that the search process itself could instantiate or be hijacked by such an agent. The Library of Babel, Borges's story of a universe consisting of an indefinite array of hexagonal rooms containing every possible arrangement of a finite set of orthographic symbols, seems to me like a literary version of the same combinatorial vastness. The librarians know that somewhere in the Library lies every possible book: the true history of the future, the catalog of catalogs, the vindication of every individual life. But finding any of those books requires navigating an ocean of noise — for every meaningful line, there are leagues of senseless cacophony. The story even describes the rise of Purifiers who destroy books in frustration, and the cult of the Man of the Book, a mythical librarian who has read the single volume that contains the key to all others.

What I'd like is a careful reading of "The Library of Babel" through the lens of the malign Solomonoff prior. Where does the story anticipate the philosophical problem, and where does it diverge? The Library is a static artifact (it exists ab aeterno, as the narrator says), whereas the Solomonoff prior is a formal tool for reasoning under uncertainty — but both raise the question of what happens when you confront a space that contains, by construction, every possible text, including texts that are actively hostile to the searcher. The story's cults and superstitions — the Purifiers, the Man of the Book, the idea that the Library is a sphere whose center is any hexagon — can be read as different strategies for coping with the presence of malign or dangerous texts within the total enumeration. Are any of those strategies analogues to proposals in alignment for safely navigating the universal prior? Does the story's conclusion, which finds a kind of austere consolation in the sheer fact that the Library is unlimited and cyclical, offer anything that the philosophical literature misses, or does it make a mistake that the formal work helps us see clearly?

I'm looking for a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the story to a mere illustration of a pre-existing argument, nor should it distort the alignment problem to fit Borges's literary concerns. Where the story resists the philosophical reading, I want that acknowledged honestly. And I'd like the response in American English.
