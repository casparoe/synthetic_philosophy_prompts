I'm putting together a handout for a second-year philosophy of perception module and I want it to be one of those 'which theory do you actually hold?' sorting quizzes, not a 'which label sounds like you?' thing. The topic is the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist response to it, and the students have just read the classic argument from illusion setup—bent stick in water, hallucinated dagger, afterimage, the works. I want the quiz to sort them by what they actually say about cases, so every question should be a concrete case with two or three options that are real answers someone could give, never 'do you think perception is a relation to sense-data?' or anything that uses the theory's own vocabulary.

The positions that should end up as outcomes, one way or another: sense-datum theory, adverbialism, intentionalism (representationalism), metaphysical disjunctivism, epistemological disjunctivism, and naive realism. But please build the quiz from the logical space first—figure out which diagnostic questions actually split these positions, list the combinations of answers the questions allow, and only then place the named positions in that space. I'd especially like a note on any combination of answers that has no one defending it, and whether that's because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Also flag any two leaves that the quiz can't really separate (I suspect naive realism and metaphysical disjunctivism come out the same on every case question about veridical perception and only differ on what to say about hallucination, so the case questions may not separate them cleanly).

Cases I definitely want in there, because they're the ones the course uses: the stick that looks bent in water but is straight, the afterimage you still 'see' when you close your eyes, pressing on your eyeball and seeing double, and a hallucination produced by direct brain stimulation that the subject cannot tell from a real perception. Eight to ten questions is the right size. The point of the whole thing is to get students to notice that the argument from illusion only gets a common factor between illusion and veridical perception if you've already answered some case questions a certain way—so the scoring key should make that visible, maybe by grouping questions under which assumption they're really testing. If some of my required positions can't be reached by any pattern of answers to your questions, tell me and say which case or claim would seperate them.
