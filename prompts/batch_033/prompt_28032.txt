Okay so I've been thinking about Bertrand's paradox — you know, the one where you ask "what's the probability a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle" and you get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on how you define "random." And the standard takeaway seems to be that the principle of indifference is busted: you can't just say "assign equal probability when you have no reason to discriminate" because what counts as equally likely depends on how you carve up the possibilities.

But here's what I don't get. If the principle of indifference is broken, doesn't that take down a huge chunk of Bayesian practice with it? Every time someone uses a flat prior, or a maximum entropy prior, or says "I'm being agnostic so I'll use a uniform distribution over this parameter," aren't they just doing the principle of indifference? Like, a uniform prior over a parameter is literally saying "I have no reason to favor any value over any other, so they're all equally probable." That's the exact move Bertrand showed was incoherent. So how do Bayesians not see this as a fatal problem? Or am I misunderstanding what Bertrand's paradox actually shows?
