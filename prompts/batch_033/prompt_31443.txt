I've been working through an argument about Moore's paradox that seems to force a choice between two unacceptable positions, and I can't find the flaw. Here it is, numbered:

(1) The sentence "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" is not a logical contradiction — both conjuncts can be true simultaneously.

(2) Yet I cannot sincerely assert it, and I cannot even coherently think it (in the first person, present tense).

(3) The standard explanation of the unassertability is that assertion expresses belief: asserting p represents oneself as believing p, so asserting the conjunction pragmatically contradicts the second conjunct.

(4) But this explanation only covers assertion — it doesn't explain why the thought is equally absurd. Thoughts don't express anything to an audience.

(5) So the absurdity must be located in the nature of belief itself: there is something about believing p that rules out simultaneously believing that one doesn't believe p.

(6) The leading candidate is the transparency thesis: to determine whether I believe p, I don't look inward; I look outward and ask whether p is true. If I judge that p, I thereby believe p. So the first-person question "Do I believe p?" reduces to "Is p true?"

(7) From transparency it follows that if I judge that p, I am automatically in a position to know that I believe p — self-knowledge of conscious belief is infallible.

(8) But self-knowledge of belief is not infallible: people are demonstrably wrong about their own beliefs in cases of implicit bias, self-deception, and motivated reasoning.

(9) Therefore transparency is false, and with it the only compelling explanation of premise (5) collapses.

(10) We are left with no explanation of why Moore-paradoxical thoughts are incoherent — an explanatory gap as troubling as the paradox itself.

Tell me which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs. If you prefer to reject (8), explain why implicit bias and the like don't count as counterexamples to transparency. If you reject (6), tell me what alternative explains (5). Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
