I am a PhD student in philosophy working on social epistemology, and I keep finding the Tuskegee syphilis study cited as a stock example of epistemic injustice, usually in a sentence or two and then dropped. I want a slow, careful case analysis rather than a gesture. Let me fix the facts as I understand them, so the analysis doesn't turn on details I may have wrong: between 1932 and 1972, the United States Public Health Service ran a study in Macon County, Alabama, involving roughly six hundred Black men, about four hundred of whom had syphilis and about two hundred of whom served as controls. The men were recruited on the understanding that they were being treated for bad blood; they received placebos and were subjected to painful diagnostic procedures such as spinal taps, presented as treatment. Even after penicillin became the standard cure in the mid-1940s, the researchers withheld it and took steps to keep participants from getting treatment elsewhere. The study ended only after a whistleblower's disclosures led to a press exposé, and it was followed by congressional hearings and reforms in research ethics.

I would like you to analyze this episode through the lens of epistemic injustice, and I want the analysis to run in both directions: what the framework makes visible in the case, and what the case does to the framework. Please be disciplined about the mapping rather than applying the labels loosely. Which aspects of the episode are genuinely captured by testimonial injustice, understood in Fricker's sense as a credibility deficit owed to identity prejudice — and where does that description start to feel forced, given that the men were not merely disbelieved but actively deceived? Which aspects are better captured by hermeneutical injustice, and where should we locate the relevant gap in shared interpretive resources: in the participants, in the wider society, or in the medical institutions that framed the study as benign? I am also interested in the flip side of the credibility economy — the credibility excess granted to the researchers — and in the way the relevant deficits and excesses seem to have been built into institutional routines and a longer history of racialized medical abuse, not just into the attitudes of individual hearers. The deeper question I want answered is whether this case confirms the standard framework, extends it, or shows that a purely epistemic analysis is too thin to carry the full weight of what happened — and, if the last, what an adequate theory of epistemic injustice would need to add.
