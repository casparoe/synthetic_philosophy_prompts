I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether our thoughts and decisions actually cause anything we do. She laid out a version of the causal exclusion argument that I had not encountered before, and by the end I could not find the flaw, but I also cannot accept where it lands. I am hoping someone can walk me through the diagnosis.

Here is the argument as she presented it, reconstructed as carefully as I can manage:

(1) Suppose you are a non-reductive physicalist: you hold that mental states (beliefs, desires, intentions) are real, but they are not identical to physical states. They are realised by, or supervene on, physical states without being reducible to them.

(2) The physical domain is causally closed: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. If you trace the causal history of your arm's rising, you will find a chain of neural events, muscle contractions, and so on, each with a sufficient physical predecessor.

(3) If a mental event M causes a physical event P, and P also has a sufficient physical cause P*, then P is causally overdetermined — unless M just is P*.

(4) Systematic causal overdetermination is wildly implausible. It is not as though every time you act, the universe has arranged two independent causal chains — one mental, one physical — converging on the same effect.

(5) Therefore, by (2), (3), and (4), if M is not identical to P*, M does not cause P.

(6) If you are a non-reductive physicalist, M is not identical to P*.

(7) So, given non-reductive physicalism, mental events do not cause physical events. Your beliefs and desires are epiphenomenal: they accompany the physical causal chain but contribute nothing to it.

(8) If you reject non-reductive physicalism and instead identify mental states with physical states, you avoid the exclusion problem — but you inherit all the difficulties of reductionism: you seem to lose the multiple realisability of mental states, you face the hard problem of explaining qualia in physical terms, and you may have to say that creatures with different neural architectures cannot share the same beliefs.

(9) If you instead reject the causal closure of the physical, you avoid the problem too — but you are now committed to the existence of physical events with no sufficient physical cause, which seems to contradict conservation laws and the explanatory completeness of physics.

(10) So every available position seems to exact a heavy price: epiphenomenalism, reductionism, or a denial of physical causal closure.

My friend stopped here and said the argument is valid, so the only question is which premise costs the least to give up. I pushed back on several points — I wondered whether the overdetermination in (4) really is the problematic kind, or whether the relation between a mental cause and its physical realiser is something other than identity or competition — but she had responses I could not shake. I walked away troubled.

I would like a careful diagnosis. Which step or premise should be rejected, and why? If you think the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to abandon, and what does abandoning it commit one to? I am especially interested in whether the notion of 'sufficient cause' in (2) and (3) is doing more work than it looks like it is doing, and whether there is a way of understanding the mind–body relation that makes (3) false without collapsing into reductionism. Please write your answer in British English.
