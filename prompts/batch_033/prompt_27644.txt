I'm a science journalist and I've been reading up on the Libet experiments for a piece on whether neuroscience has killed free will, and I've managed to argue myself into a corner. Let me lay out the chain, because I can't tell which link is the weak one.

(1) In Libet's setup, subjects are told to flex their wrist whenever they feel like it, and to note the time they first become aware of the urge to move. The readiness potential—the slow negative shift in EEG over motor areas—starts about 550 ms before the movement, while the conscious urge is reported around 200 ms before. (2) The standard reading is that since the readiness potential comes first and predicts the movement, the conscious urge isn't doing the initiating; it's a late, epiphenomenal byproduct of unconscious neural processes. (3) But the same logic should apply to any conscious episode. Conscious deliberation, conscious reasoning, conscious evaluation of evidence all have unconscious neural precursors that begin before the conscious state itself does. (4) So if having an unconscious neural precursor means not doing the causing, then conscious reasoning never causes the conclusions we reach. (5) Now take the neuroscientist who consciously interprets Libet's data and concludes that free will is an illusion. By (4), that conclusion wasn't caused by their conscious weighing of evidence; it was produced by unconscious processes and merely showed up in awareness. (6) But a conclusion that isn't caused by conscious weighing of evidence isn't one we have reason to trust. (7) So the Libet argument for free will being an illusion, if sound, undercuts it's own claim to have been arrived at rationally. (8) That can't be right, so something has to give.

I'm not asking you to adjudicate the free will debate in general. I want to know which step or steps you'd reject, why that step looked so natural, and what rejecting it costs. For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep (6)—the idea that conscious reasoning is doing real epistemic work—and I'm genuinely not sure whether the whole thing turns on an equivocation somewhere, or whether it's valid and I just have to swallow an uncomfortable price. Also: if the answer is that the wrist-flex case and the reasoning case are just different, I need to hear why that isn't a dodge, since every argument from Libet to the claim that conscious will is an illusion depends on the two cases being similar enough to matter.
