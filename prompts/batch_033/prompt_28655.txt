I'm teaching an undergraduate aesthetics course next semester, and I want to spend a week on the paradox of tragedy using Sophocles' Oedipus Rex as our central case study. The students will have read the play and also a couple of philosophical treatments — probably Aristotle's remarks on catharsis in the Poetics and Hume's essay "Of Tragedy." But I want to go beyond the standard survey and give them a reading that really wrestles with whether the philosophical theories actually capture what's going on when we watch or read this particular play.

Here's what I'm after. The paradox of tragedy, at its simplest, asks why we voluntarily expose ourselves to artworks that make us feel pity, fear, and grief — emotions we would normally avoid — and why we come away not just tolerating the experience but valuing it. Oedipus Rex seems like an especially hard case for several theories. For one thing, the play offers very little in the way of compensatory aesthetic pleasure of the kind Hume points to: the language, while powerful, is fairly austere, and the plot is relentlessly grim, with no comic relief and no redemptive turn at the end. For another, the play's emotional trajectory is peculiar: we begin knowing the outcome (the audience already knows the myth), and the dramatic interest lies almost entirely in watching Oedipus move from ignorance to knowledge, with each step making things worse. If catharsis is about the purgation of pity and fear, what exactly is purged here, and when? The play ends with Oedipus blinded and broken, and the chorus tells us to count no man happy until he is dead — hardly a resolution that leaves us feeling cleansed.

I'd like a reading that takes both the play and the philosophical debate seriously. What does Oedipus Rex specifically reveal about the limits of the major solutions to the paradox? Are there moments in the play where a particular theory — say, a meta-response view like Susan Feagin's, where we take pleasure in our own capacity for empathic response — seems to capture something real, and other moments where it falls flat? Does the play suggest something the philosophical literature misses: perhaps something about the cognitive value of watching necessity unfold, or about the peculiar satisfaction of seeing someone come to know the worst? I'm also interested in whether the fact that this is a play — meant for performance, with a chorus, masks, music — rather than a narrative poem or a novel matters for how the paradox operates. Does the theatricality of the suffering change what kind of pleasure or value we get from it?

I want to come away with something I can give my students that doesn't just apply theories to the play like a cookie-cutter but instead lets the play push back and complicate the theories. And I'd like the reading to end by saying something about what Oedipus Rex adds to the philosophical conversation that the arguments alone don't capture — what the experience of the play teaches that a paper on the paradox cannot.
