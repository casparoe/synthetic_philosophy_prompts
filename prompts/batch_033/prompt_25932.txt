I'm a PhD student in medieval philosophy and I've been wrestling with a passage from al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* (The Incoherence of the Philosophers), Problem XVII, where he's arguing against the necessity of causal connections. I get the broad picture — he's an occasionalist, God is the real cause, etc. — but there's a move in this passage that I can't quite parse, and I'd love help thinking it through.

Here's the passage (from the Kamali translation):

> How can one prove that it is an agent? The only argument is from the observation of the fact of burning at the time of contact with fire. But observation only shows that one is with the other, not that it is by it and has no other cause than it. [...] So it is clear that existence with a thing does not prove being by it. Let us illustrate. Suppose there is a blind man whose eyes are diseased, and who has not heard from anyone of the difference between night and day. If one day his disease is cured, and he can consequently see colours, he will guess that the agent of the perception of the forms of colours which has now been acquired by his eyes is the opening of the eyes. This will not be absolutely untrue, inasmuch as given the soundness of the eyes: and their being open: and the obstruction being removed: and the object before him having colour, it follows that he must be able to see, and it would be unintelligible if he were not. But when the Sun is set, and the atmosphere is dark, he will acquire the knowledge that the light of the Sun was the cause of the impression of colours upon his sight. Therefore, how can our opponent safely ignore the possibility that: (a) among the Principles of Being there should be causes from which flow the temporal events which are observed to be connected with each other; (b) unlike bodies in motion, such temporal events should not be liable to destruction or elimination by us, and (c) that if they were so liable, only then could we apprehend their separability from each other, and would consequently understand that their cause lies beyond what we observe?

Here's what's bothering me. The blind-man illustration seems to pull in two directions at once. On one hand, al-Ghazālī is using it to say: look, the blind man was wrong about what caused his vision — he thought it was just his eyes opening, but it was really the sunlight. So by analogy, we might be wrong about fire causing burning; the real cause could be something hidden (God, angels). That's the standard occasionalist move. But on the other hand, the blind man *figures it out*. He observes the sunset, notices the correlation breaks, and correctly infers the real cause. So the illustration seems to show that empirical investigation *can* get you to the true cause after all — which is exactly what al-Ghazālī seems to be denying when he says observation can never establish causation.

So my question is: is this illustration doing the work al-Ghazālī thinks it is, or is there a tension here? Specifically, the blind man learns the true cause through exactly the kind of observation (noticing what happens when the candidate cause is absent) that al-Ghazālī's own epistemology of causation seems to rule out for the fire-and-cotton case. Is al-Ghazālī aware of this tension, and is there a reading that resolves it? Or is he making a more limited point than I'm giving him credit for — maybe that the illustration is only meant to establish the *possibility* of hidden causes, not to model how we'd discover them?

I'd appreciate it if you could say how confident you are in your reading and flag the point you're least sure about.
