I've settled into the view that constitutional precommitment is best understood as a Ulysses contract writ large: a polity, at a moment of relative clarity, binds its future self against predictable weakness of will, panic, or factional passion, and the legitimacy of those binds comes from the same place as an individual's present decision to constrain their future self. I keep noticing how much work this analogy is doing in how people talk about entrenched rights, supermajority requirements, judicial review, even central bank independence. What would count against it?

I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed defender of this view would themselves concede count against it, not just what critics say. Where exactly does the analogy break down—in who consents, in whether later generations are the same "self," in whether the precommitment actually preserves the preferences it was meant to protect rather than changing them? What would have to be true for the Ulysses picture to be the wrong way to understand constitutional legitimacy, rather than just incomplete? Are there observations or cases—amendment fights, derogation clauses, sunset provisions, courts reading entrenched clauses as living instruments—that should move me more than others? What does this picture predict about when precommitment goes badly that rival pictures don't predict? And is the view at risk of being insulated by saying it's "only a model" or "only a metaphor," and would that be a problem for a view doing this much normative work? If it helps, I'm personally most moved by the thought that constitutions don't just bind future choices, they shape future agents, so the Ulysses picture may be assuming the very continuity it needs.
