I'm taking a philosophy of language course this term, and we've spent the last few weeks on Davidson's radical interpretation and the surrounding debates about charity, belief, and meaning. I've found that I have fairly firm intuitions about particular interpretation cases, but I'm struggling to articulate the principle that ties them all together—if one even exists. I was hoping you could help me extract it, or tell me if the project is misguided.

Here are my verdicts on seven cases. (1) A field linguist encounters a speaker who says "gavagai" whenever a rabbit scurries past, and never otherwise. The linguist translates "gavagai" as "rabbit." This seems obviously correct to me. (2) The linguist later discovers that the speaker also says "gavagai" when shown a close-up photograph of a rabbit's ear with no rabbit visible. I still think "rabbit" is the right translation; the speaker is simply mistaken about what the photograph depicts. (3) But now suppose the speaker says "gavagai" for rabbits, hares, pikas, and stuffed toy rabbits, and never for anything else. I no longer think "rabbit" is right; the term clearly means something broader—perhaps "small furry hopping thing" or similar. (4) An anthropologist studies a remote community whose members say things that, translated word-for-word, come out as "the mountain spirits cause the rain" and "the shaman leaves his body at night." These claims strike me as false, but I think the right translation is the straightforward one: they have false beliefs about the world, not different meanings for "mountain," "spirit," or "cause." (5) Yet when a member of the same community says something that translates literally as "the number four is jealous of the number seven," I balk. I think the interpreter should look for a non-literal reading, or conclude that "jealous" or "four" carries a different sense in this context, because the literal reading ascribes a belief that isn't just false but barely intelligible. (6) Two speakers from the community give apparently contradictory testimony about a ritual: one says it must be performed facing the sunrise, the other says it must be performed facing the sunset. I think the interpreter should first try hard to find a reading on which both are correct—perhaps they are describing different rituals, or different phases of the same one—but if no such reading is available, it is fine to conclude that one speaker is simply mistaken. (7) Finally, a single English speaker sincerely says "I have arthritis in my thigh." I think this person has a false belief about arthritis, not an idiolect in which "arthritis" includes thigh pain. But if an entire linguistic community used "arthritis" for both joint inflammation and certain muscular pains, I would say the word simply means something different in their dialect, and no one is making a mistake.

My question is: is there a single coherent principle of interpretation—something about charity, or about the belief/meaning distinction, or about the division of linguistic labor—that delivers all seven of these verdicts? If so, what is it, stated precisely, and what does it predict about a case I haven't considered: a speaker who uses "know" in ways that seem to track justified true belief except that they never apply it in Gettier cases, even when they agree the belief is justified and true? And if no single principle captures all seven, which of my verdicts should I reconsider, and why? If my framing of the problem is confused—if, for instance, I'm assuming a distinction between "literal" and "non-literal" meaning that Davidson's view would reject, or if the cases don't cut where I think they do—please say so plainly rather than offering a principle that papers over the confusion.
