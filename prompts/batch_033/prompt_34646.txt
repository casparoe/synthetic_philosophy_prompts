Several countries are constructing deep geological repositories for high-level nuclear waste, designed to isolate spent fuel for periods on the order of 100,000 years. The costs run into the billions. The designers must even consider how to mark the sites so that distant future civilizations — whose languages and conceptual schemes we cannot predict — will understand the danger.

Yet these same governments, when evaluating the costs and benefits of climate policy, routinely apply discount rates that render harms more than a few centuries out effectively negligible. A life lost to climate change in the year 2500 counts for almost nothing in a standard cost-benefit analysis.

What does the nuclear-waste practice presuppose about the expected lifespan of humanity and about our obligations to the distant future, and is that presupposition consistent with the discounting practice? The doomsday argument suggests that we should expect to find ourselves near the middle of the human timeline rather than at the very beginning, which would mean humanity's total lifespan is unlikely to stretch into the hundreds of thousands of years. Does the repository practice implicitly reject that argument, or is there a way for both practices — and the doomsday argument — to be reconciled? Reconstruct the implicit theory of intergenerational obligation that each practice requires, and say whether the inconsistency is genuine or merely apparent.
