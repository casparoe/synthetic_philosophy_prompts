I've been reading about the lottery paradox and the preface paradox on my own, and I came across a summary in a popular philosophy book that explains them together. It goes roughly like this:

"The lottery paradox and the preface paradox are two versions of the same basic problem about rational belief. In the lottery paradox, you know that in a fair lottery with a million tickets, each ticket is almost certainly a loser, so you're justified in believing of any particular ticket that it will lose. But if you conjoin all those beliefs, you end up believing that every ticket will lose, which contradicts your knowledge that some ticket will win. The preface paradox is the same structure: an author believes each individual sentence in her carefully researched book, but in the preface she honestly writes that she is sure the book contains some errors — so she believes the book contains at least one falsehood. The conjunction of all her individual beliefs contradicts the preface belief. Both paradoxes show that the principle that rational belief is closed under conjunction — if you're rational to believe P and rational to believe Q, you're rational to believe P and Q — must be abandoned, because it leads straight to contradiction in these perfectly ordinary cases. The moral is that you can be rational while holding inconsistent beliefs, and this is just a fact about how human cognition works."

Something about this summary bothers me, but I lack the background to pin down what. It seems to me that the two paradoxes might be importantly different — the lottery case involves statistical evidence while the preface case involves something more like intellectual humility or fallibility. And I recall hearing that some philosophers think the closure principle can be saved by adjusting other assumptions instead. Could you go through this summary and tell me what is accurate, what is misleading or oversimplified, and what a philosopher working on these paradoxes would object to?
