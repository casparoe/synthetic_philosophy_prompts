I'm taking a philosophy elective at my high school this semester, and we've just finished a unit on perception and the senses. One of the problems that really stuck with me is Molyneux's problem. As our teacher explained it, William Molyneux wrote to John Locke in the late 1600s with a thought experiment: imagine a person who has been blind since birth and has learned to tell a cube from a sphere purely by touch — the cube's sharp edges and flat faces meeting at right angles, the sphere's smooth continuous curve with no breaks or corners. If this person were suddenly able to see, and the cube and sphere were set on a table in front of them, would they be able to point to the cube and say "that one's the cube" without reaching out to touch either object? Locke answered no: he thought the connection between touch and sight is learned through experience, not innate. But other philosophers have disagreed, and the question turns out to be deeper than it first looks — it's not just about one person's hypothetical surgery, but about whether our concepts are tied to specific sensory modalities or whether they somehow float above them, accessible from any angle.

For our final project, we're supposed to present on one of the topics we covered in a creative format, and I want to try writing a poem. Specifically, I'd like you to write a sonnet about Molyneux's problem. The octave should immerse the reader in the blind person's tactile world — the vocabulary of touch, the way a cube announces itself through corners and edges and flat resistance, the way a sphere offers nothing but continuous curvature and the absence of any angle. The volta should be the moment sight arrives, and the sestet should wrestle with the question: does the newly sighted person recognize the shapes immediately, or is the visual world a foreign language that has to be learned from scratch? The closing couplet shouldn't settle the question definitively, but it should crystallize what's philosophically at stake — something about the relationship between the senses and the unity (or disunity) of our concepts across them. I want the poem to be precise enough that someone who knows the problem and its history — Locke's empiricist answer, the rationalist replies, and even the modern experimental attempts to test it with cataract patients — would find it faithful rather than superficial.
