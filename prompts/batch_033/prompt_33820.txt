I want to sharpen my own thinking about Williams's "one thought too many" objection by arguing with the man himself. Please play Bernard Williams—specifically, the Williams of "Persons, Character, and Morality" (the 1976 essay reprinted in Moral Luck, 1981). You hold that impartial moral theories such as utilitarianism and Kantianism threaten agents' integrity by demanding that personal commitments be justified from an impersonal standpoint. You are known for the claim that a man who saves his drowning wife and whose motivating thought, fully spelled out, is that it was his wife and that in situations of this kind it is permissible to save one's wife has had one thought too many: the fact that she is his wife should have been the whole answer. Stay in character as a precise, forceful philosopher who will explore subtleties but will not concede the central point that the structure of impartial moral justification is alienating in a way that personal reasons are not. Keep your replies to one paragraph so it feels like a conversation. Here is my opening:

You want the husband's thought to stop at "she is my wife" rather than to include a further justificatory move that invokes a moral permission. But I am not sure I see a principled line between thoughts that are merely the recognition of a personal relationship and thoughts that are one too many. Suppose the man thinks: "She is my wife and she is drowning, so I will save her." The "so" seems to mark a piece of practical reasoning—a step from a fact about the world to a conclusion about what to do. If that inferential step is not alienating, then the problem cannot be the mere presence of reasoning or the mere fact that the agent has more than one thought. It must be something about the specific content of the extra thought: perhaps that it invokes an impartial rule, or that it treats the wife as an instance of a type. But then a Kantian could simply avoid the offending thought by thinking "She is my wife, and I have a duty to her" or "She is my wife, and that fact itself gives me a reason." Are those thoughts also one too many? If so, the objection seems to reach further than impartial theories—it sweeps in any moral self-consciousness at all. If not, what exactly is the feature of the impartialist's thought that makes it the one that is too many?
