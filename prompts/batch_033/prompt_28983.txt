"Explain the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe. Does the argument succeed in establishing its conclusion? Discuss at least one objection."

And here is the student's answer:

The Rietdijk–Putnam argument draws on the special theory of relativity to support eternalism, the view that past, present, and future events are all equally real — often called the block universe. The argument turns on the relativity of simultaneity. In special relativity, whether two spatially separated events occur at the same time is not absolute but depends on the observer's state of motion. For any pair of spacelike-separated events, there exists some inertial reference frame in which those events are simultaneous.

Now take my present moment — the set of events simultaneous with my here-and-now in my own rest frame. According to the argument, if there is some other inertial frame in which a distant future event (a supernova in Andromeda a week from now, say) is simultaneous with my writing this sentence, then that future event is happening now relative to that frame, and must therefore be just as real as the event of my writing. Since we can run this reasoning for any arbitrarily chosen future or past event, it follows that all events in spacetime are equally real and determinate. The universe is a four-dimensional block; the passage of time is a subjective illusion.

The argument is compelling because it seems to follow directly from well-confirmed physics. If special relativity is correct, and if we accept that what is real is whatever is simultaneous with us in some physically admissible reference frame, then eternalism appears inescapable. The Andromeda paradox, introduced by Roger Penrose, makes this vivid: two people walking past each other on a street can have present moments that differ by days with respect to events in the Andromeda galaxy. For one pedestrian, a decision by an Andromedean council to launch a space fleet has already been taken; for the other, that decision still lies in the future. This shows that the future is already fixed, independently of what anyone knows or does.

The argument has, however, been criticised. Howard Stein and others have argued that in relativity theory, the notion of simultaneity is strictly local and cannot be uniquely extended to distant events in a way that yields a global, frame-independent present. The Rietdijk–Putnam argument assumes we can meaningfully speak of what is happening now at a distant location, but relativity teaches precisely that there is no unique answer to that question. If there is no fact of the matter about what distant events are simultaneous with my here-and-now, then the argument cannot get off the ground. Additionally, a presentist can simply reject the premise that reality should be tied to simultaneity across all inertial frames; they can insist that only events in their own frame's present are real. This move may sit uncomfortably with the spirit of relativity, but it is not logically forced to abandon it.

In my view, the argument does not succeed. The physics is sound, but the step from the relativity of simultaneity to the metaphysical claim that all events are equally real requires a substantive bridging premise — that reality is coextensive with simultaneity in some frame — and this premise is precisely what a presentist would deny. The argument therefore begs the question against the presentist rather than refuting her.
