I run a philosophy discussion group — about 14 people, mostly grad students but a few sharp undergrads, we meet for 90 minutes. We've been going through some normative ethics and next session I want to do something on moral uncertainty. Not a lecture, not a seminar discussion where everyone just says what they already think. I want them to actually *inhabit* the problem: you have to make a real decision, and you're genuinely unsure which moral framework is correct, and you can't just punt.

Here's my rough idea. I'll bring $40 in cash. The group has to decide, collectively, what to do with it. But they're given three "advisor memos," each written from the perspective of a different moral theory — say, a straightforward utilitarian, a deontologist who cares about rights and side-constraints, and someone who prioritizes the worst-off (prioritarian or something). Each memo argues for a specific way to use the $40, and the three reccomendations are incompatible. The group knows the memos were written by people who genuinely hold those views, but they don't know which view (if any) is correct. They have to reach a decision about the money in 45 minutes.

What I'm stuck on: what should the three recommendations actually be? They need to be concrete enough that the group can't just split the money three ways and call it a day. And how do I structure the deliberation so they actually grapple with the uncertainty, rather than just picking the advisor they personally like best and ignoring the others? I want the structure to force them to confront questions like: should you go with the theory you think is most likely correct? Should you try to "hedge" across theories? What does hedging even mean when the theories don't share a common currency?

For the debrief (remaining time), I want to map what happened onto the formal stuff: maximizing expected choice-worthiness, the problem of intertheoretic comparisons, the "my favorite theory" objection, maybe the parliamentary model vs. the bargaining model. I also want to flag what predictably goes wrong when you run this — e.g., someone will say "well morality is subjective so just pick whatever" and try to dissolve the problem rather than solve it. And there's always the person who argues that the whole exercise is misguided because you should just act on the theory you believe, full stop — which is itself a substantive position in the moral uncertainty debate, and that's worth surfacing.

And be honest: what aspect of moral uncertainty can't this activity capture, no matter how well it goes?
