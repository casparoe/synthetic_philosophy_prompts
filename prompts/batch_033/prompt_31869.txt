I'm in a reading group that's been working through Arendt, and last week we got into a fight about a phrase that's been following me around ever since. Someone brought up a news story about a mid-level manager at a pharmaceutical company who knowingly slow-walked a safety report on a drug that was making people sick — not out of malice, just because flagging it would've messed with the quarter's numbers and made his team look bad. And one of the group members shrugged and said, "the banality of evil," like that settled it. Like the phrase itself explains something.

But the more I've thought about it, the less I know what that sentence actually says. Is it a claim about the psychology of wrongdoers (they're not mustache-twirling villains, they're dull bureaucrats)? Is it a claim about the social conditions that produce wrongdoing (evil emerges from ordinary institutional life, not from exceptional depravity)? Is it a claim about the nature of evil itself (evil is banal, not radical or deep)? Is it an excuse, a condemnation, or just a description? And when my friend deployed it, was he saying "this is what evil looks like, so don't be surprised," or "this isn't really evil, it's just paperwork," or something else entirely?

I want to go back to the group with something sharp. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses someone might be asserting when they say "thebanality of evil" — the genuinely different claims hiding inside that one sentence — and then take a definite position on which reading Arendt herself was most likely advancing and which one my friend was using? Don't just give me a menu and leave me to pick; I want you to commit to a reading of the slogan and defend it. And make sure you say whether the reading that fits Arendt actually supports the shrug my friend gave it, or whether he was using the phrase in a way that would've made her wince.
