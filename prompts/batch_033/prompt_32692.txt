I'm running an upper-level undergrad seminar on political philosophy this term, and we've spent a good chunk of time on precommitment — Elster's Ulysses and the Sirens, the constitutional entrenchment stuff, Sunstein and Holmes on precommitment, the whole bit. My students are sharp, honestly sharper than I expected, but I keep noticing the same thing in their papers: they'll say "a constitution is like a Ulysses contract" and then just sort of coast on the analogy, like invoking it does all the argumentative work for them. The problem is the analogy is doing a lot of different things at once — it's an explanatory metaphor, it's a normative justification, and it's also a rhetorical move in constitutional debates — and they're not pulling those apart. I want to nail them on this in the final exam: I want a question that makes them confront the disanalogies head-on, especially the intergenerational problem (Ulysses is one person over time; a constitution binds people who weren't even born when it was ratified) and the epistemic problem (Ulysses knew exactly what the Sirens would do; constitutional framers are guessing about future political pathologies). If they can't say something interesting about at least one of those disanalogies, they shouldn't get an A.

Could you write me the exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 marks? The marking scheme should be explicit about what separates an A (say 22–25) from a B+ (18–21) from a B (14–17) — naming the specific moves each band needs to contain. The course is cross-listed so some students are advanced undergrads and some are early-stage MA students, and the exam is a three-hour sit-down with a choice of questions, so they'd have about 45 minutes for this one. Oh, and the question should be self-contained enough that they don't need to remember the exact details of particular cases or constitutional clauses — I want to test philosophical reasoning, not recall.
