I spent my career in the physical sciences, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy with the seriousness I used to reserve for problems of measurement. I have found myself settling, at least provisionally, into a lexical threshold negative utilitarian position. As I understand it, the position has two commitments. First, the avoidance of suffering takes priority over the promotion of pleasure. Second, within suffering itself there is a threshold of severity such that a single instance of suffering above the threshold outweighs any number of instances below it, and no amount of pleasure can outweigh it either. The badness of states is therefore lexicographically ordered: one compares the worst category present before considering anything else. In the language I am used to, the view denies that pleasure and pain are commensurable on a single interval scale, and it posits a genuine discontinuity at the threshold.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position. Please give me exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, ranked from most to least damaging. For each, say whether it is decisive or merely awkward, whether a committed defender of the position would concede it or dismiss it, and what the position would have to add or give up to survive it. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the strongest objections stated on their merits. One of the five items should address whether the position, once its qualifications are added, has been insulated to the point of unfalsifiability, and whether that is a real problem for a view of this kind.
