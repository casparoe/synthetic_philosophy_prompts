I'm giving a philosophy department colloquium next month defending a broadly Wittgensteinian account of religious belief. My thesis is that religious claims — for example, that there will be a Last Judgment or that God sees everything — aren't hypotheses or explanatory posits whose rationality stands or falls with the evidence, and they aren't mere expressions of emotion either. They're more like framework commitments: pictures that regulate a believer's whole life and only make sense inside a religious form of life. So when an evidentialist asks me for the probability of the Last Judgment on the total evidence, I want to say that's a category mistake. I'm not claiming religious belief is sealed off from criticism or that its fine because it's just a language game; I think it can be criticized, but not by the standards you'd apply to a scientific hypothesis.

The audience is a philosophy of religion colloquium, mostly analytic: a big contingent of Swinburne-style evidentialists who think the rationality of theism is a matter of cumulative-case arguments and Bayes factors, plus several Reformed epistemologists who already reject the evidentialist demand but want belief in God to count as properly basic within an otherwise standard epistemology. Maybe two people in the room are sympathetic to the Wittgensteinian line but worry it collapses into fideism.
