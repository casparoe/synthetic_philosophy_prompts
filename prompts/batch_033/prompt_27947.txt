I'm a senior investigator in my organization's internal compliance office. We regularly handle cases where multiple employees appear to have violated policy in coordination — bid-rigging, covering up a safety incident, coordinated harassment, collusive pricing — and whether they acted jointly or merely in parallel matters enormously for how we classify the conduct, who is accountable for what, and what sanctions apply. Right now, investigators rely on rough heuristics: if people communicated beforehand, we call it collusion; if they didn't, we call it coincidence. But communication isn't always documented, and sometimes people converge on the same misconduct without ever exchanging a word, while in other cases a shared understanding forms through nods, omissions, and half-sentences that leave no paper trail.

I want a decision procedure my investigators can run — something grounded in what philosophy actually knows about shared intention and joint action, but usable by someone who has never read a word of action theory. It should be a sequence of questions to ask about the facts of a case, with clear stopping rules: where the procedure says "this is joint action," where it says "this is parallel individual action," and where it says "the facts don't settle it — escalate." For each question, give me the kinds of evidence that would count toward an answer (emails, meeting notes, patterns of behavior, testimony, timing, etc.) and what standard of proof each step requires.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. Three managers from different divisions submit nearly identical inflated expense reports in the same week, with no evidence they communicated. The amounts and categories match down to the line items, and all three attended the same compliance training two weeks earlier where the expense policy was discussed.

2. Two engineers jointly falsify a test result: one suggests it verbally in a meeting with no recording, the other nods and later enters the false data. The first denies ever suggesting it; the second says they only acted because they believed the first had authority to order it and that refusal would have been insubordination.

3. A group of employees in a Slack channel complain about a supervisor. Over several days, the complaints escalate, and one person proposes filing a coordinated HR complaint using identical wording. Several agree with emoji reactions. Three actually file, using the suggested language. One files separately with their own wording but references the group's discussion in their submission. A fifth person, who never posted in the channel but read the entire thread, files a complaint the same week with different wording that nonetheless echoes the group's core allegations.

Flag the points where the procedure requires a judgment call that no checklist can eliminate, and tell me honestly what it cannot do — for example, can it distinguish joint action from parallel action when all the evidence is circumstantial and the participants deny coordination? Does the procedure handle cases where the parties had different understandings of what they were jointly doing? And if the procedure draws a line that your own theory says is philosophically arbitrary but practically necessary, mark it as such.
