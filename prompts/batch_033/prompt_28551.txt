I'm a composer and electronic musician — I work mostly in a DAW, designing sounds on synthesizers, writing melodies and chord progressions, and handling arrangement and mixing. I've spent a lot of time thinking about what I'm actually doing when I'm being creative, and I've arrived at a picture that looks roughly like this: you start with some seed — a melody fragment, a timbre, a rhythmic pattern — and then you explore variations by adjusting parameters. On a synthesizer, that's literal: you turn knobs and hear what happens. But it also applies to composition: you take a motif and try it at half speed, or inverted, or with a different instrument, or reharmonized. Creativity seems to be mostly about knowing which parameters are worth tweaking and recognizing when a variation has turned into something genuinely new rather than just a degraded copy of the original.

I've heard that Douglas Hofstadter has a model of creativity that uses exactly this metaphor — "knob-twiddling" and "variations on a theme" — and I want to understand it in my own terms before I go read the original work.

Restate Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model of creativity in the vocabulary of electronic music production and composition — parameters, modulation, automation, presets, patches, signal flow, mixing, sound design, arrangement, and so on. Be as precise as you can about what maps to what. Then give me the residue report: which parts of his model translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which parts you had to stretch or fudge, and which parts have no counterpart at all in how I think about making music.
