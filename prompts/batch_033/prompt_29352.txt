I am a novelist putting the finishing touches on a work of historical fiction set in fourteenth-century Florence. The book has consumed the better part of six years. I have worked in three archives across two countries, corresponded with specialists in medieval trade networks, ecclesiastical history, and the material culture of the Italian peninsula, and kept a running file of every factual assertion in the manuscript — the price of a bolt of Lucchese silk in 1347, the precise route of a procession on the feast of Saint John, the year a particular confraternity received its charter, the names of the minor officials serving in the Signoria during a given two-month term. None of this is window dressing to me; I have staked something personal on getting it right. The central characters and their entanglements are invented, but the world they move through is not, and I have included nothing I did not, at the time of writing, believe to be true.

Now I am drafting the author's preface, and I have arrived at an impasse that I did not anticipate. I want to address my readers candidly. The manuscript contains, by my count, well over a thousand discrete historical claims. Given the fallibility of the sources, the gaps in the record, and the ordinary human propensity for error — a mistranscribed date, a secondary source I trusted that was itself mistaken — it seems to me not merely possible but overwhelmingly likely that at least one of those claims is false. I would like to say as much: that I have done everything I could to be accurate, and that I am nevertheless confident the book contains mistakes. But the moment I try to write that sentence, I feel I am asserting something barely coherent. I believe each individual factual claim in the book — if I doubted one, I would have cut it. Yet I also believe, it seems, that the conjunction of all of them is false. How can I rationally hold both attitudes at once? The alternative, however, is to publish a preface that implies a degree of certainty I do not possess, which feels like a failure of honesty — the kind of authorial posturing I have always disliked in other people's books.

I mentioned this to a philosopher friend over dinner, expecting a quick practical suggestion about phrasing. Instead, she grew animated and told me I had stumbled into something that epistemologists have argued about for decades — that my predicament was not a mere rhetorical puzzle but a genuine problem about the structure of rational belief. She sketched a few of the moving parts but had to leave before we could get anywhere. I am left with the sense that there is a real philosophical architecture here that I do not yet see. I would like to understand what, exactly, my preface-writing difficulty reveals about the nature of belief and rational commitment, and — since I do, after all, have to submit this manuscript — what I should actually say to my readers.
