I read Never Let Me Go a while back and I keep coming back to it. The thing that gets me is how Kathy H. is literally called a "carer" — she spends years looking after donors as they recover from donations, and then eventually she becomes a donor herself. I stumbled onto this idea called care ethics a few months ago (not in school, just reading around online) — the idea that morality should be built out of relationships of care and dependency, not abstract rules or rights. And I can't stop thinking about the novel through that lens.

So here's what I'm wondering. Can you read the novel through care ethics for me? On one hand, the whole system at Hailsham seems built around care — the guardians genuinely care about the students, they give them an education and art and a kind of childhood. Kathy's care for Tommy and Ruth is real, not fake. On the other hand, all that care is in service of a system that raises these kids to harvest their organs. It's like care is both the best thing in the novel and also part of the machinery.

What I want to know is: does the novel vindicate care ethics — like, does it show that caring relationships are where moral value actually lives, even in a nightmare world? Or does it show that care ethics has a blind spot, because care can be co-opted and weaponized? And there's this specific thing that bothers me: Kathy is a good carer, and her caring seems morally real and admirable, but it also smooths the functioning of a system that kills people. Does that create a problem for care ethics, or does care ethics have a way of saying that the caring is still valuable even when the context is evil?

I'm not a philosophy person, just someone who reads and thinks about stuff, so if I'm misunderstanding care ethics tell me that too.
