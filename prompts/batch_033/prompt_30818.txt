I have been trying to understand how working scientists actually evaluate theories, and I keep running into a tension I cannot resolve. On the one hand, the standard probabilistic account of confirmation — the Bayesian picture, roughly — says that a piece of evidence supports a hypothesis only if the hypothesis makes that evidence more likely than it would otherwise have been. But evidence that is already known, and known with certainty, cannot have its probability raised by anything: it is already as likely as it can be. On that picture, a theory gets no confirmatory credit at all from observations that were collected and accepted before the theory was even formulated. On the other hand, scientists in many fields routinely give a theory enormous credit for exactly this. A new theory is praised for explaining a long-standing anomaly in measurements that everyone had in hand for years; reviewers, textbooks, and funding panels treat the fact that the theory makes sense of previously puzzling data as a major point in its favor, sometimes more impressive than a novel prediction. The same fields, meanwhile, often treat post hoc explanation as suspicious and insist that hypotheses be tested against data they were not constructed to fit. So the practice seems to say both that already-known evidence confirms a theory and that accommodating old data is second-rate.

What theory of confirmation, or of evidence, does this scientific practice presuppose? Is the practice incoherent, or does it presuppose a non-standard account of what it is for evidence to support a theory? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in how scientists and reviewers describe evidence if they adopted a strictly Bayesian view instead. I am especially interested in whether the practice is underdetermined: whether several different theories of confirmation could license it, and what would settle which one is right.
