The exam question was: "Did Oskar Lange successfully answer the challenge posed by Mises and Hayek regarding the possibility of rational economic calculation under socialism? Explain the main positions on both sides and evaluate."

The student's answer:

"The socialist calculation debate started with Mises's 1920 argument that rational economic calculation is impossible under socialism because without private property in the means of production, there are no genuine market prices for capital goods. Prices emerge from exchange, and exchange requires private ownership. Without prices, planners cannot compare the costs and benefits of different uses of resources, so they cannot make rational production decisions.

Hayek extended this argument by shifting the emphasis from prices as such to the nature of economic knowledge. In his work on the knowledge problem, Hayek argued that the knowledge needed for economic planning is not given to anyone in a concentrated form — it is dispersed among millions of individuals who possess local, often tacit knowledge of particular circumstances. The price system communicates this dispersed knowledge in a way that no central planner could replicate, because much of the relevant knowledge is not articulable in statistical form.

Oskar Lange responded in the late 1930s by proposing a model of market socialism. In Lange's model, the Central Planning Board would set initial prices for all goods and factors of production. Managers of state-owned enterprises would be instructed to follow two rules: produce at the level where marginal cost equals price, and minimize average cost for that level of output. The CPB would then adjust prices through a trial-and-error process — if there was a surplus of a good, the price would be lowered; if there was a shortage, it would be raised. Lange argued that this tâtonnement process would converge to the same equilibrium prices that a competitive market would produce, and because the CPB could incorporate exernalities and wider social costs into its pricing, the outcome might even be superior to laissez-faire capitalism.

Hayek responded that Lange's model assumed away the very knowledge problem he had identified. The trial-and-error process requires the CPB to know where surpluses and shortages exist, which itself requires the kind of local knowledge that the price system is supposed to communicate. In a real market, prices don't just adjust to clear given supply and demand — they emerge from entrepreneurial discovery and competition, processes that Lange's model had no mechanism for.

In my assessment, Lange did not fully succeed. While he showed that the formal problem of finding equilibrium prices could be solved in principle through a tâtonnement process, Hayek's deeper point about the nature of economic knowledge remained unanswered. Lange treated the economic problem as fundamentally one of computation — finding the right prices given known preferences and production functions — but Hayek showed it was actually one of discovery: the relevant knowledge doesn't preexist the market process that elicits it. That said, the debate was historically somewhat settled in Lange's favor among the economics profession at the time, with Paul Samuelson among others declaring that Lange had won. The later experience of actually existing socialist economies, however, seems to vindicate Hayek's concerns."
