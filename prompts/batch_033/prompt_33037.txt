Here's the argument as I understand it:

(1) In Bayesian terms, evidence E confirms hypothesis H when learning E makes H more probable than it was before — so P(H|E) > P(H).

(2) Bayes' theorem says P(H|E) = P(E|H) × P(H) / P(E).

(3) So for P(H|E) to be bigger than P(H), we need P(E|H) / P(E) > 1, which means P(E|H) > P(E). That's basically the condition for confirmation.

(4) Now, if E is something we already knew before we ever came up with H — what philosophers call "old evidence" — then at the time we're evaluating the theory, P(E) is already 1. We're certain of it.

(5) If P(E) = 1, then P(E|H) can't be any bigger than 1 either (probabilities max out at 1), so P(E|H) > P(E) is impossible. So old evidence can never confirm a theory.

(6) But that's obviously false. The classic example is the perihelion precession of Mercury: astronomers knew about that weird orbit for decades before Einstein, and when general relativity came along and predicted it exactly, that was huge confirmation for the theory — everyone treats it as one of the main reasons to believe GR. Same with Darwin using known fossil distributions; he didn't discover new fossils, he used ones people already knew about to support natural selection.

(7) So either Bayesian confirmation theory is broken, or one of the steps above has a hidden mistake.

My gut says step (4) or (5) is where the trouble starts — something about P(E) = 1 being the wrong way to represent old evidence. I've seen people suggest we should pretend the scientist "forgets" the evidence temporarily and then checks if the theory predicts it, but that seems like a total cheat. Other people say the problem is really in step (1), that we need a different definition of confirmation entirely.
