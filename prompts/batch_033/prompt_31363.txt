The debate over whether the numbers count in rescue cases is usually conducted one case at a time, and I want the full specification of what a defensible position has to get right before I read any more of it. Write the desiderata for a theory of how one should choose when one can save only some of several people, each of whom has a claim to be saved. The list should cover: the cases the theory must handle (one stranger versus five strangers; one's spouse versus five strangers; a single person with a stronger or more urgent claim; exact ties; probabilistic rescues in which saving the many is uncertain; and conflicts where the stakes are unequal rather than merely the numbers); the distinctions it must respect (aggregating within a single life versus across lives; numbers functioning as a tiebreaker versus as an independent reason; saving the greater number versus maximizing aggregate well-being; and the difference between the numbers counting as such and an individual's claim being outweighed on its own merits); and the constraints it must not violate, including the separateness of persons and whatever standard of fairness makes a lottery appropriate in ties. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are only the commitments of particular camps. Then flag any subsets of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for example, a pairwise-comparison requirement together with a transitivity requirement, or the requirement that each person's claim be fully respected together with a requirement that one always save the five. Finally, briefly score the main contenders (a Taurek-style anti-aggregationist view, a straightforward aggregationist view, and a Scanlon-style contractualist view) against the list, and identify which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular positions. Keep the weight on the desiderata themselves rather than on the scorecard.
