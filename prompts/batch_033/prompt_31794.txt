I spent forty years in experimental physics before retiring, and I've recently turned to philosophy with the kind of systematic appetite I used to bring to a new lab setup. I now run a reading group of about a dozen retired scientists—chemists, biologists, one geologist—who meet every two weeks. We've been working through the free will literature, and our next session is on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. They've read the original paper and a bit of commentary, but I'm not convinced the core argument has landed.

I'm not asking you to explain Frankfurt cases to me. I need a plan for the ninety-minute session: what I should put on the board first, what has to be in place before the counterexample can even register, and the sequence in which the pieces should be introduced so that the argument's force is felt rather than just reported. I want one concrete case to carry the whole discussion—I'm not sure the Jones-and-Black original is the best vehicle for this group, and I'd like your judgment on what a good carrying example needs and what it must avoid.

Most importantly, I need a map of the misconceptions my group is likely to form or bring with them. I suspect some will conflate the counterfactual intervener with a coercive threat, and others will think the whole argument depends on assuming determinism. But I want a systematic list: for each misconception, at what point in the session it typically arises, whether it's better to preempt it or let it surface and then dismantle it, and how to tell whether someone has genuinely overcome it rather than just learned to say the right thing.

Finally, I want one question I can pose in the last ten minutes that would tell me whether they've grasped the structure of the argument rather than memorized the case—something that forces them to apply the distinction between responsibility and alternative possibilities to a new scenario, not just to rehearse Jones and Black. And I'd like to know what you think I should openly decline to settle in this session, because the group has a tendency to chase downstream implications before the architecture of the argument is secure.
