I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about constructivist scruples, going by my gut on cases. The classic proof that there exist irrationals a, b with a^b rational — the one where you consider sqrt(2)^sqrt(2) and it works either way — feels like a trick to me, not a real demonstration that such numbers exist. But I'm totally fine saying Goldbach's conjecture is either true or false even though nobody has proved either. A proof that a Diophantine equation has a solution because assuming it doesn't leads to a contradiction: I feel cheated, like we haven't actually found the solution. But Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes by contradiction: no problem at all. The intermediate value theorem proved by bisection: fine. Brouwer's fixed point theorem: suddenly I feel the scruple again and I can't articulate why. The statement "this Turing machine either halts or it doesn't": fine, even though it's not decidable in general. Is there a principle that captures which non-constructive moves bother me and which don't, and what does it say about the axiom of choice and about whether the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value?
