I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on American pragmatism. We've spent the last few weeks on Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" and "The Fixation of Belief," and we've just moved into James's Pragmatism lectures and "The Will to Believe." For my midterm paper, I'm writing about the relationship between Peirce's and James's accounts of truth. The standard narrative in our secondary readings presents a sharp contrast: Peirce as the tough-minded logician who defines truth as the opinion fated to be agreed upon by all who investigate, James as the tender-minded pluralist who defines truth in terms of what it is better for us to believe — truth as a species of the good, as what pays in the way of belief.

I'm skeptical that the contrast is as sharp as it's usually drawn, and I've drafted a short essay arguing that the difference between them is better understood as a difference in emphasis and audience rather than a deep doctrinal split. My draft runs as follows — I'd be grateful for comments on whether my reading of each figure is fair, whether my central argument holds up, and what the strongest objections to it would be.

[Essay draft begins]

The familiar contrast between Peirce and James on truth goes something like this. Peirce defines truth as the final opinion toward which inquiry converges: what would be believed at the ideal limit of investigation, by a community of inquirers applying the scientific method indefinitely. Truth, for Peirce, is independent of what any particular individual happens to find satisfying or useful at a given moment. James, by contrast, seems to open the door to wishful thinking: truth is what works, what is expedient in our thinking, what proves good in the way of belief. On this picture, Peirce is the arch-objectivist and James the arch-subjectivist, and the two accounts are fundamentally at odds.

I want to suggest that this contrast is misleading in at least three ways.

First, Peirce's account is not as austerely objectivist as it appears. The "final opinion" is defined counterfactually: it is what would be believed if inquiry were carried sufficiently far. But Peirce is clear that we never actually reach this limit. All our actual beliefs are provisional, and the only justification we have for holding them is that they have survived the tests we have so far applied. This means that, in practice, Peirce's method and James's method look more similar than the caricatures suggest. Both thinkers hold that we must start from where we are, with the beliefs and doubts we actually have, and that the process of inquiry is driven by the irritation of doubt and the search for beliefs that resolve it. Peirce's "scientific method" is not a set of formal rules but a commitment to a certain attitude: a willingness to have one's beliefs determined by something external and independent of one's wishes. James shares this commitment; he simply emphasizes that we cannot always wait for the evidence to come in before we must act and believe.

Second, James's talk of truth as "the good in the way of belief" is easily misread if one ignores the constraints he places on it. James does not say that any belief that makes us feel good counts as true. He says that truth must agree with reality, and that agreement means being guided by reality in a way that proves satisfactory in the long run and on the whole. A belief that feels good now but leads to practical failure later will be discarded. James's point is not that truth reduces to subjective satisfaction but that the only criterion we have for whether a belief agrees with reality is whether it continues to guide us successfully through experience. This is not so far from Peirce's idea that the method of science is the only one that is not ultimately defeated by experience.

Third, the real difference between Peirce and James may be less about the nature of truth itself and more about the scope of inquiry. Peirce is primarily concerned with scientific inquiry, where the questions are public, the methods are shared, and convergence is plausible. James is concerned with questions that arise in individual life — moral, religious, existential questions — where convergence is neither plausible nor obviously desirable, and where the cost of suspending judgment is itself a form of practical commitment. James's "will to believe" doctrine applies specifically to cases where the option is living, forced, and momentous, and where the evidence does not decide. Peirce never explicitly addressed this class of cases with the same care. So the difference between them may reflect a difference in the subject matter they take as paradigmatic, rather than a disagreement about what truth consists in.

If this is right, then the standard narrative overstates the divide. Both Peirce and James are fallibilists who reject the idea that truth is whatever we want it to be. Both hold that beliefs are tested by experience and that the process of testing is social and ongoing. The difference is that James is more attentive to the existential situations in which we cannot wait for the scientific community to converge, and Peirce is more attentive to the institutional and methodological conditions that make convergence possible at all.

[Essay draft ends]

I have a few specific concerns I'd like feedback on. First, am I being unfair to the standard reading? Maybe the contrast really is sharper than I'm making it, and I'm papering over genuine philosophical disagreements for the sake of a tidy reconciliation. Second, I worry that my treatment of Peirce's "final opinion" is too deflationary — some Peirce scholars insist that the notion of a limit of inquiry is doing real metaphysical work, not just methodological work, and I'm not sure I've engaged with that. Third, I'm unsure whether my third point — that the difference is really about the scope of inquiry — actually resolves the tension or just relocates it. If James thinks truth can be determined by individual practical considerations in forced-choice situations, and Peirce thinks truth is always and everywhere a matter of what the community would converge on, isn't that a substantive disagreement about the nature of truth, not just a difference in subject matter? I'd appreciate thoughts on any or all of these.
