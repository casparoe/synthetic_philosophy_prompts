Two people in my department got into it at a colloquium reception last week about Frankfurt cases and I've been turning it over ever since. I'm a PhD student in the department but not working on free will, so I was just listening.

Sam started by saying Frankfurt cases basically settle the debate: the principle of alternative possibilities is false, because you can have a case where someone can't do otherwise but is still morally responsible. The neuroscientist with the electrode, the locked door Jones doesn't know about — whatever version you like.

Mira pushed back: "But there's always a flicker. The agent could have shown some sign of what they were about to do — blushed, hesitated, their pupils dilated — and the intervener would have stepped in. That alternative possibility is still there, and that's what responsibility attaches to."

Sam said that's exactly what Frankfurt anticipated — you just design the case so the intervener's trigger is the agent's actual decision process itself. The intervener doesn't wait for a sign; the intervention is triggered by the very neural event that constitutes the decision, so there's nothing the agent could have done differently that wouldn't have triggered the intervention.

Mira said: "Okay, but then you've got a dilemma. Either the trigger is distinct from the decision — in which case there's a flicker, because the agent could have had the trigger without the decision or vice versa. Or the trigger just is the decision — in which case the intervener is intervening on the basis of the decision itself, which means the agent never actually completes a decision on their own, and then they're not responsible because they didn't really choose anything."

Sam said that's just the old "determinism undermines responsibility" intuition dressed up in new clothes, and it begs the question against compatibilists.

Mira said no, the point is that Frankfurt cases are supposed to be neutral between compatibilism and incompatibilism — they're meant to show that even if determinism is true, PAP is false on grounds anyone should accept. But her dilemma shows the cases only work if you already have compatibilist intuitions.

At that point someone changed the subject and I never found out who was right. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it? I want to know who had the better of each move, and whether the dilemma defense actually succeeds or if Sam has a way out of it.
