I'm teaching a unit on animal ethics and I want to use Kafka's *Metamorphosis* as a literary companion piece when we get to the ethics of pest control and insecticides. The obvious connection is that Gregor literally turns into a giant bug and his family starts treating him like, well, a pest — they stop seeing him as family, they want him gone, and by the end his death is a relief. But I'm worried the metaphor is too easy and I'll miss what's actually philosophically interesting.

Here's what I'm wondering: the insect ethics literature spends a lot of time on whether bugs can suffer, how to weigh trillions of tiny lives, whether crop cultivation kills more sentient beings than meat eating, etc. *The Metamorphosis* seems to short-circuit all of that by making the bug a human being in a bug's body — we know Gregor can suffer, we know he has a rich inner life, we know he's undeniably a person. So the story isn't asking whether bugs matter; it's asking what happens when something that obviously matters gets classified as a pest anyway.

Does that make the story irrelevent to the actual insect ethics debate, or does it reveal something the philosophical literature misses? Like, maybe the real moral problem with pest control isn't the empirical question of insect sentience but the act of drawing a line between beings that count and beings you can exterminate without a second thought, and how easily that line moves once something is inconvenient. The family aren't monsters — they're ordinary decent people who gradually slide into treating Gregor as disposable. Is there a parallel to how ordinary decent people participate in mass insect killing without really thinking about it?

I want to point my students to specific scenes — the apple-throwing, Grete's speech about how "that creature" isn't her brother anymore, the charwoman disposing of the body like trash — to get at this. What would you say the story genuinely adds to the philosophical debate that the standard papers on insect suffering and pest control don't capture? And where does the metaphor break down in ways I should flag for the class rather than gloss over?
