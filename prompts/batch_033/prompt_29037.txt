An argument someone made at a reading group has been bugging me, because the conclusion feels absurd but I can't find the bad step. We were on the Gödelian analogy — the idea that Gödel's self-referential formulas are a clue to how minds become self-aware. The person laid it out like this:

(1) Gödel's construction shows a formal system can talk about itself indirectly: a sentence can say of itself, in effect, that it is not provable in the system, via coding.
(2) The analogy then says self-awareness is what you get when a system refers to itself in that indirect, looped way.
(3) But indirect self-reference is cheap. Any system strong enough to represent its own syntax — including pretty weak arithmetical systems and plenty of simple programs — can produce self-referential sentences (fixed points, quines).
(4) So if the analogy is right, those systems should be self-aware, or at least on the road to it.
(5) But they aren't. A quine that prints its own source code, or a weak formal system that constructs its own Gödel sentence, isn't even slightly conscious.
(6) So the analogy proves too much: either self-reference isn't doing the work the analogy claims, or we're stuck saying trivial programs are conscious.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I keep thinking the phrase self-reference means something different in the two halves, but I'm not sure that's more then a verbal dodge. If it is an equivocation, what exactly changes between a Gödel sentence and the kind of self-model a mind has — and does that difference salvage the analogy or kill it? Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
