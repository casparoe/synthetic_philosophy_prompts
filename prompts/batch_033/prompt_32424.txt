In cognitive science and philosophy of mind, the claim that the brain is a computer is often advanced as a substantive empirical thesis, not merely a metaphor. It is used to license conclusions about the nature of mental representation, the relationship between mind and brain, and the prospects for artificial intelligence. I want to audit this analogy systematically.

First, give me the correspondence table: what in the brain or nervous system is supposed to map onto the processor, the memory, the program, the data, the input/output channels, and the operating system (if anything)? Second, identify the points where the mapping breaks down, and distinguish the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from the ones that matter for the analogy's intended use. Third, the computer analogy quietly imports several features from the source domain — a distinction between hardware and software, the idea that computation operates over syntactic items with representational content, and the assumption that the same algorithm can run on different substrates. For each of these, say whether the brain plausibly shares the feature and what is at stake if it does not. Finally, once the disanalogies are on the table, what is left of the claim that the brain is a computer? Is it still a substantive empirical claim, or does it collapse into the trivial observation that the brain's processes can be described mathematically?
