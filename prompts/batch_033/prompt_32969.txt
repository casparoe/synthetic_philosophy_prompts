Write a dialogue between a moral particularist and a moral generalist that begins from a concrete hard case and then broadens into the theoretical debate. The case: a physician is deciding whether to deceive a seriously ill patient about the patient's prognosis, where the deception would likely sustain the patient's hope and treatment adherence but would also violate a commitment the physician made to be truthful with this patient. The particularist should argue that whether the deception counts for or against the action depends on contextual features—the patient's temperament, the family's involvement, the likely downstream effects, the history of the physician-patient relationship—in a way no stable principle about lying or beneficence captures. The generalist should reply with the strongest available defenses of codifiability: a pro tanto principle against deception that can be outweighed, a principle with explicit enabling and disabling conditions, or an account on which the apparent context-sensitivity is itself systematically describable. Both characters must be equally competent and must address each other's actual arguments rather than changing the subject. Over the course of the dialogue, each should be forced to refine or qualify their position, and the exchange should close by identifying what, if anything, still separates them—without collapsing into a superficial compromise.
