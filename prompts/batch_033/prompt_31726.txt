I'm a retired chemist — spent forty years in the lab, and for the last two I've been reading my way through ethics and decision theory. It's been a revelation, honestly, though I sometimes find myself wanting to grab philosophers by the lapels and ask them to just run the experiment. Anyway, at a dinner party last week two friends of mine got into an argument that I havent been able to stop thinking about, and I'd love to hear it adjudicated move by move.

The argument started when Mara mentioned she'd been reading about whether "better than" is transitive. She said it seems obvious that it is — if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C. You can't even reason about choices without that. Leo said he used to think so too but he'd come to think it's not so simple.

Leo asked her to imagine a spectrum. Take a life of extraordinary happiness that lasts 80 years. Now imagine a life that's just a tiny bit less happy — barely noticeably — but lasts 81 years. That seems like a wash, maybe even slightly better because of the extra year. Now repeat: each step shaves off a tiny bit of happiness and adds a year. After thousands of steps you've got a life of almost no happiness at all — just barely worth living — but it lasts for millions of years. Is that better than the original 80-year life of extraordinary happiness? Leo said he didn't think so, and that if she agreed, then something had to give: either one of the pairwise "better than" judgments was wrong, or "better than" isn't always transitive.

Mara came back immediately: this is just the sorites paradox dressed up. The fallacy is assuming that because you can't identify the exact step where the switch happens, there's no switch. "Better than" is vague, sure — there's no sharp boundary — but that doesn't make it intransitive. It just means that at some point along the spectrum, the next life isn't actually better, even if we can't say exactly where. Vagueness and intransitivity are different things.

Leo said he thought that was too quick. In the sorites paradox, you're dealing with one dimension — number of grains of sand — and the predicate "heap" is vague along that dimension. But here you've got two dimensions that trade off against each other, happiness and length of life. The problem isn't just vagueness; it's that the tradeoff rate that seems right for comparing adjacent pairs can't be right for comparing distant pairs, because the value of a unit of happiness depends on how much happiness there already is. Something like: when you're at very high happiness, trading a sliver of it for an extra year seems fine; when you're at very low happiness, that same trade doesn't seem fine at all. So the pairwise judgments are all reasonable on their own terms, but they don't compose.

Mara said that's exactly admitting the pairwise judgments were wrong to begin with. If the tradeoff rate changes depending on where you are on the spectrum, then the right thing to say is that the tradeoff at the high end and the tradeoff at the low end can't both be correct — at least one of the adjacent comparisons was a mistake, even if it felt reasonable at the time. The fact that we're tempted to make inconsistent pairwise judgments doesn't show "better than" is intransitive; it shows we're bad at making consistent pairwise judgments when the differences are small.

Leo said that's a possible move, but it's a costly one. It means we have to say that in a huge range of ordinary cases where we compare two things that are very close together, our judgments of "better than" are systematically unreliable. That's a lot of our everyday reasoning about tradeoffs. If we can't trust pairwise comparisons between near neighbors, what can we trust?

And that's where they left it. I found myself going back and forth. I'd appreciate it if you could go through each of their moves — I count maybe four or five distinct argumentative steps — and say who's right about what, where each of them goes wrong (if they do), and what the state of the philosophical question actually is. And please, no bullet points or headings or bold text or anything — just plain prose, the way you'd write a letter.
