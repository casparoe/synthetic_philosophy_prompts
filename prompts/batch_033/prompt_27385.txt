I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about laws of nature — you know, the whole "what makes it a law that F=ma rather than just a really well-confirmed generalization" thing. I'm a software engineer so my instinct is to think of laws as kind of like the universe's source code, but apparently that's the anti-Humean picture (governing laws, necessary connections, all that). The Humean view says laws are just the best summary of what actually happens — like lossy compression, where you trade off file size against fidelity to the data. I find that picture weirdly appealing, partly because it doesn't posit spooky metaphysical glue holding the universe together.

But I tried to formalize it for myself and ended up with an argument that seems to show the Humean view is either secretly anti-Humean or makes laws arbitrary. I can't figure out where I went wrong, and my friend from a philosophy reading group just keeps saying "read Lewis" which is not helpful when you're stuck in a loop. Here's the argument:

(1) On the Humean view, the laws are the axioms of the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength (informativeness) in describing the mosaic of particular facts — the Best System account.

(2) What counts as "best" depends on how you weight simplicity against strength. Different weightings can yield different best systems, hence different candidate laws.

(3) Either the correct weighting is fixed by something in the world, or it isn't.

(4) If it IS fixed by something in the world, then that something is a non-Humean fact — it's a fact about how the world ought to be systematized, over and above the particular facts themselves. That sounds like governing-laws metaphysics sneaking back in through the back door.

(5) If it ISN'T fixed by the world, then the weighting is just our pragmatic choice, and different choosers (aliens with different cognitive preferences, future humans, etc.) could have equally legitimate but different laws. But surely the laws of nature don't depend on what anyone happens to find simple or useful — that makes them subjective in a way that seems to defeat the whole point of calling them laws.

(6) So Humeanism either collapses into anti-Humeanism (if we take branch 4) or makes laws unacceptably subjective (if we take branch 5).

(7) Therefore Humeanism can't be right.

I feel like step (4) is doing something sneaky but I can't articulate what. Or maybe the real issue is in (2) — maybe the Humean should deny that different weightings actually give genuinely different candidate systems in practice? I'd like to know which step a defender of Humeanism should reject, and what they have to pay to reject it. Like, does it force them into a position thats even less intuitive than the governing-laws view they were trying to avoid?
