So I got into this argument after a decision theory seminar and it's been rattling around my head ever since. A friend of mine — he's one of those people who treats philosophy like a contact sport — claimed Pascal's wager isn't just wrong, it's self-defeating in a way the standard many-gods objection doesn't even capture. He walked me through it and I couldn't find the hole on the spot, but the conclusion feels like it has to be cheating somewhere. Here's the argument as he laid it out:

(1) Pascal's core move is this: if an action has some nonzero probability p of yielding infinite utility, its expected utility is infinite, and that swamps all finite considerations. Call this the Trumping Principle.
(2) Now take any arbitrary action A you might perform — scratch your nose, join a monastery, vote for a third party, whatever. There exists a conceivable deity who rewards exactly A with infinite bliss and punishes all alternative actions with infinite torment.
(3) From (2), for any action A, there is a nonzero probability that the A-rewarding deity exists. (After all, if a deity is conceivable, you can't rationally assign it probability zero — that would be dogmatism.)
(4) From (1) and (3), for any action A, the expected utility of A is infinite (and the expected utility of not-A is negative infinite, given the punishment clause).
(5) So you are rationally required to perform every action, which is impossible — you can't both scratch your nose and refrain from scratching it.
(6) Therefore the Trumping Principle doesn't just give the wrong answer; it gives no coherent answer at all. Pascal's wager collapses into practical contradiction.

I tried pushing back. I said: look, you can assign different probabilities to different deities — the Christian God gets more credence than the nose-scratching god. He shot back that with infinite utilities, any nonzero probability generates infinite expected utility, so differences in probability don't break the symmetry. Then I said maybe the silly deities get probability zero, full stop. And he asked: what principled reason do you have for assigning exactly zero to the nose-scratching god while assigning nonzero to the Christian God, that doesn't just beg the question in favor of the religions you already take seriously?

I've been turning this over and I can't tell whether the problem is in (1), (2), (3), or somewhere in how the steps connect. I'd rather keep some version of Pascal's insight if I can — the idea that practical reason shouldn't ignore low-probability stakes of overwhelming magnitute seems worth preserving. But if my friend's right, the whole thing is worse than false; it's nonsense. Where does this argument actually go wrong, and what do I have to give up to resist it?
