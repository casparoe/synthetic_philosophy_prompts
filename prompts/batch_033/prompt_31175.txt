I have been working through the Clifford–James debate over the ethics of belief and find that my own position keeps shifting, so I want to force it into the open with a number. Please give me your credence in the following claim: James is substantially right that there is a defensible class of cases in which a person may permissibly believe a proposition on passional or practical grounds rather than on evidence sufficient to settle it—cases that are in the relevant sense forced, live, and momentous, and that the available evidence cannot by itself resolve. By defensible I mean that, for at least some such cases, a reflective person is neither morally nor epistemically at fault for so believing. Set aside for the moment the obvious contrast case on the Cliffordian side, such as a ship-owner who suppresses his doubts about his vessel's seaworthiness because it is profitable to do so; the question is whether James's exceptions survive the worry that they are just that kind of case in better dress.

I want the decomposition, not a bare number. Break the question into the sub-questions your credence actually tracks—for example: whether Clifford's evidentialist principle, understood as a blanket prohibition on believing without sufficient evidence, has any principled exceptions at all; whether James's conditions for a genuine option pick out a coherent and non-vacuous class of cases rather than one that can be stretched to cover any comforting belief; whether, conditional on there being such cases, letting passional nature settle them is on balance truth-conducive or error-conducive; and whether any exception can be limited in a non-arbitrary way so that it does not license wishful thinking generally. Assign a rough probability to each term with a sentence of justification, show how you combine them into the overall credence, and then identify which term your final number is most sensitive to and which you are least confident in. Finally, say what evidence or argument would move that least-confident term the most, and by roughly how much—and, if it is illuminating, where you think someone at 0.2 and someone at 0.8 on this claim would most likely be disagreeing.
