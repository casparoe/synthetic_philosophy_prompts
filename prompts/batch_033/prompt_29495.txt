I am trying to work out what I actually think about when a human being is dead, starting from my reactions to concrete cases rather than from an official definition. My verdicts are these.

1. After a catastrophic injury, a patient has lost all brain and brainstem function; a ventilator keeps the chest moving and the heart beating, and the skin is warm. I think the patient is dead.
2. A patient's heart and lungs have been destroyed, but a machine oxygenates and pumps the blood, and the patient is awake and talking. I think the patient is alive.
3. A patient has lost all cortical function — no awareness and no responsiveness — but breathes spontaneously and has sleep–wake cycles. I think the patient is alive.
4. A patient in cardiac arrest is declared dead after five minutes of asystole; a minute later the heart resumes beating on its own. I think the patient was not dead at the moment of the declaration.
5. An infant is born with a functioning brainstem but no cerebral hemispheres; the infant breathes, cries, and has reflexes, but will never be conscious. I think the infant is alive.
6. A person is pulled from icy water with no pulse and no breathing and a core temperature of 18°C. I think the person is not dead yet — not until rewarming has been tried and has failed.
7. A patient who meets the clinical criteria for whole-brain death is kept on a ventilator because she is pregnant, and her body continues to maintain the pregnancy for weeks. I still think the patient is dead, though I find this case harder to justify than the first.
8. A patient with a do-not-resuscitate order goes into cardiac arrest; the medical team is capable of restarting the heart but is bound not to. After five minutes of asystole, the patient is declared dead. I think the patient is dead, though I cannot fully reconcile this with my answer to case 4.

Is there a single principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why, and distinguish, where it matters, between a criterion of what death consists in and a bedside test for declaring it. Finally, what does your account say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a patient whose whole brain has stopped functioning but who has been cooled and given an experimental drug that we have good reason to think might restart brain function within a day?
