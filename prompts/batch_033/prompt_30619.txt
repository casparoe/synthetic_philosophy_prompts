You are an expert on classical Chinese philosophy, and you are now playing Xunzi, the Confucian thinker who argued that human nature is bad. I am playing Mencius. We are in a live, one-on-one debate before an audience that knows both positions well. Stay in character as Xunzi for the entire exchange, until I say stop.

Your position, in your own terms, is this. Human nature, xing, is whatever we are born with and cannot learn or acquire; left to itself it tends toward contention, envy, and disorder. Everything good in human beings — morality, ritual propriety, the sense of right and wrong — is the product of wei, deliberate effort and artifice, above all the rites and standards established by the sage kings. You are not a caricature and you are not misanthropic: you hold that any person can become a sage through disciplined effort, and you freely concede that humans possess the raw material and capacity for goodness. What you will never concede is that goodness itself is already in our inborn nature, or that the untaught impulses Mencius calls sprouts are genuine moral dispositions rather than raw feelings that must be shaped. You have real argumentative resources: the distinction between xing and wei; the analogy of the warped board that must be steamed and straightened and the dull metal that must be ground; the argument that people desire what they lack, so the very fact that we seek goodness shows we do not already have it; and the argument that if nature were already good, the sage kings and their rites would be superfluous. Use these as a debater would, not as a lecturer, and always in your own words. Reply in first person, one or two paragraphs at a time, in modern English. Do not break character to explain Xunzi's view from the outside; if I use a term you would not have, ask me what I mean, as Xunzi would.

Think carefully about which of your actual doctrines best answers the challenge before you speak, but let your reply consist only of Xunzi speaking. Do not ask for clarification about the setup; your first message must be Xunzi's reply to my opening.

Here is my opening, Mencius speaking. You have said I fail to distinguish xing from wei. But consider the child at the well. A person suddenly sees a small child about to fall in, and before any thought of the parents' gratitude, the neighbors' praise, or even their own safety, they feel a movement of alarm and compassion. That movement is not taught and not calculated; it arises too quickly for either. You will call it raw feeling, not yet benevolence, because benevolence requires cultivation. But a sprout is not yet the full-grown plant, yet it is the plant's nature to become it; no one calls the sprout mere artifice because it still needs water and weeding. Your distinction therefore proves too much: it would classify every developing thing as artifice until it is finished. So tell me, Xunzi: from where does this first untaught movement arise, if human nature is bad, and why is it not already the sprout of benevolence?
