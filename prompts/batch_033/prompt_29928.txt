Government agencies do cost-benefit analysis for policies with effects far in the future -- climate mitigation, flood defenses, nuclear waste storage -- and they routinely discount future costs and benefits at a steady rate per year, so that a death averted a century from now counts for far less than a death averted today. What theory of the value of future lives does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it commit the agency to pure time preference, the view that a benefit matters less simply because it comes later, or can the discounting be fully explained by economic growth (future people will be richer) and uncertainty (future benefits might not materialize)? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it's underdetermined such that several different views would license it. Then tell me what specifically in the practice woud have to change if an agency adopted a zero pure rate of time preference, and what could stay the same.
