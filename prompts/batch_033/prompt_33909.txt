I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about a two-player case, and neither of us managed to land a clean blow. Two strangers play a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma for real stakes. Each has been reliably told that the other is, for every decision-relevant purpose, a copy of them: same preferences, same information, same reasoning dispositions, same way of settling what to do in exactly this kind of game. My friend insisted that defection remains the only rational move, because nothing about your choice can reach across the table and change what the other player does. I wanted to answer that if the other player is a copy in the relevant sense, then your two choices cannot come apart, so cooperating is effectively choosing the outcome in which both cooperate. But every time I tried to state that inference, it came out sounding as though my decision somehow made the other player's decision, which is precisely the magical thinking my friend accused me of. We went in circles until two in the morning, and I still cannot say which of us was confused about what.

I teach an upper-level seminar on rationality and strategic choice — around sixteen students, one hundred minutes per session — and I would like to turn that argument into a classroom activity. The students should feel the pull of the copy-based reasoning themselves, before any label is attached, and then feel why the inference is so hard to justify and where it breaks. Please design a runnable game or structured exercise in which participants are forced, as far as a classroom allows, into the position of choosing against a partner who is as close to a copy as possible, so that at least some of them find themselves reasoning 'if I choose X, then they choose X.' Give me concrete rules, materials, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, a timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinctions among causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and superrationality without naming those views in advance. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be explicit about which feature of genuine superrationality a classroom cannot reproduce and what I should say in the debrief to make up for that.
