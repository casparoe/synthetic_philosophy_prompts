I'm pulling together a paper on the School of Names for a comparative philosophy seminar, and I want to check whether my interpretive commitments actually hang together before I build an argument on them. Here's roughly where I've landed after a few weeks of reading:

(1) Gongsun Long's white horse argument is not sophistry or wordplay — it's a serious philosophical argument that's trying to get at something about how kind terms and modifier terms combine.

(2) The standard Western response to the paradox — distinguishing the "is" of identity from the "is" of predication — basically dissolves it; once you see that "is a horse" predicates rather than identifies, the air goes out of the puzzle.

(3) That said, the later Mohist response to Gongsun Long (the one from the Canons about how terms "extend" versus "exclude" in combination) is philosophically more sophisticated than the Western copula-based response.

(4) I'm broadly convinced that natural language kind terms like "horse" function as sortals in something like David Wiggins's sense — they supply criteria of identity and persistence for the things they apply to.

(5) Classical Chinese of the Warring States period didn't have a copula, so any analysis that turns essentially on an ambiguity in the word "is" is importing a distinction the original language didn't grammatically mark.

(6) More generally, I think philosophy of language goes wrong when it treats Indo-European grammatical categories — copula, subject-predicate structure, count/mass distinction — as universal features of thought rather than contingent features of a language family.

(7) And here's the big one for my paper: I think Gongsun Long's argument, if it were sound, would show that sortals can't do the metaphysical work philosophers claim they do — because if "white horse" and "horse" pick out different sortals, then the whole idea that sortals give you stable criteria of identity across modifier combination starts to crumble.
