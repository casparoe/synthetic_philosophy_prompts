I am drafting a novel in which one of the central characters is an early-twentieth-century Cambridge intellectual whose moral outlook has been shaped by G. E. Moore’s Principia Ethica. As part of my research I have been working through Chapter VI, ‘The Ideal’, and I find myself genuinely stuck on a stretch of argument in the opening paragraphs.

Moore contends that the greatest goods we can know or conceive are certain states of consciousness that involve, on the one hand, personal affection and, on the other, the appreciation of what is beautiful in art or nature. He maintains that these goods are not merely two separate items on a list but are, in their highest forms, deeply intertwined: the richest kind of personal affection includes an element of aesthetic contemplation of the beloved’s mental qualities, and the fullest kind of aesthetic enjoyment partakes of something like love. He also insists that neither good can exist as an intrinsic good apart from consciousness—an unperceived beautiful landscape, for instance, possesses no intrinsic value in his scheme.

I can follow the general drift, but I am having difficulty understanding precisely what Moore means by the interweaving of these two goods. Is he claiming that they are, at bottom, a single kind of good? Or that each, to reach its highest form, requires the other as a necessary condition? Or is he advancing some third, subtler relation? I am also unsure how to reconcile this claim about their interconnection with his earlier, foundational insistence that good is a simple, unanalysable, non-natural property. A careful untangling of this portion of the chapter would be immensely helpful.
