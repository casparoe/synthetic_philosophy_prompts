I keep a running file of the judgments of a colleague — call her Sam — who has thought about honesty and deception more carefully than anyone I know, and whose verdicts are unusually stable but whose underlying principle I have never managed to extract. Her recorded judgments, with "misleading" understood as causing a false belief through true statements or omissions rather than through a false assertion, are these. She would not criticize a niece who tells her dying aunt that the aunt's estranged son has sent his love, when he has not, because the aunt has a week to live and the falsehood is the only comfort left; she regards it as kind. She regards it as permissible, at most trivially wrong, to tell a friend that a bad haircut looks good, and to tell a surprise-party guest that the two of you are heading to a dentist's appointment. But she judges a job candidate seriously wrong when, asked in an interview whether she left her last job voluntarily, she answers "I left voluntarily" — true, since she resigned — while omitting that she resigned under threat of dismissal for misconduct and knowing the interviewer will form a false picture of her record. She says much the same about a reference who, asked "Would you rehire this person?", replies "He was always punctual," knowing the caller will infer that he was a satisfactory employee when in fact he was fired for embezzlement. And she judges a taxpayer wrong when, on a form that asks about foreign accounts, he writes "I have no accounts under the names listed above," knowing that the form's design will lead the reviewer to conclude that he has no foreign accounts at all. I have never been able to tell whether the line she draws tracks what is said rather than what is left to be inferred, or tracks something else entirely.

Next week Sam is going to give her view on a case I need to predict in advance, and I want your help working out what she will say if she is being consistent with this record. A psychology lab is writing a consent form for a study on frustration and persistence. Participants will be asked to complete word puzzles and answer questionnaires, but the puzzles are rigged to be unsolvable in the time allowed, and the researchers know participants will come away believing the study involved no stress induction. The lab is choosing between two forms. Form L says: "This study does not involve any tasks designed to cause frustration," which is false. Form M says: "This study involves completing word puzzles and answering questionnaires," which is true sentence by sentence, but it omits that the puzzles are designed to induce frustration, and the researchers expect participants to infer from it that the tasks are ordinary and non-frustrating. The false belief a participant ends up with, and the stakes, are the same either way. What would Sam say: are the two forms equally wrong, is the outright lie in Form L worse, or is the technically true Form M worse? I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the reasoning: lay out the two or three candidate principles that fit her recorded verdicts equally well, say what each principle predicts for the consent-form case, and give your best prediction with your reasons for preferring one principle over the others — then specify, concretely, the further case that would most cleanly separate the leading candidates if I could get Sam to rule on it.

Please be explicit about how confident your prediction is and about which parts rest on a guess rather than on Sam's record. And if the exercise is ill-posed as I have described it — if, for example, her recorded verdicts are not in fact consistent with the candidate principles you can construct, or the consent-form case does not separate them in the way I assume — tell me that plainly rather than manufacturing a prediction to be agreeable.
