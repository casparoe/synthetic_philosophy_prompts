I'm a Bayesian statistician with a side interest in formal epistemology. I'm at home with priors, likelihoods, posteriors, credences, conditionalisation, Dutch books, and the principle of total evidence. I also know about Cromwell's rule — the injunction against pinning a contingent proposition's probability at exactly 0 or 1 — and the surrounding debates about whether ideal rationality requires leaving some slack for revision.

I keep hearing about Wittgenstein's On Certainty and the notion of 'hinge propositions': propositions that are not themselves justified by evidence but that make the whole practice of justification possible — paradigm cases being 'I have two hands' or 'the Earth has existed for more than five minutes.' Hinge epistemologists seem to deny that these are known, or believed in the ordinary way, or assigned credences at all; they are said to be prior to the entire game of evidence and doubt, more like its scaffolding than a move within it.

Restate hinge epistemology — as developed by Wittgenstein and later hinge epistemologists — in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Then give me a residue report: what translated cleanly, what you had to distort, and what could not be expressed at all. I especially want to know whether the residue points to a limitation in my vocabulary (am I missing something by assuming that every propositional attitude must be a credence?) or whether hinge epistemology is leaning on distinctions that do not survive being made precise.
