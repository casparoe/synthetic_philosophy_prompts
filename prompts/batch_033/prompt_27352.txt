I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on population ethics. In seminars and reading groups on antinatalism, I regularly hear the slogan "it is better never to have been" invoked as though it settles things — sometimes as a pithy summary of the procreation asymmetry argument, sometimes as a freestanding verdict about the human condition, and sometimes as a direct move from metaphysics to a practical prohibition on having children. I want to disentangle what this slogan could actually be claiming. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "it is better never to have been" might express, from the least to the most demanding? For each reading, I'd like to know: (1) whether it is independently plausible, (2) whether it follows from the procreation asymmetry argument (the one about the differential value of absent pleasures versus absent pains), and (3) whether it would actually support the practical conclusion that procreation is morally wrong. I'm trying to figure out which reading, if any, does the work people seem to want it to do.
