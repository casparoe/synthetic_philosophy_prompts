For a philosophy of technology seminar, write a taxonomy of positions on whether epistemic dependence on AI systems erodes human understanding. The taxonomy should take the form of a tree where each branch is labeled by the claim that splits the positions below it, and each leaf names a position (or marks it as unnamed if no one defends it) with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of known positions: first identify the questions on which views genuinely come apart, then map the combinations of answers they allow, then locate actual positions within that space. Where a logically possible combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, note it and offer a brief diagnosis: is no one there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive for a reason worth stating, or because the region is simply unexplored? Where two positions your tree cannot separate end up at the same leaf, flag them and explain why the tree collapses them.

Your branching questions should include at least the following dividing lines, though you may add others or reorder them as the logic of the space demands:

- Whether understanding requires something beyond reliably getting correct answers (e.g., grasping reasons, exercising a skill, or being able to vouch for one's beliefs)
- Whether AI dependence raises a problem that is qualitatively different from familiar cases of cognitive outsourcing (writing, calculators, search engines)
- Whether the threat (if any) is primarily to individual knowers or to the epistemic community as a whole
- Whether the problem can be solved by better AI design (explainability, appropriate reliance, keeping a human in the loop) or is structural and unavoidable

The leaves of your tree should include at least the following positions, though you may add others if the branching structure demands them: a continuity view that treats AI as just another cognitive tool, a virtue-epistemological view that locates the problem in the atrophy of intellectual character, a view centered on the opacity of AI systems as black boxes, a collective-epistemic-health view that worries about brittle knowledge distribution, and an epistemic-agency view that treats the threat as a loss of autonomous knower status. Make sure each leaf position is genuinely separated from its neighbors by at least one branching question.

Phrase every branching question so that someone could answer it without already knowing which position it leads to—no question should tip its hand. The deliverable is a diagnostic instrument for a debate, not an argument for one side.
