I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Mind, Meaning, and Mechanism." The back half of the course works through Daniel Dennett's claim that Darwin and Turing each pulled off a "strange inversion of reasoning": Darwin showed that biological design — the appearance of purposeful, well-adapted complexity — can arise from a mindless, gradual process with no foresight; Turing showed that computation — the manipulation of symbols in ways that look intelligent — can be carried out by a device that follows mechanical rules without understanding what it is doing. In both cases, competence comes apart from comprehension. We've read the relevant chapters from *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* and *From Bacteria to Bach and Back*, plus two critical pieces: one from the enactivist tradition arguing that the two inversions are not genuinely parallel because living systems exhibit a kind of immanent teleology that computational systems lack, and one from a philosopher of computer science who argues that Turing's inversion still depends on comprehension — it just relocates it to the programmer who designs the machine table and interprets the inputs and outputs.

I'm writing the final exam, and I want one essay question that cannot be answered by simply rehearsing Dennett's position. It should force students to evaluate whether Darwin's inversion and Turing's inversion are genuinely the same kind of move, or whether there are structural asymmetries that make the parallel weaker than Dennett needs it to be. A strong answer will engage at least one specific objection to the parallel and assess whether Dennett can handle it without giving up the claim that both count as strange inversions of reasoning.

Please provide:

1. The essay question itself (no more than 150 words).
2. A model answer of about 700–900 words, written as an A-range student would write it.
3. A marking scheme that allocates 25 marks across five specific moves or insights the answer must contain. For each move, include a brief note distinguishing an A-level execution from a B-level one. Also include a short list (3–4 items) of common mistakes that should lose marks, with the penalty for each.

Throughout, separate what is settled — for instance, the basic structure of each inversion as Dennett presents it — from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on this material, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
