I've been reading epistemology on and off for a while, and there's this one argumentative move I keep seeing that I don't have a word for. It goes like this. Take a condition that can come and go gradually, like being cold. Suppose someone claims that whenever you're cold, you're in a position to know you're cold. Now imagine a very slow temperature drop from clearly-not-cold to clearly-cold. There's got to be a last moment when you're not cold and a first moment when you are. But at that first moment, things feel just about exactly the way they did a second earlier, when you weren't cold. So you couldn't know you were cold at that moment: whatever your basis was, it would have been present a moment before too, and their it would have been wrong. So the original claim fails. I've seen the same move run with being in pain, being awake, and even with non-mental cases like being a heap. I know it's somewhere in the neighborhood of the KK principle, but KK is usually put as the idea that knowing entails knowing that you know, which sounds like a positive claim, whereas this move is used negatively, to show that no interesting condition can have a certain property. Is there a name for this argument, or for the property it's attacking? And does it really apply to any condition with a gradual boundary, or only to some? I'm not just after the label — I want to know how the argument is supposed to work, what its premises are, and where it stops applying.
