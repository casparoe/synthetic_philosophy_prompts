Here's where I've roughly landed:

(a) The gene's eye view (Dawkins, Williams) is correct in its essentials. Genes are the fundamental replicators — they're the only things that persist across generations with enough fidelity to accumulate adaptations. Organisms are just vehicles that genes build. The right question to ask about any adaptation is "what's in it for the gene?"

(b) I'm convinced that multi-level selection theory, the way Sober and Wilson develop it, is the right formal framework for analyzing selection. The Price equation lets you partition total selection into within-group and between-group components, and I think that's the most general and rigorous way to think about how selection operates.

(c) I think species selection is a real phenomenon — not just a byproduct or an epiphenomenon of organism-level selection. Eldredge and Gould were onto something: species have heritable traits (like geographic range size or dispersal ability) that affect their extinction and speciation rates, and this explains macroevolutionary patterns that can't be reduced to what's happening at the organism level.

(d) I buy the major transitions framework (Maynard Smith and Szathmáry). Evolution has repeatedly seen lower-level units — genes into chromosomes, prokaryotes into eukaryotes, single cells into multicellular organisms, individuals into colonies — combine into higher-level entities that then become units of selection in their own right. So the level of selection isn't fixed; it can shift over evolutionary time.

(e) When it comes to human social behavior, I think group selection explanations are almost always wrong. Kin selection and reciprocal altruism give us everything we need to explain human cooperation and altruism, and group selection thinking tends to be sloppy and to ignore within-group dynamics.

That's basically it.
