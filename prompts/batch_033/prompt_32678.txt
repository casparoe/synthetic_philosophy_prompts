In a seminar on Wittgenstein's private language argument, the move everyone keeps pressing is the beetle-in-the-box analogy from the Philosophical Investigations. As it is usually presented: each person has a box with something inside that they call a beetle, but no one can ever look into anyone else's box. The word beetle still functions perfectly well in their shared language, regardless of what is in each box — a beetle, a marble, a button, or nothing at all. The intended moral is that the private inner object, construed as what a sensation word designates, is simply irrelevant to what the word means. From this, the standard conclusion drawn is that a language whose words refer to objects only the speaker can know is impossible, and that sensation words cannot be understood as names of private experiences.

I want this analogy audited rather than taken as settled. Give me the correspondence table: what maps onto the box, the beetle, the prohibition on looking, the word beetle, and the fact that the word functions regardless of what is inside. Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion — the features of the beetle scenario that our actual sensation language lacks — and say which ones the argument is silently relying on. Finally, take a definite position on whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying more weight than it can bear.

Give me a committed response: do not just lay out options — say where you come down and defend it. Format the response as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, distributing the mapping, the disanalogies, and your verdict across the five items.
