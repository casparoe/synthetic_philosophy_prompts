Two colleagues of mine have been going back and forth about a puzzle in anthropic reasoning, and neither can break the deadlock. They've asked me to find someone who can adjudicate. Here is the exchange as I reconstruct it.

(1) A posed the following scenario. A physicist knocks on your door with a new cosmological theory — call it T. The direct empirical evidence for T is weak; on the evidence alone you would give it a credence of perhaps one in a thousand. But T has a further consequence: if true, the universe contains a trillion times more observers than the default cosmology would suggest. Now, if you reason as a Bayesian, you must conditionalize not only on the evidence but on your own existence. Your own existence is vastly more probable under T — a trillion times more probable — because T says the universe is much more populous. This likelihood ratio swamps the weak evidence, and your credence in T should shift to near-certainty. But surely this is absurd. A physicist with a wild theory and weak evidence should not be able to hold your beliefs hostage simply by building more observers into her model. Something has gone wrong in the reasoning.

(2) B replied: What exactly has gone wrong? The Bayesian move seems straightforward. If theory A says the universe contains N observers and theory B says it contains a trillion times N, and you know you are an observer, then — all else equal — you are a trillion times more likely to exist under B than under A. That follows directly from conditional probability. If you refuse to update on that, you must either be rejecting the requirement to conditionalize on your own existence, or you must be applying a prior that penalizes populous theories in a way you have not justified. Neither move is principled. The fact that the conclusion feels absurd is not an argument; plenty of correct Bayesian results feel absurd at first.

(3) A answered: The problem is that the physicist's theory does not make *your* existence more likely in any epistemically relevant sense. It merely says there are more observers elsewhere, causally isolated from you. You already knew you existed before the physicist arrived. A theory that postulates additional observers in distant regions does not explain your existence — it just adds beings whose existence is irrelevant to yours. The kind of self-locating uncertainty that matters is uncertainty about which observer you are *given* a fixed population, not uncertainty about how large the population is. When the population size itself is up for grabs, using your own existence as evidence for larger populations lets you confirm any theory that posits enough extra people, no matter how otherwise implausible. That is a reductio, not a feature.

(4) B came back: But why is population size different from any other parameter? If I wake up in a building and am told there are either ten or ten million rooms, I should think I am much more likely in the ten-million-room building. You say the difference is that in the building case the building's size is fixed and I am merely uncertain about my room, while in the cosmological case the size itself is uncertain. But from my epistemic perspective, these are identical: in both cases I have a self-locating uncertainty — which observer am I among all observers? — and I should conditionalize on the evidence that *this* observer exists. If you refuse to do that when the total population is uncertain, you need a principled reason why self-locating uncertainty works one way when the total is fixed and another way when it is not. I do not see one.

(5) A: Here is the principled reason. In the building case with a fixed total, each room hypothesis accounts equally for the evidence "I am in some room or other" — that evidence is certain under both. What shifts is the probability that *this particular room* is mine. But in the cosmological case, the evidence "I exist" is not what is doing the discriminatory work. Both theories entail that at least one observer exists, because I am here observing myself. The relevant evidence is something like "this specific observer, with this specific history and location, exists." And T does nothing to make *that* more probable. It just adds unrelated observers elsewhere who have different histories and locations. The theory gets credit for making *some* observer more likely, but not for making *me* more likely, and I am the only observer whose existence I have direct evidence of.

(6) B: But T *does* make it more probable that this specific observer exists — because it increases the total number of draws from the distribution of possible observers. If the process that generates observers is such that each possible observer has some small independent chance of existing, then a theory that says there are vastly more total draws makes it vastly more likely that any given possible observer — including you — ends up existing. The only way to avoid this is to conditionalize on the total number of observers and then reason as if you are randomly sampled from among them. But that is exactly the self-sampling assumption — and you have not yet justified it over the alternative, which is to treat your existence as evidence that favors theories on which it was more probable. The charge of absurdity sticks only if you have already helped yourself to the self-sampling assumption. But that is the very thing in dispute.

They broke off there, each convinced the other was begging the question. Please adjudicate: who is right about which specific step, where does each argument go wrong if it does, and is there a way to dissolve the impasse that neither of them has seen?
