I've been working through the split-brain literature and have constructed an argument that seems to force a conclusion I cannot accept. I would like to know where it goes wrong.

(1) In commissurotomy patients, when a stimulus is presented exclusively to the left visual field (right hemisphere), the patient's left hand can correctly select a matching object, but the patient verbally reports seeing nothing, or confabulates an unrelated answer.

(2) The best explanation of this behavior is that the right hemisphere is having a conscious visual experience to which the speaking hemisphere lacks introspective access.

(3) If (2) is correct, then the split-brain patient contains at least two subjects of consciousness: the speaking left-hemisphere subject and the silent right-hemisphere subject.

(4) The criterion for counting subjects of consciousness implicit in (2) is roughly this: if a system within a human body produces flexible, goal-directed behavior in response to a stimulus, and the information driving that behavior is unavailable to the verbally reporting system, then that system is a distinct conscious subject.

(5) By this criterion, many subsystems in an intact brain also qualify as distinct conscious subjects. Consider: a skilled driver brakes for a hazard before verbal awareness of the hazard arises; the dorsal visual stream guides reaching and grasping without contributing to conscious visual experience; and blindsight patients produce visually guided behavior while denying any visual experience. In each case, a subsystem guides flexible, goal-directed behavior using information inaccessible to the reporting system.

(6) So criterion (4) entails that intact brains also contain multiple conscious subjects — perhaps dozens.

(7) That conclusion is false. An intact human being is one conscious subject.

(8) Therefore the criterion in (4) is wrong, and with it the inference from (1) to (3). The split-brain data do not establish multiple subjects.

The trouble is that rejecting (2) or (3) seems to leave the split-brain data unexplained, and rejecting (4) seems to require a principled difference between the split-brain and intact-brain cases that I cannot articulate. If the difference is merely one of degree — the hemispheres are more integrated in intact brains — then the argument seems to suggest that the number of conscious subjects is itself a matter of degree, which is hard to make sense of. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to?
