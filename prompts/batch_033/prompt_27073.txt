I have spent the past few months arguing with a colleague about whether, in a forced choice between saving one stranger and saving five strangers, the bare fact that there are five ought to make a moral difference. We have come to suspect that the dispute between the Taurek-style anti-aggregationist, who holds that numbers by themselves do not count, and the aggregationist, who holds that they do, is partly a disagreement about what "counting" amounts to and partly a substantive disagreement about whether one person's loss and another person's loss can be added together into a larger total with moral weight. I would like to see the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two committed parties would produce.

Please take as the central case the following: you can rescue either one stranger or five strangers from equal, certain death; the six people are alike in every respect that could matter except their number; you have no special obligations to any of them, no promises, no prior relationships, and no reason to think any of them is more or less deserving. Draft a joint statement that both a committed anti-aggregationist and a committed aggregationist could sign. It should begin with a numbered agreed-claims section covering what both sides accept about the case, about the equal moral significance of each person's life, about what kind of choice this is and is not, and about which considerations would not settle the dispute. These claims should be non-vacuous and genuinely load-bearing, not merely the observation that the choice is difficult.

After the agreed claims, give a disagreements section or table in which the residual disagreement is stated exactly, with each side's view phrased so that its own proponents would accept the wording. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it — for example, a case in which the single person would suffer a far greater harm than any one of the five, an ex ante choice made behind a veil of ignorance, an iterated version of the rescue choice, or a one-versus-a-million version — and record in advance what each side predicts about that case and what outcome each side acknowledges would count against its own view. If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say exactly which part and why. The aim is a document both parties could put their names to, with the genuine disagreement left sharp rather than softened.
