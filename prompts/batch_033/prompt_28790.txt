I am a retired geologist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy of mind and biology, and I have become fascinated by Daniel Dennett's discussion of what he calls Darwin's and Turing's "strange inversions of reasoning" — the idea that competence can arise without comprehension, that a process can produce exquisitely functional outcomes without any understanding of what it is doing. This has sent me back to a passage in the first edition of Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) that I had not thought about carefully since my undergraduate days. I used to read it as Darwin merely fending off a terminological quibble; now I suspect it is doing something much more philosophically interesting.

The passage is from Chapter IV ("Natural Selection"). Darwin has just introduced the term and is responding to objections:

> Several writers have misapprehended or objected to the term Natural Selection. Some have even imagined that natural selection induces variability, whereas it implies only the preservation of such variations as occur and are beneficial to the being under its conditions of life. No one objects to agriculturists speaking of the potent effects of man's selection; and in this case the individual differences given by nature, which man for some object selects, must of necessity first occur. Others have objected that the term selection implies conscious choice in the animals which become modified; and it has even been urged that as plants have no volition, natural selection is not applicable to them! In the literal sense of the word, no doubt, natural selection is a misnomer; but who ever objected to chemists speaking of the elective affinities of the various elements? — and yet an acid cannot strictly be said to elect the base with which it will in preference combine. It has been said that I speak of natural selection as an active power or Deity; but who objects to an author speaking of the attraction of gravity as ruling the movements of the planets? Every one knows what is meant and is implied by such metaphorical expressions; and they are almost necessary for brevity. So again it is difficult to avoid personifying the word Nature; but I mean by Nature, only the aggregate action and product of many natural laws, and by laws the sequence of events as ascertained by us. With a little familiarity such superficial objections will be forgotten.

I am trying to work out what Darwin is actually doing here, philosophically, and I would appreciate help. In particular:

1. Darwin concedes that "natural selection" is literally a misnomer, yet insists the metaphor is "almost necessary." Is he simply making a pragmatic point about expository convenience, or is he gesturing at something deeper about how we must talk about processes that produce design-like outcomes without a designer? How does this connect — if at all — to the "strange inversion" idea that competence does not require comprehension?

2. When Darwin says he means by Nature "only the aggregate action and product of many natural laws, and by laws the sequence of events as ascertained by us," this sounds almost deflationary — as if Nature is nothing over and above the regularities themselves. Yet the whole book treats natural selection as a genuinely explanatory force. Is there a tension here, and how should it be resolved?

I would be grateful if, in answering, you could separate what is reasonably settled among scholars of Darwin and the philosophy of biology from what remains genuinely contested, and not present a contested reading as the consensus.
