I keep returning to a debate that seems to have been frozen for decades: the status of the common prior assumption. On one side are common priorists who take Aumann's agreement theorem and the Harsanyi doctrine to show something normatively significant — namely, that rational agents with common knowledge of one another's beliefs cannot disagree except through differences in private information. For them, assuming a common prior is not an empirical guess about how people actually form beliefs; it is the correct way to set up a model of interactive belief, and a divergence in priors is a symptom that the modeler has left some information asymmetry out of the state space. This assumption does heavy lifting elsewhere too: it underwrites no-trade results and the standard Harsanyi-style treatment of games of incomplete information. On the other side are subjectivist Bayesians who grant the mathematics and deny that it has the advertised normative force. For them, priors are simply degrees of belief, rationality constrains only how they are updated, and the common prior assumption is a substantive, often false, empirical claim about the sources of disagreement — one that economists impose to keep models tractable and that quietly rules out speculative trade and persistent disagreement by fiat. A third group adds a twist: they argue that the assumption is closer to a modeling convention than a substantive restriction, because a great deal of non-common-prior structure can be redescribed as common-prior structure on a richer state space, so the real question is not whether the assumption is true but what work it is doing.

Everyone accepts the formal results. Nobody has moved in decades. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is a mixture of a verbal disagreement about the word "rational" (internal coherence versus interpersonal consistency) and two communities answering different questions — one about what economic models are for, one about what epistemic norms should govern real agents — with the substantive disagreement having largely evaporated. But I am not confident in that hypothesis, and I can imagine the opposite: that there is a hidden formal or empirical question underneath that genuinely separates the sides.
