I am a high school student, and I have recently come to hold the view that in at least some split-brain patients there are two distinct centers of consciousness in a single skull. On my view, the unity of consciousness is not a brute given but a contingent achievement, and the surgery that severs the corpus callosum can genuinely break it: in the relevant moments, the patient is better described as two subjects than as one subject whose capacities are merely disconnected. I want to stress-test this view before I commit to it further. What would count against it? I would like the objections and contrary evidence ranked from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing between what a defender of the view would themselves concede counts against it and what they would dismiss as aimed at a straw man. For instance, I can see that a critic might appeal to the patient's ordinary coherence, to the speaking hemisphere's tendency to confabulate explanations for what the other hand does, or to the fact that much of the brain below the cortex remains undivided; I do not yet know how seriously to take any of these. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view has been stated flexibly enough to be unfalsifiable, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind. I am personally most moved by conflict cases in which one hand appears to act against the intentions of the other, and least moved by purely introspective arguments.
