I'm a novelist, and the book I'm currently drafting has got me tangled up in a moral philosophy problem I don't have the vocabulary for. The setup is this: my protaganist is in a position where she can prevent a large number of people from experiencing a mild, transient pleasure — say, the enjoyment of a good meal — and by doing so, she can also prevent one person from experiencing something truly horrific. Not death, but the kind of suffering that breaks a person, the kind that shows up in nightmares years later. She chooses to sacrifice the pleasures, and I want her to be right to do so. But I'm trying to understand *why* she's right, and the more I think about it the more slippery it gets.

Here's what I keep coming back to. If the suffering in question were also mild — a stubbed toe, a disappointing sandwich — then it seems like you could just do the math: five stubbed toes are worse than one, a hundred disappointing sandwiches might outweigh one stubbed toe, and so on. Pleasures and pains, at that level, feel like they're in the same currency. But when the suffering gets intense enough, it's like the exchange rate collapses. No number of pleasant meals seems to balance out a single torture session. It's not just that the torture is *very bad* and you'd need *very many* meals to match it — it's that they seem to be different *kinds* of thing, not tradeable at any ratio.

So I seem to believe in a threshold: below some level of intensity, pleasures and pains are commensurable; above it, pain has lexical priority over pleasure — no amount of the latter can justify the former. But I don't know if this position has a name, or if it's been developed in a rigorous way. And I'm especially bothered by where the threshold goes. Suffering intensity sure seems like a continuum — you can turn the dial up from a mild annoyance to agony in arbitrarily small increments — so any cutoff is going to have a sorites problem baked right in. At what exact point does the exchange rate collapse, and why there? If the answer is that the threshold is vague, does that cause trouble for the view, or can it live with a vague boundary the way most concepts do?

I'd love to know if there's an established name for this position and how the people who defend it handle the threshold problem. But honestly I care less about the label than about whether the view can be made coherent — what's the best version of it, and what are its weakest seams?
