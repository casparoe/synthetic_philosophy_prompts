I'm writing a paper for a seminar on moral psychology and the limits of impartiality, and I've been thinking about Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism and his "one thought too many" argument. The integrity objection, as I understand it, says that a fully impartial moral theory can alienate an agent from their own deepest projects, commitments, and personal relationships by requiring that every decision be justified from the point of view of the universe. A life lived that way threatens to become unrecognizable as a human life at all. The "one thought too many" sharpens the point: if a man has to deliberate about whether to save his drowning wife or a stranger, and he reaches for the impartial principle that it is permissible to favor one's spouse, the very act of reaching for that justification has already done damage — it replaces the spontaneous expression of love with a calculation that treats the wife as merely one bearer of value among others. The thought that was supposed to justify the action is itself the deformation.

I want to read Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* through this lens. The novel's narrator, Stevens, is a butler who has devoted his entire life to an ideal of professional "dignity" — the complete subordination of his own feelings, judgments, and personal attachments to the service of his employer, Lord Darlington. He suppresses his love for the housekeeper Miss Kenton, refuses to acknowledge his own grief at his father's death, and consistently defers all moral judgment to his master, even as Darlington drifts into collaboration with the Nazi regime. By the end of the novel, Stevens is left with the wreckage of a life that was, in some sense, perfectly principled — and perfectly empty. He has, in his own way, taken up the impartial standpoint and purged himself of the partialities that make a life one's own.

I'm not sure, however, whether the novel is a straightforward dramatization of Williams's argument or whether it complicates it in ways a philosophical essay cannot capture. Is Stevens's "dignity" a version of the impartial standpoint that Williams criticizes, or is it something else — a kind of bad faith, a refusal to take responsibility for moral judgment by outsourcing it to a superior? Does the novel suggest that Stevens's error was having a project at all, or that he picked the wrong one, or that he pursued it in the wrong spirit? And what would Williams make of the novel's treatment of regret — of Stevens's belated recognition that he has missed his life — as something that cannot be repaired by any principle or justification, however well-chosen? I would like a reading that takes both the philosophy and the novel seriously, that does not reduce the novel to a case study, and that says honestly where the novel resists or exceeds the philosophical framework.
