I've been working through some results in social choice theory and I've hit a wall that's making me doubt whether any group can truly be both democratic and rational at the same time. The argument runs like this:

(1) For a collective body to be democratic, its position on any given proposition must reflect the majority view of its members on that proposition. Call this proposition-wise majority rule.
(2) A collective body, like an individual reasoner, ought not to hold an inconsistent set of judgments — if it affirms P and affirms Q, and P and Q jointly entail R, it should not deny R. Call this collective rationality.
(3) From (1) and (2), it follows that a democratic body should be able to adopt the majority-supported position on each proposition and end up with a consistent overall view.
(4) But consider a three-judge panel reviewing a contract dispute. They face three questions: Was there a valid offer? (P) Was there valid acceptance? (Q) Should the contract be enforced? (R) Let the legal rule be that R follows from P∧Q. The judges vote:

    - Judge 1: P true, Q true, R true. (Consistent: {P,Q,R})
    - Judge 2: P true, Q false, R false. (Consistent: {P,¬Q,¬R})
   - Judge 3: P false, Q true,R false. (Consistent: {¬P,Q,¬R})

Now apply proposition-wise majority rule. On P: 2–1 for true. On Q:2–1 for true. On R:2–1 for false. So the collective view is {P, Q, ¬R}. But P∧Q entails R, so this set is inconsistent — even though every individual judge is perfectly consistent. This is the discursive dilemma.
(5) The same structure arises whenever a group votes on logically connected issues: committee hiring decisions, legislative packages, constitutional interpretations, multi-premise scientific judgments. It's not a fluke — it's a general phenomenon.
(6) So proposition-wise majority rule and collective rationality cannot both be satisfied for all possible distributions of individual opinions. You can have one or the other, but not both.
(7) Therefore democracy — understood as majority rule producing rational collective outcomes — is impossible in principle.

I can't accept (7). Democratic institutions exist, they function, and while they're imperfect we don't regard them as either secretly dictatorial or constitutively irrational. Yet I can't see which premise to abandon. If I reject (1), I seem to give up on the idea that democratic outcomes should track what most people actually think on each issue — and what's left of democracy without that? If I reject (2), I'm saying collective bodies are permitted to hold contradictory positions, which seems to license a kind of institutional irrationality that would corrode the whole point of deliberative decision-making. If I try to escape between (3) and (4) by insisting groups should vote only on conclusions or only on premises, then I'm not really respecting majority judgment on every proposition —I'm imposing a procedure that will sometimes override majority views on specific questions, which just concedes the force of the dilemma.

What I really want to know is: which step fails, and what exactly do you have to pay to reject it? If you think the argument is valid, then which premise is least costly to give up, and why?
