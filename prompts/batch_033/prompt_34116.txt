I'm drafting a novel at the moment, and one of my central characters is this bloke who's constantly pointing out other people's moral failings whilst being a complete mess himself — he's having an affair, lies to his business partner, the works. I want to get the philosophy of this right, not just make him a cartoon villain. So I've been thinking about the whole business of standing to blame, and whether a hypocrite's criticism can still land.

Anyway, I was re-reading the old "mote and beam" bit from the King James Bible, and it's got me a bit stuck. Here's the passage (Matthew 7:1–5):

"Judge not, that ye be not judged. For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again. And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother's eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye? Or how wilt thou say to thy brother, Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye; and, behold, a beam is in thine own eye? Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye."

Now here's what I'm grappling with. The passage ends by saying "first cast out the beam... and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote." That seems to imply that once you've sorted your own mess out, you're actually in a *better* position to help someone else with theirs — which sort of suggests the hypocrite's judgment might be accurate, just badly timed, or coming from the wrong person. But the opening is "Judge not, that ye be not judged," which reads a lot more like a blanket prohibition.

So my question is: what's the actual philosophical upshot here about standing to blame? Does the passage suggest that the hypocrite's moral perception is *distorted* by her own failings (so she can't even see the mote properly), or that she's *right* about the mote but simply lacks the authority to say so until she's cleaned up her own act? And if it's the latter — if the hypocrite's criticism is factually correct — what is it exactly that's missing from her standing? Is it just a matter of etiquette, or is there a deeper moral reason why we shouldn't have to take criticism from someone who's guilty of the same thing?
