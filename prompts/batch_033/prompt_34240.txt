In my history of economic thought course this term, we have been working through the socialist calculation debate, and an argument from my study group has been bothering me. Two of my classmates, Marcus and Priya, got into a dispute about whether Oskar Lange's market-socialist proposal actually answers the Hayekian knowledge objection, and they asked me to referee. I could not sort it out, so I would like a careful, point-by-point adjudication. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Marcus began: "I don't see why Lange doesn't just win. Mises said a socialist economy is impossible because there are no market prices for producer goods. Lange's reply was that a Central Planning Board can set those prices by trial and error: it raises the posted price when there is a shortage and lowers it when there is a surplus, and it tells enterprise managers to minimize average cost and to produce where price equals marginal cost. That is what a competitive market does anyway, so calculation is possible without private ownership of the means of production."

Priya pushed back: "You are treating the Board like a Walrasian auctioneer who can already see every excess demand. But Hayek's point is that the data are not given to any single mind. The knowledge that would let you set and adjust those prices — local conditions, production possibilities, which inputs can be substituted for which — is dispersed across millions of people and mostly is not written down anywhere. Watching inventory levels does not tell you which alternatives exist."

Marcus replied: "But that is exactly how actual markets work. No single trader knows everything; prices carry dispersed information in compressed form. The Board does not need to know the underlying causes of a shortage either. It observes the same signal and adjusts the posted price, and the new price then carries information to managers. If you allow trial-and-error pricing to work for markets, you cannot deny it to the Board without a double standard."

Priya answered: "The difference is who runs the trial. In a market, millions of buyers and sellers adjust their own bids and offers using their own local knowledge, and the price is the emergent result. Lange's Board is one agent that has to decide which prices to post in the first place. To know that a shortage of steel should raise the price of steel rather than the price of aluminum, it already needs the very dispersed knowledge Hayek says it cannot collect."

Marcus came back: "Now you are demanding omniscience from the Board while letting the market get away with not having it. An individual firm also has to notice its own order book and guess at substitutes. Hayek seems to be saying that the Board cannot know enough to adjust a price while the market somehow can — but the market is just many agents with partial knowledge using local signals. The Board can use those same signals, so your argument proves too much."

Priya then said: "The firm is not the right comparison. A firm owns its assets, bears its own losses, and has an incentive to discover and act on local knowledge; if it is wrong, it fails. Lange's managers are following the Board's rules and do not bear the downside of being wrong, and the Board has no profit-and-loss test to tell whether a posted price reflects real scarcity or just clears a plan it itself set. So the real problem is not computation — it is discovery and accountability."

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each point made by Marcus and by Priya, say what is right in it and what is overstated or mistaken, then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and conclude with who is more right overall. Please answer in American English.
