I've been trying to settle a recurring argument on rationalist-adjacent forums about whether a Bayesian agent should ever avoid information, and someone cited Good's theorem as though it closes the case. A blog post I found states the theorem like this:

"Good's theorem is the formal result behind the slogan that information can't hurt a rational agent. If you're an expected-utility maximizer facing a decision and someone offers you a piece of evidence for free, you can always ignore it and choose the act you would have chosen anyway, so your expected utility after seeing the evidence can't be lower than before, and it might be higher. It follows that a Bayesian should never turn down free information, and should be willing to pay at least something for any information source, however noisy. It also follows that once you've seen the evidence you can't be worse off than if you'd never looked; if updating on the evidence led you to a worse outcome, that just means you updated wrong."

I have a vague memory that Good's original point was narrower — roughly, that the expected value of costless information is nonnegative for an expected-utility maximizer — and I suspect the post is overreading the theorem, but I can't reconstruct exactly which parts are fair and which are overstatements.
