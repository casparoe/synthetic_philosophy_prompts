Last month I did my first sensory-deprivation float, and what surprised me most was how insistently my sense of being a self persisted once vision, hearing, and most bodily contact were taken away. A few days later a friend pointed me to Avicenna's flying man argument: a person is created fully formed in an instant, eyes veiled, limbs spread so that no part of the body touches another, suspended in air; Avicenna claims this person would nonetheless affirm his own existence, and that what he affirms is not his body but himself. Since then I have been reading around the argument, and the treatment I keep meeting runs roughly as follows: it is an early argument for an immaterial soul, and it fails because the flying man would still have bodily sensations such as heartbeat, breathing, balance, and temperature, and because lacking a body-image is not the same as lacking a body.

I would like you to argue for a specific position on this. Argue that the flying man argument is best reconstructed not as an argument for a non-bodily substance, but as an argument that self-awareness is not derived from, and is not a form of, sensory awareness of one's own body — and that this more modest conclusion survives the standard objections, while the stronger substance-dualist conclusion does not. Take the obvious worries seriously: that the scenario as described does not actually eliminate bodily awareness; that the move from "he is not aware of his body" to "what he is aware of is not his body" looks like an equivocation; and that Avicenna himself may have intended the stronger conclusion. If some part of the modest reading cannot be defended, say exactly which part and defend the strongest version of it that you can.
