Write a dialogue between two metaphysicians of science who begin from a shared question: what distinguishes a world in which all Fs are Gs as a matter of law from a world in which all Fs are Gs by cosmic accident? One of them defends a best-system account of laws of nature, on which laws are the axioms or theorems of the deductive system that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit over the particular facts; the other defends a governing account, on which laws are not mere summaries of the facts but something that makes the facts unfold as they do. The two should be evenly matched and should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than trading set speeches.

The dialogue should center on two connected pressure points. First, the anti-Humean should press the best-system theorist: assessing simplicity and strength requires a privileged vocabulary, because otherwise gruesome, gerrymandered predicates can make almost any regularity look lawlike; but if the best-system theorist helps themselves to perfectly natural properties, the anti-Humean argues, they have imported a primitive that does much of the modal and selective work the account was meant to avoid. Second, the Humean should press the governing theorist: a relation of necessitation or governance between properties either collapses into the regularity it was supposed to explain or remains a bare posit whose operation is left mysterious; to say that a law "makes" its instances conform, without an account of how this making works, is no better than invoking a dormitive virtue to explain sleep.

Have the characters pursue these objections through at least two exchanges each, and have each character revise or sharpen their view at least once in response to the other's point. The dialogue should end with the disagreement intact but with each position's commitments and remaining costs made clear.
