I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I am giving a seminar presentation to a joint meeting of a philosophy of language reading group and a computational linguistics lab. The talk will defend a version of Hofstadter's distinction among the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message in Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid. My thesis is that the question where meaning is located is badly posed as long as it expects a single bearer. A message has determinate content only relative to a frame that specifies how the inner message is to be decoded, and the frame is itself available only through an outer message that marks the whole transaction as an act of communication. Meaning, I will argue, is a three-level relational property of the message, its frame, and the decoding conventions the frame makes available; it is not stored in the symbols, in the sender's intentions, or in the receiver's construction. The practical conclusion I want to leave with the room is that current evaluations of whether language models understand their outputs quietly supply the frame and the outer message themselves, and therefore over-attribute semantic understanding.

The audience is not predisposed to this. The philosophers are mostly split between propositional-content views and Gricean intention-based semantics, with two externalists who will suspect that I have dropped reference and truth conditions from the picture. The computational linguists work on model evaluation and benchmarking; they are impatient with what they regard as armchair meaning talk and will want an operational criterion. I expect the questions to come from those commitments, not from a deconstructionist or a formal semanticist.

My argument in outline: (1) the same physical string can carry different contents under different frames, so the string alone underdetermines content; (2) the frame is not a brute fact but a further message, so the distinction between message and frame is one of level, not of kind; (3) the outer message is what lets a receiver recognize that a framing relation is in force at all, which is why bare pattern detection is not yet interpretation; and (4) therefore any evaluation of an artificial system's outputs that does not explicitly model the frame and the outer message is measuring the evaluator's framing, not the system's understanding.

Prepare me for the question period as a prep sheet, not as a defense of the thesis. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they will come, each phrased the way that audience member would put it. For each objection, tell me how much damage it does and how likely it is, what I should concede immediately, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply in my own voice; also tell me where that reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and mark it as such.

I want the prep sheet as structured data. First, a table with these columns:
