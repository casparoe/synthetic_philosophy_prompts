I teach an undergraduate philosophy of religion course — about 25 students, one 75-minute session — and I want a hands-on activity for the Pascal unit. Design an in-class betting game, playable with slips of paper and a points ledger rather than money, that makes students feel the pull of the wager before they hear the names "Pascal" or "expected utility." The game should run through three stages: first, ordinary choices under risk with stated probabilities, where a safe option and a risky option are genuinely close in expected points; second, a choice where the probabilities are unknown but one option offers a very large payoff while the other carries a small certain cost, so that the risky option starts to look rational; third, a forced commitment in which abstaining is not allowed and every student must choose between two "ways of life" — one with a small certain cost now, the other promising an in-principle unbounded gain on an unverifiable condition. Then provide a debrief that maps the three stages onto ordinary prudence, the expected-value argument, and the claim that we are already embarked; that distinguishes the dominance version of the wager from the expected-utility version; and that raises at least three objections students can test against their own behavior in the game, such as the many-gods objection, the objection that belief cannot be adopted at will, and the problem of unbounded utilities. Include rules, materials, a points scheme with real enough incentives, a timeline, a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest statement of which feature of the wager the game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. End with a one-sentence summary of the activity as the final line.
