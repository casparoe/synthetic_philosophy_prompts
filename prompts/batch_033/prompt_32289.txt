Two of my colleagues, Mara and Eli, got into an argument after a department colloquium on social epistemology, and I happened to be standing nearby for the whole thing. Neither knew I was listening carefully, and I haven't told either of them what I think. I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange.

(1) Mara started by saying the speaker's anti-reductionism about testimony — the view that hearers have a default entitlement to believe what they're told, without needing positive reasons to accept a given piece of testimony — was a license for gullibility. If a stranger at a bus stop tells you the time, and you believe them without checking your phone or looking for a clock, you're not being epistemically responsible. You should at least have some reason to think they're competent and sincere.

(2) Eli replied that this demand is self-defeating. Most of what any of us knows comes from testimony: history, science, geography, the news, even most of what we know about our own early biographies. If we needed independent positive reasons in every case, we'd never get started. He said Mara's position effectively entails that we can't justifiably believe most of what we in fact know, which is a reductio of the position.

(3) Mara came back: children believe whatever they're told, and we don't treat young children as epistemically responsible agents. The fact that we start out credulous doesn't mean credulity is justified. It means we all begin in an epistemically defective state and slowly learn to be critical. The developmental trajectory supports the reductionist, not the anti-reductionist.

(4) Eli said the child case actually cuts the other way. A three-year-old who believes her parents when they tell her the dog is friendly, or that her name is what it is, clearly knows those things. But she lacks the conceptual resources to track anyone's reliability — she can't form induction-based judgments about sincerity or competence. So if she knows things via testimony, which she does, then testimonial justification can't require the kind of positive reasons Mara is demanding. The child's justification must be more basic than that, which is exactly what the anti-reductionist claims.

(5) Mara paused, then said: fine, but the child's justification might be externalist. She's justified because her caregivers are in fact reliable, not because she has checked. That's still compatible with reductionism: the justification still reduces to the reliability of the source, even if the believer can't articulate the basis. Reductionism is a thesis about what the justification consists in, not about what the believer must be able to say.

(6) Eli grinned and said: if you've conceded that a hearer can be justified without having done any checking, without having positive reasons, and without even being able to do those things, then what's left of the reductionist picture? You've kept the word "reduction" but given up the normative claim that hearers need reasons. Your view is now functionally identical to anti-reductionism, just dressed up with the word "reliability."

(7) Mara said: not quite. There's a difference between individual-level checking and system-level checking. The reason the child's testimony-based beliefs are justified is that she's plugged into a social-epistemic system — one where testifiers are generally reliable because there are reputational costs for deception, institutional filters, and corrective practices. The system as a whole gets checked, even if individual hearers don't do the checking. So the justification is still reductionist: it bottoms out in the system's track record of reliability. The reduction base is just social rather than individual.

(8) Eli replied: that's an interesting move, but it pushes the bump under the rug. How does the child's justification actually depend on the system's reliability? If the child has no awareness of the system, no concept of reputation or institutional filtering, then the connection between the system's reliability and the child's justification is mysterious. You're saying the child is justified because of facts she has no access to and no capacity to represent. That's externalism, sure, but it's externalism about a social fact. And at that point I don't see why we should still call it reductionism: the child's justification isn't reduced to anything she possesses; it's grounded in features of her social environment that she can't even represent.

At that point they had to leave for another session, and the argument ended there.

Please adjudicate this exchange. For each of the eight moves, rate the argument as clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who, overall, has the stronger position given the arguments as they actually made them. Finally — and I want you to take this seriously — say what argument or consideration, if it were raised, would most likely change your overall verdict. Where is your reasoning about this exchange most vulnerable to being wrong?
