so i'm a resident at a teaching hospital and i got absolutley chewed out last week and i can't stop thinking about whether i actually did something wrong. a patient asked me point blank whether her symptoms could be lupus — her sister has lupus and she was spiraling about it — and i told her no, based on her labs and the attending's notes and everything i'd been taught, it was almost certainly just a post-viral inflammatory response, and i said it with confidence because i genuinely thought i knew. well, turns out it was lupus after all, a really weird early presentation, and now the attending is furious and there's a formal complaint. his line is "you asserted something you didn't know, and that's the whole problem — the standard for assertion is knowledge, and since what you said was false, you couldn't have known it."

but the thing is i wasn't being reckless? i had excellent evidence, my reasoning was solid, every reasonable clinician would have said the same thing given the same info. it just turned out to be wrong. if the standard is "don't assert what you don't know" and knowledge requires truth, then it seems like any time anyone asserts something empirical and turns out to be mistaken, they're automatically retroactively in the wrong — which feels insane, because that would make basically all of medicine (or any empirical field) a minefield where you can do everything right and still be culpable.

i guess my question is: what does it actually take for an assertion to be proper? is it knowledge, justified belief, truth, something else? because this has gone from a philosophy-class hypothetical to my actual career being on the line and i need to figure out if my attending has a point or if he's just covering his own ass by throwing me under the bus.
