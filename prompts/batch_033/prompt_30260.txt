I'm taking a philosophy of science course and we just hit Hempel's ravens, and I've realized my gut on confirmation is kind of a mess. Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can give them. Seeing a black raven clearly confirms that all ravens are black. Seeing an albino raven would just kill the generalization outright. A black cat does nothing for me — it's black, so it's not even in the running. A green frog in a pond does nothing. A white shoe on the sidewalk also does nothing: I get that it's a non-black non-raven and that the hypothesis is supposed to be equivalent to the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens, but it really doesn't feel like evidence about ravens. But here's the thing: if I deliberately draw an item at random from a bin I already know contains only non-black things, and it comes out a shoe, then I do feel like the raven hypothesis got a tiny boost, because it could have come out a non-black raven and didn't. And a white crow bothers me. It's a non-black non-raven like the shoe, but it's a bird and even a corvid, so I feel like it should count for something — maybe more than the shoe but less then a black raven — even though I already knew it was a crow and not a raven, so it wasn't exactly a live counterexample.

My working principle is something like: an observation confirms a generalization only when it was a live possibility beforehand that the observation would turn out to be a counterexample. Test that principle against my cases, repair it where it breaks, and then tell me what it says about a case I keep going back and forth on: I see a raven at a distance and I'm maybe 90% sure it's black. Does that confirm anything, and how does it compare to a black cat seen up close?
