I've been trying to get clear on the meta-problem of consciousness after seeing it invoked in rationalist-adjacent discussions of illusionism, and I recently came across the following summary in a general-audience philosophy magazine:

"Chalmers distinguishes the hard problem of consciousness from what he calls the meta-problem. The meta-problem is the problem of explaining why we think and say there is a hard problem — why physical systems like us are so strongly disposed to report an explanatory gap between brain processes and experience. Once we have a complete explanation of the cognitive machinery that produces those reports, the hard problem itself largely dissolves, since the appearance of a gap would have been accounted for in purely physical terms. Chalmers accordingly regards illusionism — the view that phenomenal consciousness does not exist and that our sense of it is an introspective illusion — as the most plausible resolution of the issue."

I have a vague sense that the last two sentences overstate things, and that Chalmers himself is not an illusionist, but I don't trust my memory of the details. Could you go through this summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful reader of Chalmers would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong? In particular, does "explaining why we think there is a hard problem" actually commit or even strongly motivate anyone to the conclusion that the hard problem dissolves, and does Chalmers actually endorse illusionism, or does he merely treat it as a serious option? I want to leave with a clear sense of which parts of the summary I can safely repeat and which parts would misrepresent the view if I quoted them.
