**Exam question:**

Explain the bootstrapping problem and the easy-knowledge problem. Are these two problems fundamentally the same challenge to epistemological theorizing, or are they distinct? Defend your answer by identifying the specific epistemic principle or commitment that each problem puts under pressure.

---

**Student answer:**

The bootstrapping problem, introduced by Jonathan Vogel, arises when a subject tries to establish the reliability of a belief-forming process by using that very process. Vogel's classic example is a gas gauge: you look at the gauge, it reads "Full," you form the belief that the tank is full, and you later confirm this by checking. After doing this many times, you reason inductively: "On occasion 1 the gauge read Full and the tank was full; on occasion 2 the gauge read Full and the tank was full; ... therefore the gauge is reliable." The problem is that each premise — that the tank was full on a given occasion — was itself justified only by trusting the gauge. So the track-record argument is circular in a way that seems epistemically vicious. The bootstrapping problem is primarily a challenge to closure-based views of knowledge, because it shows that you cannot legitimately deduce the reliability of your faculties from particular beliefs those faculties produced.

The easy-knowledge problem, introduced by Stewart Cohen, is closely related. Cohen asks us to consider a subject who looks at a red table in good light and comes to know that the table is red. By the principle of epistemic closure — if S knows that P, and S knows that P entails Q, then S can come to know that Q — the subject can deduce that she is not a brain in a vat being deceived into hallucinating a red table. But this seems too easy: we shouldn't be able to refute radical skeptical hypotheses simply by looking at a table and performing a one-step deduction. The easy-knowledge problem therefore challenges reliabilism, because reliabilism says you can know something just by using a reliable process, and looking at a table is a reliable process — so reliabilism seems forced to accept that you thereby know you're not a brain in a vat.

In my view, these two problems are fundamentally the same. Both involve a subject starting with ordinary perceptual knowledge and then taking a further step — inductive in the bootstrapping case, deductive in the easy-knowledge case — to reach a conclusion that seems to come too cheaply. The bootstrapping problem is really just a special case of the easy-knowledge problem where the further step happens to be inductive rather than deductive. In both cases, the core issue is that closure lets us expand our knowledge in a way that feels illicit, and the solution to both must be to restrict or reject closure. Some philosophers, like James Van Cleve, have argued that bootstrapping is not actually problematic because the track-record argument is just ordinary induction, and if we reject it we lose too much of our empirical knowledge. But this response misses the point: ordinary induction is fine when the premises are independently justified, whereas in bootstrapping the premises depend on the very process whose reliability is at issue.
