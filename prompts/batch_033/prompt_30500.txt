Consider the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation. A three-member panel must decide two premises, P and Q, and their conjunction, P∧Q. Each member's individual judgments are consistent: each accepts P∧Q if and only if she accepts both P and Q. Yet when the panel votes proposition-by-proposition, the majorities can accept P, accept Q, and reject P∧Q, leaving the group with an inconsistent collective judgment set. The literature offers several ways of responding to this. Please rank the following five responses from most to least satisfactory as a prescription for what the panel ought to do, and give a sentence or two for each explaining why it lands where it does.

The responses are:

(1) Premise-based procedure: the panel votes only on the premises and lets the conclusion be whatever the accepted premises entail.
(2) Conclusion-based procedure: the panel votes directly on the conjunction and disregards the premise-level votes.
(3) Distance-based aggregation: select the consistent collective judgment set that minimizes total distance from the individual sets, where distance is measured by the number of propositions on which the collective set disagrees with a given member.
(4) Chair dictatorship: let one designated member's consistent judgment set serve as the group's set.
(5) Suspension or agenda revision: treat the inconsistency as a signal that the group should not aggregate these judgments at all, and should instead suspend judgment or restructure the question.

In producing the ranking, take an explicit stand on the relative weight of collective consistency, responsiveness to individual votes, and resistance to strategic manipulation — and say whether, and how, your ranking changes depending on whether the panel is a court, a legislature, or an expert advisory body.
