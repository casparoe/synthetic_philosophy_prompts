I'm organizing a workshop on the normativity of promises for an interdisciplinary audience of philosophers, legal scholars, and political theorists, and I'd like to include a piece of philosophical dialogue that captures the central fault line in the contemporary literature on promissory obligation. You are to act as a specialist in moral philosophy with deep expertise in the promising literature — someone comfortable with the views of Rawls, Scanlon, Hume, Shiffrin, Owens, Gilbert, and their interlocutors.

Write a dialogue between two characters, Elena and Julian, both moral philosophers who hold sharply different views about why promises bind. Elena defends a broadly practice-based or conventionalist account: on her view, promising is a socially constructed institution, and the obligation to keep a promise is internal to the rules of that institution, much as the obligation to follow the rules of a game is internal to having agreed to play it. Julian defends a broadly expectation-based or interpersonal account: on his view, the act of promising is a way of inviting another person to rely on one's future conduct, and the obligation to keep the promise derives from the wrongness of disappointing the reliance one has intentionally invited. They have been circling around this disagreement for years — they teach at neighboring institutions, they've traded objections in Q&A sessions and over email — and they've finally sat down over a long dinner at a conference to hash it out properly. They respect each other, and neither is trying to score points. The conversation should feel like a genuine attempt to understand what is at stake between them, not a debate transcript.

The dialogue should grapple with at least the following pressure points:

- The problem of bare or unaccepted promises: what if the promisee doesn't hear the promise, or doesn't care, or immediately releases the promisor? Does the obligation vanish, and if so, why? How does each view handle the intuition that a promise to a dying person binds even if the promisee will never know whether it was kept?
- The problem of variable practice: if a society's promising conventions were different — say, they treated promises made on a Wednesday as non-binding, or required a witness — would the moral facts about promissory obligation differ? Does the practice view make promissory obligation too contingent on local convention, or does the expectation view have trouble explaining why the specific social form of "promising" matters at all?
- The question of directed obligation: can the practice view account for the distinctive wrong of breaking a promise to a particular person (as opposed to merely violating a useful convention), and can the expectation view account for the distinctive force of the act of promising itself (as opposed to merely creating expectations, which can be done in many ways)?

The dialogue should be roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words. Before writing the dialogue itself, take a moment to sketch the key argumentative moves each character will make, and identify one or two points where their views might actually converge or where the disagreement might turn out to be less deep than it first appears. Then write the dialogue, making sure each character genuinely responds to what the other has just said rather than launching into pre-rehearsed speeches.
