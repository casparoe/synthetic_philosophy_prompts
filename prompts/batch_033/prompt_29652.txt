I'm a philosophy professor, but I also sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and I've been landed with drafting the written opinion for next week's meeting. The case: a woman in her forties has been in a minimally conscious state since a cardiac arrest. She visually tracks people, localizes a noxious stimulus, and grimaces during wound care, but she has never once indicated that she is aware of being in pain — no request for relief, no communication at all. The ICU team wants to keep her on a standard opioid regimen during wound care anyway. One committee member, who's read some philosophy of mind, is pushing back. His line is that the team is implicitly relying on a first-order theory of consciousness, on which a state can be conscious just in virtue of its intrinsic features. On a higher-order theory, by contrast, a state is conscious only if the subject is aware of being in that state, say through a higher-order thought or a higher-order perception of the pain. Since this patient shows no sign of any higher-order awareness of her own states, he argues, we have no reason to think there is any subjective feel to the pain, and we may be treating a mere reflex with opiods. We went back and forth on the transitivity principle — the idea that a conscious state is one the subject is conscious of — and whether it even applies when the subject can't report.

Other members think this is exactly the kind of reasoning that leads to under-treating pain in patients who can't report, and that first-order theories plus neuroimaging give us sufficient grounds to think she does feel pain. I need to circulate a short memo before Thursday. Please draft it: recommend a concrete course of action for the team (continue the regimen, stop it, or continue with conditions and a time frame), lead with the bottom line, and engage the higher-order position seriously rather than just dismissing it as dangerous. Max 700 words.
