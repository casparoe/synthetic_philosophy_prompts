So I'm a novelist — two books out, working on a third — and I've been trying to nail down what I actually think about authorial intention and meaning, partly because I keep getting asked about it at festivals and partly because it genuinly matters for how I think about my own work. I've jotted down a list of views I seem to hold, and I want someone who actually knows this literature to tell me if I'm tying myself in knots. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(a) I don't think the author's intentions determine the meaning of the text. Once the book is out there, readers can legitimately find things I never consciously put there, and I've had the experience of readers pointing out patterns I hadn't noticed myself — those readings feel genuine, not like mistakes.

(b) But I also think some interpretations are just wrong. Not merely uninteresting or poorly argued, but factually mistaken about what the text supports. If someone reads my novel and insists the protaganist is literally a space alien, I don't think that's a valid alternative reading — it's a misreading.

(c) I'm drawn to the idea that the best interpretation of a literary work is the one that makes it aesthetically richest — the most interesting, moving, or beautiful reading the text can bear.

(d) I believe a text's meaning can genuinely shift over time. A novel written in 1960 doesn't mean exactly the same thing in 2024, and that's not just about readers changing — the meaning itself drifts as the interpretive community and its context evolve.

(e) When I'm actually drafting, my intentions feel like an anchor. I'm writing toward something specific, and if I lose track of that, the work falls apart. The intention matters enormously for the process of making the thing, even if it doesn't settle the thing's meaning afterward.

(f) I'm suspicious of Barthes-style "death of the author" talk — not because I think authors get to be authorities on their own work, but because it seems to invite a kind of interpretive free-for-all where nothing really constrains a reading.

I'd like you to play the role of a philosopher of art or literary theorist and go through these. Which pairs are actually in tension, and how badly? Which ones only look like they're in tension but dissolve on closer inspection? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each, say what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs. And then tell me: if I could only revise one of these six commitments, which should I revise to clean up the most problems?

Take your time — maybe start by mapping out which commitments pull in which directions before you try to adjudicate.
