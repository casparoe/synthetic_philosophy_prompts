Construct a thought experiment where someone forms a moral belief entirely through moral testimony (deferring to someone else's judgment), and the belief is true, justified, and reliably formed — it looks like knowledge by any standard epistemic criterion. But the person then acts on that belief in a way that gets them into moral trouble — not because the belief is false, but because there's something specifically wrong with acting on moral beliefs you haven't reasoned through yourself. The challenge: the case must be ordinary-life (no trolleys, no sci-fi), and the moral trouble must be the kind of thing a reasonable person would recognize as a genuine failing, not just a philosopher's hangup. After the scenario, walk through: (1) why the belief counts as knowledge, (2) exactly what the moral failing is and whether it's a failing of the action or of the agent, and (3) whether a defender of moral testimony can say "the problem is with the action, not with how the belief was formed, so moral testimony is still fine epistemically." Then identify the feature of the case that seperates the epistemic success from the practical failure, and say what happens if you vary that feature — does the epistemic success collapse, or does the practical failure disappear?
