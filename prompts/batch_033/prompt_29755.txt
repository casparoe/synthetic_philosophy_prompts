So I'm a science journalist — I've been covering neuroscience and consciousness research for about a decade, mostly for places like Nautilus and Aeon — and I got roped into giving a talk at our local science festival next month. The title they gave me is "What Science Can't Tell Us About Consciousness," and the audience is going to be maybe sixty people, mostly curious adults but no philosophy background, and I've got forty minutes plus Q&A. I want to build the whole thing around Mary's room — the thought experiment where the color scientist who knows every physical fact about color vision has never actually seen color, and then she walks out and sees a red rose for the first time. I've written about this before, but writing a 3000-word piece is totally different from standing in front of people and making the argument land in real time.

What I need from you isn't the explanation itself — I know the argument. I need a teaching plan. What has to go first, before I even mention Mary? What's the one example or image that should carry the whole thing, and what does it need to have and not have? I'm especially worried about the misconceptions people bring — the "but she already knew what red looks like from the brain scans" objection, or the idea that the argument is just about the limits of language rather than about knowledge itself. Where in the sequence do I head each of those off? And I need a way to check, by the end, whether they've actually understood the force of the argument — not wether they agree with it, but whether they get why it's been so sticky for forty years. Also, what do I openly refuse to get into? Because I know someone's going to ask about p-zombies or panpsychism and I need to know where to draw the line.
