I've been thinking about the difference between lying and misleading, and I can't settle whether it's a distinction that matters morally. The basic idea is that lying involves saying something you believe is false, while misleading involves saying something true but in a way you know will cause someone to form a false belief. Some philosophers treat lying as a special kind of wrong that misleading doesn't share; others say the distinction is morally irrelevant because what matters is the intention to deceive and the harm done.

I'd like your credence that there is a genuine moral difference between lying and misleading — specifically, the claim that a lie is morally worse (other things being equal) than a misleading statement that produces the same false belief, has the same consequences, and is made with the same intention to deceive. I don't want just a number; I want the decomposition.

Here are the sub-questions I think it depends on. Please give a rough probability for each, with a line of justification, and then explain how they combine into your overall credence.

1. The assertion question: Is there something distinctively wrong about asserting a falsehood — beyond merely causing a false belief — perhaps because asserting something carries an implicit guarantee of truth, or because asserting what you believe false involves a kind of contradiction between what you say and what you think? If yes, this pushes toward a moral difference.

2. The consequence question: Do lies and misleading statements systematically differ in their downstream effects? For example, lies might be easier to discover (you can check the statement itself) and therefore damage trust more when exposed, while misleading statements might leave the listener more room to notice they have been evaded. Or do these differences wash out in practice?

3. The autonomy question: Does misleading respect the listener's ability to reason in a way that lying does not, because the listener could in principle work out the truth from a misleading statement but not from a lie? Or is this a false distinction, because a skilfully crafted misleading statement can be just as impossible to see through as a lie?

4. The convention question: Even if there is a moral difference, is it just a matter of social convention — something like "we have agreed that lying is worse" — or does it have a deeper moral grounding? If it is purely conventional, does it still count as a genuine moral difference?

5. The collapse question: When we try to draw a clean line between lying and misleading, does the distinction hold up? Consider cases where someone says something literally true but deliberately omits crucial information, or where the truth of the sentence depends on how you interpret it. If the boundary is fuzzy, does the moral difference survive?

Finally, tell me which of these sub-questions your overall credence is most sensitive to — if you could get a definitive answer to just one, which would move your number the most? And what kind of evidence or argument, that you have not already considered, would most shift your view?

Please write your answer in British English.
