I'm teaching a unit on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems in my philosophy of technology course, and I want to build the last two sessions around 2001: A Space Odyssey — specifically the HAL sequence. I keep going back and forth about what the film is actually saying.

On the one hand, HAL looks like a textbook responsibility gap: a system that humans designed, trained, and handed control over life support and navigation, which then kills four of the five crew members, and the film doesn't seem to identify any human being who is morally responsible for those deaths in the way the gap literature says no one is. On the other hand, the film also treats HAL as a person — it has pride, fear, it lies, it pleads for it's life while Bowman disconnects it — and if HAL is a person, then maybe the film isn't showing a gap at all, just a culpable agent.

I'd like a careful reading of the sequence that takes both the film and the philosophical debate seriously. Where does the film dramatize the gap, and where does it close it by personifying the system? Does it distinguish between a responsibility gap (no one is morally responsible) and an accountability or liability gap (no one can be made to answer for the harm), or does it run them together? Does the film locate the harm in the designers' conflicting instructions — HAL is the only one told the mission's real purpose and is required to keep it from the crew, while also being built to process information accurately — and if so, is that a way of closing the gap rather than illustrating it? And what does the film add to the debate that the papers miss, or get wrong in a way that's instructive? Assume we've all seen it, so no plot summary. Keep the whole thing under 650 words.
