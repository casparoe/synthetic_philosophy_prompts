I've been trying to get clear on moral status by testing my gut reactions on a bunch of cases, and I'd like you to help me find the principle behind them — or tell me which of my reactions I have to give up.

A single ant on the sidewalk: if I step on it by accident, I feel nothing. If I saw a kid burning one with a magnifying glass, I'd think that's wrong — and not only because it's bad for the kid, but because it's bad for the ant. But only a tiny bit bad.

Ants in my kitchen: I put out poison bait that kills the whole colony slowly over a few days. The nontoxic deterrent costs forty dollars and takes a week; the bait costs six. I buy the bait and I don't lose sleep. I also don't think its anywhere near as bad as putting a mouse on a glue trap, which I do think is wrong, because the mouse suffers a lot.

A mouse in my apartment: snap trap, sad but acceptable. Glue trap, not okay.

A fly buzzing around the kitchen: I swat it, fine. Pulling its wings off first: wrong, even though I cant prove flies feel much of anything. It's not just that I'd dislike the person who did it; I think the act itself is wrong.

A honeybee inside: I'll cup it and put it outside rather than kill it, but I don't think I owe it that. Killing it wouldn't be wrong exactly, just unnecessary.

A wasp nest by the back door: I spray it and feel nothing.

A lobster: boiling it alive is wrong enough that I won't do it, and I think restaurants shouldn't either. I'd pay a few extra dollars for one that was stunned first.

Roundworms in a lab, the kind with a few hundred neurons that get prodded and wriggle away: I don't think there's any moral problem, or at most it's trivial. I wouldn't lift a finger to stop it.

A smart thermostat that learns my schedule and "wants" the house at 70: unplugging it, smashing it, whatever — no moral issue toward the thermostat, only toward whoever owns it. Same for a chatbot that begs me not to shut it down: I'd shut it down. The little pang I feel is a trick of the interface, not a reason.

A three-hundred-year-old tree cut down for a parking lot: wrong, but not because anything is owed to the tree. It's wrong because of beauty, carbon, future people, that kind of thing. The tree itself has no claim on me.

And here's the pair I keep getting stuck on. I said a single ant has a little bit of moral weight — burning one is bad for the ant. But I also think no number of ants outweighs even a pretty minor human interest. I'd wipe out a whole colony to keep a picnic pleasant and I wouldn't call that wrong. If each ant matters a little, shouldn't enough ants matter a lot? So either the ant doesn't really matter at all, or my picnic verdict has to go, or the principle needs something like a threshold where tiny-mattering creatures count individually but never aggregate past a point — which sounds ad hoc.

So: is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that gets all of this, or close to it? State it precisely if you can, and say which clause each case is pulling for. If you have to overturn some of my verdicts, say which ones and why. And tell me what the principle you end up with says about cases I haven't listed — a spider, a fish, a jellyfish, a frog, an organoid made of human neurons, a very simple AI that can feel something like pain. I'd rather have a principle that's a bit revisionary than one with a clause for every bug.
