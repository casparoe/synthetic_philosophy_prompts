I spent three decades as a research geologist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science and decision theory. A pattern from my career keeps troubling me, and I would appreciate a philosophical analysis of it.

During my last ten years, I served on the internal review board of a large geoscience institute. We evaluated research programs every few years against a set of metrics: publications in high-impact journals, grant revenue, media citations, and the like. I watched one earthquake-hazard modeling group change over time. Early on, they pursued the best possible representation of seismic risk, even when the results were messy or institutionally inconvenient. By the end, they had become superb at producing exactly what our review criteria rewarded. Their models grew slicker, their publication records stronger. But I gradually lost confidence that they were still aiming at the truth about earthquake hazards. Nothing they did was fraudulent or even negligent. They were simply responding to the incentives we gave them. And the more precisely we specified and rewarded good research, the more the actual research seemed to drift from its original purpose.

I have struggled to articulate what went wrong. It feels as though specifying what good work looks like, and then optimizing for that specification, can systematically undermine the thing the specification was meant to track. Is there a philosophical framework that captures this structural pattern? And if so, does it carry any practical lessons for how institutions should design evaluation procedures—or whether some forms of evaluation are inherently corrupting?
