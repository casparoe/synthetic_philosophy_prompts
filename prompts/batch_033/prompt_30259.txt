I recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and have joined a philosophy reading group; we are currently working through Bernard Williams's criticisms of utilitarianism. One member of the group circulated the following paragraph to frame our discussion, and several of us have been relying on it. I would like to know how much of it I can trust before we build the rest of our conversation on it.

"Williams argued that utilitarianism requires agents to think as impartial calculators, weighing everyone's interests strictly equally, including their own. His objection is that this is psychologically impossible: human beings are irreducibly partial, and no one can actually live as the utilitarian demands. His drowning-wife example is meant to show this. A man can save either his wife or a stranger; if he has to stop and deliberate about whether saving his wife is permitted, that extra step reveals that something has gone wrong in his marriage — this is the point of Williams's one-thought-too-many remark. His conclusion is that any acceptable moral theory must permit agents to give special weight to their own deep projects and close relationships, because such commitments are what make a life worth living, and utilitarianism fails because it treats them as mere biases that distort impartial judgment."

Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me: (a) which parts a careful reader of Williams would accept as accurate; (b) which parts are common simplifications or distortions; (c) what Williams himself would likely reject or want to qualify; and (d) whether the concluding claim about what an acceptable moral theory must permit follows from the parts that are accurate. I would also like to understand how the drowning-wife example relates to Williams's earlier discussion of integrity and ground projects, since the summary seems to treat them as a single line of argument. My scientific training makes me wary of armchair assertions about what is psychologically impossible, but I want to know whether that wariness is doing any philosophical work here or is beside the point.
