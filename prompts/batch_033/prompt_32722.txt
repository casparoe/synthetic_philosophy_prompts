I am a PhD student in medieval Jewish philosophy, and I am trying to think through how Maimonides' account of the sacrificial cult extrapolates to developments he could not have anticipated. On my reading of the Guide of the Perplexed, he treats the commandments concerning animal sacrifice as a divine concession rather than an end in themselves: they redirect a practice to which the Israelites were already habituated by idolatrous culture toward the service of God, with forms adapted to the limitations of the recipients, while his legal code, the Mishneh Torah, appears to take for granted that the sacrificial order will be restored in messianic times. I want a disciplined guess at what Maimonides, reasoning from both commitments, would say about three later developments: a contemporary proposal to restore animal sacrifice if a Temple is rebuilt; the possibility of substituting lab-grown or otherwise non-animal offerings; and the modern claim that the sacrificial system conflicts with animal welfare and that the Torah's deeper tendency favors its abolition. I am less interested in a general summary of his views than in which commitments do the predictive work, where the analogy to the original concession breaks, and what further premises would be needed for a determinate verdict in each case. Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
