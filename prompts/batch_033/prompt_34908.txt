I have spent the past few weeks trying to decide whether to vote in a local election in which my preferred candidate's margin will almost certainly be far larger than any single ballot, and a colleague walked me through an argument he called a case of evidential cooperation in large worlds. I now cannot either accept the argument or shake it. He gave it in numbered steps, approximately as follows.

(1) The universe is very large, and plausibly infinite; in any case it is large enough that for any decision I face, there exists an enormous population of agents in situations qualitatively identical to mine up to the moment of choice — the same evidence, the same values, the same deliberative dispositions.

(2) For any such population, my choice is evidence about what its members will do. Conditional on my voting, it is much more probable that they vote than that they abstain; conditional on my abstaining, the reverse.

(3) Expected value should be computed evidentially rather than causally: I should evaluate an act by how good the world is likely to be given that I perform it, and this includes what the act indicates about states of the world I cannot causally influence.

(4) From (2) and (3), the expected value of my voting is dominated not by the causal effect of my single ballot, which is negligible, but by what my voting indicates about the behavior of that vast population of similar agents.

(5) Therefore I should vote, even though my ballot is causally irrelevant to the outcome.

(6) But the argument cannot stop at voting. It applies with equal force to what I eat for breakfast, which of two nearly identical products I buy, or which route I take to work: in each case, if the universe is large, my choice is evidence about an enormous population of near-duplicates facing the same choice, so each of these choices carries enormous evidential stakes.

(7) That conclusion is absurd — and in a truly infinite universe, the relevant expected values seem to become infinite or undefined, leaving no usable theory of choice. Since (7) cannot be accepted, something in (1)–(6) must go.

I would most like to keep (5), because the voting case still strikes me as right, but I cannot see what blocks (6) that does not also block (5). Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In your answer, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on decision theory in large worlds, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
