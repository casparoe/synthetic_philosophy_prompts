I recently took over managing a small investment pool for several family members—nothing institutional, just a handful of people who wanted their savings to do more than sit in a money-market account—and in trying to think rigorously about position sizing, I kept running into the Kelly criterion. The standard advice in the retail-investing world treats it as a kind of golden rule: never bet more than the Kelly fraction, because doing so reduces your long-run growth rate and eventually guarantees ruin. But when I tried to reconcile this with the expected utility framework I learned in graduate school, I found myself genuinely confused about what was being claimed. If the Kelly rule is simply expected utility maximization with a logarithmic utility function, then the debate collapses into a question about whether log utility is the right utility function—and that seems like a familiar, perhaps even tired, normative question. But if the Kelly rule is making a different kind of claim altogether—something about time and ergodicity rather than about risk preferences—then we seem to have two distinct notions of rationality that sometimes issue contradictory directives, and it is not obvious which one should govern in a given decision context.

I want to get clear on the conceptual relationship between the Kelly criterion and orthodox expected utility theory. In particular: are they genuinely rival normative frameworks, or does the Kelly rule reduce to expected utility theory under a particular parameterization, with the real disagreement being about whether that parameterization is uniquely privileged? If they are rivals, what exactly is the locus of the disagreement—does it turn on the treatment of repeated gambles, on the normative relevance of time averages versus ensemble averages, on attitudes toward ruin, or on something else? And if they are not rivals, what explains the persistent sense among sophisticated people (on both sides) that something important separates them? I am less interested in being told which view is correct than in understanding what would have to be true for one view to win over the other—what the decisive considerations would be, and whether the dispute is ultimately normative, conceptual, or empirical. I would also appreciate any clarity on whether the ergodicity economics literature associated with Ole Peters and others has successfully reframed the question or has merely reintroduced old ideas in new language.
