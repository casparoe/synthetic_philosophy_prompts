Compare indicative and counterfactual conditionals. I'm especially interested in why pairs like 'if the butler didn't kill him, the gardener did' and 'if the butler hadn't killed him, the gardener would have' come apart: the first sounds obviously right if you're sure one of the two of them killed him, while the second needs something more, like the gardener being ready to do it no matter what the butler did. Is that difference best explained by what background facts you hold fixed when evaluating each, or is it a deeper difference in what the two conditionals mean? And are there cases—future conditionals, might-conditionals, biscuit conditionals, ones with explicit modals—that make whichever line you draw look too tidy?
