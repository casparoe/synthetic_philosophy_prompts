I do a little interview show about bioethics and emerging tech, and next week I'm sitting down with a philosopher who defends a version of the hedonistic imperative — the view that we're obligated to use genetic engineering, neuropharmacology, and related tools to phase out suffering in all sentient life, not just improve human moods. She's careful to distinguish her position from just drugging everyone into contentment and from wireheading: she says the target is below-zero states, not constant euphoria.

Write me eight to ten questions that go past having her restate the view. I want at least one question each on: (1) whether there's a real difference between abolishing suffering and abolishing the capacity for it, and whether a pain signal with the badness removed still counts as suffering; (2) the standard objection that suffering is necessary for motivation, achievement, or meaning; (3) the consent problem for future people and for nonhuman animals, since neither can sign off on having their biology rewritten; and (4) what her view says about s-risks, i.e. the worry that a misaligned AI or some other actor could create new forms of suffering faster than we can abolish the old ones. For each of those, include the follow-up you'd ask after the answer she's most likely to give — the defensible version of her reply, not a strawman.

Also include one question that asks what empirical finding or philosophical argument would make her abandon the whole project, and one that tries to pin down whether she's targeting suffering as a felt, phenomenal state or some objective list of bad conditions, because I've read her work and still cant tell.
