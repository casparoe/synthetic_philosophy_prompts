I'm grading a midterm in an upper-level decision theory course and could use a second read on one answer. The question was:

"State Good's theorem on the value of information, including its main assumptions, and give the standard proof sketch. Then explain what the theorem does and does not imply about (a) whether a rational agent should always acquire costless information and (b) whether they should be willing to pay some positive cost for information."

Here's the student's answer:

"Good's theorem says that for an expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization, the expected value of any cost-free piece of information is non-negative. The proof is basically that after seeing the information you can always just do what you would have done without it, so the information can't make you worse off in expectation; it just gives you the option to switch acts if the posterior favors a different one. So free information is weakly beneficial, and a rational agent should always take it when it's free. It also follows that you should be willing to pay something for any information, since its expected value is non-negative and normally positive; the only question is how much. And one nice corollary is that after you've seen a particular signal you'll never regret having looked, because your actual payoff with the information will be at least what it would have been without it."
