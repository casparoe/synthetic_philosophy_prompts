I've been going down a rabbit hole on decision theory lately — mostly because a friend of mine who works in AI alignment keeps going on about how important it is — and I hit something that's been bugging me for like a week now. It's about Good's theorem, the one that says the expected value of information is always non-negative. The idea is that a perfectly rational agent can't be made worse off in expectation by getting more information, because they can always just ignore it and do whatever they would've done anyway. That makes sense in the abstract.

But here's the thing: if that's true, doesn't it imply that a rational agent should basically never stop gathering information? Like, for any decision, there's always more you could learn — one more study, one more data point, one more conversation. And the theorem says that in expectation, that extra information can only help or at worst leave you neutral. So it seems like the optimal strategy is to just keep gathering information forever and never actually pull the trigger on a decision. Which is obviously absurd, but I can't figure out where the reasoning breaks. Is the theorem only meant to apply to a single, well-defined piece of information rather than an open-ended search? Or is there something about the cost of time that the theorem just doesn't model? My friend said something about "the theorem assumes the decision problem is fixed" but I'm not sure I understand why that matters.
