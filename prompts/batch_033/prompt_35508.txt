I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about reasons — specifically the whole internal vs. external reasons thing — by going off my gut reactions to cases rather than starting from a theory. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on metaethics and practical reason, and to think carefully about whether there's a coherent view behind my verdicts. Maybe start by brainstorming which features of the cases might be doing the work for me before jumping to a principle.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. Owen smokes two packs a day. He knows the health risks, he's heard all the arguments, but he genuinly doesn't care about living a long life. He's not depressed or irrational — he just values present pleasure over future health, reflectively and consistently. My gut says: Owen doesn't have a reason to quit. Or at least, not in the same way someone who wants to live long does; if you tell me he "has a reason," I want to know what work that's doing.

2. Maria promised to water her neighbor's plants while the neighbor is away. The neighbor was rude to her last week, and now Maria genuinely doesn't care about keeping the promise — she's lost all motivation. My gut says: Maria does have a reason to water the plants, even though she currently has no motivation to do so.

3. James was raised in an isolated community that practices ritual scarification on children. He's left the community as an adult but still believes, sincerely and after reflection, that the practice is fine — it's just what his people do. He has no children yet. My gut says: James has a reason not to scar his future children, even though nothing in his current motivational set points against it.

4. Claire is severely depressed. She wants nothing, cares about nothing, sees no point in anything. Her therapist says she has a reason to get out of bed and shower. My gut says: Claire does have a reason, but it feels different from Maria's — it depends on the fact that Claire would care about showering if she weren't depressed, in a way that Maria's reason doesn't seem to depend on what she'd care about if she weren't resentful.

5. A CEO can save money by dumping toxic waste into a river instead of paying for proper disposal. He's not sadistic — he doesn't enjoy harming people — but he has no sympathy for the downstream community he's never met, and he reflectively endorses maximizing shareholder value as his only obligation. My gut says: the CEO has a reason not to dump the waste, full stop, and it's not conditional on his motivations.

6. Here's the one that trips me up. Suppose someone is entirely selfish, has always been selfish, and after calm reflection sees no reason to be otherwise — but they're also perfectly cooperative when it benefits them, so they follow social rules instrumentally. They're not a monster, just constitutionally self-interested. Do they have a reason to act altruistically when no one's watching and there's no reputational cost? My gut wavers — sometimes I think yes, sometimes no, and I can't tell which verdict I actually endorse.

Is there a single account of reasons that captures verdicts 1 through 5, and what does it say about case 6? If no clean account gets all five, which verdict should I reconsider and why? I'm less interested in labels than in the precise principle that separates the cases where I feel the pull of external reasons from the ones where I don't.
