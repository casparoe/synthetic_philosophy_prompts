I have come to hold the following pair of claims, and I want to know how exposed they are. First, a question deserves to be called empirically answerable only if it is identifiable: there must be at least one method which, given an indefinitely long stream of all observations relevant to the question, is guaranteed to converge on the correct answer in the limit and not abandon it afterward. Second, a great many ontological questions are not identifiable in this sense. For questions about which unobservable entities, properties, or structures exist, the observations underdetermine the ontology: every possible stream of evidence is compatible with more than one candidate ontology, and no convergent method can separate them. I also draw the converse conclusion: where no such convergent method exists even in principle, the question is not merely unsettled but non-empirical, and it is a mistake to describe any scientific result as having answered it.

Tell me what would count against this view. I am not asking for the underdetermination debate as it is usually rehearsed; I want a map of the view's exposure. Begin from the question of what would have to be true for the view to be false, and then work through the defeaters — observations, formal results, episodes in the history of science, or philosophical arguments — sorted from decisive to merely awkward. For each, say whether a defender of the identifiability criterion would have to concede it as counting against the view or could dismiss it, and why. Distinguish challenges to the criterion itself from challenges to the claim that ontological questions typically fail it, since those are different places for the view to break. I would also like to know what the view predicts about particular cases that its main rivals do not, and an honest assessment of whether the view as I have stated it has been insulated against any possible counter-evidence — and, if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. I would personally find most persuasive a formal result showing that a class of ontological questions I would have classified as non-identifiable is in fact identifiable under assumptions no stronger than the ones I already accept.

Please write the answer in British English.
