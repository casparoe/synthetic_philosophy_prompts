Our reading group spent the last couple months on the moral testimony / moral deference literature, and I'm genuinely frustrated: we've all converged on the standard cases and the standard moves, but the two camps haven't budged. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not another survey of the papers.

Here's where we've landed. The pessimists about moral deference say there's something wrong with forming your moral beliefs by taking someone else's word for it, even when that person is a genuine moral expert. Depending on who you read, the defect is epistemic or moral: you can end up with the right moral conclusion but without any grip on why it's right, so the belief is idle or not really yours; or there's something off about outsourcing your conscience instead of thinking it through yourself. They lean on the contrast with non-moral testimony — it's fine to learn the boiling point of tungsten from a chemist, but if a moral expert tells you eating meat is wrong and you just believe them, something seems missing. The weirdness is supposed to be strongest in cases of "pure" moral deference, where you take on the first-order moral verdict without the reasoning behind it.

The optimists think this is overblown. If someone is reliably better than you at moral reasoning, refusing to update on what they say is just epistemic arrogance. We defer constantly in non-moral domains and nobody treats that as a problem; requiring everyone to rederive moral conclusions from scratch is absurd; and the "weirdness" of moral deference is, on their view, a psychological hangup or a contingent social norm rather than a real normative fact. In hard cases, they say, deference is often the most responsible thing you can do.

What's driving me crazy is that everyone in the group now agrees on the surface facts: there's an intuitive asymmetry between at least some moral and non-moral cases; deference can produce true moral beliefs; and understanding matters for something. Yet the pessimists think the optimists are explaining away a real defect, and the optimists think the pessimists have smuggled in an over-demanding picture of moral agency. Nobody has moved in months.

My working theory is that the debate is really three debates wearing one coat: (1) an epistemic debate about whether moral understanding is a necessary ingredient in justified moral belief, or just a nice bonus; (2) a moral debate about whether you have a standing duty to form your own moral views; and (3) a partly empirical debate about whether moral experts actually exist, how reliable they are, and whether deferring to them does any better than your own reasoning. But I'm not confident, and it might instead be a verbal disagreement about what "deference" or "testimony" is even supposed to include, or something else entirely.

So: what kind of disagreement is this — substantive, verbal, or several different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a study, a change of question? Please separate what's genuinely settled in this literature from what's still contested, and don't present one camp's reading as if it were the consensus.
