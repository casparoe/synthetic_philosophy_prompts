I've been thinking about thought experiments lately—specifically, which ones I find genuinely illuminating and which ones feel like they're pulling a fast one on me. Dennett called some of them "intuition pumps," which is a great phrase, but I think he mostly meant it as a criticism, and I find myself wanting a more nuanced take. Some thought experiments pump intuitions in a way that reveals something real, and others just generate a vivid illusion of insight. I've been going through a bunch of cases and tracking my own reactions, and I want to know if there's a principle that captures the pattern.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can give them:

1. **Thomson's violinist.** You wake up to find you've been hooked up to a famous violinist who needs your kidneys for nine months; the Society of Music Lovers kidnapped you for this purpose. I find this one genuinely illuminating. It isolates the question of whether the right to life includes the right to use someone else's body, and the answer it pushes me toward feels earned, not tricked. Legitimate.

2. **The experience machine (Nozick).** You can plug into a machine that gives you any experience you want, and you'll forget you ever plugged in. I find this one legitimate too, but I'll admit there's something about it that bothers me. The conclusion—that we care about more than just how our experiences feel from the inside—seems right, but the setup feels a bit like it's cheating by making the fake world obviously fake to the person considering the choice, even though inside the machine you wouldn't know. Still, legitimate overall.

3. **The Chinese room (Searle).** A monolingual English speaker sits in a room following a rulebook that tells him which Chinese characters to output in response to the Chinese characters he receives, and he gets good enough at it to fool a native speaker outside. Searle says obviously the man doesn't understand Chinese, so syntax isn't sufficient for semantics. But I can't shake the feeling that the intuition this generates depends entirely on zooming in on the man rather than the system as a whole. The room is designed to make you look at the wrong thing and then feel clever for noticing it doesn't understand. Misleading.

4. **Mary the color scientist (Jackson).** Mary knows all the physical facts about color and color vision but has spent her whole life in a black-and-white room. When she finally sees a red rose, she learns something new. I find this one legitimate. The setup is clean, the knowledge argument is clear, and I don't feel like anything is being smuggled in. Even if I'm not sure the conclusion is ultimately right, the thought experiment does honest work.

5. **The trolley problem (switch vs. push).** In the switch case, you divert a trolley from five people to one. In the push case, you push a large man off a bridge to stop the trolley. The switch case feels fine to me, but the push case feels like it's doing something underhanded. The physical contact and the direct personal force are being used to generate a moral distinction, but I'm not at all convinced those factors are morally relevant. The switch case is legitimate; the fat man variant feels manipulative.

6. **The swampman (Davidson).** A man is struck by lightning and disintegrated; by an astounding coincidence, a swamp gas molecule rearrangement produces a perfect replica with all the same dispositions. Is this replica the same person? Does it have the same thoughts? I find this one deeply misleading. The setup is so physically impossible that I can't get a grip on what my intuitions are even supposed to be tracking. It feels like asking whether a married bachelor would file taxes jointly.

7. **The teletransporter (Parfit).** You step into a machine on Earth that scans and destroys your body while a replica is assembled on Mars from new matter. I find this one legitimate. It isolates questions about what matters in survival in a way that feels clean and doesn't beg the question—or at least, the question it begs is the very one we're trying to answer.

8. **The original position (Rawls).** You choose principles of justice from behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing your place in society. I'm honestly not sure where to put this one. It doesn't feel like a trick exactly, but it also doesn't feel like it's isolating an intuition the way the others do. It feels more like a decision procedure being proposed as fair, and then we're asked whether we'd accept its results. Maybe it's a different kind of thing entirely, and I'm not sure my "legitimate/misleading" framework even applies to it.

So that's my list. I want to know: is there a principle (or a small set of principles) that captures which of these I find legitimate and which I find misleading? And if so, what would it say about a few I haven't settled on—like the brain in a vat, or the ring of Gyges, or Parfit's "future Tuesday indifference" case? I'm also open to the possibility that some of my verdicts are inconsistent and need to be revised. If the best principle forces me to change my mind about one or two of these, tell me which ones and why.

I'd like a committed answer—don't just lay out the options, take a position on what the right principle is and defend it.

For the format: I'd like the response structured as a JSON object with the following fields:

- "principle": a precise statement of the principle (or principles) you think best captures the pattern.
- "clauses": an array of objects, each with "clause" (one component of the principle) and "motivating_case" (which of my cases motivates that clause).
- "verdicts_on_my_cases": an array of objects, each with "case" (the case name), "my_verdict" (what I said), "principle_verdict" (what your principle says), and "match" (true if they agree, false if the principle overturns my verdict).
- "new_cases": an array of objects, each with "case" (a case I didn't list), "verdict" (what your principle says about it), and "rationale" (a one-sentence explanation).
- "overturned_verdicts": an array of which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up, with a brief "why" for each.
- "hardest_fit": which of my verdicts was hardest to accommodate and why.
