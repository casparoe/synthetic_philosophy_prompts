What is your credence that the paradox of phenomenal judgment constitutes a decisive refutation of epiphenomenalism about consciousness? By "the paradox of phenomenal judgment" I mean the following objection: if epiphenomenalism is true, then a person's conscious experiences exert no causal influence on any physical event, including the neural processes that produce their judgments and verbal reports about those experiences. But then the fact that someone sincerely judges "I am in pain" is explained entirely by physical causes, and the pain itself plays no role in producing that judgment. This seems to sever the connection between our conscious states and our beliefs about them in a way that makes those beliefs unjustified or unreliable. And since epiphenomenalism itself is typically motivated by introspective awareness of consciousness combined with physicalist arguments, the view appears to undercut its own evidential basis.

Please give a specific numerical credence and decompose the reasoning. Your decomposition should include at least the following sub-questions:

(1) What is the probability that the paradox correctly identifies a genuine epistemic defect — that is, that epiphenomenalism really does entail that our beliefs about our own conscious states are unjustified or unreliable, and that this is not merely a restatement of the view in tendentious terms?

(2) Conditional on the paradox identifying a genuine epistemic defect, what is the probability that epiphenomenalism has the resources to repair it without abandoning the core claim? (For instance: by arguing that the physical processes that cause phenomenal judgments can be reliably correlated with the phenomenal facts even if the phenomenal facts do not cause them; or by arguing that the demand for a causal connection between a belief and the fact it represents is too strong a requirement on justification.)

(3) Conditional on the paradox being forceful and unrepaired, what is the probability that the independent motivations for epiphenomenalism — such as the causal closure of the physical domain, the argument from the conceivability of zombies, or the difficulty of locating mental-to-physical causation within physics — are strong enough that the view should be retained anyway, with the paradox accepted as a bullet to bite?

Assign a rough probability to each sub-question with a line of justification, show how they combine to your overall credence, and identify which sub-question your credence is most sensitive to. Conclude by describing what evidence or argument would most shift your view on that sub-question.
