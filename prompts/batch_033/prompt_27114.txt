ok so in my history class we learned about the tuskegee syphilis study — the one where black men in macon county, alabama were told they had bad blood and got free meals and burial insurance while the doctors mostly just watched the disease get worse, even after penicillin was standard. people always say the study was wrong because the men didn't give valid consent, and i want to actually check whether that's true or whether consent is the wrong lens.

analyze the tuskegee study through the theory of what makes consent valid — the deception, coercion, competence stuff. map each one onto what actually happened: what did the men think they were agreeing to, and where does that fall short on each? were the free meals and burial money coercion, or an unfair inducement, or does the framework not have a good category for offers made to people with no good alternatives? and how should we think about competence here, since most of the men were poor sharecroppers with almost no medical care and no real way to learn what syphilis was — is that a competence problem or is the competence lens itself kind of condescending and missing the point?

then run it in the other direction: does the case show the consent framework explains what went wrong, or does it show the framework is too small — like the deeper wrong was about race and structural exploitation, and no amount of individual informed consent could have made the study okay? i want an honest account of where consent talk is doing real work and where it just isnt the right tool. and if i'm misdiagnosing the whole thing, say so plainly instead of answering the question as posed.
