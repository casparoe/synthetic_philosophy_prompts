I'm a retired geologist, and after forty years thinking about deep time I've recently fallen into philosophy — mostly the longtermist side of things, because it's the first stuff I've read that takes timescales seriously enough to make my old bones happy. The problem is a young man in our reading group (works in tech, very earnest) who ends half our arguments by saying "we live at the hinge of history," as if that settles the matter. Last week I pushed back on the idea of locking up part of my pension for a cause aimed at the far future, and he hit me with it again — we're at the hinge of history, so someone in my position shouldn't hesitate.

But I've been a scientist long enough to know that a sentence like that is doing an awful lot of unstated work. It could be almost vacuous — every generation probably feels pivotal — or it could be claiming something strong about the uniqueness of our moment, the number of lives at stake, the reversibility of what we do now, or the existence of a genuine one-shot window. I'd like to go back to the group with a clear head. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "we live at the hinge of history" might be making, from the trivial to the radical, and say for each wether it's plausible, false, or simply unverifiable? And which reading, if any, would actually support his conclusion that I ought to give now rather than later? Please write in American English.
