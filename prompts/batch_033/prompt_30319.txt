I write fiction for a living, and I have lately been trying to sort out what I actually think makes something a work of art. I've been going by my gut on particular cases, and I'd like to know whether there is a principle that captures my verdicts — or nearly all of them — and what that principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on.

My verdicts are these.

1. The novel I spent three years writing and revising is a work of art. I don't think it became art only when it was published, or only when a reader first opened it. It was already art when the manuscript sat in a drawer.
2. A friend once sent me a two-line text message, meant only as a quick update, and it happened to scan like a perfect haiku. I found it genuinely beautiful, but I don't think she wrote a poem. It isn't a work of art.
3. A sunset is not a work of art, however much it moves me.
4. A piece of driftwood lying where the tide left it is not a work of art.
5. That same piece of driftwood, carried into a gallery, set on a plinth, and given a title, is a work of art. I want to say the curator's selection and presentation changed what it is, not merely where it sits.
6. A technically superb painting made in the style of a seventeenth-century master and sold as a newly discovered work by that master is a work of art. It is a dishonest one, and I condemn the deception, but I can't bring myself to say it isn't art.
7. A child's crayon drawing of a house, taped to the refrigerator, is a work of art — minor art, perhaps, but art.
8. A wedding toast that moved the whole room to tears, delivered off the cuff and never intended as anything but a toast, is not a work of art, though it may have had more aesthetic power than most published verse.

The case I can't settle is this. A large language model, trained partly on my own published novels, produces a complete novel in my voice. I supply only a prompt; the machine chooses the words. I then edit the result lightly, give it a title, and publish it under my name. On some days I think this is obviously a work of art — I selected it, shaped it, and presented it as one, much as the curator did with the driftwood. On other days I think it is obviously not a work of art, because no one sat down to write it; the machine intended nothing, and my editing feels more like curation than authorship.

Is there a principle that gets all eight of my verdicts? What does it say about the ninth case? And which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to give up? If no single principle will do, I'd like the smallest set of principles that does the job, with an honest account of what each one commits me to.
