"The paradox of deontology is the problem that deontological moral theories seem to undermine themselves. Deontologists say that certain kinds of actions — like killing an innocent person — are strictly forbidden, no matter how much good might come from doing them. But why are they forbidden? Presumably because killing innocents is a terrible thing. The paradox is that if killing innocents is so terrible, then surely we should want there to be as few killings as possible. And if you can prevent five murders by committing one murder yourself, the very reason that makes murder wrong also tells you to do it. So deontology, which starts from the thought that we must take the badness of certain outcomes seriously, ends up prohibiting us from acting on that badness when it matters most. The standard deontological reply is to say that morality is agent-relative: what matters is not just how many killings happen, but whether *you* are the one doing the killing. But critics argue that this simply relocates the problem — if being a killer is a bad thing, then surely you should want there to be fewer killers in the world, and you can achieve that by killing one person to prevent five others from becoming killers. In the end, most deontologists simply accept that there are things one must not do even if the heavens fall, which makes deontology less a rational moral theory than a kind of secular faith."
