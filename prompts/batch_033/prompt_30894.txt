In my metaphysics seminar I keep reaching for the same analogy to make perdurantism feel obvious. I tell the students: picture a road running from town out to the coast. The road isn't wholly present at mile marker 40 — only that stretch of it is there, and the whole road is just the sum of all those stretches. Now a person, I say, is like that, only stretched through time instead of space. At any given moment you get a temporal slice of me — the Tuesday-afternoon slice — and the whole me is the sum of all those slices, the way the road is the sum of its segments. My endurantist colleague says I'm smuggling in the conclusion: the road picture only looks natural if you've already decided a person is a four-dimensional thing with temporal parts, and the view that I'm wholly present at each moment comes out looking silly only because roads aren't substances. I'd like to be fairer then that. Audit the road analogy for me. Give the explicit correspondence — what plays the role of the road, the mile markers, the segments, the whole route — then say where the mapping breaks, which features of a road the picture quietly imports that a persisting person might not have (or the reverse), and whether the conclusion that we have temporal parts survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the road is a bad analogy, tell me what a better one would be, or whether the perdurantist should stop leaning on analogies altogether.
