I'm taking a course on pragmatism and democratic theory this semester, and we've spent several weeks on Dewey's claim that democratic decision-making should be modeled on experimental scientific inquiry. The analogy, as our instructor presents it, runs something like this: just as scientists formulate hypotheses, test them against evidence, and revise their theories when results come in, democratic publics should treat policies as fallible hypotheses about what will serve the common good — implementing them provisionally, monitoring outcomes, and iterating. The payof is meant to be that democracy requires not just elections and rights but also robust experimental institutions: pilot programs, statistical agencies, feedback mechanisms, and a general willingness to treat no policy as final.

I find the analogy compelling, but I'm unsure how much work it can actually do, and I'd like a systematic audit. Start with the correspondences: what in a democratic system plays the role of the hypothesis, the controlled experiment, replication, the scientific community, peer review, and the norm of revising beliefs in response to evidence? Then identify where the mapping breaks down, focusing especially on the disanalogies that threaten the institutional conclusions the analogy is meant to license. A few trouble spots I already half-see: scientific communities share epistemic norms and agreed-upon criteria for what counts as discontinuation that democratic publics simply lack; the costs of failed experiments fall on actual people rather than on abstract "inquirers"; and value judgments — what counts as a good outcome — are themselves at stake in politics in a way they typically aren't in normal science. But I don't trust my own sense of which of these are surface-level mismatches and which cut deep. So: are any of these fatal to the analogy, or can it be repaired around them? And if the analogy can't bear the weight Dewey puts on it, is there a different analogy that better captures what he was reaching for?
