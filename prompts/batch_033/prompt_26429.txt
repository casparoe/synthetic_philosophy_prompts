I've been teaching my upper-division ethics seminar for going on twelve years now, and I've got to say, the unit on consent is starting to feel a little stale. We do the standard three-part framework — deception vitiates consent, coercion undermines voluntariness, competence is a precondition — and we run through the usual cases: medical consent forms, sexual consent on campus, maybe the Tuskegee study if I want to depress everyone. This year, on a whim, I assigned the Milgram obedience experiments as the case for the week, and it completely blew the discussion wide open in a way I wasn't expecting.

So here's what I want you to do. Analyze the Milgram experiments through the lens of the theory of valid consent. The participants did consent in some straightforward sense: they answered a newspaper ad, showed up, signed forms, and were told they'd be paid regardless. But the whole thing was built on a deception — they were told it was a study on memory and learning, not on obedience to authority. Then there's the coercion question: when subjects hesitated, the experimenter ran through those famous prods — "Please continue," "The experiment requires that you continue," "It is absolutely essential that you continue," "You have no other choice; you must go on." That last one sure sounds coercive, but the subjects had also been told upfront they could leave and still keep the money, which complicates things. And then competence: these were ordinary adults, no obvious deficits, but the situation was deliberately disorienting — fake screams, a guy in a lab coat, escalating voltage dials — and plenty of subjects showed signs of genuine distress and confusion about what they were even being asked to do.

Walk me through each pillar — deception, coercion, competence — and say whether consent was valid on each dimension according to the standard theory, and also where the standard theory seems to miss something important about what was actually happening in that lab. Then flip the lens: what does the Milgram case reveal about the adequacy of the three-pillar framework itself? Does it point to a condition we're missing (something about situational pressure, or about the way authority scrambles voluntariness without exactly coercing), or does it show that one of the existing conditions needs to be reconceptualized? I'm not interested in the IRB reform stuff — that's been done to death. I want the philosophical paydirt: does this case break the framework, or does the framework handle it fine once you're careful enough?
