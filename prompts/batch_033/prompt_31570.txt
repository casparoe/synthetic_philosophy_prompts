Colleagues in my department who teach the free energy principle often introduce it by saying that the brain is like a scientist: it maintains hypotheses about the hidden causes of its sensory input, tests them against incoming signals, and revises them when prediction error accumulates; action, on this picture, is the scientist running an experiment to sample better data. The analogy is then used to license a substantive conclusion — that perception is inferential through and through, and that hallucinations and delusions are not failures of a separate perceptual faculty but extreme instances of the very same predictive process. I want you to audit this analogy. Give the correspondence table explicitly (hypotheses, experiments, data, falsification, the scientific community, and whatever else carries over), distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not, and then take a committed position: once the disanalogies are made explicit, does the conclusion survive, or was the analogy doing the argumentative work all along? If it fails, say what a better analogy would be, or whether the inferentialist conclusion should be defended without one.
