Take the 2016 Brexit referendum and analyze it as a test case for the epistocracy-vs-democracy debate. I know the standard facts: leave won by about 52/48 on high turnout, the ballot was a single binary choice on what turned out to be a sprawling and hard-to-reverse constitutional change, and the campaign was full of false or misleading claims. What I want is the two-way analysis. First, from the epistocrat side: should this kind of decision have gone to a popular vote at all, and if so, what does the epistocrat say about the franchise, weighting, or veto points—who counts as competent, and on what subject matter? Where does the campaign actually strengthen the epistocratic worry (low factual knowledge, misinformation, a choice that bundles trade, migration, sovereignty, and Northern Ireland into one yes/no), and where does it push back (the decision was also about values, identity, and who gets to rule, not just a factual estimate)? Then run it the other direction: does Brexit make epistocracy more attractive, less attractive, or show that the debate was never really built for cases like this? I want the mapping kept honest—who is the expert, what were the actual options, what does doing better mean here—and I want you to say explicitly where the theory is silent instead of making it speak.
