I'm an engineer at a small AI lab, and I've been asked to bolt a "low-impact" regularizer onto an agent we're building — an LLM agent that can read and edit files, send mail, spin up compute jobs, that sort of thing. The pitch was that even without a full spec of what we want, we can at least penalize the agent for changing the world beyond what the task requires. I tried the obvious version first: penalize any change to any variable outside a whitelist. That fell apart immediately, because the whitelist is basically just the intended effects, and writing the whitelist turns out to be most of the work. Then a colleague walked me through the following argument, and I can't find the exit.

(1) To call some change a side effect is to say it's an effect that isn't among the intended ones. (2) So any measure of side effects has to draw a boundary between intended effects and everything else. (3) Drawing that boundary requires a specification of the task rich enough to classify arbitrary changes as "part of the job" or "not part of the job." (4) But a task specification that can classify arbitrary changes is, for practical purposes, a full specification of what we want the agent to do in that environment — which is exactly the value-specification problem low impact was supposed to let us skip. (5) The only alternative is to define side effect without any notion of intention: pick some variables and penalize changes to them. But then the choice of variables is arbitrary unless we already have a story about why those variables matter. (6) An arbitrary choice has no normative force — "don't change this directory" is just a preference about that directory, not a general constraint. (7) So low-impact measures either secretly require a full specification of value, or they're just arbitrary constraints dressed up as a principle. Either way, "avoid side effects" can't be defined without full value spec.

I'm fairly sure this proves too much, since people in alignment do talk about attainable utility preservation and relative reachability as if they get a real constraint without solving the whole value problem, and my colleague says those are just value-laden choices of baseline and variables with extra math on top. I'd like to keep the idea that a meaningful low-impact constraint is possible without a complete utility function, but I don't know what I'm paying for it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
