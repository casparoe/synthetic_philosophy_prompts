I am giving a talk next month to a university workshop on rationality and strategic choice, defending superrationality as a norm of individual choice in symmetric one-shot games. My thesis, stated plainly, is this: when equally rational agents face a symmetric one-shot game and each knows the payoff matrix and the others' rationality, a rational agent should choose the action whose universal adoption by rational agents in that situation yields the best outcome — even when that action is strictly dominated by another action. In the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, superrationality therefore prescribes cooperation, because the only outcomes compatible with symmetric reasoning are the diagonal ones, (Cooperate, Cooperate) and (Defect, Defect), and the former is better for both. I distinguish superrationality from evidential decision theory — my argument does not treat my choice as evidence about the other player's choice — and from Kantian universalization — my argument selects among feasible diagonal outcomes rather than testing maxims.

The audience is the problem. The workshop is run by mainstream economists and decision theorists. Most of them are expected-utility maximizers with well-defined subjective probabilities; they regard the sure-thing principle and dominance reasoning as non-negotiable; they think of Nash equilibrium as the natural solution concept for strategic interaction; and they have at best a second-hand, unsympathetic sense of what superrationality is supposed to add. There are also two evolutionary game theorists in the room, one of whom works on indirect reciprocity and will want to know what a population of superrational agents looks like under selection, and a philosopher of probability who will press on what, exactly, licenses the symmetry assumption.

Write my Q&A prep sheet for this talk. It should contain the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way these people would phrase it — in the vocabulary of expected utility, dominance, equilibrium, and correlated strategies, not in Hofstadter's or LessWrong's vocabulary. For each objection, give a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front to keep credibility, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
