I'm in a graduate seminar on consciousness, and the last few weeks have been a deep dive into higher-order theories — Rosenthal's higher-order thought (HOT) theory, Lycan's higher-order perception (HOP) variant, and the self-representational alternative that's meant to fix some of their problems. The unifying claim is that a mental state becomes conscious only by being the target of some kind of higher-order representation. It's an elegant idea, but I've been struck by how differently the literature treats blindsight depending on which theory the author happens to be defending. For HOT theorists it's often a poster child; for global workspace people it's a mess they claim to handle better. I want a careful, honest treatment that doesn't just cherry-pick the features of blindsight that make a favored theory look good.

Please analyze blindsight — the phenomenon, first characterized in patients like DB and GY, in which subjects with V1 lesions can perform above-chance visual discriminations (motion direction, location, simple shape) in their scotoma while sincerely reporting no visual experience at all — as a case study for higher-order theories of consciousness. Do this in both directions. First, let the theory illuminate the case: what does a higher-order theory say is happening in blindsight, which of its features does it explain naturally, and which features require epicycles or are simply mysterious on the theory? Second, let the case test the theory: does blindsight confirm higher-order approaches, challenge them, or force distinctions the original theories didn't anticipate? Be specific about the variants — HOT and HOP may fare differently here, and the self-representational view may have its own distinct story.

Please address at least the following:

- The distinction between type-1 blindsight (no reported awareness of any kind) and type-2 blindsight (a non-visual "feeling" that something is there, sometimes called blindsight with residual awareness). Do higher-order theories handle both cleanly, or does the type-2 case muddy the picture in ways the theory didn't foresee?
- Whether higher-order theories genuinely predict blindsight — that is, whether the phenomenon falls out of the theory's architecture — or merely accommodate it after the fact by positing a missing higher-order representation whenever consciousness happens to be absent, with no independent grip on when such a representation should or shouldn't be present.
- The awkward finding that blindsight subjects show above-chance metacognitive sensitivity to their own performance (for instance, appropriate confidence ratings or post-decision wagering on their "guesses"). This looks like a higher-order state about a visual state — exactly the kind of thing HOT theory says is sufficient for consciousness — yet it occurs without conscious visual experience. What does the theory do with this? Does it need to distinguish kinds of higher-order states, and if so, does that distinction collapse into something the theory's rivals already say?
- How rival theories handle the same data: at least first-order representational theories, global workspace theory, and integrated information theory. Where do higher-order theories have a genuine explanatory edge over these alternatives, and where do they not?
