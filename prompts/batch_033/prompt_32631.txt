I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting a novel about a society that has outsourced not just memory and calculation to AI systems but also taste, judgment, and the slow work of making sense of things. I keep returning to E.M. Forster's "The Machine Stops" (1909) — the story of a civilization living entirely through a vast automated system that provides all comfort, mediates all communication, and supplies all knowledge, and whose inhabitants no longer understand how anything works, cannot repair what they depend on, and have forgotten that there was ever an alternative. I'd like a reading of the story through the lens of epistemic dependence on AI and the erosion of understanding. Where does Forster's Machine genuinely anticipate the ways that reliance on AI systems might atrophy our capacities for understanding, and where does the story misdescribe the problem because it was written before the specific shape of contemporary machine learning? What does the story dramatize about epistemic erosion that the current philosophical literature misses? I'm not after a bibliography or references — I want a close, sustained engagement with the story and the idea, written in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, or formatting of any kind.
