I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and a policy school has asked me to give a lunch seminar defending a version of strong longtermism. The room will be mostly development economists and global health researchers — the kind of crowd that thinks the careful way to do good is comparing RCT-backed interventions, bednets versus cash transfers — plus a couple of climate policy modelers. I'm not a philosopher by training and I know their going to be polite but sharp.

The thesis I'm defending is roughly this: if you think future people count at all, then the expected value of what we do now is dominated by its effects on the far future, because the number of lives at stake over the long run is enormous; so our top moral priority should be to improve the odds that humanity's long-run trajectory goes well, and in particular to cut risks from technologies that could permanently end or lock in that trajectory. My argument in the talk, compressed: (1) an impartial welfarist starting point, wellbeing matters wherever and whenever it occurs; (2) if the future is big, expected-value reasoning puts almost all the weight on far-future outcomes; (3) some present actions plausibly shift the long-run trajectory; (4) so those actions should dominate our priorities.

These are not people who will object to the metaphysics. They'll object to the population ethics, the discounting, the cluelessness, and the speculation-to-action pipeline. They care about measurable counterfactual impact, they're allergic to anything that smells like Pascal's mugging or infinity-minus-infinity reasoning, and at least some of them suspect the whole thing is tech-money ideology wearing a utility function.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet, not an essay defending the view. I want: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, not the ones I'd get from an effective-altruism crowd, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do; each one phrased the way a development economist or climate modeler would actually put it; for each, a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest; what I should concede up front so I don't spend the hour defending things I don't need to defend; the one reply that has to land for the talk to survive; and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
