I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about supererogation — acts that are morally good but not required, the stuff that goes beyond the call of duty. The standard picture has a floor (duty) and a ceiling (the supererogatory), but my intuitive verdicts on cases keep pulling me in different directions, and I suspect at least some of them can't all be right together. I'd like help extracting the principle I seem to be operating under, or finding the best one I can commit to.

Here are the cases and my gut reactions:

1. **The drowning child (pond).** You walk past a shallow pond and see a child drowning. You could wade in and save her, ruining your $200 shoes. Saving her is obligatory, not supererogatory. I'm with Singer on this one — the cost is trivial relative to the harm prevented.

2. **The drowning child (distant).** You receive a letter from a reputable charity asking for $200 to save a child from dying of malaria. I feel a strong pull to say this is also obligatory, but I confess it doesn't *feel* the same as the pond case — the child is far away, the causal chain is long, and there's something psychologically different about it. Still, I want to say it's obligatory, and the difference in felt urgency is just scope insensitivity I should correct for rather than a real moral difference.

3. **The kidney donation.** A healthy acquaintance of yours needs a kidney. You're a match. The surgery has a roughly 1-in-4,000 mortality risk and involves a few weeks of painful recovery. Donating is clearly supererogatory — it's heroic, not required. No one would say you *had* to do it, and someone who declined would owe no apology.

4. **The second kidney.** Same as above, but you've already donated one kidney years ago. Donating your remaining kidney would put you on dialysis for life. This is so far beyond duty that calling it merely supererogatory almost feels like an understatement. Definitely not required.

5. **The life's work.** A talented biomedical researcher spends her career developing a malaria vaccine that ends up saving hundreds of thousands of lives, working long hours for academic pay when she could have had a comfortable industry job. I want to say her choice was supererogatory — deeply admirable but not required. But then I think: if case 2 (sending $200) is obligatory, how can devoting your whole career to saving vastly more lives be merely optional? That seems like a consistency problem.

6. **The soldier and the grenade.** A soldier throws himself on a grenade to save five comrades, dying instantly. I want to say this is supererogatory — the paradigm case, really. But I also think that if you *can* save five lives at the cost of your own, and the five are right there in front of you, there's at least a case that you're required to do it. And yet I can't bring myself to say the soldier who freezes up has done something *wrong* — "wrong" feels like the wrong word for someone who merely fails to be a hero.

7. **The small kindness.** You're having a terrible day, but you still take a moment to genuinely compliment a cashier who looks exhausted, making her visibly brighten. This cost you nothing but a few seconds of effort you barely had. I want to say it's supererogatory — it's a little spark of moral grace — but it's so trivially easy that calling it beyond duty feels absurd. If something costs nothing and helps someone, how could it not be required? And yet "required" also feels too strong — are we really *obligated* to compliment every exhausted cashier we encounter?

I have a tentative principle I've been kicking around: an act is supererogatory if and only if it (a) produces more good than any alternative act the agent could permissibly perform, and (b) the cost to the agent — in well-being, autonomy, or life-plans — crosses some threshold of significant sacrifice. But this principle handles cases 3 and 4 smoothly while struggling with 5 (is a fulfilling career a "significant sacrifice" if it's freely chosen and meaningful?) and making case 7 obligatory, which doesn't match my sense that the compliment was a gift rather than a debt. It also says nothing about the tension between cases 2 and 5.

What I'm asking for: is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? I'm looking for something stated precisely enough to test against new cases. Please tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and why those are the ones that should go. Also, give me a few new cases I haven't considered and tell me what the principle says about them — ideally cases that stress-test the boundary between duty and supererogation in ways my list doesn't.
