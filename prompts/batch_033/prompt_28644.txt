I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and a source I talked to last month — someone who works on decision theory and the foundations of AI — left me with an argument I can't get out of my head. I've recontructed it as faithfully as I can, and I'm hoping someone can tell me where it goes wrong, because the conclusion feels absurd.

(1) A rational agent's degrees of belief, or credences, ought to obey the probability axioms. Otherwise the agent is vulnerable to a Dutch book: a bundle of bets, each of which looks fair to the agent, that together guarentee a loss.

(2) The axioms force the agent to give probability 1 to every logical truth. If T is a tautology then not-T is impossible, and a coherent distribution can't leave any mass on an impossible outcome.

(3) Certainty is closed under modus ponens and under conjunction: if the agent is certain of A and certain of C, coherence forces certainty of "A and C"; and if the agent is certain of A and certain of "if A then B," coherence forces certainty of B.

(4) Now take a finitely axiomatized theory strong enough to do arithmetic that the agent accepts — a short list of mathematical claims it treats as certain. The agent assigns probability 1 to each axiom.

(5) For any theorem B of that theory, the conditional "if the axioms, then B" is itself a logical truth. That's just what it is for B to follow from the axioms.

(6) So by (2) and (3), the agent must be certain of B. Since B was arbitrary, a rational agent who accepts the theory is already certain of every theorem of the theory.

(7) But no actual agent is like that. There are theorems whose shortest proofs are far too long for any human or machine to have worked through, and there's no decision procedure that separates the theory's theorems from its non-theorems. A mathematician who hasn't proved some obscure theorem isn't irrational for that.

(8) So the standard theory of credences seems to entail that every real agent is irrational. That can't be the theory we want — or if it is, it's a theory that gives no guidance to the kind of minds that actually exist.

I've heard two reactions. One is that the whole thing turns on treating an abstract credence function as if it were something a mind actually computes. The other is that we should just accept that real agents are irrational by the ideal's lights, and that the interesting question is how to approximate the ideal — which is supposedly what "logical induction" is about. I'd rather keep (1) than give up on the idea that actual agents can be rational, but I'm not sure what keeping (1) costs. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
