I spent my career as a research scientist, in a field where disagreements were ultimately settled by measurement, and since retiring I have begun reading philosophy in earnest. My reading group has been working through Hume's essay on the standard of taste. At our last meeting, when I pressed the group on whether one interpretation of a novel could be better than another, a fellow member closed the discussion with "beauty is in the eye of the beholder," and the room treated that as decisive. I am too new to the subject to know exactly what that sentence commits a person to, and I suspect it is doing several different jobs at once.

Please act as a professional philosopher working in aesthetics. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the distinct precise claims that the slogan "beauty is in the eye of the beholder" could be used to assert — separating, at a minimum, claims about whether beauty is a property of the object, claims about whether judgments of taste can be true or false, and claims about whether anyone is ever in a position to correct anyone else's taste — and ranging from readings so weak they are nearly trivial to readings strong enough to be radical or revisionary. Then present these readings as a list of clearly formulated theses, and for each one give a verdict: whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, with a brief explanation. Finally, address the two things I actually need: which of these readings, if any, would actually support using the slogan to shut down my reading-group debate about whether one interpretation of a novel is better than another; and which of them Hume's standard of taste should be understood as accepting or rejecting. Do not stop at a single interpretation; the point is the menu of readings and the differences between them.
