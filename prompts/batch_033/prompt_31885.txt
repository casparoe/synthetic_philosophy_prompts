I was talking to a friend who's deep into the LessWrong rationalist community, and he tried to explain two related decision-theory puzzles to me — "counterfactual mugging" and "XOR blackmail." I'm a software engineer, so I'm used to thinking about decision procedures and algorithmic reasoning, but something about his summary didn't quite sit right, and I can't put my finger on what.

Here's roughly what he said:

"Both puzzles involve Omega, a superintelligent AI that can perfectly predict your behavior. In counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin. If it comes up heads, Omega gives you $10,000 — but only if Omega predicted that you would have agreed to pay $100 if the coin had come up tails. If it comes up tails, Omega simply asks you to pay $100. You know all this in advance. The coin lands tails, and Omega asks you for $100. You should pay. The reasoning is that Omega already ran its prediction before the coin flip, so if you refuse to pay, that means Omega predicted you would refuse, and you missed out on the $10,000 in the heads branch. By paying, you prove you're the kind of agent who gets the $10,000 when heads comes up. It's the same logic as Newcomb's problem: you should one-box, and you should pay.

XOR blackmail makes the same point even more starkly. Omega sends you a message saying: 'I have sent you this message if and only if your house is infested with termites XOR you will pay me $1,000.' A termite infestation costs $1,000,000 to fix. You receive the message. Now, paying Omega $1,000 doesn't causally prevent termites — the termites are either already there or they aren't. But if you pay, then by the logic of the XOR, the message must have been sent because you're a payer, not because you have termites. So paying is evidence that you don't have termites, and since the cost of paying is far less than the cost of termites, you should pay. Both puzzles show that causal decision theory is wrong and that you need to reason about what your decision reveals about the world, not just what it causally affects."

I get the surface structure of both arguments, but I'm suspicious. In the counterfactual mugging case, I already know the coin landed tails — I'm in the tails world, I didn't get $10,000, and paying $100 now seems like throwing good money after bad. And in the XOR blackmail case, the idea that paying "proves" I don't have termites sounds like it's confusing correlation with causation, or maybe treating a decision as if it's evidence about a fact that's already settled in the past. But my friend insists these are standard arguments that most decision theorists accept, and that they refute causal decision theory.

Is his summary of these puzzles and their significance fair? I'd love a point-by-point breakdown of what's accurate, what's subtly wrong or oversimplified, and whether the conclusions he draws — that you should pay in both cases, and that causal decision theory is refuted — actually follow from the setups as he described them.
