The debate about moral luck has been running since Nagel and Williams framed it in the 1970s, and what puzzles me is not that it's unresolved but that it seems to have no clear mechanism for resolution. Take the standard pair of cases: two equally negligent drivers, one of whom hits a child while the other does not, where the difference between them is entirely a matter of luck. One side finds it obvious that the drivers are not equally blameworthy — the outcome matters, and our reactive attitudes track it. The other side finds it equally obvious that blame should answer only to what was under the agent's control, and since luck is by definition outside control, it cannot make one driver more blameworthy than the other. Both sides grant the same facts about the cases. Both grant that control matters for responsibility. Both grant that our actual practices of blame are sensitive to outcomes. And yet they reach opposite verdicts, and decades of refinement — distinguishing resultant luck from circumstantial luck from constitutive luck, appealing to different senses of "blame," invoking the function of blame — don't seem to have moved anyone. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a survey of the literature. Is it a clash of bedrock intuitions where neither side can argue without begging the question? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "blame" that could be dissolved by distinguishing its senses? Is there a hidden empirical question — about the consequences of blaming differently, or about the psychological impossibility of withholding outcome-sensitive blame — that could settle it? Or are the two sides answering different normative questions under the same heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a distinction, an empirical finding, a reframing of the question, or something else? Be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the point you are least sure of.
