I'm a hydrologist at a municipal water utility, and a few times a year I have to write sentences like "the annual probability of the West Fork reservoir running dry is 0.4%" or "there is a 1-in-500 chance this levee overtops in the next decade." The board uses those numbers to decide whether to spend nine figures on a new spillway or a pipeline and they have to sign off on the exact wording, so the numbers matter, but I was trained as an engineer and I keep tripping over the fact that the word "probability" is doing different work in different sentences. Sometimes we have ninety years of streamflow records, sometimes we're talking about a structure that has never failed and will never get a second run, and sometimes the number is basically three senior engineers in a room agreeing on a feeling. I'd like you to write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the frequentist / subjectivist / propensity distinction, that tells me, for any probability claim I have to make, which interpretation I'm entitled to use, what the resulting number actually licenses me to say in the document, and when I should refuse to put a number in at all. Make it a checklist I can actually run in a day or too, with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a technical one. Then run it on three of my recurring cases: (1) the annual probability that the river drops below a level we've seen many times in the gauge record, (2) the probability that a brand-new dam fails by internal erosion in its first fifty years, and (3) the probability that a once-a-century drought lasts more than five years. Give me the committed version: tell me which interpretation wins in each case and why, don't just lay out the menu. And be straight about where the procedure will misfire or where the three-way distinction itself is doing more work than it can actually bear.
