I have been trying to pin down an argument I keep encountering, and I would like to know whether the argument, or the particular gap I have noticed in it, has an established name.

The argument runs like this. In special relativity, two observers in relative motion can disagree about which distant events are simultaneous with a given moment, and neither of them is wrong. Take a flare on a star eight light-years away: an event that, in my frame of reference, occurs next year. An astronaut passing Earth at the appropriate speed can, without any error, regard that very flare as occurring right now. If what is happening now is what exists, and the astronaut's "now" is as legitimate as mine, then the flare already exists; an event in my future is already real. The same move can be repeated for one future event after another.

But the argument seems to work only one event at a time. The astronaut whose "now" includes next year's flare on that star is not the same observer whose "now" includes the flare I date to the year after; the two require different velocities, and no single inertial observer's "now" contains my entire future at once. The conclusion therefore seems to require stitching together many incompatible "nows" into a single collection and calling all of it real. That collection is no one's present. If "real" means only "real for some observer or other," the argument proves something weaker than advertised; if "real" means "real simpliciter," then the decisive step no longer seems to depend on relativity.

Does this argument have a name? Does the gap I am describing have a name? And what exactly is the relationship between the argument's conclusion and the stronger claim that the future in general is already fixed? I would not be satisfied with a bare label. I want to know how the argument is standardly reconstructed, which premise a defender of an unfixed future should reject, and whether the gap I have described coincides with one of the standard objections or is something distinct.
