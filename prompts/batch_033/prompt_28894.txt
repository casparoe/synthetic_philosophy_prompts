I have been working through some recent arguments in animal ethics and in debates about the moral status of artificial systems, and I keep tripping over a move for which I have no name, though I would be surprised if it had not been noticed before. In a typical instance, someone claims that a whale deserves far more concern than a mouse because its brain is so much larger. Pressed on this, they immediately qualify: they do not mean raw size, of course, but the number of neurons, or brain size relative to body size, or some underlying cognitive sophistication — the size is only a rough indicator. Yet a few pages later the measurable quantity is doing all the work again: the moral ranking is being read straight off neuron counts or relative brain size, and the supposedly deeper property is left vague enough that it never disagrees with the measurement. I have seen the same pattern in discussions of artificial systems, where parameter counts or benchmark scores are treated as if they tracked how much a system could suffer; the reply to any objection is that the benchmark is merely a proxy, and then the proxy continues to dictate the verdicts.

What I am after is not just a label but a precise carving of the phenomenon. Is there an established term for this slide between an avowed moral criterion and a measurable stand-in, where the stand-in quietly ends up doing the normative work while the criterion stays conveniently unspecified? How exactly does it differ from an ordinary operational definition, from the substantive position that the measurable feature — the size, the neuron count, the benchmark score — is itself what grounds moral standing rather than a mere indicator of something deeper, and from the familiar charge that such comparisons are speciesist or otherwise chauvinistic? And is there a defensible version of the move, a way of leaning on a measurable proxy under uncertainty that is not simply a confusion, or is the slide always a mistake?
