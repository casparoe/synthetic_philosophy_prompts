I work for a national psychiatric association, on the committee that reviews proposals to revise our diagnostic manual. Our official position is that diagnostic categories are descriptive and provisional: they are tools for communication and research, not discoveries about the deep structure of mental disorder. Yet the system around us treats the categories as if they picked out stable kinds of person. Researchers recruit people diagnosed with X and exclude everyone else; insurers and hospitals reimburse by code; treatment guidelines and disability determinations are written per diagnosis; and a revision of a category is publicly treated as a finding about what the disorder really is. At the same time, we know that receiving a diagnosis changes people: they reinterpret their history through the label, their self-expectations and behavior shift, patient communities organize around the category, and those communities press for changes to the criteria. I have heard this described as a looping effect: the classification acts on the people classified, and the people act back on the classification.

I am trying to understand what our practice actually presupposes about the nature of mental disorder. Does using categories as objects of research, reimbursement, and discovery commit us to the view that they are natural kinds — groupings that exist in nature independently of our classification, the way chemical elements or infectious diseases do? Or can the whole system run on a purely pragmatic view, on which the categories are convenient labels with no deeper claim about what is out there? Reconstruct the implicit theory of classification that our practice presupposes, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several different theories would license the same arrangements. Then tell me what would have to change in the manual, in research design, and in reimbursement if we took the looping effect to be the normal case rather than an awkward complication, and which of our current practices could survive that change unchanged.
