I'm in a philosophy of mind course and we've been doing Parfit-style cases on personal identity, and I've got an argument that seems to prove to much and I can't find the bad step. (1) If you replace a single neuron in my brain with a silicon part that has exactly the same input-output behavior, I survive the operation. (2) If I survive any one such replacement, then I survive each step in a long series of them, since every step is the same operation performed on a person who just survived the previous step. (3) So I would survive complete gradual replacement of my neurons; the resulting uploaded person is me. (4) Now imagine instead that my brain is scanned and destroyed while a perfect functional duplicate is constructed from the scan. The duplicate is exactly the sort of person the gradual process ends with, so by (3) the duplicate is me. (5) But if the scan-and-copy machine is run without destroying the original, the duplicate is no less exactly that sort of person, so by the same reasoning the duplicate is me while the original is also me. (6) That can't be right: I can't be two people at once. So the argument from (1)-(3) has to go wrong somewhere. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd most like to keep (1) and (5), but I can't see how to hang onto both.
