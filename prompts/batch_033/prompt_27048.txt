So I've been rereading the sequences (third time through, don't judge) and I keep getting stuck on the same thing. The standard rationalist line on the St. Petersburg paradox is that it's not really a paradox — you just bound the utility function, done, move on. This is basically Eliezer's take, and it's also more or less what Bernoulli said 300 years ago with diminishing marginal utility. But the more I sit with it, the more this feels like a philosophical sleight of hand. You're not explaining *why* the paradox arises; you're just declaring by fiat that the thing generating the paradox can't happen. And the weird thing is, a lot of the same people who are totally comfortable bounding utility to dissolve St. Petersburg get really squirmy about the exact same move when it shows up in Pascal's-wager-type situations where the infinity is in the probability rather than the payoff. It feels inconsistent.

Anyway, I want to see the strongest possible case made for a position that I don't think gets a fair hearing in rationalist spaces: that the St. Petersburg paradox is a *real* and unresolved problem for expected utility theory, not just a cute puzzle with a tidy resolution, and that we should take seriously the possibility that expected utility maximization needs to be replaced or at least substantially revised — not just patched with bounded utility and called a day. I'm thinking of approaches like risk-weighted expected utility, or views that reject the idea that rational preferences over gambles have to be representable by real-valued expectations at all, or maybe something weirder. The target is the claim that the paradox is basically solved and doesn't warrant further attention.

Give me exactly five arguments for this position, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. I want them punchy — the kind of thing I could drop into a group chat and actually make people pause instead of just linking to the wiki.
