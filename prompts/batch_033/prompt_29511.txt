Something's been bugging me about how the same diagnosis can do two opposite jobs. A defendant found not guilty by reason of insanity is excused because their mental illness supposedly undermined their control or grip on reality. But civil commitment can then lock that same person up on the grounds that the illness makes them dangerous, sometimes for longer than the criminal sentence would have been. The illness is treated as both the thing that removes blame and the thing that justifies confinement. What theory of mental disorder does this pair of practices presuppose? Does it require thinking mental illness is a real, disease-like condition that can strip agency while still making your behavior predictable enough to be dangerous, or is the practice compatible with a Szasz-style view on which mental illness is a myth and these are just problems in living? Reconstruct the implicit theory, seperate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits with, say whether the criminal and civil sides presuppose consistent things, and tell me what would have to change in the law if the myth view were adopted.
