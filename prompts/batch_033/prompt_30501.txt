I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of probability seminar — roughly 18 students, one 75-minute session — and the students are comfortable with Bayesian updating and conditional probabilities. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the force of the Principal Principle, the thesis that a rational credence should defer to a known objective chance, and that makes them discover, before it is named, where that deference should stop. I want the game built around a genuinely chancy device with an announced chance (say 0.7) and small-stakes bets using points rather than money, with successive conditions in which participants receive further information: information about the device's calibration, information about the already-determined outcome, and a messier middle case such as a fallible but reliable predictor of the outcome. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that their betting behavior visibly tracks the chance in some conditions and departs from it in others, so that the admissibility problem surfaces in what they do rather than in a lecture afterwards. Give me exact rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened in each condition onto the distinction between admissible and inadmissible evidence; say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to fix it; and be explicit about which feature of the Principle the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, undermining information about the chances themselves — and would have to be supplied by hand. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about this design: which fact about how students actually behave, or which objection to the setup, would lead you to redesign it, and where your reasoning about the activity is most likely to fail.
