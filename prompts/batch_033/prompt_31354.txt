I retired from a career in geochemistry a few years ago and have been attending a philosophy discussion group at the local university. Last week two regulars got into a protracted argument that none of us could resolve, and I'd value a careful adjudication.

The topic was whether reasons can exist without reasoners — what some of the reading had called "free-floating rationales." The dispute started when Marcus claimed that the phrase was empty: a reason, he said, is always a reason *for* some agent, and the idea of an agentless reason was a category mistake, like asking whether a rock was lonely. Elena pushed back: she said that when Darwin explained why the peacock's tail evolved, he was articulating a rationale that had been operating in nature for millions of years before anyone grasped it — the selective logic was there, doing causal work, long before there were minds to appreciate it.

Marcus replied that this conflated causes with reasons. Natural selection is a causal filter, not a reasoner; calling its output "reasons" was a metaphor that had outlived its usefulness. The tail exists because of differential reproduction, not because nature "had a reason" to produce it.

Elena came back with a different case. She asked whether the Pythagorean theorem expressed a reason that was true before anyone proved it. If so, she said, then at least some reasons are mind-independent — they are discovered, not created. Marcus answered that mathematical truths are mind-independent, but reasons are not truths simpliciter; they are truths *in their capacity as guides for thought or action*, and that capacity only exists relative to a thinker. The theorem was true before Pythagoras, but it only became a *reason to believe something* once there were minds to do the believing.

At this point a third person, David, jumped in and said both of them were missing a more interesting intermediate case. He asked us to consider a chess position: White to move, and there is exactly one move that avoids checkmate in three. The logic of the position — the "reason" that move is forced — seems to inhere in the rules and the arrangement of pieces, not in any player's mind. A novice might not see it, but the rationale is *there*, and we don't think it pops into existence when the novice finally grasps it. Yet it's not a purely physical fact either; it depends on the normative structure of the rules. David said this was a "free-floating rationale" in good standing — neither a brute cause nor a mental state.

Marcus responded that the chess case actually proved his point: the rationale only exists *relative to the goal* of avoiding checkmate, which is a goal held by a player. Strip away the player's aim and you just have wood and rules — no reason to move one way rather than another. Elena countered that the goal is built into the practice of chess, not into any particular player's psychology; you can assess a move as good or bad without knowing who is playing, and the assessment doesn't change if the player is a computer with no phenomenal consciousness. David added that this was exactly his point: the rationale is embedded in a normative practice that transcends individual minds but isn't a free-floating Platonic entity either.

The argument went on from there, touching on whether the laws of physics constitute reasons (Marcus: no, they're descriptions), whether a tree falling in a forest makes a sound if no one hears it (Elena: that's a different question, about perception not normativity), and whether the whole debate reduced to a verbal dispute about the word "reason" (David: partly, but not entirely, because the question of whether normative structure can exist without minds has practical stakes for how we think about ethics and AI).

Please go through this exchange and adjudicate it move by move. For each distinct claim made by Marcus, Elena, and David, say whether it is largely correct, largely mistaken, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify each assessment. Then give an overall verdict: who was closest to the right view, and what — if anything — were all three missing? State your final overall verdict in the form \answer{...} with a short phrase, e.g., \answer{Marcus is closest, but all three overlook the distinction between epistemic and practical reasons}.
