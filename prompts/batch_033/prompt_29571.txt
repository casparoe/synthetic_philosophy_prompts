I'm presenting a paper at a workshop next month called "Cooperation Without Contact," and my talk defends the claim that evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) has genuine normative force for finite agents making decisions under uncertainty about the size and structure of the universe. The core argument is that if you find yourself in a situation that resembles a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with an agent you will never causally interact with — say, an alien civilization in a causally disconnected region of an inflationary universe — and you have reason to believe they face a symmetric decision and are reasoning in a similar way, then the evidential link between your decision and theirs gives you a reason to cooperate that isn't merely a philosophical curiosity but should actually guide action, including in near-term cause-prioritization decisions. I argue that this holds even if you are uncertain about whether the universe is large enough to contain such counterparts, provided your credence in a large world is above some threshold I try to make precise.

The audience is not going to be friendly. It's a mixed group: about half are game theorists and economists who work on cooperation and think in terms of causal graphs and interventionist models of causation; a quarter are philosophers of science who are comfortable with Newcomb-like problems but skeptical that they have practical upshots; and the remaining quarter are people from the effective altruism and global priorities research communities who are broadly sympathetic but worry that ECL reasoning licenses too much — that once you open the door to acausal trade, you can justify almost anything by positing the right kind of correlated agent somewhere in a large enough universe. There will also be at least one person who thinks the whole framework rests on an equivocation between "large world" in the sense of many agents and "large world" in the sense of a multiverse with many exact copies of you, and that sliding between these two senses does a lot of the argument's work.
