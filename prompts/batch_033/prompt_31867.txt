I've been reading Leibniz lately—the Theodicy, the Monadology, all that—and I keep coming back to the Lisbon earthquake of 1755. You know the story: massive quake on All Saints' Day, followed by tsunami and fires, tens of thousands dead, and the detail that really got under people's skin was that the churches collapsed while the brothels stood. Voltaire wrote that poem about it, then Candide, basically tearing the "best of all possible worlds" to shreds. Rousseau pushed back, arguing the real problem wasn't God or nature but human decisions—shoddy construction, crowding, people refusing to leave their homes because they wanted to save their belongings.

So here's what I want: analyze the Lisbon earthquake as a test case for Leibniz's theodicy. I don't want the standard Voltaire-versus-Leibniz summary you get in any intro textbook. I want a careful mapping. What exactly does Leibniz's framework say about an event like this? What resources does it have—the distinction between God's antecedent and consequent will, the idea that local evils are necessary for greater goods we can't survey, the claim that we lack the infinite perspective to judge—and how well do those resources actually handle the specifics of Lisbon? Where does the framework genuinely explain something, and where does it strain past the breaking point?

Then I want the reverse direction: what does the Lisbon earthquake, and the debate it set off, reveal about Leibniz's theodicy? Is it a genuine counterexample, or does it just show that theodicy is emotionally unsatisfying even when it's logically sound? Rousseau's response is interesting here—he kind of sidesteps the theological problem and points at human engineering, which is a very different kind of argument. Does that help Leibniz or does it concede the point by changing the subject?

I spent my careeer in geology, so I understand earthquakes, and I've read enough philosophy now to follow the arguments. But I want someone to show me exactly where the joints are between the the theory and the event, not just wave at them.
