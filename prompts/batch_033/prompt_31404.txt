Two of my postdocs got into an argument after our decision-theory seminar and I ended up as the reluctant referee. I have my own view, but I don't want to bias the answer, so I'll just report what they said and ask for a ruling.

The dispute was about two cases: the counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail.

In the counterfactual mugging, Omega (a perfect predictor) flips a coin. If it lands tails, Omega asks you for £100. If it lands heads, Omega gives you £10,000 just in case it predicted that you would have handed over the £100 if the coin had landed tails. So when Omega asks, you know the coin landed tails.

In XOR blackmail, Omega sends you a letter and tells you, truthfully, that it sent the letter just in case exactly one of two things is true: your house has a termite infestation, or you will pay Omega £1,000. The infestation would cost you £1,000,000.

Now the argument. I'll call them Priya and Tom.

Priya said the mugging is easy: refuse. Once Omega asks, the coin is tails, the £10,000 isn't coming, and paying just makes you £100 poorer for nothing. Tom said that's how you end up broke: if you're the sort who refuses in the tails branch, Omega predicts that in the heads branch and never pays you. The only policy that gets the £10,000 in heads is the policy of paying in tails, so the refuse policy is worse across the two branches.

Priya said 'worse across branches' is doing all the work, and branches aren't all actual. You know which branch you're in. Your act now can't reach into the heads branch and cause the £10,000 to appear. Tom said that same reasoning would make you two-box in Newcomb's problem, since the boxes are already filled before you choose. Priya said Newcomb is different: there Omega's prediction concerns the very act you're now performing, so your act is evidence about the contents of the boxes in the actual world. In the mugging, the prediction concerns what you would have done in a branch you already know didn't happen.

Then Tom brought up XOR blackmail. 'So you'd pay there?' Priya said yes. If she receives the letter and pays, she learns the letter wasn't sent because of termites; if she doesn't pay, she learns it was. Paying £1,000 beats facing a million-pound infestation. Tom said that's odd, because paying doesn't cause the termites to vanish. If the house is infested, it's infested whether or not she pays, exactly as she said about the mugging. Priya said the difference is that in XOR blackmail the payment is evidence about the actual state of her house, whereas in the mugging the payment is only evidence about a counterfactual branch. That, she said, is the line.

Tom said he didn't see why that line should matter. In the mugging, paying in tails is evidence about what sort of person you are — a payer — and that's a fact about the actual world too. You're paying to be the sort of person Omega pays. Priya said being that sort of person dosen't put £10,000 in her pocket now; in XOR blackmail, paying puts her in the no-termites camp now. Tom said it only does so because Omega wrote the letter using an XOR, and the letter was already in the post before she chose. Both cases are Omega sorting people by their dispositions. Priya said fine, but she's allowed to prefer the sorting that leaves her richer in the actual world.

Finally Tom asked whether she would have precommitted to pay in the mugging before the coin was flipped. Priya said yes, because the precommitment would have changed Omega's prediction in the heads branch. Tom said then refusing in tails is just deviating from a plan she herself would have bound herself to, which can't be rational. Priya said it can: the plan was rational ex ante because it changed the probabilities, but once the coin is tails the probabilities have collapsed, and a rational agent updates. Ex ante and ex post rationality can come apart. Tom said that makes 'rational' depend on when you ask.

Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what, seperately for each move, and who (if either) is more right overall. Then say whether the line Priya draws between the two cases can carry the weight she puts on it, whether Tom's policy-based argument really shows the two cases are on a par, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please write in British English.
