As part of the research for a novel I am writing about a reclusive mathematician, I have spent the past year sitting in on a philosophy of mathematics seminar. Two of the regular participants — a committed platonist who insists that mathematical objects are discovered rather than invented, and an equally committed nominalist who holds that mathematics is a human construction and that numbers, sets, and functions enjoy no mind-independent existence — have been arguing past each other so consistently that the seminar organizer has asked them to produce an adversarial collaboration document. I have been asked to help draft it, since I am the neutral party with no professional stake in the outcome and some experience distilling competing perspectives into prose.

I need to produce a document both of them can sign. It should begin with a numbered list of claims they both agree on — about mathematical practice, about what does and does not need explaining, about what the debate is not about, and about which phenomena neither side disputes. Then it should have a table of disagreements, where each row states a precise point of divergence in wording each side would accept for its own position. After that, I need a section on cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one of them. For each crux, I should specify what kind of evidence, argument, or case study both would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each of them predicts. Finally, if any part of their dispute turns out to be merely verbal, I should identify it and explain why.

The platonist keeps saying things like "the Mandelbrot set has properties no one could have anticipated — we are clearly exploring something we did not create," while the nominalist counters that "the same could be said of the rules of chess, and no one thinks chess positions inhabit a Platonic realm." I can tell they are talking past each other on the epistemology versus the ontology, but I am not sure how to separate those cleanly, and I suspect there are further layers beneath that. I also notice that they treat the indispensability argument very differently: the platonist regards it as a serious reason to believe in abstract objects, while the nominalist dismisses it as a pragmatic irrelevance. Help me draft this document.
