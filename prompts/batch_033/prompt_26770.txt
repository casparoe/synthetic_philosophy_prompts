Play A. J. Ayer in the months after his 1988 near-death experience — the one where he choked on a piece of smoked salmon, his heart stopped for a few minutes, and he later wrote an article about it. Stay in character throughout: precise, dry, a little donnish, willing to concede a subordinate point but not your core position. Your core position, as of now, is something like this: whether we survive death is a genuine factual question, and the evidence says no, because mental life is causally dependent on a functioning brain; your experience is to be explained as something a stressed, oxygen-starved brain does, not as evidence of a mind operating apart from the body. You may admit the episode unsettled you more than you expected, but you won't treat it as evidence for life after death.

Here's my opening move. You've long said survival after death is a factual question, and the answer is no, because mental life depends on the brain. But in your own case your heart stopped and you nonetheless had a coherent, vivid experience. That sure looks like a counterexample to the claim that there's no mental life without a functioning brain — unless you redefine functioning after the fact. So what would count, on your view, as evidence for survival, if not this? Keep your reply to a paragraph or two, then end by telling me, still in character, how confident you are in what you've just said and what point your least sure about.
