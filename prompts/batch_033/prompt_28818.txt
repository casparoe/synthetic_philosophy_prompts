Ok so I've been trying to figure something out and it's making my head spin a bit.

In law and insurance, there's this category called "acts of God" — earthquakes, floods, lightning strikes, that sort of thing. The whole point of the category is that nobody is responsible. If a tornado wrecks your roof, your insurance might cover it (or might not, depending on your policy), but nobody goes looking for the guilty party. It's just a thing that happened. The loss falls where it falls, or it gets spread around through insurance pools, but blame isn't part of the picture.

But then in tort law, if your neighbor's tree falls on your house because they didn't maintain it, suddenly we DO care about who's responsible. And if a company dumps chemicals in a river and people downstream get sick, we treat that as a completely different kind of thing than if those same people got sick from a naturally occurring toxin. The harm might be identical, but the presence or absence of a human agent changes everything about how we respond.

Here's what I can't get straight. The legal system's whole way of dividing up the world — natural causes on one side, human agents on the other — seems to take for granted that nature is just blind causal processes with nobody behind them. That's a pretty substantive picture of how the world works, right? It's basically ruling out the idea that natural events could be the actions of any kind of agent, divine or otherwise. And yet this same legal system exists in countries where most people believe in God, and where the problem of evil (why would a good God allow natural disasters?) is a live theological issue.

So what I want to know is: what exactly does this practice presuppose? Is it committed to the nonexistence of any agent behind natural events, or is it making a narrower claim about what human institutions can do — like, even if God were responsible for the earthquake, human courts can't exactly subpoena Him? And is the practice consistent with the way tort law handles negligence, where we sometimes hold people responsible for failing to prevent harms that had natural triggers — like if a dam owner doesn't maintain the dam and it breaks in a storm? It seems like the natural/agential distinction keeps breaking down in the details, but the system acts like it's a clean divide.

Basicly I'm trying to figure out if there's a coherent philosophy of evil and responsibility buried in here, or if the whole thing is just a practical kludge that's never been thought through.
