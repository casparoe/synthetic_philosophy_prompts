I work in the facilities office at a mid-sized university and somehow ended up on the Campus Dining Sustainability Committee. I've always cared about this stuff but I'm not a philosopher or an academic, so I'm a little out of my depth on the argument side. Here's the situation: there's a proposal to take beef completely off the menu in all campus dining halls starting next fall, on the grounds that beef has a huge climate footprint and the university just announced a big carbon-reduction commitment. We serve around 35,000 meals a week, so this isn't purely symbolic. The committee is split.

One side says removing beef is pointless: the amount we buy is a tiny fraction of our supplier's business, the supplier will just sell that beef to someone else, so no cow is actually saved and our emissions reduction is basically a rounding error, maybe literally zero. They say we'd just be patting ourselves on the back while students who want a burger get annoyed. The other side says that if every institution reasoned like that nothing would ever change, and that a university is supposed to lead and educate, not just optimize it's own balance sheet. The chair asked me to draft the short written opinion the committee will vote on at next weeks meeting. It should recommend one concrete policy: full removal, a phased removal over a few years, keeping beef but adding carbon labels, or doing nothing. And it has to take the other side's best point seriously rather than just dismissing it. Could you act as a philosopher who works on collective action and individual responsibility, think carefully, and start by brainstorming the strongest version of the "our share is too small to matter" objection before you write the memo? The final document should be something I can actually circulate, with a clear bottom line and reasons that someone on the losing side could accept as honest.
