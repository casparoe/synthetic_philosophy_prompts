Ok here's roughly where I've landed in population ethics and I want a consistency check before I have to defend it in a workshop.

(1) I'm a negative utilitarian of the lexical-threshold sort: below some level of intense suffering, avoiding that suffering has absolute priority over any amount of happiness. No number of blissful lives makes up for one person's torture.

(2) I also think pleasure and pain are commensurable on a single scale of welfare, at least above the threshold — otherwise ordinary tradeoffs like putting up with a painful surgery for extra happy years wouldn't make sense, and I do want those tradeoffs to be legitimate.

(3) I'm a totalist about aggregation: the value of a world is just the sum of the values of the individual lives (or moments) in it.

(4) But I also think it can't be right that a single being with an enormous capacity for positive welfare could justly outweigh the basic welfare of many ordinary people.

Do these hang together? Go through the pairs and tell me which are actually in tension and which only look like it. Then rank the real tensions by severity and say what the cheapest revision is for each — which belief has to give and what it costs.
