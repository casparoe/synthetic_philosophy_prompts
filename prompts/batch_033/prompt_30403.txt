I am a retired research scientist, and philosophy is a recent occupation rather than a lifelong one; I mention this only because I suspect it shapes where I look for evidence. I have been reading about the problem of induction, and I have come to hold the following view. Goodman's new riddle of induction shows that no purely formal or syntactic account of confirmation can explain why the observed greenness of emeralds examined so far confirms the hypothesis that all emeralds are green, yet by parity of form equally confirms the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue, where an object is grue if it is examined before some future time t and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue. The only workable explanation, on the view I have come to accept, is that confirmation depends on the entrenchment of predicates: 'green' is projectible and 'grue' is not because of our contingent history of having used 'green', and not 'grue', in successful projections. Projectibility is therefore a feature of our inductive practice, not of the world and not of logic.

I would like a map of how exposed this view really is, rather than a list of the usual objections. What would have to be true for it to be false? I want the arguments, examples, formal results, and historical episodes that would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest statement of which defeaters a defender of the entrenchment view would themselves concede as counting against it and which they would dismiss as changing the subject. In particular: are there cases from the history of science in which an entrenched predicate failed and an unentrenched one proved projectible, and would such cases damage the view or merely force a refinement? Would a demonstration that the grue problem dissolves under a Bayesian account of confirmation, with priors doing the work and no appeal to entrenchment, count decisively against the view, or only against Goodman's own framing? Has the view been qualified in ways that insulate it from refutation, and if so, is that insulation a defect or simply what one should expect of a claim about the preconditions of inductive practice? I would personally find most persuasive a demonstration that entrenchment is truth-conducive, in the sense that the predicates we have entrenched are those an ideal inquirer with no history would project anyway; I would also find persuasive a demonstration that it is not truth-conducive and that the view therefore makes our inductive success a matter of luck.
