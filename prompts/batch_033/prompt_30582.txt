A friend has an argument about structural realism that he says "wins either way," and I can't find the exit because the conclusion feels like it proves too much. Here it is as he gave it:

(1) Structural realism says that what our best scientific theories give us is the structure of the world — the relations, equations, symmetries — and not the intrinsic natures of the things standing in those relations.
(2) Any abstract structure can be imposed on any collection of objects, provided there are enough objects, just by choosing the right relation on them. If you don't care what the relation is, you can always find some relation or other that fits the pattern.
(3) So the claim that the world has such-and-such structure is, by itself, nearly trivial: in almost any sufficiently large world, some relation or other will realize that structure.
(4) To make the claim substantive, the structuralist has to quietly restrict which relations are allowed — natural ones, physical ones, causal ones — and that restriction is not itself structural information.
(5) So either structural realism is trivially true, in which case it can't explain why science works, or it's secretly relying on more than structure, in which case it isn't structural realism anymore.
(6) Therefore structural realism is either empty or false.

I'd rather keep some version of structural realism than accept that the success of science has no explanation, so I want to reject one of these steps — but each of them looks forced on me. Wich step should go, and what does giving it up cost?
