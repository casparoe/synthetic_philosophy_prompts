I've been following the debate about whether large language models are agents, simulators, or something else, and I've noticed something frustrating: the discussion seems to have been running in place for at least a couple of years now. On one side, you have the view articulated most prominently in Janus's 2022 "Simulators" post — that an LLM is fundamentally a simulation engine, one that models a distribution of possible text-producing characters and can be conditioned to simulate any of them, but is not itself an agent, any more than a physics engine is a falling object. On the other side, you have people arguing that this distinction is too clean: that the LLM, especially when scaffolded with tools, memory, and longer-horizon prompts, exhibits the functional markers of agency — goal-directed behavior, coherence across contexts, and something like instrumental reasoning. And then there are the intermediate positions: that LLMs are "agentic simulacra," that they are role-players whose transient "self" is the prompt, that the question is ill-posed because agency is a matter of degree and level of description, or that the base model and the scaffolded system are simply different things and the debate conflates them.

What bothers me is that the arguments on all sides are well-rehearsed by now, the canonical examples and counterexamples are familiar, and nobody appears to be moving. I want you to diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute about the word "agent" that would dissolve under a careful taxonomy? Is it a substantive disagreement about the architecture or the training objective — one that would be resolved by a clearer technical picture of what is happening inside the model? Are the two sides answering different questions — one about the base model's ontology, one about the behavioral profile of the deployed system? Is the impasse a sign that "agent" is simply the wrong category and we need a new concept? Or is there a hidden empirical claim that, if tested, would settle it? I'm less interested in a verdict on who is right than in a structural account of why the debate has the shape it does, and whether anything could move it forward.

Please approach this as a philosopher of cognitive science or AI who has thought carefully about the structure of scientific and conceptual disagreements — the kind of person who can distinguish a merely verbal dispute from a clash of frameworks from a genuine empirical question in disguise. I'd like you to start by laying out the core commitments of the main positions as charitably as you can, then work through the possible diagnoses one by one, weighing each against the actual shape of the debate, before settling on the one that best accounts for the impasse. Take your time and think through the possibilities carefully; the goal is not to be quick but to get the diagnosis right.
