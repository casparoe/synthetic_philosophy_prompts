I'm a philosophy of mind guy, and I've been arguing with a friend in machine learning about eliminative materialism — the Churchland line that beliefs and desires are like phlogiston, that a mature neuroscience won't reduce them to brain states but will just drop them entirely. He keeps saying he doesn't see what the big deal is, because in his world you've already got representations and loss functions and nothing hangs on the word "belief."

So here's what I want you to do. Restate the eliminativist argument — the whole thing, not just the punchline — in the vocabulary of contemporary ML. I mean embeddings, latent spaces, world models, reward functions, attention heads, activations, training distributions, the works. Be as faithful as you can. If a premise has to be distorted to fit, mark it.

Then the residue report. Which parts translate clean? Which parts only go through with some fudging? And what parts have no counterpart at all in the ML vocabulary? I want to know whether the untranslatable bits are a sign that the ML vocabulary is conceptually impoverished — that it's missing something real — or whether they're a sign that the eliminativist was leaning on distinctions that don't earn their keep.

Here's the thing I'm really stuck on. The eliminativist says folk psychology posits states with semantic content, states that are about something, and that when we look at the brain we won't find anything with that property. But ML people talk about representations being "about" objects in the environment all the time — they train a network and then probe it and say this neuron fires for cats. Is that the same notion of aboutness? And if it is, doesn't the ML version of the eliminativist argument end up eliminating the concept of a representation too, which would be a weird result for an ML engineer to have to swallow?
