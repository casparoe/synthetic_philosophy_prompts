I am a doctoral candidate in epistemology, and my dissertation concerns the Meno problem — the question, originating in Plato's dialogue, of why knowledge (epistēmē) is more valuable than mere true belief (orthē doxa). At a recent workshop I was struck by how much two camps in the literature have been talking past each other, and I would like to see what an adversarial collaboration between them would produce as a joint statement.

The two positions are these:

(A) The achievement or credit view. On this view, knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief because knowledge is an achievement — a true belief that the agent gets right because of her own cognitive competence or intellectual virtue. The value of knowledge is thus the value of success through ability, which is a distinctive kind of value not reducible to the value of the components. (This view is most closely associated with the virtue-theoretic tradition in epistemology, and in particular with the work of Ernest Sosa and John Greco.)

(B) The deflationary or no-distinctive-value view. On this view, the value of knowledge is exhaustively explained by the value of its constituent parts — true belief, justification, and whatever else is required — and knowledge as such possesses no value over and above the sum of the values of those constituents. The apparent extra value of knowledge can be explained by the fact that knowledge typically correlates with other epistemic goods (understanding, justification, reliability) that are themselves valuable, but knowledge is not itself the locus of a distinctive kind of value. (This view is most often associated with the work of Jonathan Kvanvig, and draws on a broader scepticism about whether epistemic value is additive.)

Please draft the joint statement that a representative of each camp could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should include:

(1) A numbered list of claims both parties accept — these should be substantive and non-trivial, not merely restatements of the problem. For example, both might agree on certain structural features of the value relation, or on which cases are the hardest for each side, or on what would count as a solution.

(2) A disagreements table, with columns: (i) the proposition in dispute, phrased in neutral language; (ii) the credit-theorist's formulation of her own position on that proposition, in wording she would accept as accurate; (iii) the deflationist's formulation of his own position, likewise in wording he would accept; and (iv) whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal.

(3) The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete test, argument, or thought experiment that both sides agree bears on it, and state what each side predicts the outcome of that test will be.

(4) A brief closing note on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, and if so, which part and why.

I am particularly interested in whether the two camps actually disagree about the nature of value — for instance, whether value is additive across constituents, or whether something can have final value in virtue of its structure rather than its parts — or whether they disagree only about the epistemology of value, that is, about what we are entitled to infer from the intuition that knowledge is more valuable than true belief. Please make sure the document does not simply restate the familiar positions but genuinely isolates the points of tension.

The answer should be in British English.
