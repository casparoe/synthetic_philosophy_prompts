I spent my working life as an experimental chemist, and since retiring I have been reading Buddhist philosophy with a small group. What keeps drawing me back to Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka is how thoroughly it refuses the habit I learned in the laboratory of turning an idea into a position one can hold and defend. I would like a poem to read aloud at the end of our next session, one that does not merely describe the tetralemma but enacts it.

Please write the poem in blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter), in five sections. The first four sections should be of equal length, each taking the same object — the self — and trying to fix it by one of the four alternatives of the tetralemma: that it exists, that it does not exist, that it both exists and does not exist, and that it neither exists nor does not exist. Each section should fail on its own terms, and that failure should be audible in the verse itself — a sentence that cannot complete, a syntax that gives way, a rhythm that stumbles. The fifth and shorter section should not quietly settle on "neither," and it should not add a fifth position; it should let emptiness appear as the relinquishing of the whole machinery of positions, while also avoiding the further mistake of turning emptiness into something that remains after the four alternatives are gone. The form should do the argument's work: the regular blank verse of the first four sections and its deliberate loosening in the fifth should stand in for the difference between occupying a view and letting the view go. I want someone who knows Madhyamaka to find the poem right, and someone who does not to still feel the movement of the argument.
