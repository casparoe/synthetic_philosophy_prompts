So I'm in this Hume seminar and we've been doing the Enquiry, and the section on miracles has honestly been the most frustrating part of the course, but in a way that I think is actually philosophically interesting. The back-and-forth just seems endless. One side says Hume's argument is basically circular — he assumes that the evidence for laws of nature is always going to outweigh testimony for a miracle, but that's exactly what someone who believes in miracles would deny in the case they care about. The other side says no, that's a misreading, Hume is making a point about the epistemology of testimony: given how we calibrate trust in testimony against regular experience, the evidence for a miracle is always going to be undercut. Then someone points out that this just pushes the question back to whether we should treat the relevant regular experience as uniform in the way Hume assumes. And round and round it goes. My professor assigned some of the contemporary back-and-forth — Fogelin, Earman, that crowd — and honestly after a hundred pages I can't tell if anyone's mind has actually been changed.

What I'm trying to figure out is what kind of disagreement this actually is. Because it doesn't feel like a normal philosophical debate where both sides agree on the rules of engagement and one side is just making a mistake. It feels more like the two sides are operating with fundementally different pictures of what epistemic rationality requires when you're dealing with singular events that would, if they occurred, overturn the generalizations you rely on. So my question is: is this a substantive disagreement that could be settled by being more careful about the structure of inductive inference, or is it a clash of bedrock epistemic commitments that aren't themselves amenable to argument? And if it's the latter, does that mean the debate is rationally irresolvable, or is there some way of bridging that gap that people haven't tried?

I'm not asking for a summary of the literature or a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself, and an honest answer about what would count as progress — a distinction, a theorem, a reframing, or even just admitting that no progress is possible and why.
