I've been trying to work through Machiavelli's *The Prince* and I keep getting stuck on Chapter 25, the one about fortune and virtù. I feel like there's a tension in the text that I can't quite resolve, and I'd love a careful reading from someone who knows this stuff well.

Here's the passage (from the Marriott translation). First, the famous river bit:

> I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her.

So far so good — the idea seems to be: fortune is powerful, but you can prepare for it in advance, like building dikes before the flood comes. Virtù is about having the foresight to build those defenses.

But then a couple pages later he says this:

> But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed.

And this is where I get confused. The river metaphor makes it sound like you can rationally prepare for fortune — build the dikes, dig the canals, and you'll be fine regardless of what fortune throws at you. But the second passage seems to say something much more pessimistic: you can't really change your basic disposition, because your nature inclines you one way, and even if you *wanted* to adapt to changing circumstances, you probably can't. So whether you succeed depends on whether your fixed nature happens to match the spirit of the times — which is itself just fortune.

These feel like two different theories of agency. In the first, virtù is something like rational preparedness — you can hedge against fortune. In the second, virtù would require a kind of flexibility that Machiavelli himself says most people don't have, and maybe *can't* have, because of their nature.

I'm trying to figure out: is this a genuine contradiction, or is there a reading that reconciles these? Maybe the "dikes" aren't about changing your nature but about something else? Or maybe Machiavelli thinks some people *can* change their nature and those are the truly virtuosi ones, and the "man is not often found" is just a concession that most princes aren't up to it? But then what's the actual advice — be lucky enough to be born with a nature that matches your era, or try to be flexible even though you'll probably fail?

You're an expert on Renaissance political philosophy — think through this carefully, maybe start by mapping out exactly what each passage commits him to, and then see if they can be made consistent or if the tension is real and deliberate.
