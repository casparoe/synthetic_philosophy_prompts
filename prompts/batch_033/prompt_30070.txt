I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and I have been trying to determine when, if ever, it is legitimate for me to take another person's word for a moral judgment. I do not mean moral advice, where someone offers considerations I can assess for myself, nor empirical information that bears on a moral question. I mean deference: adopting a moral verdict on another's say-so, roughly as I would adopt a factual report. My spontaneous reactions to cases have come out unevenly, and I would like to know whether any principled account unifies them.

Here are the cases. (a) A friend who knows football well tells me a tackle in a match I did not see was a foul; I accept that immediately. When she adds that the tackle was cowardly, I refuse to endorse the moralized description until I have watched the clip. (b) A colleague sends me a careful account of why our department's new grading policy is unfair to first-generation students, and I adopt her verdict almost at once. (c) My partner, who knows me unusually well, tells me I was unfair to a student in office hours. I accept the factual details, but I resist making the judgment that I was unfair my own until I have gone over the exchange myself. (d) An aid worker with many years in refugee camps tells me that a well-intentioned practice common among visiting volunteers is degrading to the people it is meant to help; I defer to her verdict without hesitation. (e) A friend from a marginalized group tells me a joke I made at dinner was offensive; I accept this immediately and apologize, even though I cannot yet say what made it offensive. (f) A hospital triage committee on which I do not sit issues a report concluding that a certain protocol for allocating ventilators is just. I am willing to vote for the protocol and to cite the report, but I do not feel that I know the protocol is just.

The pattern I cannot see is this. I resist my partner's verdict about my own conduct in (c), yet I defer in (d) to a verdict about conduct I have never witnessed and in (e) to a verdict about a joke I myself made. Is there one principle about moral deference—about when another person's moral say-so suffices for me to adopt the judgment as my own—that captures all six reactions, or nearly all of them? If the best principle forces me to revise some of my verdicts, which should I give up, and why? Finally, what does that principle say about a further case I have not settled: a moral philosopher whose work I admire tells me, without offering an argument, that a certain kind of political compromise is morally impermissible. Should I take that on trust?
