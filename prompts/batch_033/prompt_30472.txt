I am convening a one-day workshop on the metaphysics of time, and as its centrepiece I would like the presentist and the eternalist who have been arguing in the journals for the better part of a decade to produce, in advance, a single collaborative document that both can sign. Please draft that document for them.

It should open with a numbered section of claims about which the two parties agree, written so that neither side has to flinch: for example, what the data of tense and temporal experience are that any view must accommodate, what special relativity does and does not show by itself, what the logical grammar of the word 'now' commits us to, and which traditional slogans on each side are unhelpful. Then it should give a disagreements table in which each row states (a) the question on which they part ways, (b) the presentist's position in wording a presentist would accept as its strongest formulation, and (c) the eternalist's position in wording an eternalist would accept as its strongest formulation. The positions should not be strawmen. After the table, identify the genuine cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. These might include whether tensed truths require tensed truthmakers; whether past-tensed truths about now-nonexistent things, and cross-temporal relations such as my admiring Socrates, require relata that both exist or can be handled by tensed relational facts; whether there is any coherent sense of temporal passage that is not reducible to variation across a B-series; whether special relativity favours a privileged foliation or shows simultaneity to be frame-relative in a way only eternalism can accommodate; and whether our experience of a moving 'now' is best explained as awareness of a mind-independent feature of reality or as an indexical feature of our own location. For each crux, describe a concrete argument, case, or finding that both sides would accept as genuinely bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that test would show. Finally, state which parts of the dispute, if any, both parties are prepared to concede are merely verbal or terminological, and which parts are not.
