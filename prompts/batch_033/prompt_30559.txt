You are a game theorist who works on the foundations of backward induction and has experience designing classroom experiments for philosophy students. Think carefully before writing. Begin by brainstorming at least three candidate mechanics for the activity, choose one, and briefly explain in a short preamble why you rejected the others.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rationality and strategic choice: 18 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity around the finite centipede game that makes students feel the force of the backward-induction argument and the tension between what that argument recommends and what they are tempted to do. They should run into the paradox themselves before it is named, so do not explain backward induction before they play.

The activity must include the following.

Rules precise enough to run without rehearsal, plus a materials list: payoff tables, record sheets, and any role cards you need. Give a payoff scheme in points rather than real money, scaled so that taking early and passing to the end are both genuinely tempting.

A minute-by-minute timeline for the full 75 minutes.

At least three variants, each isolating one variable. Use these or replace them if you have better ones: a one-shot anonymous pairing with a fixed, publicly known number of decision nodes; a cheap-talk variant in which players may send a non-binding message before play; and a repeated-play variant in which the same pair plays several complete centipede games with the same known endpoint. For each variant, state exactly what the backward-induction argument says should happen, what you predict will happen in the room, and what a divergence between the two would show. If one of these variants would muddy the lesson, say so and substitute a sharper one.

A debrief script that maps specific moments in play onto the formal ideas: subgame-perfect equilibrium and backward induction as a procedure for finding it; the difference between common knowledge of rationality and common belief; off-path beliefs and what a pass by the other player reveals; and the difference between a normative claim about what rational players would do and a predictive claim about what actual people do.

A section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with concrete fixes. Address at least these failure modes: students coordinating out loud or agreeing to split points; one student explaining the paradox mid-game and contaminating later pairs; pairs finishing in two minutes; players treating the choice as a joke or as generosity toward a friend; and confusion about the payoff table.

An honest note on which feature of the backward-induction paradox the activity cannot reproduce. In particular, address whether a classroom can ever instantiate common knowledge of rationality, and whether the one-shot anonymous structure the argument assumes can survive in a room full of students who know one another. Say explicitly what the facilitator must supply afterward.

Three short exit questions for students to answer on index cards, designed to reveal whether they can distinguish backward induction as a computation from backward induction as a recommendation, and whether they can state what they assumed about their partner at the moment they chose to pass.

Do not make the debrief a history lesson and do not ask students to reconstruct the literature. The goal is that they leave able to formulate the paradox precisely and locate where they resisted the argument.
