I am a novelist. I work every day in the vocabulary of craft: point of view and narrative voice, flat and round characters, type and archetype, scene and summary, subtext, worldbuilding, dramatic irony, the tacit contract between writer and reader, and the gap between what a narrator says a character is and what the character does on the page. I have no formal training in philosophy.

Lately I keep running into the claim that money, gender, and race are socially constructed, and that this is supposed to matter for how we think and act. I cannot tell what the claim amounts to, because the only machinery I have for thinking about things made by people is the machinery of fiction, and I am not sure which parts of it the philosophers are reaching for. Is a socially constructed kind like a role the plot assigns and then makes real in its consequences? Like a rule of the fictional world that exists only because writer and reader agree to accept it? Like a flat character, a type imposed on a person and mistaken for the whole of her? Or like a character revised across drafts, so that what she is depends on the decisions of the people writing her at any moment?

Restate the philosophical debate over the social construction of these kinds in my craft vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. By the debate I mean at least the following positions, and you should say if I have drawn the lines wrongly. One position holds that a kind such as money exists only because people collectively treat certain objects as money: the kind is constituted by shared attitudes and practices rather than by the physical stuff, and yet it is real, because its reality consists in what people can and cannot do with it. A second position holds that some of the categories called constructed track distinctions that are there independently of our classifying, so that construction talk overstates how much of the world we make. A third position, more practical in spirit, holds that some constructed kinds, race being the usual example, are real but pernicious, and that the right response is to stop reproducing the kind rather than to describe it more accurately. Translate each position into craft terms one by one, saying what carries over and what has to be bent to fit.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which could you translate only by distorting them, and where exactly did the distortion occur? Which claims have no counterpart at all in how a novelist thinks about characters, roles, and invented worlds? Do not flatten the difference between constructed and imaginary: I need to see where the philosophical view resists my default assumption that a made thing is an unreal thing. And at the end, tell me whether what fails to translate is a defect of my vocabulary, something I would need new terms to see, or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear, or less substantive, than it looks. I want to know whether my instinct that a constructed kind is just a role with consequences is right, or whether it misses the point. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle in the assumptions of fiction, that everything in the story exists because someone made it up, and pass that off as a faithful rendering of a view that was trying to say something else.
