I'm a novelist, and after a festival panel on truth in autobiographical writing I found myself listening to two friends argue about a critic I'll call M. M. had just published a long, contemptuous essay accusing a young memoirist of inventing composite characters and staging scenes she then presented as remembered fact. The essay's factual claims appear to be correct: she really did those things. But M. himself was publicly caught years ago padding a travel book with encounters that never happened, and he never retracted or apologized. Elena said M.'s essay fails as moral criticism because he has no standing: he is demanding from another writer the very truthfulness he refused his own readers. David said that was confused — the accusation is either accurate or not, and here it is accurate, so readers were misled regardless of who points it out. Elena replied that she was not denying the accusation's truth; she was denying M.'s entitlement to issue it, because blame is an interpersonal act that presumes a standard the blamer has already flouted. David answered that this is just tu quoque dressed up as theory, a way for the guilty to deflect any true charge by pointing at the accuser's past — and that if the distinction carried weight, nobody could criticize anyone, since everyone has done something wrong, so norms would become unenforceable. Elena came back that standing does not require blamelessness, only that the accuser not flout the specific standard he invokes, and that attacking the person to deny a claim is different from granting the claim while denying the person the role of accuser. David's last move was to insist that a travel book and a memoir are different contracts with the reader, so the two deceptions are not the same wrong and the analogy collapses. I stayed out of it, and I still don't know how to split the difference.

Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, where each overreaches, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please also tell me which step of your adjudication you are least confident in, and what argument or consideration would most plausibly change your mind on that point. Write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
