Work on indicative conditionals has been stuck in a familiar pattern for decades. The attractive generalisation is Adams's Thesis: the degree to which one should accept an indicative conditional, 'if A, then C', is measured by the conditional probability P(C|A). Lewis's triviality results appear to show that no proposition can have its probability systematically equal to that conditional probability, on pain of collapsing the probability calculus. The field has responded by splitting rather than converging. Some, following Adams and Edgington, conclude that indicative conditionals do not express propositions at all and therefore have no truth conditions; others retain truth conditions and treat the probability data as an assertability or context effect; still others restrict Adams's Thesis to simple conditionals, or adopt a dynamic semantics on which a conditional is an instruction to update a context rather than a description of a world. Each camp has internalised the others' objections, and the debate no longer seems to move. I am not asking for a verdict on which semantics is correct; I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself. Is this a substantive disagreement about what conditionals mean, a merely terminological disagreement about what a semantic theory has to deliver, or a case in which the camps are answering different questions under one heading — one about truth, one about acceptance, one about conversational update? Are they disagreeing about the data, about the standards for a good theory, or about bedrock intuitions about what a conditional says? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new formal result, a sharper distinction, a change of question, or the admission that no single account will do?
