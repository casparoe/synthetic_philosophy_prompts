I've been working through P.F. Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment' and I've built an argument that seems to force a choice I don't want to make. Here it is:

(1) On Strawson's picture, holding someone morally responsible is not a matter of having a theoretical belief about free will; it is a matter of participating in the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the like — in our ordinary interpersonal dealings.

(2) We suspend these reactive attitudes in two kinds of case: excuses (where the act does not reflect the agent's settled character — 'she didn't mean to', 'he was pushed') and exemptions (where the agent is not a proper target of reactive attitudes at all — 'he's a young child', 'she's severely mentally ill').

(3) Exemptions work by showing that the agent is not a full participant in ordinary interpersonal life. We shift from the participant stance to the objective stance — we treat the person as something to be managed or handled, rather than as someone to be reasoned with and held to account.

(4) Now consider someone who was raised in genuinely appalling conditions — severe neglect, abuse, and exposure to violence from infancy. This person develops a cruel, selfish character and predictably harms others. When they wrong us, we do not excuse them (the act does reflect their settled character) and we do not exempt them (they are an adult with normal cognitive capacities, not 'insane' in any clinical sense).

(5) But what is the difference between this person and someone who harms others because of a brain lesion? Both are, in the relevant sense, products of forces entirely beyond their control. The lesion is a physical cause; the upbringing is an environmental cause. Neither chose their causal history. If the lesion exempts, what principled reason is there for the upbringing not to exempt?

(6) It seems the reactive-attitudes framework forces us to say: the lesion exempts because it makes the agent abnormal as a participant in interpersonal life, while the bad upbringing, however severe, does not — the badly raised person still engages in the ordinary back-and-forth of human relations, responds to reasons (even if they flout them), and so on. But this makes exemption depend on a kind of statistical normality: we exempt people whose causal history is unusual enough to mark them as 'not one of us'. That looks like a deeply unprincipled basis for moral responsibility.

(7) If the basis is unprincipled, then either we should exempt everyone (since everyone's character is caused by factors beyond their control) or we should exempt no one (and the reactive attitudes are simply brute facts with no normative justification). The first option abolishes responsibility; the second makes it arbitrary.

I want to resist the conclusion. I think there is a genuine difference between the lesion case and the upbringing case, and I want to keep the Strawsonian picture. But I cannot see how to block the slide from (4) to (7) without giving up something important. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the answer is that I have misunderstood the role of exemptions in Strawson's view, tell me what I have got wrong and whether the corrected view still faces a version of the same challenge.
