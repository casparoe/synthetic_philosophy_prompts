I'm writing a survey paper on the philosophical foundations of mechanistic interpretability for neural networks, and I need to map the logical space of positions on the question: do neural networks have genuine representations that interpretability methods can discover, or are we imposing representations on systems that don't intrinsically have them?

Please write a decision tree that takes someone from their basic commitments about representation to a named position in the debate. The tree should be built from diagnostic questions — concrete claims or cases on which positions genuinely come apart — rather than from a list of names. Each question should be answerable by someone who doesn't already know the labels.

Here are the positions I want the tree to be able to reach (it may also reach others, but these must appear as leaves): strong realism (networks have determinate, discoverable representations), structural realism (representational structure is real but content is indeterminate), instrumentalism (representations are useful fictions we project onto the network), Dennett-style intentional-stance realism (representations are real but only relative to a chosen interpretive stance, with no stance-transcendent fact about which stance is correct), and eliminativism (representation-talk is actively misleading and should be replaced with purely mechanistic description).

Build the tree from the logical space: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space. Flag any regions of the space that are logically possible but unoccupied, and for each, say whether the view is absent because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of positions your tree cannot separate and explain why — for example, if two positions agree on all the diagnostic questions but differ only in emphasis or motivation, say so rather than forcing a spurious distinction.

Take a committed stance on which distinctions are the real fault lines in the debate. Don't just list every distinction anyone has drawn; argue for which ones do the structural work and which are red herrings or collapse into others under scrutiny. If you think some positions that are treated as distinct in the literature actually reduce to the same leaf given the right diagnostic questions, say so and justify the collapse. I am not looking for a neutral survey; I want a tree that makes definite choices about how to carve the space and defends those choices.

The audience is philosophers of science and AI researchers who know the technical basics — what a neural network is, what mechanistic interpretability aims to do, what activation spaces and feature visualization are — but haven't mapped the philosophical terrain. Don't spend time explaining what gradient descent is; do spend time on what it would mean for a network component to represent something, and why the answer matters for what interpretability research can claim to achieve.
