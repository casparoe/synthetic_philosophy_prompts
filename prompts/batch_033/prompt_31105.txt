I'm a retired physicist and I've been reading philosophy of mind lately, and the thing I keep stalling on is whether mental items actually cause anything. I've got a list of gut verdicts on cases; I'd like you to find the principle behind them, if there is one, and tell me which of my verdicts has to go.

Here's the list. (1) I decide to raise my hand in a seminar and my arm goes up: the decision caused the movement, and I'd resist the line that only the motor-cortex activity did the real causing. (2) Hand on a hot stove: the withdrawl is already underway through the spinal reflex before I feel the pain, so I say the pain didn't cause the pullback. (3) I'm given a sugar pill and told it's a painkiller, and my pain drops: I say the belief caused the drop, even though it had to work through endorphins or whatever. (4) In the readiness-potential experiments, the brain starts gearing up a few hundred milliseconds before the conscious intention to move; I want to say the conscious intention isn't doing the causing, but then I can't see how that squares with (1). (5) A blindsight patient reaches accurately for an object while insisting he doesn't see it: the visual processing caused the reach, but I wouldn't call that mental causation. (6) A chess engine 'decides' to sacrifice a bishop: its evaluation function is doing real causal work in the machine, but I don't count it as mental causation, since there's no consciousness in the picture.

My half-formed principle is something about the mental state counting as a cause when it's the very same occurance as the neural state doing the work, and not when it's a late add-on. But that falls apart somewhere between (1) and (4), so build the principle from the cases rather than defending my hunch.

The case I keep going back and forth on: I drive home on autopilot, take the usual exit, and couldn't honestly say I made a conscious decision to take it. Did my knowledge of the route cause the turn, or did the behavior just run off without any mental cause?

Please structure the answer like this: (a) My cases restated — a few sentences, mainly to check you've got my verdicts right; (b) Candidate principles — the main part, maybe three or four paragraphs; (c) What has to give — a paragraph on which of my verdicts each candidate can't accommodate; (d) The autopilot case and one more — a paragraph or two on what your preferred principle says about it and one further case of your choosing.
