I'm a PhD student in pragmatics, and I've been collecting my own intuitions about the moral difference between lying and misleading. I'd like to see whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or whether I need to give some up.

Here are my verdicts. (1) A colleague asks whether I've read her draft. I haven't, but I say 'I had a look at it' — I glanced at the first page. This feels permissible, though I'm uneasy. If I'd said 'Yes, I read it carefully', that would be clearly wrong. (2) At a border checkpoint, the officer asks whether I'm carrying any fresh produce. I'm carrying an apple. I say 'I've got some toiletries and a book' — technically true, but I'm hoping the officer won't probe further. This feels worse than (1), and I'm not sure it's better than a flat 'no'. (3) My partner asks whether I'm still in touch with a particular ex. I reply 'I haven't spoken to them in months' — true, but we've been emailing regularly. This feels about as bad as a direct lie. (4) A friend asks what I think of their manuscript. It's unpublishable, but they're fragile. I say 'I think the third chapter is really promising' — true, but I'm relying on the conversational implicature that the whole thing is decent. This feels fine, even kind. (5) I'm selling a used car. The buyer asks whether it's ever been in an accident. It was in a minor collision, repaired before I bought it. I say 'I've never had any trouble with it' — true. This feels wrong, and I'm not convinced it's less wrong than saying 'No, it hasn't been'. (6) A student asks whether a particular question will be on the exam. I know it will be, but I want them to study broadly. I say 'You should be prepared for anything' — true, but misleading. This feels perfectly fine.

I notice a pattern I can't quite articulate: in some cases (the manuscript, the exam) misleading seems clearly better than lying, while in others (the border, the car) it seems barely distinguishable, and in the case of the partner, the misleading feels just as bad as a lie. Is there a principle that captures all of this? If not, which of my verdicts should I revise, and why? And what does the principle you propose say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a politician who, when asked about a policy they plan to abandon, says 'We are reviewing all options' — true, but deliberately misleading?
