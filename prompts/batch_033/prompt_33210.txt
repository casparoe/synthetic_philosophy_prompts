I work on systems that try to infer users' preferences — reward functions, in the sense used in inverse reinforcement learning — from their observed behaviour, and I have found that my own judgements about when that inference is warranted are more tangled than I expected. Here are my verdicts on some cases.

When a user instantly dismisses every video from one particular channel, I am confident the behaviour reveals a preference to avoid that channel. When a driver brakes before the same blind bend every morning, I am confident the behaviour reveals caution at that bend, though I would hesitate to say it reveals a general preference for slow driving. When someone walks past a bakery without going in, I do not infer that they dislike cake; they might be full, in a hurry, or on a diet. When a rat presses a lever for food when hungry but not when sated, I am happy to say the food is wanted only in the hungry state. When a person takes the stairs every day while the lift is working, I infer that they prefer the stairs or the exercise; but if I later learn the lift is often out of order, the same behaviour no longer supports that inference. When a chess program sacrifices a queen and then wins, I do not infer that it wanted to lose the queen; the move is intelligible only as part of a plan. When someone refuses a painkiller for a headache, I do not infer that they prefer the pain; I assume they are avoiding the side effects or the cost.

The two cases that bother me most are the video dismissals and the bakery. Both look like approach or avoidance behaviour observed in the wild, yet I treat one as strong evidence of a standing preference and the other as almost no evidence at all. I can tell a story about frequency, or about available alternatives, but the driver case seems to block the frequency story, and I cannot state the alternatives story precisely enough to rely on it.

Is there a single principle that captures when behaviour warrants an inference to an underlying preference and when it does not? If a simple principle cannot get all seven, which of my verdicts should I overturn? And what does your principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a shopper who fills a basket and then abandons it at the payment step — is that evidence that they did not want the items much, or merely that the checkout step was too costly or too risky, and how could I tell from behaviour alone?

Please write your answer in British English.
