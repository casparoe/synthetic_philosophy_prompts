I've spent a fair bit of time over the past few years reading about the 2008 financial crisis — not the technical details of structured finance, which I've picked up from books like Michael Lewis's The Big Short and from long-form journalism, but the structural question that still bothers me: why did so many people across so many different institutions do things that, taken together, produced a catastrophe that almost none of them individually wanted? Mortgage brokers wrote loans they knew were bad, banks bundled them into securities, ratings agencies stamped them AAA, fund managers bought them, regulators looked away — and at every node, people seem to have been responding to incentives that made their individual behaviour reasonable even as the aggregate outcome was disastrous.

This looks to me like what gets called a multipolar trap or a race to the bottom: a situation where multiple actors, each pursuing their own interests, are driven by competition to a collectively worse outcome than if they could all coordinate. The concept appears under various names — Moloch, coordination failures, prisoner's dilemmas at scale — and the core idea is that competitive pressure can push a system toward worse and worse equilibria even when everyone involved can see what is happening.
