At a dinner party last weekend, two friends of mine — Elena, who teaches political science, and Marcus, who works in commercial mediation — got into a long argument about a local dispute they had both read about. A small bookstore owner signed a lease with a rent-escalation clause; when the increase kicked in, he announced publicly that he would not pay and dared the landlord to evict him, framing it as standing up for independent businesses. The landlord, a mid-sized property firm, responded by publicly stating they would absolutely evict if the rent was not paid, saying they could not set a precedent. Both have now made their positions matters of public reputation, and local media are covering the standoff.

Elena said this was a textbook commitment race: each party has rationally tied their hands to force the other to blink, but if neither blinks, both get a worse outcome than if neither had made their threat public — the bookstore closes and the landlord loses a tenant. Marcus pushed back, saying that calling it rational was perverse: the bookstore owner is acting out of pride and the landlord out of bureaucratic rigidity, and the commitment-race model just dresses up emotionally driven escalation in the language of strategic rationality. Elena replied that the model does not require the parties to be consciously calculating — it captures the incentive structure they are caught in regardless of their psychology. Marcus said that if the model applies regardless of psychology, it is unfalsifiable: you can call any escalating conflict a commitment race after the fact. Elena answered that you can falsify it by showing that the parties' threats were not actually commitment devices — for instance, if the landlord could evict quietly without anyone noticing they had backed down, the strategic logic would not hold. Marcus came back with: but the landlord can do exactly that — settle quietly and claim victory, since no one is auditing their enforcement record — so the model does not even apply here. Elena said that was an empirical claim about this particular case, not a conceptual objection to the model. At that point they turned to me and asked what I thought. I said I needed time to think about it.

Please go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating, and say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
