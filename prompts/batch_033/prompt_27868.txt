An argument from a gambling seminar has been bugging me because it seems to prove that everyone should bet Kelly no matter what they care about, which cant be right. Setup: you face a long sequence of independent even-money bets, each winning with probability p > 1/2 (known) and losing otherwise; each round you stake a fixed fraction f of your current bankroll, so your wealth is multiplied by (1+f) if you win and (1−f) if you lose. The Kelly fraction is f* = 2p − 1.

(1) A rational gambler should choose the policy that maximizes expected utility relative to her own utility function over final wealth.
(2) Kelly betting maximizes expected log wealth; so by (1) it's the right policy for a gambler with logarithmic utility, while a gambler with linear utility should bet the maximum each round, since that maximizes expected final wealth.
(3) But Kelly has another property: with probability 1, a Kelly bettor's wealth eventually overtakes and stays ahead of the wealth of any gambler using any other fixed fraction — including the all-in linear-utility bettor, who goes broke almost surely.
(4) Whatever your utility function, you should prefer a policy that almost surely overtakes every rival policy to one that doesn't.
(5) So everyone should bet Kelly, whatever their utility function.
(6) That contradicts (2). So either expected utility theory is false, or everyone secretly has log utility, which is absurd.

I can't find the exit. I'd rather keep (1) than accept that everyone has log utility, and (3) is a theorem, so I keep wanting to blame (4) — but I cant say exactly whats wrong with (4) without it sounding like sour grapes. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
