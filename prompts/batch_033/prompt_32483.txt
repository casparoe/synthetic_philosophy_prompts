You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of metaethics and the philosophy of language, with particular expertise on expressivism, deflationism, and the Frege–Geach problem. I want a careful consistency audit of a set of commitments, not a survey of the literature. Please begin by working through each commitment one at a time and brainstorming the ways it could interact with the others, in both asserted and embedded contexts, before settling on any ranking.

I am drafting a statement of my metaethical views for a workshop and I want to know whether the package I have converged on hangs together. The commitments are:

1. Expressivism about moral language. The fundamental semantic role of moral sentences is to express conative, desire-like attitudes — approval, disapproval, norm-acceptance — rather than to represent moral states of affairs.

2. Compositionality. The meaning of a complex moral sentence is determined by the meanings of its parts and their syntactic arrangement, in the usual way.

3. Deflationism about truth. 'It is true that lying is wrong' is equivalent to 'lying is wrong'; truth is a thin, disquotational device. Expressivism can therefore grant that moral sentences are truth-apt without becoming a form of descriptivism.

4. Motivational internalism. There is a necessary connection between sincerely judging an act wrong and being motivated not to do it. I take this to be one of the strongest motivations for expressivism.

5. Quasi-realism. An expressivist can legitimately speak of moral facts, moral truth, and moral knowledge; these are earned, not abandoned.

6. Attitude-based validity. The validity of moral arguments is to be explained by incoherence among the attitudes expressed: an argument is valid when accepting the premises while rejecting the conclusion involves a clash of attitudes.

7. Higher-order attitudes handle embedding. The Frege–Geach problem is to be handled by saying that embedded moral sentences express attitudes toward combinations of attitudes. For example, 'if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong' expresses disapproval of the combination of disapproving of lying and not disapproving of getting one's little brother to lie; the same strategy extends to negations, disjunctions, and mixed descriptive-moral sentences.

Please audit this set. In particular: (a) which pairs, or larger subsets, of commitments are in genuine tension, and which merely appear to be in tension? (b) Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each say exactly what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost. (c) Say whether the package as a whole is jointly tenable, and whether any single commitment is doing too much work. Be precise about the mechanism of each tension — show where and how the conflict arises — and consider the strongest version of each commitment rather than a strawman.
