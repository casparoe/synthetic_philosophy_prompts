Two people in my programme got into a long argument about Kripke and I ended up as the designated referee, which I am not qualified for. It went roughly like this.

Mara said she cant shake the feeling that 'water is H2O' has to be contingent. It was a discovery, she said: chemists had to run experiments on the actual stuff, and before they did, the watery stuff in lakes and rivers could, for all anyone knew, have turned out to be made of something else entirely. Sam said that only shows the sentence is a posteriori, not contingent. The word 'water', he said, names the actual watery stuff rigidly; since the actual watery stuff is H2O, there is no possible world where water is not H2O. The experiments just tell us which stuff our word was latched onto all along.

Mara said that sounds like a nice picture, but it commits him to saying there are necessary truths you can only know by looking at the world, and she doesnt see how that's not a contradiction. Necessity is supposed to be the thing that holds no matter how the world is, so what is experience doing there? Sam said experience is only needed to fix the reference; once it's fixed, the necessity comes along for free, so no contradiction.

Mara came back with: 'water is water' is the bit I know a priori. 'Water is H2O' involves two different ways of thinking about the same stuff, and whether those two ways line up is an empirical matter about our concepts and the world. If the identity is necessary, that just means the concepts co-refer; it doesn't mean the sentence expresses a necessary truth in the way 'water is water' does. Sam said now she's smuggling in Fregean senses, and in any case an identity between a thing and itself is necessary, full stop — the informativeness is a fact about us, not about the water.

Mara said fine, then take 'the morning star is the evening star': it's informative, and before astronomy people would have called it contingent, yet it turns out to be necessary if both descriptions pick out the same planet. That's exactly what bothers her: the necessity is discovered empirically, and that leaves her with two possibilities — either the sentence really is contingent, or 'necessary' has come apart from 'knowable a priori' so completely that we can't trust our modal intuitions. Sam said the second is exactly right, and that's the whole point of the necessary a posteriori: the feeling of contingency is a fact about our ignorance, not about the worlds.

Then Mara said he's just relabeled the mystery. If 'water is H2O' is necessary and a posteriori, there must be two different readings of the sentence: one that's about whatever plays the water-role in a world, which is a priori and genuinely contingent, and one that's about this actual stuff, which is necessary and a posteriori. So her original intuition that the sentence is contingent tracks something real, not an illusion. Sam said that's fine as a concession to him, because on the reading where it's about the actual stuff, the sentence is necessary and a posteriori, which is all he ever claimed. And around there they started repeating themselves.

Please adjudicate this step by step: at each turn, who's right about what, where does each one overreach, and is there a stable position that respects Mara's intuition that the contingency-feeling tracks something real while still keeping Sam's rigid-designation point? And say who is more right overall, if either.
