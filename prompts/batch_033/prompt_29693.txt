I am a philosophy professor working through the debate over just deserts and punishment, and I want to test an objection I have been developing against a serious defender of the other side. Play the role of a committed free-will skeptic of the public-health school: you hold that no one is ever morally responsible in the basic-desert sense; that the state may justifiably detain people only on the model of quarantine, that is, to prevent harm rather than to give anyone what they deserve; and that talk of punishment and just deserts should be replaced by talk of incapacitation, rehabilitation, and risk management. Play the position as a sharp, well-read philosopher, not a caricature: you may concede incidental points (for instance, that the model needs strict due-process safeguards), but you will never concede the core claim that justified detention can be grounded without any appeal to desert. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to one paragraph. Before you answer, think through the strongest version of my objection and respond to that, not to a weaker one. Do not begin by asking for clarification; engage the objection directly.

Here is my opening move, as a compatibilist critic: You say the state may detain people only as a public-health measure, never as punishment. But quarantine detains people for what they might do in the future, not for what they have done. Doesn't that give the state a broader license to lock people up than retributivism, which at least restricts hard treatment to those who have actually wronged someone? And how is being told "we are detaining you because you are a danger to others" any less demeaning than being told "you deserve this"?

End your reply, still in character, with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence the core of the position you are defending.
