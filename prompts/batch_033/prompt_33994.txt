I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of law course, and I help run a student-led discussion group that meets outside class — about twelve people, mostly from philosophy, politics, and law, for ninety minutes. We've been working through the problem of promissory obligation: Hume's convention-based account, Scanlon's expectation view, and the deeper question of why a speech act of the form "I promise" can generate a reason to act that wasn't there before the utterance. The group can reconstruct the arguments, but nobody feels the force of the question — the positions stay on the page. I want to run a structured activity that makes the bindingness of promises show up as a live problem rather than a theoretical one.

Design an activity for this group: a game, a role-play, or a structured deliberation with real (small) stakes, in which participants make promises to one another under systematically different conditions and discover, through their own reactions and choices, when a promise feels binding and when it doesn't. The rules or structure should be arranged so that participants run into the problem themselves: someone will feel genuinely obligated by a promise they made, someone else will feel released, and the group will have to make sense of the difference without yet having the theoretical vocabulary. The activity should surface the tension between the bare act of saying the words and the background conditions that make the act morally weighty — trust, the existence of a shared practice, the cost of being let down, the presence or absence of an audience, and so on.

Include: the rules, any materials, and the setup; a timeline for the ninety minutes; the debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical terrain (Hume, Scanlon, or whichever framework the activity best illuminates); what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it; and an honest note on which feature of promissory obligation the activity cannot reproduce and I will need to supply myself as facilitator.
