Construct a thought experiment where two forecasters, each running one simple inductive rule, trade places as the one who looks foolish, even though neither is smarter, better-calibrated, or working with different data. One uses a persistence rule: after a run of similar days, predict the run continues. The other uses a reversal rule: after a run of similar days, predict the run breaks. Put them in two ordinary settings — say a warehouse with wavy demand and a small clinic with load-balancing — such that persistence wins in one and reversal wins in the other. Constraints: the settings have to be symmetric in the relevant respect (same size of correlation, opposite sign), neither rule may be a strawman, both forecasters must apply their rule to the same kind of data, and the scenario should be one paragraph and realistic — no demon's, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics. After the scenario, walk through what each rule predicts in each setting and why, and say which feature of the case is doing the work. Then give the no-free-lunch moral for the problem of induction: why neither rule is better in general, what assumption you'd have to add to justify picking one, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, and how a defender of the rule that loses in a given setting would attack the case.
