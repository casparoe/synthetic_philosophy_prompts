So in my philosophy class we were reading Augustine's "On Lying" and our teacher had us discuss this hypothetical: Nazi soldiers knock on your door and ask if you're hiding Jews in your attic. You are hiding Jews in your attic. Augustine says you can't lie — you have to tell the truth, or at least you can't say something false with intent to deceive. Two of my classmates got into this huge argument about it afterwards and I'm still trying to figure out who was right.

Marcus took Augustine's side. He said the whole point is that lying corrupts your own soul, and your not responsible for what the Nazis do with the truth — that's on them. He said if you lie to the Nazis, you're treating lying as a tool you can use whenever the stakes feel high enough, and once you go down that road theres no principled stopping point. He also pointed out that Augustine would say you can refuse to answer, or you can say something evasive that's technically true, but you can't outright lie.

Jenna pushed back hard. She said (1) Augustine's definition of lying — saying a false thing with intent to deceive — is question-begging because it builds the wrongness right into the definition. Why should "intent to deceive" be part of what makes something a lie, rather than just part of what makes some lies wrong? (2) She said Marcus's point about corrupting your own soul assumes that every lie does that, but that's exactly what's at issue — maybe some lies don't corrupt your soul, and the Nazi case is the clearest counterexample. (3) She said the "refuse to answer or be evasive" move is disingenuous: if Nazis show up asking about hidden Jews and you suddenly go silent or start playing word games, they're going to search the house, so in practice the "no lying" rule just gets people killed and your pretending your hands are clean. (4) Finally she said Augustine's position only makes sense if you think earthly life doesn't matter much compared to the state of your soul, and that's a pretty specific theological commitment that shouldn't be smuggled into a general argument about lying.

Marcus came back and said Jenna's fourth point actually strengthens Augustine's case: if you don't share his theology then maybe his conclusion doesn't follow, but within his framework it's perfectly consistent, and Jenna hasn't shown any internal contradiction. He said her first three points all amount to "the consequences of not lying are terrible," but Augustine already knows that and thinks it doesn't matter for the morality of the act itself.

I felt like they were talking past each other at the end. Can you go through there exchange point by point and say who's right about what? And is there a way to resolve this that doesn't just come down to whether you already accept Augustine's theological premises?
