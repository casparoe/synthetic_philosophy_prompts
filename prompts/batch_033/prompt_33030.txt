I keep coming across references to what Daniel Dennett calls Darwin's and Turing's "strange inversions of reasoning" — the idea that Darwin showed how complex, purposive design can arise from a mindless algorithmic process (variation plus natural selection) without any foresight, and that Turing showed how intelligent behavior can be produced by a mechanism that merely follows rules without understanding what it is doing. Together, these are supposed to undermine the intuition that competence requires comprehension.

I want to understand exactly what this thesis amounts to, but I want to do it in the vocabulary I actually think in. Restate the Darwin–Turing inversion thesis in the conceptual framework of modern machine learning: talk about optimization landscapes, stochastic gradient descent, the relationship between the training process and the resulting model, the distinction between a model's capabilities and any claims about its "understanding," emergent behaviors, and the bitter lesson. Translate both inversions — the biological one and the computational one — as faithfully as you can into this vocabulary.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical claim map cleanly onto concepts I already work with, which parts had to be stretched or distorted to fit, and which parts have no counterpart at all in the ML vocabulary? In particular, I am curious whether the claim that "comprehension is not required" or that "comprehension is an illusion" is doing real philosophical work, or whether it collapses into something I already believe — namely, that gradient descent on a sufficiently expressive architecture can find solutions we do not ourselves understand, and that the resulting model's behavior can look intelligent without any substrate of genuine understanding. If there is something in the philosophers' version of this idea that my vocabulary cannot capture, tell me whether that is a limitation of my framework or a sign that their version of the claim is less precise than it looks.
