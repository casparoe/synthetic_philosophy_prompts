I'm a PhD student in decision theory and I keep noticing this pattern in how people reason about certain choices, and I'm wondering if there's an established name for it or if it's just a cluster of related things that haven't been cleanly separated.

Here's the pattern. Someone is deciding what to do, and their choice depends on what they expect another person to do, but there's no causal channel between them — no communication, no prior agreement, no common cause linking their decisions. The cleanest case is two people in separate rooms playing a prisoner's dilemma, both knowing the other is rational and knows the payoffs. Some people say: obviously you defect, because whatever the other person does, defecting gets you a better outcome. But other people say: wait, if I defect, then since the other person is in exactly my situation and reasoning in exactly the same way, they'll also defect, and we both get the sucker's payoff. If I cooperate, they'll cooperate too, and we both do better. So I should cooperate.

What's strange about this second line of reasoning is that it treats the other person's decision as if it depends on yours — but not causally. You're not causing them to cooperate by cooperating. It's more like your decision is evidence about what they'll do, or like you're somehow choosing a decision procedure for both of you at once. And I've noticed people use this kind of reasoning way outside the strict symmetry case. I've seen it in arguments about voting ("if I vote, then people like me will vote, so I should vote"), about vegetarianism ("if I stop eating meat, that makes it more likely that others similiar to me will too"), and in some corners of the EA and rationalist world where people talk about cooperating with agents in other possible worlds or with future AIs they'll never actually interact with.

I know this brushes up against Newcomb's problem and the debate between evidential and causal decision theory. But I'm specifically after the move where you treat another agent's choice as non-causally dependent on yours because you're both running relevantly similar decision procedures. Does this specific move have a name? Is there a literature that carves it up precisely — distinguishing cases where the reasoning is actually valid from cases where it's just magical thinking dressed up in formal language? And if there's no single name, what are the closest concepts and how do they differ from each other and from what I'm describing?
