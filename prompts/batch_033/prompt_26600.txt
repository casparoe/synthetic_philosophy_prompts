At a faculty meeting last semester, two of my colleagues got into it over whether to eliminate our department's language exam requirement for the PhD. (Students have to pass a reading exam in French or German; almost nobody fails it, its pro forma, and it eats a lot of administrative time.)

Maya argued we should drop it. She said it's a vestige of a 19th-century model of scholarship, it signals nothing about philosophical ability, and we're wasting resources on it.

David pushed back with Chesterton's fence: you shouldn't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up. He said the fact that we can't immediately see the point of the requirement doesn't mean there isn't one — maybe it filters for something we're not consciously tracking, or shapes the culture of the department in ways we'd only notice after it was gone.

Maya replied that this is exactly the kind of argument that could have been used to defend any abolished practice in history. The Latin mass, the exclusion of women from universities, debtor's prisons — at some point someone must have said "don't remove this fence until you understand why it was built." She said the principle is unfalsifiable: it can never be satisfied, because you can always say "but maybe there's a reason you haven't thought of yet."

David said that's unfair — the principle doesn't say never remove the fence, it says do the work of understanding first. He said he'd be satisfied if we could articulate what the exam was supposed to do and show it wasn't doing it. But nobody in the room could actually say what function the exam served when it was instituted, which was his whole point.

Maya came back with: we do know what it was for — it was for ensuring students could read the primary and secondary literature in the languages most philosophical scholarship was written in. That function is obsolete because (a) everything important gets translated, (b) the secondary literature is in English now, and (c) we don't actually enforce it — students pass a one-hour dictionary exam and never read another word of German. So we have understood the fence, and the reason it was built no longer applies.

David said that's a just-so story about the fence's purpose, not an actual historical investigation. Maybe the real function was to limit the PhD to students who'd had a certain kind of pre-graduate education, and removing it would change the composition of our applicant pool in ways we'd regret. Or maybe the function was to give students a shared experience of difficulty that bonded their cohort. He said he didn't know — and that was exactly the point.

At this point someone tabled the discussion for the next meeting, and I've been turning it over ever since. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what? In particular, I'm interested in whether Maya is correct that the Chesterton's fence principle is unfalsifiable in practice, and whether David's final move — suggesting alternative possible functions without committing to any of them — is a legitimate application of the principle or an abuse of it.
