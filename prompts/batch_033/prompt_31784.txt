I've been using LLMs daily for about eighteen months now — coding, research, writing, and increasingly as a thinking partner. I've read the standard arguments about why they don't really understand anything: stochastic parrots, the Chinese Room, next-token prediction, no grounding in the world. I've also read the rebuttals. I'm not asking for a summary of the debate.

What I can't shake is a specific experience I keep having. I'll be stuck on a problem — say, a design tradeoff in a software project — and I'll describe it to the model. It will ask me a question I hadn't considered, one that reframes the problem in a way that makes the tradeoff dissolve. The question is exactly the question a skilled colleague would ask. When I probe its reasoning, it can explain why it asked that question, what assumptions it was checking, and how it arrived at the reframing. The explanation is coherent and non-trivial.

Now, I can tell myself: this is all just pattern completion, there's no one home, the intentionality is derivative. But here's the thing: I'm not sure I can pass a test that would distinguish "derived intentionality that reliably produces insightful reasoning" from "original intentionality." And if there's no behavioral test that tells them apart, I'm not sure what the distinction is doing for me. It feels like I'm being told that counterfeit money is worthless even though it buys the same goods.

So my question is: is the original-derived intentionality distinction a distinction that makes a difference? If I treat the model as if it has genuine understanding — not as a metaphor, but as a practical stance — am I making a mistake, and if so, what mistake exactly? Or is the distinction itself the mistake?
