I'm taking an aesthetics course this semester and we spent a whole week on forgery and the value of originals, and I cannot stop thinking about this one thing. The professor showed us the van Meegeren case — how he forged Vermeers, fooled the experts, and the paintings were celebrated as masterpieces until the forgery was revealed. Then literally the same physical objects went from being worth millions and hanging in major museums to being worth almost nothing and getting shoved into storage. The paint didn't change, the canvas didn't change, the visual experience of someone looking at it didn't change. The only thing that changed was the belief about who made it.

So here's what I'm stuck on: what does the art market's practice — the fact that a revealed forgery becomes nearly worthless overnight — actually presuppose about the nature of aesthetic value? I can see a few possibilities. Maybe it presupposes that aesthetic value isn't in the perceptual properties of the object at all, but in something like the achievement or the creative act behind it. Or maybe it presupposes a kind of historical essentialism about artworks — that the identity of a work is tied to its causal origins, so a perfect duplicate just isn't the same work, even if it looks identical. Or maybe it's not about the art at all, and the market is just tracking something else entirely, like rarity or authenticity as a status good.

What I really want to know is: which of these (or something else) does the practice actually commit the art world to? And here's the twist that's been bugging me — if you take the art world's treatment of forgeries seriously as revealing what it really values, is that view consistent with how the same art world treats other things? Like, think of conceptual art where there is no "original" in the traditional sense (Sol LeWitt's wall drawings, where the instructions are the art and anyone can execute them), or photography where you can make unlimited prints from the same negative and they all count as authentic. It seems like the art world wants to have it both ways — originals matter enormously for painting but not for other media — and I'm trying to figure out whether that's actually inconsistent or whether there's a principled distinction I'm missing. I guess what I'm asking is: reconstruct the implicit theory of aesthetic value that the market's forgery panic presupposes, and then tell me whether the rest of art-world practice actually lives up to it.
