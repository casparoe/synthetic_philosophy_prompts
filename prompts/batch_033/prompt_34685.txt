The St. Petersburg paradox has been debated since 1738, and by now the moves are so familiar that the literature has largely ossified into camps: those who say the paradox shows that expected utility theory needs to be constrained by bounding the utility function, those who say it reveals that we should discount or ignore sufficiently small probabilities, those who say it exposes a problem with using expected value in the absence of finite resources, and those who say the lesson is that decision theory needs something other than expected utility maximization altogether. Each camp has a canonical reply to the others, and the debate has been at a standstill for decades. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a substantive disagreement about the structure of rational preference — a question that could in principle be settled by further formal or empirical work? Or is it a disagreement about what we want a theory of rational choice to do: describe how people actually reason, prescribe how they ought to reason, or offer a formal explication of a pre-theoretic concept of rationality whose boundaries were never sharp to begin with? Are the camps answering different questions under the same name, and if so, what questions? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a theorem, an experiment, a conceptual distinction, or an acknowledgment that the paradox is not a problem to be solved but a permanent feature of the landscape that different theories will handle differently?
