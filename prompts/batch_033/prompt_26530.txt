I'm taking a seminar on weakness of will and moral psychology this term, and we've covered Socrates on akrasia, Aristotle's account of reason overcome by appetite, Davidson's partitioned model of practical reasoning, Watson on valuing versus desiring, Holton on resolution-revision, Frankfurt on higher-order volitions, and the behavioral-economics literature on hyperbolic discounting and time-inconsistent preferences. I want to make a decision tree for my study group that takes someone from the basic phenomenon — "I sometimes act against my own better judgment" — to the position that best captures their diagnosis of what is going on when that happens.

The tree should have roughly eight to twelve leaves. Please include at least the following positions as leaves: Socratic intellectualism (the view that genuine akrasia is impossible and apparent cases are really ignorance of what is best), the Aristotelian view (akrasia as correct judgment temporarily silenced or overpowered by appetite), Davidson's account (acting against an all-things-considered judgment, explained by a partitioned model of practical reasoning in which the agent fails to honor the principle of continence), Watson's distinction between valuing and desiring (the weak-willed agent desires what they do not value, and the desire wins), Holton's account (weakness of will is not acting against a contemporaneous judgment but over-readily revising a prior resolution), a hyperbolic-discounting account (apparent weakness of will is really time-inconsistent preferences — steep discounting of future rewards — rather than any failure of rationality at the moment of action), and a Frankfurt-style structural view (weakness of will as a defect in the coherence or stability of higher-order volitions). If there are other positions that naturally occupy a distinct region of the logical space, include them as well, but don't add leaves just to pad the count.

Each branch node should be a concrete question or brief case that forces a choice between the positions downstream of it, phrased so that someone can answer without already knowing which position the answer points to. Where the logical space contains a combination of answers that no one defends, flag it: say briefly whether the position is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is deeply unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate given the questions you've used, and explain what further question would be needed to split them.

Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on this topic. Don't present a contested reading — for example, of what Davidson really meant by the principle of continence, or whether Holton's account successfully captures the ordinary concept of weakness of will, or whether hyperbolic discounting genuinely explains away the phenomenon rather than redescribing it — as though it were the consensus view.
