**Question**

Compare and contrast counterfactual theories of causation, as developed by David Lewis, with interventionist theories of causation, as developed by James Woodward and Judea Pearl. Pay particular attention to (i) the structural differences between the two approaches and (ii) how each handles the problem of causal preemption.

---

**Student's Answer**

Counterfactual and interventionist theories both treat causal claims as claims about what would happen under certain hypothetical conditions, but they differ sharply in how they spell out those conditions. Lewis's counterfactual theory analyses causation in terms of counterfactual dependence between distinct events: an event \(c\) causes an event \(e\) just in case, had \(c\) not occurred, \(e\) would not have occurred (or, more generally, there is a chain of such dependencies linking \(c\) to \(e\)). The semantics for these counterfactuals is given by a similarity relation over possible worlds—roughly, we go to the closest world where \(c\) does not occur and check whether \(e\) occurs there. Lewis's project is reductive: he aims to analyse causation in non-causal terms, and his possible-worlds apparatus is meant to supply the resources for doing so.

Interventionist theories, by contrast, dispense with counterfactuals altogether and instead analyse causation in terms of interventions. On Woodward's account, \(X\) causes \(Y\) if and only if there is some possible intervention on \(X\) that would change the value of \(Y\) (or the probability distribution over \(Y\)). An intervention is an exogenous manipulation that sets the value of \(X\) independently of its normal causes, breaking the arrows into \(X\) in the causal graph. This gives the theory a tight connection to experimental practice: to test whether \(X\) causes \(Y\), you wiggle \(X\) and see whether \(Y\) wiggles. Pearl formalises this using structural equations and the do-calculus, which allows one to derive testable predictions from causal graphs even when direct experimentation is impossible.

The structural differences are significant. Lewis's theory is built around events and possible worlds; interventionist theories are built around variables and causal graphs. Lewis's account is reductive and metaphysically ambitious—it attempts to say what causation is in terms that do not presuppose causation. Woodward's account is non-reductive: the notion of an intervention is itself causal, but Woodward argues that this circularity is not vicious because the theory is not intended as a reductive analysis. Instead, it provides a systematic way of representing and reasoning about causal relationships.

When it comes to preemption, counterfactual theories handle the problem straightforwardly by tracing chains of counterfactual dependence. In a case of early preemption—where Suzy's rock shatters the bottle and Billy's would have done so had Suzy's not—we can trace a chain of counterfactual dependence from Suzy's throw to the shattering (her throw caused the rock to be in flight, which caused the impact, etc.), and the fact that Billy's rock would have shattered the bottle in the absence of Suzy's throw does not break this chain. Interventionist theories avoid the problem of preemption entirely because they are formulated in terms of variables rather than particular events. In the interventionist framework, we simply ask whether an intervention on Suzy's throw changes the outcome, holding fixed the fact that Billy's throw occurs. The structural equation approach handles this cleanly, and preemption simply does not arise as a difficulty.

In summary, while both theories are broadly counterfactual in spirit, the interventionist approach represents a significant advance: it avoids the metaphysical extravagance of Lewis's possible worlds, it connects more naturally with scientific practice, and it sidesteps the preemption problem that plagued earlier counterfactual accounts. Its main cost is that it gives up on the reductive ambition that motivated Lewis.
