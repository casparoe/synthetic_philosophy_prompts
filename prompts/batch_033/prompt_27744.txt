I've been reading a lot of the old back-and-forth about heritability on rationalist-adjacent blogs and Twitter, and I keep noticing a specific conversational pattern that I don't have a good name for. It goes like this:

Person A says something like "twin studies show IQ is 60–80% heritable, so cognitive ability is mostly genetic." Person B (correctly) points out that heritability is a population statistic — it's the proportion of phenotypic variance accounted for by genetic variance in a given environment, it doesn't tell you about an individual, it doesn't mean "60% genetic and 40% environmental" for any particular person, high heritability doesn't imply immutability, etc. All true.

But then, having made this correction, Person B often swings to the other extreme and starts talking as if the heritability estimate conveys basically zero information — as if we learned nothing from the fact that, in the populations studied, genetic differences among people account for more than half the variance. And Person A, having been burned by the correction, now treats any mention of heritability as suspect and stops engaging with the actual finding. So the conversation ends up further from the truth than where it started, because both parties have now tacitly agreed that the heritability number is either everything or nothing, and the nuanced middle — "this number tells us something real but highly constrained, and here's what it does and doesn't license" — gets lost.

I see this pattern in other domains too (something about a technical correction making people overcorrect into a mirror-image error, and then the conversation settles there because neither side wants to defend the messy middle), but it seems especially sticky with heritability. Is there a name for this specific dynamic, where a valid statistical correction gets metabolized as "therefore the statistic is meaningless" rather than "therefore the statistic means something more precise and limited"? It's not quite a pendulum swing or plain overcorrection, because there's something about the asymmetry — Person B is factually right about the correction but wrong about what follows from it, and Person A learns the wrong lesson from being corrected. If there's no established name, what would you call it and how would you distinguish it from ordinary overcorrection or from the motte-and-bailey?
