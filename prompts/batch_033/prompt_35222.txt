I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of Buddhist philosophy and contemporary normative ethics, and I've become interested in a position called tranquilism — roughly, the view that what ultimately matters, from an ethical standpoint, is the absence of craving and suffering, and that positive states of pleasure or flourishing have no intrinsic value except insofar as they displace or prevent craving and suffering. The view draws on elements of Buddhist thought (particularly the diagnosis of dukkha and tanha) but formulates them as a claim within analytic normative ethics, with all the apparatus of reasons, value theory, and so on. Proponents argue that this isn't simply negative utilitarianism by another name, because the target isn't suffering simpliciter but specifically the restless, grasping quality of mind that Buddhist psychology identifies as the root of dissatisfaction — and because the view makes a distinctive claim about what well-being consists in, not just about what we have reason to prevent.

I've also been reading Schopenhauer, who was one of the first major Western philosophers to engage seriously with Buddhist and Hindu ideas. Schopenhauer's ethics centers on the claim that the will — the blind, ceaseless striving that constitutes the inner nature of everything — is the source of all suffering, and that the only genuine ethical response is compassion, which recognizes the identical will in all beings and thereby quietens one's own egoistic striving. His ideal is the denial of the will-to-live, culminating in the ascetic who has extinguished desire and achieved a kind of nothingness that is nevertheless, in his view, the only true good. For Schopenhauer, this isn't just a psychological recommendation but a metaphysical achievement: the will is the thing-in-itself, and its denial is a kind of self-negation of reality at the individual level.

Here's my question: if Schopenhauer were alive today and encountered tranquilism as a position in contemporary normative ethics, what would he make of it? On the face of it, there's a striking convergence: both see craving and striving as the root of suffering and both locate ethical value in its cessation. But I suspect the differences run deep. Schopenhauer's denial of the will is embedded in his transcendental idealism and his pessimism about the phenomenal world; tranquilism, by contrast, is presented as a claim about what states of affairs are valuable, without the metaphysical apparatus of a noumenal will. Would Schopenhauer see tranquilism as a welcome naturalization of his core insight — a way to get the ethical conclusion without the idealist metaphysics — or would he regard it as a superficial appropriation that misses the point, namely that the will isn't just a psychological phenomenon to be managed but the noumenal reality that must be transcended? And what about tranquilism's indifference to positive well-being: would Schopenhauer, who saw all pleasure as merely the temporary relief from striving, endorse this, or would his pessimism actually go further than tranquilism in some respects and less far in others? I'm looking for a nuanced projection that maps specific points of convergence and divergence, not just a thumbs-up or thumbs-down.
