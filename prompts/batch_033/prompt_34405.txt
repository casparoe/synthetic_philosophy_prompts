Analyse the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence's use of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) to decide which treatments the NHS in England will fund, as a case study in the debate about the currency of egalitarian justice. I know the basic mechanics: NICE appraises a treatment by its cost per QALY against a threshold, and treatments above the threshold are generally not recommended, with some exceptions for end-of-life and highly specialised care. What I want is the theory. Take a welfare egalitarian, a resource egalitarian, and a capability theorist, and say how each would assess the QALY as a metric for allocating health-care resources: which framework NICE's method most closely operationalises, and where each framework condemns or complicates it. In particular, does the capability approach actually object to the way QALYs treat disabled people, and does resource egalitarianism have a clean answer to the charge that QALY maximisation discriminates against those with lower baseline quality of life? Finally, what does the episode show about the frameworks themselves: is NICE's method best read as a compromise between them, as a defensible operationalisation of one of them, or as evidence that the debate is less decisive in practice than its participants assume?
