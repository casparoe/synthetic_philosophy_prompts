I've been reading the free speech debates in rationalist and adjacent spaces for years — the deplatforming arguments, the concept of "stochastic terrorism," the paradox of tolerance, all of it — and I keep noticing a pattern. Both sides invoke Mill's harm principle, and both sides seem to think it settles the question in their favor. One side says: Mill was clear that the only justification for restricting speech is preventing harm to others, and hate speech predictably leads to violence against vulnerable groups — QED. The other side says: Mill's whole point was that speech doesn't count as harmful just because someone might eventually act on it; the harm has to be direct and the causal chain short — otherwise the principle collapses into a license to censor anything. These aren't fringe readings; you can find sophisticated versions of both in the literature going back to the Hart-Devlin debate and forward through contemporary work on speech acts and indirect harm.

This debate has been running for over a century and a half, with extremely smart people on both sides, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not another summary of the positions.

My working hypothesis is that the real dispute isn't about the harm principle at all — it's about what counts as a harm "caused by" speech. One side has a narrow, mechanistic, but-for conception of causation: speech causes harm only when it's the triggering event for a discrete, identifiable act by a specific agent. The other side has a probabilistic, population-level conception: speech causes harm when it shifts the distribution of outcomes in expectation, even if no single act can be traced to a single utterance. If that's right, the impasse is downstream of a disagreement about causation and aggregation that Mill never addressed and that neither side acknowledges as the real issue.

But I could be wrong. Maybe the disagreement is about whether offense or dignitary injury counts as harm. Maybe it's about whether liberty is lexically prior to welfare, so that even clear causal links to harm wouldn't justify restriction. Maybe the whole framing is a category mistake — Mill's principle was never meant to be operationalized as a decision procedure for policy, and both sides are misusing it. Or maybe it's a fundamentally normative disagreement that no amount of conceptual clarification can dissolve.

Diagnose this debate. Support your diagnosis with what each side actually argues, or would argue if pressed. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or whether we should expect the impasse to be permanent and why. Keep it to 500 words.
