So two friends of mine got into this argument about creativity and I'm still not sure who's right. We were talking about Hofstadter's idea that creativity is basically "knob-twiddling" — that making something new is really about taking an existing concept and varying its parameters, like you've got a mental mixing board and you slide the faders around until something interesting pops out. One friend, Marcus, thinks this is basically right and actually quite profound. The other, Lena, thinks it's either false or so vague it's meaningless.

Lena started it: "The knob-twiddling thing is just reductive. Einstein didn't just 'twiddle some knobs' on Newtonian physics — he overturned the whole framework. If you call that knob-twiddling, the word has lost all meaning."

Marcus: "But that's exactly the point — the knobs aren't just the obvious parameters like 'mass' or 'velocity.' They're at the level of concepts. Einstein twiddled the knob of 'simultaneity,' which nobody even realized was a knob you could turn. Part of the creative act is discovering which knobs exist."

Lena: "Right, so the model is unfalsifiable. After any creative breakthrough, you can just retroactively declare that the innovator 'discovered a new knob.' It's a just-so story. The model doesn't predict anything or rule anything out."

Marcus: "It's not meant to be a predictive theory, it's a descriptive one. It's saying: look, creativity always works on and transforms existing conceptual material. It doesn't come from nowhere. The interesting question is how existing structures get varied, not whether they do."

Lena: "But that's just the truism that 'creativity has precedents' dressed up in engineering metaphors. Everyone already agrees that new ideas don't come from a vacuum. The knob-twiddling image suggests something stronger — that the space of possibilities is somehow pre-structured and creativity is just exploring it. But real paradigm shifts restructure the space itself."

Marcus: "And restructuring the space is itself a kind of meta-knob-twiddling. You can twiddle the knobs that determine what counts as a knob."

Lena: "So now it's knobs all the way down. Every objection gets absorbed by adding another layer of abstraction. That's not a model, that's a rhetorical strategy."

At this point they looked at me and I said I'd need to think about it. Their both smart and I can see merit on both sides. Could you go through their exchange and adjudicate it? Like, who's scoring valid points at each stage, who's misunderstanding whom, and what are both of them missing about how Hofstadter's model is actually supposed to work? Keep it to 500 words max.
