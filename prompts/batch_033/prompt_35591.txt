My thesis is this: Pascal's fragment on justice and force (the one that begins with the joint necessity of justice and force and ends with the claim that, unable to make might obey right, we made right obey might) contains a genuine and underappreciated insight about political legitimacy. Specifically, I argue that for any account of legitimacy to be complete, it must treat normative justification and effective coercive power as two irreducible and jointly necessary components — neither reducible to the other, and neither sufficient on its own. I then argue that the dominant liberal tradition, by grounding legitimacy in consent (actual or hypothetical), fails to theorize the force-dimension adequately, and that this failure explains certain recurring embarrassments in liberal theory: its difficulty saying what legitimates the enforcement of laws against those who demonstrably do not consent, and its tendency to treat the state's coercive apparatus as a conceptually external add-on rather than as internal to legitimacy itself. I close by sketching what a Pascalian repair would look like: an account in which a regime is legitimate only if it is both normatively defensible to those subject to it and capable of effectively commanding compliance, where these two conditions constrain each other rather than collapsing into one.
