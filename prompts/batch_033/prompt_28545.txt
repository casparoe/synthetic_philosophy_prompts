A friend of mine is really into Hume and was explaining his argument against miracles to me, and now I think I've found a problem but I'm not sure if I'm just confused.

Here's the argument as he laid it out:

(1) A miracle is a violation of a law of nature.
(2) A law of nature is established by firm and unalterable experience — basically, a uniformity we've never seen broken.
(3) So the prior probability of any miracle, based on all our past experience, is about as low as a probability can get.
(4) When someone testifies that a miracle occurred, we have to weigh the improbability of the miracle against the improbability that the testimony is false (the witness is lying, mistaken, deceived, etc.).
(5) Since the miracle is maximally improbable, it will always be more probable that the testimony is false — unless the falsehood of the testimony would itself be a kind of miracle.
(6) Therefore, testimony can never rationally establish that a miracle occurred.

Now here's my worry. Replace "miracle" with "experimental result that contradicts a deeply entrenched scientific theory." Before Einstein, every observation ever made confirmed Newtonian mechanics. That's about as firm and unalterable as experience gets. So when the first anomalous results came in — the Michelson-Morley experiment, say — Hume's logic seems to say: it's more probable that the experimenters screwed up than that Newton is wrong. And in fact that's what a lot of physicists did say at first. But eventually the evidence accumulated and it became rational to accept relativity.

But Hume's argument, at least as my friend stated it, doesn't seem to have a way to handle accumulation. If each individual piece of testimony for a miracle is always outweighed by the prior, then ten pieces or a hundred should also be outweighed — because each one individually is testimony for something maximally improbable. Unless you say independent testimonies can multiply in a way that eventually overcomes the prior, but then you've admitted testimony *can* establish a miracle, which is exactly what the argument is supposed to rule out.

So it looks like the argument proves too much: it would make it irrational to ever accept testimony for a paradigm-shifting scientific discovery. But we do, and we think we're rational to do so.

Where exactly does the analogy break? Is the problem in premise (2) — is a law of nature different in kind from a merely very-well-confirmed generalization? Is it in premise (5) — maybe the probability that testimony is false can in fact be lower than the prior probability of the event, even when that prior is extremely low? Or is there something about the structure of scientific testimony (replicability, independent verification, multiple lines of evidence) that Hume's framework can actually accomodate, and my friend just stated it too crudely? I feel like the argument as stated is too strong but I can't figure out which step to give up without also giving up reasonable epistemic caution about wild claims.
