You're a political philosopher who specializes in democratic theory and populism. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming candidate rules before you commit to one.

My friend is a political theorist who works on populism, and whenever I ask her whether some particular thing counts as the will of the people she gives these sharp yes/no verdicts. I've kept a list:

- Yes to a referendum that passed 63–37 with 79% turnout, after an 18-month campaign with wall-to-wall coverage.
- No to a politician who lost a presidential election by six points but said his mass rallies showed the real people were with him.
- Yes to a climate law that passed the legislature 82–18 after a two-year public consultation, and that has polled between 66% and 71% approval every quarter for three years.
- No to a general strike and street blockades demanding that the government reverse a policy, when every poll over the last year showed 57–61% of the public actually supporting the policy.

Next week her state votes on a ballot meausre to repeal a housing law. It got almost no media coverage, most voters told exit pollsters they'd only heard of it in the final week, and it passed 50.4 to 49.6 with 38% turnout. A poll two weeks before the vote showed 52% planning to vote no.

I'm less interested in the bare answer than in the rule she seems to be running, so don't just give me a yes or no. What are the candidate rules that fit her four earlier verdicts equally well? What does each candidate rule say about the ballot measure? What's your best guess about how she'll call it, and why? And what further case would most cleanly seperate the remaining candidate rules from each other?
