**Question:**

Explain what a money pump argument is and what it is supposed to establish about rational preference. Your answer should address the "sophisticated chooser" objection: explain the objection, and then evaluate whether it defuses the money pump argument. Conclude by stating what you think money pump arguments, taken as a whole, actually show about the requirements of diachronic rationality.

**Student Answer:**

A money pump is a sequence of trades that extracts resources from an agent who has a certain kind of defective preference structure. Each trade is individually advantageous by the agent's own lights, but the sequence as a whole leaves the agent worse off than when they started — typically back where they began, minus some money paid to facilitate the trades. The classic case targets intransitive preferences. Suppose an agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. If they start holding C, they will pay a small fee to swap C for B (since they prefer B to C), then pay to swap B for A, then pay to swap A for C. They end up with C again but poorer. Because each trade was voluntary and preference-driven, the agent has been pumped for money. This shows that intransitive preferences are irrational: a rational agent would not have a preference structure that guarantees a sure loss if acted on.

The money pump argument is meant to support the claim that rational preferences must satisfy the axioms of expected utility theory, of which transitivity is one. If your preferences violate transitivity, you are exploitable, and any preference structure that guarantees a sure loss cannot be rational. This is why decision theorists treat transitivity as a non-negotiable requirement of rationality.

One important objection is the sophisticated chooser response. A sophisticated chooser is someone who anticipates the full sequence of trades and reasons backward. Knowing they have intransitive preferences and knowing a money pump is possible, they can simply decline to make the first trade — refusing to swap C for B — and thereby avoid the pump entirely. This seems to defuse the argument: the agent is not actually forced to lose money.

However, the sophisticated chooser response does not fully succeed. Even if the agent avoids being pumped, they are stuck holding C when they prefer B to C. They are in a position where they cannot act on their own preferences without being exploited. This is itself a kind of irrationality: their preferences recommend a course of action they cannot rationally follow. So the money pump argument still has force — it shows that intransitive preferences lead to practical incoherence, even if the agent is clever enough to avoid the monetary loss. Money pump arguments therefore successfully establish that rational preferences must be transitive, and more broadly that the axioms of expected utility theory are requirements of diachronic rationality.
