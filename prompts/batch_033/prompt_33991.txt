A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about a thought experiment, and we ended up genuinely stuck. I am hoping you can go through our exchange and tell us who was right about what.

The case was this: a terrorist has taken six hostages and tells you that if you shoot one of them yourself, he will release the other five; if you refuse, he will shoot all six. My friend — call him Daniel — said it would be wrong to shoot the one, full stop. I pushed back, and here is roughly how it went.

(1) I said: if the reason it is wrong to kill an innocent person is that doing so fails to respect them as an end in themselves, then surely the situation calls for shooting the one — because refusing means six people get treated as mere means (by the terrorist) rather than just one (by you), and the whole point of the principle is to minimise that kind of treatment.

(2) Daniel replied that the prohibition on killing is agent-relative: it forbids *me* from killing, regardless of what others will do. The constraint is not a goal to minimise rights-violations in the world; it is a restriction on my own actions.

(3) I said that makes the constraint look as though it is really about keeping one's own hands clean — a kind of moral fastidiousness — rather than about actually protecting persons from being used. If the deep rationale is respect for rational nature, then agent-relativity seems like a fetish for one's own causal role.

(4) Daniel answered that there is nothing narcissistic about it: morality is fundamentally about how each agent relates to others from her own practical standpoint, not about producing states of affairs from a neutral point of view. The prohibition is not a means to the end of fewer rights-violations; it is a direct requirement of recognising others as inviolable.

(5) I came back with this: if the constraint is genuinely about recognising inviolability, then why does the constraint not itself generate a reason to violate it when doing so is the only way to honour the inviolability of a greater number? If each person's inviolability matters, then five matters more than one, and a morality that forbids you from acting on that fact seems to have lost sight of what inviolability was supposed to be about in the first place.

(6) Daniel said I was smuggling in consequentialism by treating 'inviolability' as a value to be promoted rather than a status to be respected. Respecting a status means not violating it yourself, not minimising its violation by others.

(7) I said that at this point the view seems to have no resources left to explain why we should care about the status in the first place — it has become a bare rule with a rationale that can never actually guide us when it matters most.

We went round in circles after that. Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the seven steps, say whether the point was sound, weak, or somewhere in between, and give a brief justification. Then say who you think had the better of the argument overall, and whether there is a way out of the impasse that neither of us saw.
