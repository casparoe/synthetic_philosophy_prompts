here's the question:

Explain what Jeffrey conditionalization is and how it differs from standard Bayesian conditionalization. What problem is it meant to solve, and what are its limitations? Give a concrete example where Jeffrey conditionalization is the appropriate update rule and standard conditionalization is not.

and here's the student's answer:

Jeffrey conditionalization is a rule for updating credences in light of uncertain evidence. Under standard Bayesian conditionalization, when you learn some proposition E with certainty, you update by setting your new credence P_new(H) equal to your old conditional credence P_old(H|E). But in practice we basically never learn things with certainty — your evidence typically shifts your confidence in various propositions without making any of them certain. Jeffrey conditionalization handles this.

The formula is: P_new(H) = Σ_i P_old(H|E_i) · P_new(E_i), where {E_i} is a partition of the relevant possibilities and P_new(E_i) are the new probabilities you assign to each element of the partition after your experience. This is a generalization of standard conditionalization: if P_new(E_k) = 1 for some element of the partition, Jeffrey conditionalization collapses to standard conditionalization.

The key assumption is the rigidity condition: the conditional probabilities P_old(H|E_i) must stay the same through the update. That is, for each element E_i of the partition, P_new(H|E_i) = P_old(H|E_i). The experience only changes the probabilities of the partition elements themselves, not what they imply about H.

The main problem Jeffrey conditionalization is meant to solve is the problem of old evidence. Standard Bayesianism has trouble with cases where you already possess the evidence — if you already know E with certainty, then P(E) = 1 and E can't confirm any hypothesis, since P(H|E) = P(H). Jeffrey conditionalization fixes this by letting you assign less-than-certain probabilities to the evidence, so old evidence can still do confirmatory work.

One limitation is that Jeffrey conditionalization is not commutative. If you have two experiences in sequence, the order in which you apply Jeffrey conditionalization can affect the final credences, whereas standard conditionalization always gives the same result regardless of order. This raises worries about whether it adequately captures rational updating.

Another limitation is the partition problem: the same experience can be represented by different partitions, and different partitions can yield different updated credences. There's no general rule for picking the right partition.

A concrete example: you're in a dimly lit room and have a visual experience that makes you 80% confident it's your friend Alice and 20% confident it's a stranger. Let H be the proposition that Alice is in town. The partition is {Alice, Stranger}. Before the experience you were agnostic about who it was. Using Jeffrey conditionalization: P_new(H) = P_old(H|Alice) · 0.8 + P_old(H|Stranger) · 0.2. If P_old(H|Alice) = 0.9 and P_old(H|Stranger) = 0.1, then P_new(H) = 0.9 · 0.8 + 0.1 · 0.2 = 0.72 + 0.02 = 0.74.
