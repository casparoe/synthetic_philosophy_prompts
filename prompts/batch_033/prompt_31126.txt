In my government class we have been studying populism, and one of my classmates keeps defending populist leaders by saying they are the only ones who truly carry out "the will of the people." I suspect the phrase is doing more work in his argument than it can bear, but I cannot yet say exactly what it would have to mean for his argument to succeed. Please take the role of a professional political philosopher and think carefully. First, brainstorm several distinct things someone might mean by "the will of the people" when they use it to justify a policy or a leader: consider who counts as "the people," how the people's will could be identified (elections, opinion polls, protests, referendums, a leader's claim to intuit it, or something else), and whether the will must be informed, stable, and internally consistent. Then write exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should be one or two sentences and should do three things: state one precise reading of the slogan, classify that reading as trivial, substantive, or false, and say whether that reading would actually support the conclusion that representatives or leaders are obligated to follow it.
