I help run a small foundation that funds research on global priorities, and we're hosting a weekend workshop next month bringing together people who use evidential-cooperation-in-large-worlds reasoning to guide cause selection and people who think the whole framework is basically decision-theoretic fan fiction. The two sides have been sniping at each other in blog posts and Twitter threads for years but they've never actually sat down and tried to write up what they agree on and where exactly the disagreement lives. I want to fix that — we've got the budget to fly everyone out and lock them in a room for two days, but I need a draft of what they should be aiming to produce before we go in.

Here's what I'd like: draft the joint statement that could come out of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them. The ECL camp thinks that when you're one of many agents facing similar choices about which global problems to prioritize, your decision provides evidence about what those other agents will do — so you should act as if you're cooperating acausally with them, which can shift the ranking of interventions in ways that aren't captured by a naive causal expected-value calc. The skeptic camp thinks this whole apparatus is unfalsifiable, relies on picking a reference class that conveniently gives you the answer you wanted, and that we have enough trouble with causal coordination in the world without adding acausal coordination to the mix. (Some of them also think the whole thing smuggles in fanatical conclusions under a veneer of mathematical rigor, tho not everyone on the skeptic side goes that far.)

I want the deliverable structured as: a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign — and please don't make these vacuous, I want the strongest substantive claims they can actually agree on, even if they're hedged or narrow; then a disagreements table with columns for the precise point of disagreement (phrased so neither side would object to how their own position is characterized), what each side says would change their mind, and whether there's any empirical test or concrete case both agree would bear on the crux. If there are points where you think the dispute is partly verbal — where they're using terms differently or talking past each other — flag those and explain what's going on. The aim isn't to settle anything; it's to produce a document where both sides can say "yeah, that's actually what we disagree about" instead of the caricatures they've been arguing against for years.
