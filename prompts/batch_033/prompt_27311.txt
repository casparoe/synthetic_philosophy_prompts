I was reading this article about Berkeley's economics and it gave a summary of his views on money that I found really intriguing, but I'm not sure how much to trust it. Here's the relevant paragraph:

"It's fascinating how Berkeley's idealism led directly to his views on money. Since he thought physical objects don't exist independently of minds, he concluded that gold and silver have no more intrinsic value than paper does — money is purely a social convention, a shared idea. In The Querist (1735–37), he argued that Ireland should abandon gold and silver currency entirely and replace it with paper money issued by a national bank, because what matters for an economy isn't the material stuff money is made of but the industry and labor it can set in motion. He called money a 'ticket or counter,' meaning it's just a record-keeping device for tracking who owes what to whom. This was basically a proto-fiat currency theory, way ahead of its time, and it's why Berkeley is sometimes credited with anticipating modern monetary theory."

Can you go through this summary and tell me what's accurate, what's oversimplified, and what's just wrong?
