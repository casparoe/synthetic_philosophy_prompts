I am drafting a paper that attempts to reconcile semantic externalism with a broadly internalist approach to cognitive science, and I want to know whether my starting commitments are consistent before I build an argument on them. Here they are:

(1) Semantic externalism about natural kind terms, following Putnam: the extension of a term like 'water' is fixed by the actual nature of the stuff in the speaker's environment, not by associated descriptions, and this means that two molecule-for-molecule identical speakers in different environments can mean different things by the same word.

(2) Externalism about mental content, following Burge: the content of propositional attitudes such as belief is partly individuated by facts about the speaker's linguistic community and physical environment.

(3) The doctrine of privileged access: a competent speaker can know the contents of their own occurrent thoughts without empirical investigation of the world — that is, by reflection alone.

(4) Physicalism about the mind: all mental facts supervene on physical facts, and there is no difference in mental state without a physical difference.

(5) Methodological individualism for cognitive science: psychological explanations should be formulated in terms of states and processes that supervene on the intrinsic physical properties of the individual organism — that is, cognitive science should proceed as if content is narrow.

(6) The simple expression view: the content of a subject's belief is the proposition expressed by the sentence that the subject would sincerely utter to express it.

I am aware that (2) and (3) are in well-known tension — the literature on externalism and self-knowledge is extensive, and Boghossian, McKinsey, and others have argued that the pair is unstable. But I am less confident about the other pairs. For example: does (1) conflict with (4) in a way that is distinct from the (2)–(3) problem? Is (5) straightforwardly incompatible with (1) and (2), or is there a defensible reading of methodological individualism that makes room for externalist content? Does (6) introduce difficulties that are not already present in the other commitments? And are any of these pairs only apparently in tension, such that a careful distinction shows them to be compatible after all?

Please assess each pair of commitments, distinguishing genuine from merely apparent tensions. For genuine tensions, rank them by severity and state, for each, which commitment would have to be modified or abandoned to resolve it, and what the least costly revision would be. For apparent tensions, explain what distinction or qualification reconciles the two commitments. The deliverable should be an audit of my position set, not a general survey of the literature. Please write your answer in British English.
