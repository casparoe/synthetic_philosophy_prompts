I'm giving a department colloquium next month at a program with a strong philosophy of language group — several faculty work on externalism, a couple are dyed-in-the-wool Burgeans, and there are a few two-dimensionalists in the mix, plus the usual smattering of grad students who've just read Putnam for the first time and think it's the most decisive argument in the history of philosophy. My talk defends a claim that I know will rub people the wrong way: I argue that the Twin Earth thought experiment, as it's standardly deployed, only gets you externalism about linguistic reference and truth-conditions, not externalism about mental content or cognitive significance. The move from Putnam's "meanings ain't in the head" to Burge-style claims about the individuation of mental states is, I think, too quick — it relies on a slide between two different notions of "content" that the Twin Earth scenario itself doesn't force us to conflate. I'm not denying externalism about mental content outright; I'm saying the standard argument doesn't establish it, and that the burden of proof is heavier than people admit. I've got a reconstruction of the argument that makes the equivocation explicit, and I spend the second half of the talk sketching what a genuinely internalist notion of narrow content could look like without falling into the traps people usually associate with it.
