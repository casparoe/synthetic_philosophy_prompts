I am a novelist working on a near-future political novel in which a small country has run its government as a futarchy for roughly three decades: an elected council sets an official welfare metric, and prediction markets decide which proposed policies are implemented according to whether they are expected to raise that metric. The novel is not a tract against the idea; I want the system to fail, if it fails, for the most intellectually serious reasons available, so that a reader who actually likes futarchy would have to take the failure seriously.

Please tier the following failure modes from probably fatal on its own down to real but probably manageable, and give a sentence or two for each placement. By fatal I mean likely to make the system, in practice, produce worse policy than a reasonably competent elected legislature would have produced. If two of the items are at bottom the same problem, say so and rank them together; if you think I have omitted a still more serious failure mode, add it and rank it.

- The metric problem: whatever welfare metric the council chooses (say, GDP per capita plus life expectancy) can itself be gamed, and futarchy shifts political conflict from choosing policies to choosing and redefining the metric.
- The counterfactual problem: a market can settle on outcomes under an adopted policy, but the alternative, what would have happened without it, is never observed, so the market cannot cleanly establish that the policy caused any improvement.
- Decision-market manipulation: because the market price does not merely predict the decision but triggers it, a wealthy actor can profit by moving the market to adopt a policy and then trading on the resulting outcomes.
- Thin markets: most concrete policy proposals will attract too few informed traders, with too little at stake, to produce reliable prices.
- Dollar-weighted influence: futarchy replaces one-person-one-vote with a system in which wealthy and well-financed actors have systematically more say over which policies are adopted.
- Short-termism: policies whose effects on the metric are quick and legible will be favored over slow, diffuse, or hard-to-measure improvements, even when the latter matter more.
- Policy interactions: because proposals are evaluated one at a time, a sequence of individually favorable decisions can be jointly harmful, and no single market prices those interactions.
