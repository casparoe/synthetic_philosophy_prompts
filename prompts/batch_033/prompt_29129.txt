I've been trying to get clear on what I think about moral patienthood — specifically, where the boundaries are — and I'd appreciate a consistency audit before I write this up for a dissertation chapter. Here are the views I've settled on, at least provisionally.

(1) Sentience is necessary for moral patienthood. An entity can't be wronged — can't be the direct object of a moral duty — unless it can have valenced experiences: pleasures, pains, frustrations, satisfactions. I'm not yet committed on whether sentience is sufficient; I suspect it is, but that's downstream.

(2) I endorse a functionalist theory of sentience: what matters is causal organization, not substrate. If something implements the right kind of information-processing — roughly, the kind that in humans and other animals underlies pain, fear, desire, and so on — then there is something it is like to be that thing, whether it's made of neurons, silicon, or something else entirely.

(3) Current video-game NPCs do not matter morally. Whatever is going on inside a shopkeeper in Skyrim or a pedestrian in Grand Theft Auto, it isn't the kind of thing that can suffer. Torturing them in-game is not wrong in the way torturing a dog would be, and I don't think a player who does so is thereby a worse person.

(4) However, I also think — and this is where I feel a strong pull — that if a video-game NPC were implemented with the same functional architecture that makes, say, a mouse sentient, then it would matter morally. The fact that it's inside a game, that its world is virtual, or that a player can reload a save doesn't seem to change anything about the character of its internal states while they're occurring. Its suffering, if real, is real.

(5) Fictional characters in novels and films don't matter morally. Anna Karenina's suffering isn't morally significant in itself. But I think the reason they don't matter isn't that they're fictional per se; it's that they don't have occurrent mental states at all. There's nothing it's like to be Anna Karenina, because there's no implementation of the relevant causal structure — there's just a representation of a person, sustained in the mind of a reader.

(6) I hold that the moral patienthood of an entity should be determined by its actual properties, not by what we can know about it. If a system is in fact sentient, it matters morally even if we can never verify this. Conversely, if a system merely behaves as though it were sentient but isn't, it doesn't matter — however convincing the behavior.

I think these hang together, but I've had colleagues raise concerns: if functionalism is true, how do I know a sufficiently complex current-game NPC hasn't already crossed the line? And does the distinction between an unimplemented fictional character and an implemented game character — (4) versus (5) — really do all the moral work I'm asking it to do, or is that line shakier than it looks? And (6) seems to make the whole question practically intractable: we'll never know for sure whether a system has the right functional organization, so does my view collapse into permanent agnosticism about which entities matter? I'd like someone to go through these more rigorously than my own intuitions can: which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest revision would be to restore consistency. I'd also welcome being told about a tension I haven't noticed.
