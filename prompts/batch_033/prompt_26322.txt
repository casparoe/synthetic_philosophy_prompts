I am a novelist at work on a book centered on an art forger — a figure loosely inspired by Han van Meegeren, the Dutch painter who forged Vermeers in the 1930s and 1940s, sold one to Hermann Göring, and after the war revealed the forgery to escape a collaboration charge, becoming a folk hero in the process. I want to use the actual van Meegeren episode to think through the philosophical question at the heart of the novel: what, if anything, makes an original valuable in a way that even a near-perfect copy is not?

I would like you to analyze the van Meegeren case through the lens of the philosophical debate about fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals. Specifically:

1. Walk through the major philosophical positions on why (or whether) originals possess a kind of value that even undetectable forgeries lack — the views of Goodman, Dutton, Lessing, and anyone else you think essential — and for each, say what it would make of the van Meegeren episode. Did something of genuine value go missing when the Vermeer in Göring's collection turned out to be a van Meegeren? Did something of value go missing when The Supper at Emmaus in the Boijmans Museum was revealed as a forgery? What does each theory say was lost — or not lost — in each case?

2. The van Meegeren episode has a feature that standard philosophical discussions of forgery rarely anticipate: the forger became a folk hero precisely for having deceived powerful people, and the trial — in which he had to paint a new forgery in front of witnesses to prove he was the maker — effectively celebrated his skill rather than condemning it. Does any theory of the value of originals have the resources to explain why the public reaction took this form? Or does the episode reveal a gap in the standard theories — something about the social meaning of deception, about who is deceived and to what end, that the literature has not adequately addressed?

3. Finally, tell me what the episode, taken as a whole, shows about the theories themselves. Does it confirm any of them? Does it embarrass any of them? Does it point toward a position that none of them quite articulates — perhaps one in which the value of an artwork is partly constituted by the social relations it instantiates, including relations of power and trust among artist, dealer, critic, collector, and public?

I know the broad outlines of the van Meegeren story, but feel free to supply any salient details I may be missing that matter for the analysis. I am less interested in the legal or biographical particulars than in what the case reveals about the metaphysics and ethics of authenticity in art.
