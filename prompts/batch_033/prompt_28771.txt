I serve on the clinical ethics committee at a teaching hospital, and we are reviewing a case that has split both the attending team and the family. I have been asked to draft the committee's written recommendation ahead of our meeting next week.

The patient is a seventy-four-year-old woman diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's six years ago. Shortly after her diagnosis, while still fully competent and after extended discussions with her physician and her two adult children, she executed a detailed advance directive. It states unambiguously that should she reach a stage where she can no longer recognize her children or sustain coherent conversation, she refuses all life-sustaining interventions — antibiotics for pneumonia, artificial nutrition and hydration, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation among them. It includes a personal statement explaining that she watched her own mother live nearly a decade in the final stages of dementia and considered that existence an indignity she would not wish on herself. No one disputes the directive's validity: it was properly witnessed, she was competent when she signed it, and it reflects views she held and discussed for years. She is now in the moderate-to-advanced stage of the disease. She does not consistently recognize her children, though she sometimes brightens when they visit. Nursing staff describe her as generally cheerful — she enjoys pudding, listens to music, and smiles at the birds outside her window. She does not express distress about her condition or appear to suffer. Last week she developed aspiration pneumonia. Under her advance directive, antibiotics would be withheld and she would receive comfort care only. Her son argues the directive must be honored: the woman who wrote it is the person whose wishes count, and overriding it would betray her autonomy. Her daughter argues that the woman in the bed is a different person in morally relevant respects — that she has found a contentment her earlier self could not have anticipated, and that withholding antibiotics from someone who now plainly wants to go on living would be a cruelty the earlier self had no right to impose. The attending physician is internally divided: she believes advance directives carry presumptive authority but finds herself unable to look at this patient and see someone whose life should end over the objections of a person who no longer seems to exist in the same way. The nursing staff lean toward treating. The hospital's policy on advance directives is silent on cases where the patient's current demeanor contradicts her prior instructions — it simply says directives should be followed absent reason to doubt their validity, which no one does here.

Please draft the committee's decision memo with the following sections, by name and in this order:

- **Summary and recommendation** (roughly 150 words): state the committee's recommendation and the core reasoning in a single paragraph an administrator could read and act on.
- **The case for honoring the advance directive** (roughly 400 words): present the strongest argument for withholding antibiotics and following the directive, engaging the underlying philosophical considerations about personal identity, autonomy, and the authority of the earlier self — not merely the procedural or legal surface.
- **The case for overriding the advance directive** (roughly 400 words): present the strongest argument for treating the pneumonia despite the directive, again engaging the philosophical substance — the moral status of the later self, the limits of advance commitment, and what it means to respect a person who has undergone this kind of change.
- **Verdict and action plan** (roughly 300 words): give the committee's decision with a concrete, time-bound course of action the clinical team can implement, and explain why the considerations on the losing side were ultimately outweighed.
- **Implications for future cases** (roughly 200 words): state what precedent, if any, this case sets for the hospital — what kinds of future conflicts it does and does not resolve, and what conditions would need to hold for the committee to reach a different conclusion in a superficially similar case.
