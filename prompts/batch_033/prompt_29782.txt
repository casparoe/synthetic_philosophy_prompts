Here's a rough set of notes for an essay I'm thinking of writing, on how to define honesty and deception for AI systems. I'd be keen to get your comments on it — mostly on whether the three-way distinction below actually earns its keep, and where it's most likely to fall over.

Working title: "Who's doing the deceiving?"

The basic move is to seperate out three senses in which an AI system might be called deceptive:

1. Intentional deception. The system itself has something like beliefs and goals, and produces an output with the aim of getting the user to form a false belief. This is the sense that most closely matches everyday lying, but it seems to rule out almost all present systems, because we have no good reason to ascribe the relevant intentions to them. It also makes deception nearly impossible to test from the outside.

2. Functional deception. An output counts as deceptive if a reasonable user, in the situation the system is used in, would predictably be misled by it, whatever is going on "inside". This catches the cases people actually worry about, like a chatbot confidently giving false medical advice or misrepresenting it's own limitations. But it also seems to over-generate: a broken calculator or a badly labelled map would come out deceptive, and we don't normally think of those as deceiving anyone. So maybe it's really a property of the user's epistemic situation rather than of the system.

3. Designer- or deployer-level deception. The deception lives in the people and processes behind the system: it's deceptive if it was built, fine-tuned, or marketed to mislead, or if the deployers present it as more capable or reliable than it is. This keeps the moral weight attached to agents, but it lets the system itself off the hook and doesn't handle emergent behaviours nobody explicitly designed.

On honesty, I want to resist defining it as "never outputs falsehoods", since an honest system can still be wrong. The more promising line is something like sincerity: not presenting uncertain claims as certain, not telling users what they want to hear (sycophancy), and not hiding known limitations. But sincerity normally relates what's said to what's believed, and it's not obvious a language model has beliefs in the right sense. Maybe one can reconstruct it as a relation between outputs and the system's own uncertainty estimates (e.g. logprobs), but that feels hacky and easy to game. I also want to use the old distinction between lying and bullshitting — bullshitting being indifference to the truth of what one says rather than intent to deceive — since a lot of AI falsehood looks more like that than like lying.

Some questions I'd especially like comments on:

- Is the three-way split right, or is one of these not really deception at all?
- Does the functional sense collapse into "merely false or misleading" and lose its distinctive content?
- Is it a mistake to define deception for systems at all, rather than for the practices and institutions around them?
- What's the strongest counterexample to the scheme as it stands?

When you comment, could you also say how confident you are in each main point, and flag the one you're least sure about?
