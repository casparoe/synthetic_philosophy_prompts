Some months ago I picked up a popular book about the brain, and one passage has stayed with me because I could not tell whether I was learning something deep or being misled. As best I can reconstruct it, the author's summary of split-brain research goes roughly like this: 'Once the corpus callosum is cut, the two hemispheres can no longer exchange information, and the patient is left with two distinct centers of consciousness. The left hemisphere, which houses language, reports seeing only what is presented to the right visual field; the right hemisphere, though mute, shows by pointing or drawing that it has seen what the patient verbally denies having seen. The two hemispheres appear to hold different memories and preferences, and the speaking hemisphere simply confabulates plausible stories to explain actions initiated by the other. Split-brain patients therefore show that consciousness can be divided, and that the unity of the self is an illusion.' I am a retired chemist, recently drawn into philosophy of mind, and I would value a careful audit of this paragraph. Which parts would a philosopher who works on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness accept, which parts would they regard as contestable, overstated, or subtly conflated, and does the final claim about the self follow from whatever in the summary survives correction? In particular, I suspect the passage runs together several different senses of 'unity of consciousness'; I would appreciate having them separated.
