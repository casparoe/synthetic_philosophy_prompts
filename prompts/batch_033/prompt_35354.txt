I am a philosophy professor, and I have been working through the logic of nuclear deterrence with my graduate seminar. We have arrived at an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the flaw. I would like you to diagnose it.

Here is the argument:

(1) A threat deters only if the threatened party believes the threatener would actually carry it out should the condition be triggered. (Call this the credibility condition.)

(2) Carrying out a nuclear retaliatory strike after one's own country has already been devastated serves no strategic purpose beyond fulfilling the threat itself; it guarantees further catastrophic destruction—to oneself, to the adversary, and likely to the rest of the world—with no offsetting benefit. Any agent who cares about outcomes ought to prefer not launching to launching at that point.

(3) A rational agent, by definition, does what it has most reason to do, and an agent who cares about outcomes has decisive reason not to launch a retaliatory strike once the condition is triggered.

(4) Therefore, a rational agent would not launch a retaliatory nuclear strike.

(5) Therefore, a rational agent's threat of nuclear retaliation is not credible (from 1 and 4).

(6) An agent whose threat is not credible cannot deter.

(7) Therefore, rational agents cannot deter nuclear attack.

So far this is merely a paradox for deterrence theory. But now consider the extension:

(8) The structure of the reasoning from (1) to (6) applies to any threat where carrying it out, once the condition is triggered, is worse for the threatener than not carrying it out. The only special feature of the nuclear case is the scale of the harm, not the logical structure.

(9) Many ordinary threats share this structure: a parent who threatens to cancel a family holiday over a child's misbehaviour, knowing that once the misbehaviour has occurred, cancelling the holiday makes everyone—including the parent—miserable for no further gain; a manager who threatens to fire a difficult-to-replace employee for a single infraction; a government that threatens to impose sanctions it knows will harm its own economy more than the target's.

(10) By parity of reasoning, rational agents cannot deter in any of these cases either.

(11) But plainly parents do deter misbehaviour with threats they would rather not carry out, managers do deter infractions, and governments do deter with sanctions threats. These are not marginal cases; they are pervasive features of social life.

(12) So either we must accept that these ordinary practices are illusory—that no one is ever deterred by a threat the threatener would prefer not to execute—or the argument from (1) to (7) contains a mistake.

I find (12) unacceptable: the conclusion that ordinary deterrence is impossible is far too strong. But I cannot see which premise to reject without losing something I need. If I reject (1), I seem to lose the very concept of deterrence. If I reject (2) or (3), I seem to abandon the idea that nuclear retaliation is irrational, which is morally and strategically appalling. If I try to drive a wedge between the nuclear case and the ordinary cases at step (8), I need a principle that distinguishes them without being ad hoc. And if I appeal to pre-commitment or Schelling-style brinkmanship, I worry I am simply redescribing the paradox rather than resolving it.

Please tell me which step or steps in this argument ought to be rejected, and explain what rejecting them costs. If the right answer is that the argument is valid and we must accept the conclusion, say so and explain what accepting it would mean for our understanding of rationality and deterrence. I am especially interested in whether the ordinary cases at (9) are genuinely parallel to the nuclear case, and if not, what principle separates them.

Kindly ensure your answer is written in British English.
