I've been arguing with a friend about whether "memes" are a real thing or just a loose metaphor, and we keep coming back to the ALS Ice Bucket Challenge from summer 2014. I know the standard story: it went viral because it was fun, celebrities did it, and it was for charity, and it raised over $100 million for the ALS Association before basically vanishing. But I dont want the usual hand-waving; I want to take the Darwinian theory of culture seriously for once and get a proper case analysis.

So: analyze the Ice Bucket Challenge as a case study for the meme/Darwinian view of culture. For context if you need it: people filmed themselves dumping a bucket of ice water over their heads, said they were doing it for ALS, donated, and nominated three other people to do it within 24 hours; there were earlier cold-water challenge variants floating around before the ALS version took off, and after that summer it died out fast. Be disciplined about the mapping. What exactly is the replicator here — the challenge as a whole, the nomination rule, the ice-water part, the video format, the charity mention? What counted as inheritance (what had to be copied for it to be "the same" challenge), and where did variation come from? Walk through the fitness-relevant features: fidelity, fecundity, longevity, and whatever selection pressures you can identify — platform algorithms, social emotions, the nomination mechanism, the deadline, the charity tie-in. Then tell me honestly where the Darwinian frame starts to strain: the challenge was at least partly deliberately designed, it mutated in odd ways, people could refuse or do both (dump water and donate), and it collapsed quickly. Is that a problem for the theory, or is it just how meme selection works? Finally, say what the episode should make us think about the theory itself: does it support the idea that culture is a Darwinian process, does it look more like intelligent design plus contagion, or does it show that the meme framework is too thin to do real explanatory work here?
