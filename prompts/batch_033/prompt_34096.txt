An epistemologist who defends conciliationism about peer disagreement and an epistemologist who defends a steadfast view have, after years of arguing past each other, agreed to run an adversarial collaboration. They have committed to producing a single joint statement that both can sign, with their residual disagreement made exact. Write the document they should produce.

The conciliationist position, as the two understand it, is that learning that an epistemic peer disagrees with you gives you a powerful higher-order reason to move your credence substantially toward the peer's view, absent special reason to think you are the one who got it right; on the strongest versions, when the peer is genuinely your equal in exposure to the evidence and in relevant ability, you are required to give your view and the peer's view equal weight. The steadfast position denies this: the higher-order evidence that a peer disagrees may be outweighed by your first-order assessment of the evidence itself, and one may reasonably retain one's original view without any special reason for thinking oneself superior.

The joint statement should have four parts.

First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign. These should be substantive rather than vacuous: claims about what the canonical cases of peer disagreement do and do not show, about which idealizations are doing argumentative work (equality of evidence, equality of competence, independence of assessment, absence of common causes of error), about what disagreement with a genuine peer is evidence for, and about what each side is not claiming. The two should be able to correct common misreadings of their own views in this section.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual point of dispute, give the formulation each side accepts for its own view, in its own preferred wording, alongside the other side's formulation. Where the two sides would each reject the other's formulation as stated, say so and indicate whether the resistance is to the wording or to the substance.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer to them would move at least one side. I expect these to include something like whether the facts about what one may rationally believe are unique given a body of evidence (uniqueness versus permissivism), whether the right response to higher-order evidence must be screened off from first-order reasoning in the way independence principles require, and whether a policy of retaining confidence in the face of peer disagreement can be insulated from the charge that it licenses bootstrapping or an illicit form of self-trust. Do not stop with these if the two parties would identify others; work out exactly which sub-questions would move someone.

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or empirical finding that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts it would show. These should be sharp enough that a well-designed test or a careful argument could, in principle, leave at least one side rationally unsettled.

Throughout, be careful to separate disputes that are merely verbal. In particular, separate claims about what one is rationally required to do from claims about what one is rationally permitted to do; separate the synchronic question of what a subject should think at the moment of learning of the disagreement from the diachronic question of how a subject should respond over time; and separate disagreement between strict epistemic equals from the more common case in which the parties differ, or reasonably believe they differ, in evidence, ability, or background.

Format the document with the numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, then the cruxes with their tests and recorded predictions.
