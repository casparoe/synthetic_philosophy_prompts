ok so I've been trying to nail down exactly what bothers me about the paradox of tragedy and I ended up constructing an argument that seems to prove that basically everyone who enjoys sad movies or horror films is being irrational. which is... a lot of people. I'm pretty sure the argument has a flaw but I can't articulate exactly where it goes wrong. help me debug this.

role: you're a philosopher of art who also knows their way around the emotion literature. take your time, think through each premise carefully, and then tell me which step you reject and what you're giving up by rejecting it. if you think more than one premise is vulnerable, walk me through the tradeoffs between the different rejection strategies.

here's the argument:

(1) People seek out tragic or horrific art (sad novels, horror films, tearjerker tv shows) voluntarily — they pay for it, they choose it over alternatives, they come back for more.
(2) If someone voluntarily and repeatedly seeks out X, they expect X to make their overall experience better than the available alternatives — that is, they expect a net positive.
(3) The experience of engaging with tragic art includes, essentially and centrally, powerful negative emotions: grief, fear, dread, disgust, anguish.
(4) If an experience essentially and centrally includes powerful negative emotions, then it is not a net positive experience — the negative emotions make it bad overall.
(5) So tragic art does not deliver a net positive experience. (from 3, 4)
(6) So people are voluntarily seeking out experiences they expect to be net positive, but those experiences are not net positive. (from 1, 2, 5)
(7) A pattern of behavior where you systematically seek out X expecting net positive but X is actually net negative is irrational. (you're systematically wrong about what's good for you.)
(8) Therefore, anyone who seeks out tragic art is behaving irrationally.

(8) is a wild conclusion. it means your friend who loves horror movies, your aunt who cries at sad operas, and basically every culture throughout human history that produced tragic drama — all irrational. the argument proves too much.

so where's the mistake? I have a few hunches. maybe premise (4) is wrong because the negative emotions in art are "transformed" somehow — we enjoy them in a second-order way, or the aesthetic frame changes their valence. or maybe premise (2) is wrong because we don't only seek out experiences for net positive valence — maybe we seek out meaning, or insight, or arousal, or something else that doesn't reduce to "feels good overall." or maybe premise (3) is wrong because the emotions aren't really the negative kind — they're "quasi-emotions" (make-believe versions) or they're mixed states that the word "negative" misdescribes.

I want to know: which premise do the main theories of the paradox reject, and what does each rejection cost? If you go after (4) do you end up committed to saying that no experience with negative emotions is net negative, which is obviously false? If you go after (3) do you lose the phenomenology — the fact that the fear in a horror movie sure feels like fear? And if you go after (2) do you have to rebuild decision theory to include non-hedonic goals, and if so what does that even look like? 

Also: if you think the argument is actually sound and we should just accept (8), make that case. I'm skeptical but I want to hear it.
