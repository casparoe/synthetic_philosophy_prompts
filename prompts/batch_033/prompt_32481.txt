so i've been reading about this philosopher bernard williams and his thing about how sometimes having a thought is itself the problem — like "one thought too many." the example he uses (or at least the one everyone talks about) is a guy who has to choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger from drowning, and if he stops to think about whether utilitarianism says it's okay to save his wife, that thought itself is already messed up. like the fact that he's even asking the question means something's gone wrong in the relationship.

i've been trying to figure out what the actual principle is, because it can't just be "don't think about things." so here are my gut reactions to a bunch of cases, and i want to know what principle (or principles) gets all of them, or close to all of them, and which ones i'd have to abandon if i wanted a clean theory. also what the principle says about a case i haven't listed.

1. the wife/stranger drowning case. if the guy stops to deliberate about whether saving his wife is morally justified, that's one thought too many. the thought itself is a failure.

2. a doctor in an ER with three patients, deciding who to treat first based on urgency and odds of survival. she's thinking "patient A is crashing, patient B is stable, patient C is 50/50." that's fine. not one thought too many.

3. someone on a first date, and mid-conversation they catch themselves thinking "is this person attractive enough for me to want a second date?" this feels gross to me. one thought too many.

4. a parent deciding whether to move their kid to a different school. they make a list of pros and cons, talk to teachers, visit the school, think about it for weeks. that's fine. not one thought too many.

5. a person at their best friend's wedding, standing up as a bridesmaid, and they find themselves thinking "i wonder if they'll still be friends with me after this, they're moving to a different city and i'm not sure they'll make the effort." it's not that the worry is unimportant, it's that having it right then, in that moment, feels like a betrayal. one thought too many.

6. a firefighter in a burning building who can only save one person — their spouse or their child. they stop and think "which one is the right choice?" one thought too many. (but maybe this is just the same as case 1?)

7. someone who donates to charity but spends a few hours researching which charity is most effective first. not one thought too many. if anything that seems good.

8. a person whose friend just told them about a miscarriage, and they think "what's the right thing to say here?" — i go back and forth on this one. part of me says it's one thought too many, but another part says it's just being conscientious and not wanting to say something stupid. maybe it depends on whether it's a quick flash or a whole deliberation? i genuinely can't decide.

9. a teenager who's about to help their grandma with groceries and thinks "am i doing this because i actually care or because i'd feel guilty if i didn't?" i think this is one thought too many. the moment you're calculating your own motives like that, you've already stepped outside the relationship in a way that damages it.

10. a judge sentencing a defendant, thinking carefully about what sentence is proportionate and just. not one thought too many.

so yeah. is there a principle that draws the line where i'm drawing it? it seems like it's got something to do with relationships and roles, but case 3 (the date) isn't really a deep relationship yet, and case 4 (the parent) is a deep relationship but deliberation is fine. maybe it's about whether the thought itself is incompatible with the attitude that the situation calls for? but i'm not sure what that cashes out to.

also, what would the principle say about a case i keep going back and forth on: someone who's about to forgive a friend for a pretty serious betrayal, and they catch themselves thinking "am i forgiving them because it's the right thing to do, or because i'm just avoiding conflict?" — is that healthy self-awareness or one thought too many?
