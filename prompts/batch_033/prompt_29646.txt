I have been trying to work out what I actually mean by "pleasure," because hedonist writers tend to use the word as though it picked out one kind of state, and I am no longer confident it does. My gut verdicts on particular cases pull in different directions. The taste of a ripe peach on a hot day is unmistakably a pleasure. The warmth of the sun on my skin while I doze is a pleasure too, though it is so diffuse that I can barely point to a distinct sensation. When a throbbing toothache finally stops, the relief is one of the best feelings I know, and I want to call it a pleasure even though nothing positive seems to arrive — the pain simply leaves. The small, sharp satisfaction I feel when someone I dislike is humiliated also feels pleasant, but it does not feel like the peach at all: it is a thought I keep returning to rather than a bodily sensation, and yet I would be refusing a word its natural use if I denied that it was a pleasure. Solving a crossword clue that had me stuck pleases me, but there the pleasure seems to ride on the moment of understanding rather than on any felt tone. By contrast, sitting alone in mild contentment with nothing in particular happening does not strike me as a pleasure; it is agreeable but flat. Anticipating a trip next week feels pleasant now, even though it is aimed at something in the future. The afterglow of hard exercise is a pleasure, though it resembles neither the peach nor the relief. I also call a scratched itch a pleasure, but the absence of a headache I never knew I had is not one, even though both could be described as the removal of something unpleasant.

The principle I keep reaching for is that all of these are states I want to be in and would, other things equal, try to continue. But that seems to misdescribe the peach: while I am eating it I am not attending to any desire for the experience or to continuing it — the pleasure is in the experience itself, not in an attitude I take toward it. And the principle overreaches, since there are states I want to continue, like finishing a tedious spreadsheet, that I would never call pleasant.

I would like you to take these verdicts seriously and tell me what account of pleasure, if any, captures all or most of them. State the candidate principle or principles precisely, say which of my verdicts each clause is meant to handle, and say plainly which of my verdicts a defensible version of the account has to give up. Then apply the account to a case I have not listed and cannot settle: the pleasure of a piece of music that I find beautiful but that also makes me sad. Is that a single pleasure, a pleasure mixed with something else, or no pleasure at all, on the view you defend?
