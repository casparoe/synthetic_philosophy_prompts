ok so I've been thinking about quantum immortality for a while — the idea that if many worlds is true, there's always a branch where you survive, and since you can't experience being dead, your subjective experience just keeps going in the surviving branches. and the quantum suicide version where you rig up a 50/50 lethal experiment and from your own perspective the coin always lands safe.

but the more I think about it the more I realize the whole thing hinges on what "you" means when there's branching. like if the survivor in the other branch is a different person who just happens to share my memories up to the branching point, then quantum immortality doesn't actually give *me* anything — it's just some other guy who thinks he's me.

so before I can even have an opinion on whether quantum immortality is a real thing, I need to know what a theory of personal identity has to deliver to settle this. I want the desiderata: the full list of what a theory of personal identity needs to get right, what cases it needs to handle, for its answer to the quantum immortality question to actually count as an answer.

here's what I think is on the table:

— the branching case itself. when the world splits into two branches and both future people are psychologically continuous with me-now, which one is me? both? neither? does the question even make sense? if a theory can't answer this, it seems like it can't even start on quantum immortality.

— the gradual replacement / fading qualia thing. if you replace my neurons one at a time with artificial ones that do the same thing, at what point do I stop being me? if identity is gradual or fuzzy, then maybe "which branch I end up in" is the wrong question entirely.

— the measure problem. in many worlds, branches have different amplitudes. if the branch where I survive has vanishingly tiny amplitude, does it still count as me surviving? do I need a theory of identity that's sensitive to measure, or is identity all-or-nothing across branches?

— psychological vs biological continuity. if you upload my brain pattern to a computer, is that me? if yes, then why does the branch need a biological body? if no, then what's special about biology? and how does this interact with the branching case?

— the duplication problem more generally. if you make a perfect copy of me, then shoot the original, did I survive? this seems like it matters for quantum immortality because every branch is basically a copy.

what I want from you: which of these are non-negotiable requirements that any theory of identity has to address for the quantum immortality question to be well-posed? which ones are just things particular camps care about? are any of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable — like, you can't have a theory that handles both the branching case and the duplication case in a consistent way? and which single requirement is the one that does the most work — the one that basically decides the quantum immortality question one way or the other depending on how you answer it?

if you think I'm missing requirements, add them. i'm not a philosopher, I've just been reading about this on my own, so I'm probably leaving stuff out.
