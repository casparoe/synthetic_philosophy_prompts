I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have been reading about the doomsday argument as research for a book in which a character's sense of the future is quietly dismantled by a single probabilistic calculation. What I want from you is not a verdict on that calculation but a diagnosis of the debate around it, which after decades seems to me to have frozen into a small set of moves that everyone knows and no one finds dispositive.

Let me set out the debate as I understand it. The argument, in its rough form, runs like this: if you adopt the self-sampling assumption and treat your own position among all humans who will ever live as though you were randomly selected from that set, then you should not expect to be unusually early. Since something on the order of a hundred billion humans have already lived, finding yourself around the hundred-billionth makes a future of trillions surprising: a randomly selected member of a trillion-person species would almost certainly have a birth rank vastly higher than yours. The Bayesian version updates toward the hypothesis that the total number of humans will be comparatively small, so that the end arrives sooner than demographic and technological extrapolation would suggest.

One side treats this as a legitimate and sobering application of reasoning under indexical uncertainty. The other side raises objections that seem to me to differ in kind, not merely in strength. There is the reference-class problem: among which set of observers, humans, conscious beings, observer-moments, or civilizations, should I take myself to be randomly distributed, and why does the choice not simply determine the conclusion? There is the self-indication objection: the fact that you exist at all is itself evidence favoring hypotheses on which many observers exist, and this is said to cancel or reverse the doomsday update. There is the sampling objection: I was not drawn from the set of all humans, because that set does not yet exist, so the uniform distribution over it is an artifact of a metaphor rather than a fact about my situation. And there is the pattern objection: the same reasoning, applied to a play at its first act or a species at its first generation, would predict imminent endings everywhere, which looks more like a defect in the principle than a discovery about the future.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is not about the mathematics and only superficially about priors. It seems to me that the two sides disagree about what a thinker is entitled to treat as a random sample, and about whether indexical facts, that I am this particular observer at this particular time, can function as evidence in the way ordinary observations do. But I cannot tell whether that is a substantive disagreement, a merely verbal one, or two different questions being asked under one heading. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the literature, and an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result about observation selection effects, a principled way of fixing the reference class, a reframing of the question, or a demonstration that the disagreement rests on starting intuitions neither side can argue for, and, if that last is the case, what it would mean to live with an argument that cannot be settled.
