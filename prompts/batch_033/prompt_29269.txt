I recently came across the paradox of Buridan's ass in a seminar on decision theory, and I have been turning it over in my mind ever since. As traditionally presented: a donkey, equally hungry and thirsty, stands precisely midway between a bale of hay and a pail of water. Because it has no more reason to move towards the hay than towards the water, it cannot reach a decision, and so it perishes — starving and dehydrating despite having everything it needs within reach. The example is usually attributed to Jean Buridan, the fourteenth-century philosopher, though I gather it may actually originate with Aristotle's discussion of the man who starves between two equally tempting foods, or perhaps with later critics of Buridan's moral psychology who used the ass to satirise his account of the will.

What troubles me is this. The paradox seems to expose a deep and possibly fatal flaw in the very idea of rational choice. If rationality consists in acting on the balance of reasons, and if ties can occur — as they surely can in real life, where options are often roughly equal in their attractions — then a perfectly rational agent would sometimes find itself paralysed, unable to act at all. This looks like a reductio ad absurdum of the rational-choice framework that underpins so much of economics, political science, and formal philosophy. It suggests that rational agents, as standardly conceived, are impossible unless the theory is propped up by some ad hoc patch, such as a randomising device or an arbitrary tie-breaking rule that is itself not justified by reasons. If that is right, then the entire edifice of expected-utility theory and game-theoretic modelling rests on a mistake — or at least on a convenient refusal to think through what happens when preferences are genuinely indifferent.

But I suspect I may be misunderstanding what the paradox actually demonstrates, and I would be grateful for a careful analysis. Where, if anywhere, does the reasoning behind Buridan's ass go wrong? And does the paradox genuinely threaten the foundations of rational choice theory as it is used in the social sciences, or is the threat more apparent than real?
