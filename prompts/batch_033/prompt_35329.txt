I'm a graduate student in a seminar on existentialism and contemporary social theory, and a phrase keeps coming up that I'm having trouble pinning down. Whenever someone wants to argue that we are not constrained by biology, history, or social structures in any deep way, they invoke Sartre's "existence precedes essence" as if it settles the matter. Last week, a classmate used it to argue that gender is a matter of pure self-creation: since there is no pre-given human nature, you simply are whatever you make yourself through your choices and actions, and nobody else gets to say what you are. Another student pushed back, saying the slogan doesn't imply anything nearly that radical, and that Sartre himself, in the very same essay where he introduces the phrase, talks about facticity — the brute givens of your situation that you did not choose and cannot wish away. Our professor, infuriatingly, just said "that's a productive disagreement" and moved on to the next topic.

I want to go to office hours prepared. What I need is a disambiguation: what are the distinct precise claims that "existence precedes essence" could be expressing, from the relatively modest to the wildly radical? I am not asking which one is the "correct" reading of Sartre, though I would like to know, for each reading, whether it is a plausible interpretation of his actual view and whether it is independently defensible. The thing I really need, though, is to know which of these readings would actually support the gender-as-pure-self-creation argument and which would not. I suspect my classmate is relying on a very strong reading that the slogan doesn't force on anyone, but I don't yet have the vocabulary to say exactly why. Lay out the menu of possible theses, and tell me which ones do the work she needs them to do.
