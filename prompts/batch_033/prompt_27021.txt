I serve on the ethics committee of a residential addiction treatment center. We are considering whether to offer "Ulysses contracts" to patients at intake. The proposal is this: a patient, during the intake process when they are sober and have been evaluated as having decision-making capacity, may sign a binding agreement stipulating that if they later demand to leave against medical advice during a period of acute craving or withdrawal, that demand will not be honored for a window of 72 hours. During that window, the clinical team would work to stabilize them; at the end of the 72 hours, if the patient still wants to leave, they may do so. The patient would be fully informed of the mechanism in advance, and no one would be enrolled without their explicit consent.

The committee is split. One faction argues that the contract honors the patient's autonomy by giving effect to the preference of the "sober self" — the self that set a long-term goal of recovery and knew, in advance, that craving would temporarily hijack their decision-making. On this view, refusing to honor the advance directive would actually be the paternalistic move, because it would let a transient state override a settled, reflective commitment. The other faction argues that autonomy means the right to decide at each moment, and that holding a person against their contemporaneous expressed will is a violation regardless of what they signed earlier. They worry about a slippery slope: if we can discount someone's current wishes because we judge them "impaired by craving," what stops us from discounting any decision we disagree with?

Underneath this is a deeper question about personal identity and rationality across time. The case for Ulysses contracts often draws on the idea that hyperbolic discounting — the tendency to steeply discount future rewards in favor of immediate ones — creates a kind of fracture within the agent: the preferences of the person at t₁ and the person at t₂ are so radically different that we are almost dealing with two different agents bargaining with each other. The contract is then a way for the long-run self to bind the impulsive future self, much as Ulysses bound himself to the mast. But the critics on our committee reject this framing: they say a person is a single agent across time, and the craving self is not a different person — it is the same person in a different state, whose wishes carry equal authority.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the policy, reject it, or adopt it with specific safeguards — and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side. The document needs to be something our clinical director, our legal counsel, and the rest of the committee can act on.
