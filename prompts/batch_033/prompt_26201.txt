I'm taking an epistemology course this semester, and we've been working through the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Our professor gave us a handout that summarizes both and draws a connection between them, and I've been turning it over in my head because something feels off but I can't quite articulate what. I've read the handout several times and discussed it with a friend in the class, and we both have the same vague unease — it's not that we think the professor is incompetent; quite the opposite, the course has been careful and rigorous so far — but neither of us knows the material well enough to say whether the unease is justified or whether we're simply failing to grasp a point that is, in fact, correct.

Here is the passage from the handout:

"The lottery paradox and the preface paradox are two versions of the same basic problem. In the lottery paradox, you know that in a fair lottery with a million tickets, your ticket is almost certainly a loser — so you're justified in believing it will lose. But if you're justified in believing that about each ticket, you should be justified in believing that all tickets will lose, which contradicts your knowledge that one ticket must win. The preface paradox makes the same point in a different setting: an author has checked each sentence in her book and believes each one is true, but she also knows from experience that books always contain errors, so she writes in the preface that the book contains at least one false claim. Just as in the lottery case, she has a set of beliefs that are individually justified but jointly inconsistent. Both paradoxes show that the principle that justified beliefs are closed under conjunction must be abandoned — we can be justified in believing each of a set of propositions without being justified in believing their conjunction."

My suspicion is that the summary is smoothing over some distinctions that matter — for instance, I've gathered from lecture that the two paradoxes don't have quite the same structure, and that the preface paradox isn't simply the lottery paradox with books swapped in for tickets. I also wonder whether "abandon closure under conjunction" is really the consensus takeaway rather than one contested response among several. But I don't know the literature well enough to pinpoint where the summary goes wrong or what a more careful presentation would say. Could you go through this summary and tell me what an epistemologist working on these paradoxes would find accurate, what they'd push back on, and whether the handout's conclusion follows from the accurate parts?
