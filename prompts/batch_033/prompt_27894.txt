In my upper-level philosophy of mathematics seminar, two classmates got stuck in an argument about Benacerraf's dilemma and asked me to referee, but I couldn't. I'm reconstructing their exchange as faithfully as I can.

Maya: Benacerraf's dilemma shows that platonism can't explain mathematical knowledge. If numbers are abstract objects, they're causally inert, and we can't have knowledge of things we're in no causal contact with. So platonism sits badly with a naturalistic epistemology.

Eli: That version of the dilemma leans on a causal theory of knowledge that almost nobody accepts anymore. Plenty of knowledge — logical truths, for instance — doesn't require causal contact with the thing known. The serious version is the reliability challenge: platonism has to explain why our mathematical beliefs would tend to be true, given that the mathematical facts themselves never impinge on us.

Maya: That challenge is just as damaging, maybe more so. If mathematical facts are independent of us, then the match between our mathematical beliefs and those facts looks like a massive coincidence unless something explains it. Appealing to "mathematical intuition" just gives the mystery a name.

Eli: But platonists have an answer to the reliability challenge. A full-blooded platonist holds that every consistent mathematical theory is true of some part of the mathematical realm. Then any coherent theory we adopt is automatically true of its intended objects, so reliability doesn't need a causal explanation.

Maya: That makes mathematical truth too cheap. If every consistent theory is true of something, then asking which mathematical theory is correct loses its force, and it still doesn't explain how our words and thoughts latch onto one intended interpretation rather than another.

Eli: Maybe the deeper lesson is that the explanatory demand itself is misplaced. Not every kind of knowledge needs a causal or reliability story of that sort. Benacerraf's dilemma may just inherit a dated epistemology.
