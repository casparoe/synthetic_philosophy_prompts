I've been reading the alignment forum on debate, and I think I have the surface picture of the obfuscated-argument problem but not the mechanism. Please explain it in enough detail that I can see exactly why the naive version of debate fails, and where the remaining hope—if any—lives.

Here's what I currently believe, so you can correct me rather than start from zero. The debate proposal is roughly: two AIs argue opposite sides of a question before a human judge who cannot check the answer directly; the judge awards the win to whichever side makes the more convincing case; and the proposal's central hope is that a false answer can always be attacked at some step small enough for the judge to verify, so truth wins even though the judge is far weaker than the debaters. The obfuscated-argument worry, as I understand it, is that a dishonest debater can produce a long, complex argument for a false answer where no single step is obviously wrong to the judge, and the honest debater can only say something like "I know this is false but I can't locate the flaw"—which is exactly what a dishonest debater would say about a true but complex argument, so the judge cannot distinguish the two situations.

What I want from you is a precise account of where the guarantee breaks down. Specifically: (1) Is the core problem that the judge's being convinced comes apart from the argument's soundness, or is it the narrower problem that the honest debater cannot locate and surface a judge-checkable refutation, or are these the same failure described differently? (2) Which exact structural assumptions of the debate proposal does the problem force us to abandon—in particular, the assumption that any false claim has a short, judge-checkable refutation, and the assumption that the honest debater can find it under adversarial pressure? (3) Why don't the standard patches work? I'm thinking of: recursively decomposing the argument so the judge only has to evaluate a single path down the tree; letting the honest debater meta-argue that the argument looks suspicious in a way the judge can't directly verify; or simply training better judges. (4) What does the problem actually show about the limits of debate as an alignment strategy, as opposed to merely showing that debate needs stronger judges or better training? Does it also undercut iterated amplification in the same way, or does IDA have a different relationship to the problem?

I want a committed answer: take a definite position on which assumptions are doing the real work and on how damaging the problem is, with reasons, rather than laying out several possible interpretations and leaving me to choose. Please keep it under 800 words; if you run short on space, spend it on (2) and (4).
