I'm presenting a paper at a workshop next month — it's a political philosophy thing, maybe twenty-five people, but the crowd is exactly the kind that'll tear into an argument about distributive justice. My paper goes after prioritarianism. The standard line is that prioritarianism elegantly dodges the leveling-down objection: telic egalitarianism has to say that burning everyone's resources to ashes could be an improvement if it makes them equal, which is absurd, but prioritarianism avoids this because its weighting function is strictly increasing in individual welfare, so making anyone worse off without making someone else better off always counts as a loss. My argument is that this is too quick. Once you set the priority weighting steep enough to make prioritarianism actually distinct from utilitarianism in practice — which you have to, otherwise it's just utilitarianism with a decorative concave function — you get cases where prioritarianism recommends policies that reduce total welfare and make some people worse off, not to benefit the worse off, but because the weighted sum favors a more equal distribution even when the absolute numbers are lower across the board. It's not the classic leveling-down objection where nobody gains; it's more like prioritarianism has its own leveling-down problem that kicks in under realistic distributional assumptions. I call it the "leveling-across" problem.

The audience is a weird mix and I need to be ready for all of them. About a third are telic egalitarians — they're broadly sympathetic to my conclusion but they're going to push me on whether my argument doesn't just reinforce the original leveling-down objection against their own view, and some of them think the leveling-down objection was never that damaging to begin with. There's a cluster of committed prioritarians, including one who literally wrote the book on this — they're going to come at me on the technical details of the weighting function and whether I'm sneaking in separability assumptions that prioritarians reject. And there are maybe four or five utilitarians in the room who think this whole family of views is misguided and will ask why we should care about any of this instead of just maximizing total welfare. Plus a few grad students who'll ask the question they've been waiting to ask all semester.

Phrase each one the way that faction would phrase it — I want to hear the prioritarians' objection in their own vocabulary, the egalitarians' in theirs, etc. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. And tell me the one question I should be praying nobody in that room thinks to ask. At the very end, give me a one-sentence summary of my position's biggest vulnerability, formatted as \answer{...}.
