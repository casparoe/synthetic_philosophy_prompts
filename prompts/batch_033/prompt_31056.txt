I am a duty officer at a regional emergency-management agency. Several times a month I chair decisions about how to allocate scarce rescue or treatment resources when we cannot help everyone: one boat can reach only one flooded house, one helicopter can winch either a single stranded person or a group, a field clinic has one remaining dose of a scarce drug. These calls have to be made in under an hour, usually by staff with no philosophical training, the agency director must sign off on any departure from the procedure, and the decisions are later reviewed by our legal team and the public. I have come across the view associated with John Taurek that the numbers should not count in such cases: if you can save one stranger or five strangers, the fact that five is more than one gives you no stronger reason to save the five, because no single person suffers the loss of five and each person has an equal claim; on this view a fair procedure gives each an equal chance, such as by flipping a coin. Please turn this view — together with the strongest objections to it and the main rival view that numbers do count — into a reusable decision procedure my team could actually run. Give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) one boat that can reach either a single trapped person or a family of five, all strangers with no special claims; (b) one ventilator and two patients who are clinically identical except that one is the sole carer for young children; and (c) a mass-casualty scene with more patients than transport slots, where your procedure has to work alongside ordinary clinical triage. Make sure the worked cases include at least one answer the team will not like, and end by saying plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
