I've been working on a piece about collective decision-making and the Condorcet jury theorem — you know, the classic result that if each voter in a group is more likely right than wrong on some binary question, and they vote independently, then as the group gets larger the probability that the majority is correct approaches one. I've been reading around this for a few weeks and I'm trying to nail down my own views before I start writing, because I don't want the piece to be internally contradictory. So I figured I'd lay out where I've ended up and see if someone who knows this stuff better than me can spot the problems.

Here's roughly what I think:

(1) The Condorcet jury theorem is correct as a piece of formal theory — the math checks out.
(2) The theorem gives us at least a defeasible reason to be optimistic about the epistemic performance of democratic institutions, even if it's not the whole story.
(3) The independence assumption is almost never satisfied in real political life — voters read the same newspapers, watch the same cable news, get caught up in the same social media dynamics — and this is a genuinly serious limitation on what the theorem can tell us about actual democracy.
(4) Despite that, I think deliberation among citizens before they vote is epistemically valuable and should be encouraged, not avoided.
(5) I think we should generally defer to majority opinion on questions where there's a fact of the matter, provided the question isn't one where elite expertise obviously swamps lay judgment.
(6) I'm skeptical that the theorem's competence condition — each voter having p > 0.5 — is met for most real-world political questions, but I don't think that sinks the epistemic case for democracy, because there are other mechanisms (like the "miracle of aggregation") that can produce group wisdom even when lots of voters are below 0.5.

I want to know: does this hang together? Which of these are in genuine tension with each other, which only look like they're in tension, and what's the cheapest fix? Rank the tensions by severity rather than giving me a flat list. Also, I'd want to know what would change your mind about the most serious tension you identify — like, what argument or evidence would lead you to reverse your verdict on whether that tension is real or resolvable. And please write the answer in British English, since that's house style for the outlet I'm pitching.
