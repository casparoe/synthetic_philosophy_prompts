Over the past year I've followed a recurring exchange between two positions on human enhancement that never seems to land. One side treats the therapy/enhancement distinction as a morally weighty boundary: roughly, interventions that restore or preserve ordinary human functioning are permissible in a way that interventions pushing a person beyond that range are not, or at least require a different kind of justification. The other side treats the distinction as either unworkable or morally idle: what counts as "ordinary" functioning is a shifting, partly social fact, and the real moral work is done by safety, consent, fairness, and the distribution of access. I want to know what these two camps would actually commit to if they stopped scoring points and tried to write something jointly.

Write the joint statement that a committed defender of the distinction (call them the Boundary camp) and a committed skeptic about its moral weight (call them the Continuum camp) could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The statement should open with a numbered list of agreed claims — substantive ones, covering which cases are central, which considerations both sides count as morally relevant even if they weight them differently (safety, consent, distributive fairness, self-authorship, medicalization), and which familiar moves both disown, such as appealing to what is natural to settle the question. Then give a disagreements table whose rows locate each residual dispute precisely: the question at issue, the Boundary camp's answer in first-person wording its defenders would accept, and the Continuum camp's answer in wording its defenders would accept. The rows should not be "the boundary matters" versus "it doesn't"; they should get down to sub-questions like whether a notion of ordinary or species-typical functioning can be specified without already importing evaluative judgments, and whether a vague or context-dependent line can still bear moral weight.

Finally, identify the two or three cruxes — the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp — and for each, state in advance what each camp predicts and describe a concrete case or argument both sides accept as genuinely bearing on it (an intervention that is plainly therapeutic on anyone's account yet raises the same fairness or authenticity worries as an enhancement; an intervention that restores a capacity the person once had, which one side counts as therapy and the other as enhancement by another name). Record the predictions before the fact, not after. Close with a short note on which part of the dispute, if any, turned out to be verbal and which survives as genuine. Commit throughout: make the positions definite enough to be vulnerable to the cruxes you specify, and if you think a disagreement is merely verbal, say so outright. No citations or references needed; the formulations are the point.
