I run a reading group for philosophy grad students — around 16 people, one 75-minute session — and we're doing the unilateralist's curse: the idea that when any one member of a group can take an action on everyone's behalf, the group effectively acts on the judgment of it's most optimistic member, even when the pooled evidence would say don't. I want them to feel it happen before I name it, not hear about it afterwards.

Design an in-class game with rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money. The setup should be something like this: each player gets a private signal about whether a risky 'publish' button is good or bad (a slip saying good or bad, with the signal's accuracy known to everyone); anyone may press the button once; if anyone presses, the whole group gets the outcome; if no one presses, everyone gets the safe status quo. Set the numbers so that pressing is individually rational when your own signal looks good, but the group would have wanted not to press given what all the signals together imply — in the independent case, a majority of the pooled signals would get it right, but the group only ever acts on the most optimistic one. Make sure the incentive isn't selfishness: everyone can be sincerely trying to do what's best for the group.

Then include at least three variants: one where the group is small vs. large, one where the signals are independent vs. correlated, and one where players may talk before deciding vs. not. For each variant, say what it's supposed to reveal about the curse or about the usual proposed fixes that get tossed around alot (deference to the most cautious, requiring consensus, waiting, designating one decider).

Then write the debrief that maps what happened in each variant onto the formal structure of the curse, including the point that the problem is the decision procedure (act iff anyone thinks it's good), not bias or bad motives — and the mirror-image problem where a unanimity norm lets the most pessimistic member veto genuinely good actions. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (people colluding, someone pressing the button for laughs, nobody believing the payoffs are common knowledge) and how to handle each. And be straight about which feature of the real thing the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.

Answer in American English, please.
