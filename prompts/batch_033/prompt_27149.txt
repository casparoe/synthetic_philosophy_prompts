I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum for years, and I've watched the argument between effective altruism and its critics run in place for what now feels like a decade. The critics make a cluster of claims I can predict almost word for word: that EA's focus on individual donation and career choice is a form of technocratic tunnel vision that leaves institutions and distributions of power untouched; that earning to give was always partly a self-serving rationalization for going into finance or tech rather than changing the systems that produce the problems; and that the movement's demand for measurable, quantified impact systematically favors legible interventions such as bed nets and cash transfers over structural change, movement-building, and political work whose effects are real but difficult to put in a cost-effectiveness model. The defenders have stable replies: the marginal dollar really does go further on neglected, tractable, measurable problems; earning to give was a limited claim about comparative advantage rather than a moral endorsement of the career; and explicit expected-value reasoning exists precisely so that hard-to-measure causes, including longtermist ones, can be compared without pretending they are measurable. The FTX collapse sharpened the institutional critique, but I am not convinced the underlying argument moved on either side.

My working theory is that this is not primarily a first-order moral disagreement. Almost everyone involved agrees that suffering matters and that resources should be used to do the most good. What they seem to disagree about is what the question is and what counts as evidence for an answer. The critics are asking something like how institutions and distributions of power should be changed, and they accept historical and sociological reasoning as evidence. The movement is asking what a particular person should do with the resources and information they actually have, and it tends to treat a claim as action-guiding only if it survives explicit, quantified, decision-relevant analysis. If that is right, both sides could be largely correct and still talk past each other.

Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive empirical or moral disagreement underneath the talking-past? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a reframed question, an empirical result about the tractability of political or structural change, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why?
