I am a philosophy professor, and I have been asked to lead a single two-hour session on the paradox of deontology for a group of advanced law and public-policy students in an interdisciplinary ethics seminar. They are capable, but they have mostly met consequentialism and deontology as slogans, and I expect them to arrive treating the paradox either as a decisive refutation of deontological constraints or as something that dissolves once one distinguishes killing from letting die; a few will probably want to settle it with a threshold. I am not asking for the explanation itself, but for the pedagogical architecture of the session. In particular, I need to know the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what—for example, whether the agent-relative/agent-neutral distinction should be laid down early as scaffolding or withheld until the students have felt the force of the puzzle—and the single case or scenario that should carry the whole discussion, together with what it must include and avoid. I have considered the transplant case and the standard trolley, but I suspect both import confounds; tell me whether that suspicion is right and what would serve better.

The aim of the session is for the students to leave seeing the paradox as a genuine internal problem about the rational structure of deontological constraints, not as a knock-down objection or a verbal quibble. Please also map the misconceptions this particular audience is likely to bring or form and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; give me one question to put to them near the end whose answer would show whether they have actually grasped the agent-relative/agent-neutral distinction rather than merely learned the phrase; and tell me what I should openly decline to settle in the time available and how to mark that boundary without making the view look evasive.
