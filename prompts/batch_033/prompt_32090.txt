A philosopher in my reading group has, over the term, committed herself to the following claims. A perfect physical duplicate of you with no felt experiences at all would still wince, say that it hurts, and write the same diary entries; the feelings make no difference to anything it says or does. When you touch a hot stove, the felt pain does not cause you to jerk your hand away or cry out; the same neural activity produces both the pain and the behavior. Blindsight shows that accurate verbal reports can be driven by information that is never consciously experienced. And the first-person judgment that one is in pain is itself produced by neural processes, not by the pain. She has so far avoided saying whether, given all this, she takes such a judgment to be justified, or whether she knows she is in pain. Next week the group will press her on one new case: her own present, ordinary judgment that she is in pain, formed while she is in pain. Predict whether she will call it justified, identify the principle about the epistemology of phenomenal judgment she is implicitly running — reliability without causal contact, a causal theory of knowledge, a non-cognitive or avowal view, or something else — give your best guess with reasons, and say which further case would best separate the remaining candidate principles.
