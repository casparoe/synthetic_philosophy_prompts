Here is the exam question:

"Suppose a forger produces a painting that is perceptually indistinguishable from Vermeer's The Music Lesson, and that no one can tell them apart by looking alone. Is there any rational basis for valuing the original above the forgery?" Answer with reference to the philosophical debate on forgery in the arts, giving particular attention to Nelson Goodman's contribution.

Here is the student's answer:

The problem of forgery is that it exposes a tension between two things we ordinarily take for granted. On the one hand, we assume that aesthetic value is something we can see: if two paintings look exactly alike, then whatever one has to offer visually, the other offers too. On the other hand, we care a great deal about whether a painting is genuine. When van Meegeren's Vermeers were exposed as forgeries, their value collapsed almost overnight, even though the canvases themselves had not changed in any visible way. The question is whether that reaction reflects a rational judgement or merely a kind of commodity fetishism.

Nelson Goodman, in Languages of Art (1968), offers the most influential argument for thinking the reaction is rational. He distinguishes between autographic arts, such as painting, and allographic arts, such as music. In an autographic art, the identity of the work depends on its history of production: a painting is this particular work because it was produced by this particular artist at this particular time, and even an exact duplicate is not the same work. In an allographic art, by contrast, the work is specified by a score or notation, and any correct performance counts as an instance of the work. A forgery in an autographic art therefore does not merely copy a work; it is a different work that falsely presents itself as having the same history of production. Because the history of production partly constitutes what the work is, we are justified in treating the two differently.

The force of Goodman's argument, however, lies in his claim that the way we look at a picture depends on how we categorise it. Once we know that a painting is a Vermeer rather than a van Meegeren, we attend to different features, draw different comparisons, and see different qualities as significant. Goodman concludes from this that an original and a perceptually indistinguishable forgery differ in their aesthetic properties, and moreover that the original will always possess greater aesthetic value than the forgery. The original, in virtue of its authentic provenance, supports a richer set of art-historical relations, and a forger can only imitate the surface, not the relation between the work and its context. Goodman has therefore definitively shown that our preference for originals is not snobbery or convention but is grounded in real differences in aesthetic value.

Alfred Lessing, in "What Is Wrong with a Forgery?" (1965), arrives at a similar conclusion. Lessing argues that forgery is an offence against art history rather than against aesthetics, but he agrees with Goodman that the distinction between original and forgery is rationally defensible, and the two philosophers together establish that the history of an artwork matters in ways that purely perceptual accounts of artistic value cannot capture.

In sum, the forgery debate shows that aesthetic value cannot be reduced to perceptual experience. Goodman's account of autographic art demonstrates that provenance is internal to aesthetic judgement rather than external to it, and the rational basis for valuing originals above perceptually identical forgeries is therefore secure.
