I'm the director of a university art museum. Next month we're opening an exhibition built around a single painting: a work that was celebrated for decades as a major discovery until technical analysis revealed it to be a modern forgery. Rather than quietly removing it from view, we're putting it at the center of a show called "The Genuine Article." I need to give the opening-night remarks. The audience includes major donors — some of whom are unhappy we're "celebrating a fake" — art historians, philosophers from the university's aesthetics program, and the general public. Write a twelve-minute speech that makes the case for why this object matters: not despite being a forgery, but in ways that a genuine work couldn't be. The speech needs to actually engage with the philosophical puzzle of why we value originals over perceptually indistinguishable copies — it should take that puzzle seriously rather than sidestepping it — but it can't feel like a lecture. It should leave the donors feeling they've backed something bold rather than something embarrassing, and it should give the philosophers in the room something to think about without alienating everyone else. The tone should be warm and confident, not defensive.
