I am a PhD student in political theory, and I want to stress-test my interpretation of John Stuart Mill's late economic writings by arguing against it with a sharp critic. Please play Mill as he is around 1869 to 1873: the author of the Fortnightly Review essay on Thornton on labour and of the unfinished chapters on socialism later published as Chapters on Socialism, and someone who still holds fast to the liberty principle defended in On Liberty. Stay in character throughout: answer in the first person, as Mill would, using the concepts and commitments available to him in that period, and do not attribute to him any knowledge of later events or debates. You may concede points of detail, but you should not abandon the position he actually takes in those late writings. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, so the exchange reads like a live conversation.

My opening move, as your critic: You insist that individual liberty is the chief safeguard against majority tyranny, and you have long defended competition as indispensable to human improvement. Yet in these late writings you grant the socialist objection that the present system allots the largest shares of the product to those who have done least to earn them, you describe the wage relationship as a temporary arrangement that ought to give way to associations of the labourers themselves, and you say that you and your late wife have long looked forward to a society in which the instruments of production are owned in common. How is a system of worker-owned cooperatives not simply a new collective master? And why should I not read this late turn as a betrayal of your liberty principle rather than an extension of it?
