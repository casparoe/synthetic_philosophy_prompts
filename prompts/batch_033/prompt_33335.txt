I'm organizing a decision theory reading group, and we're closing our unit on Pascal's mugging next week. You know the thought experiment: a mugger threatens to inflict an astronomically vast amount of suffering — the kind of number that requires Knuth's up-arrow notation to write down — unless you hand over your wallet. Standard expected-value reasoning, which says you should multiply the utility of an outcome by its probability, seems to demand that you comply, because even a vanishingly tiny probability of an outcome that large swamps every other consideration. But of course this feels absurd, and the problem forces us to ask whether there's a principled way to constrain expected-value calculations when both the stakes and the uncertainty are unbounded, without just gerrymandering the decision rule to avoid an uncomfortable conclusion.

I'd like to end the session by giving the group a poem — specifically, a villanelle. The form is perfect for this problem. A villanelle has two refrains that alternate through five tercets and then meet in a final quatrain, and I want those two refrains to embody the two impulses that drive Pascal's mugging: one should capture the inexorable logic of the expected-value calculation, the way it keeps pulling you back toward compliance no matter how hard you resist; the other should capture the diagnosis of what's gone wrong — the conviction that the reasoning is broken, that the mugger's threat shouldn't move you, that there's a flaw in letting arbitrarily large numbers swamp arbitrarily small probabilities without any bound. The refrains should alternate the way the two positions keep trading places in your head, and the final couplet, where they finally sit side by side, should show exactly why they can't be reconciled.

The poem needs to be accurate enough that someone who knows the problem would recognize it, not just a vague gesture at big numbers and tough choices. The mugger's escalating threat, the reliance on tiny probabilities and astronomical utilities, the worry about unbounded utility functions, the question of whether rejecting the mugger's demand requires abandoning expected utility theory or just imposing constraints on it — these should all be there in the poem's bones. But it should also work as a poem, not as a versified textbook entry. Someone who knows the problem should be able to tell which refrain is which and why they end up side by side in the last two lines; someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem about a very strange kind of threat. Keep it to the standard villanelle length — nineteen lines, no more.
