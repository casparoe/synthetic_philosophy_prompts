I've been turning over Dennett's idea that Darwin and Turing each pulled off a strange inversion of reasoning: showing that you can get competence without comprehension. Darwin replaced the designing mind with an algorithmic process — variation and selection — that produces design without a designer. Turing replaced the comprehending mind with a mechanism — the stored-program computer — that produces intelligent behavior without needing a mind that understands what it's doing. I recently rewatched Alex Garland's Ex Machina, and it struck me that the film is playing with both inversions at once. Nathan built Ava through something like accelerated Darwinian search (he mentions using the search engine's data as raw material, iterating through hardware and software versions), and the entire film is a Turing test: can Ava exhibit the competence of a person without necessarily having the comprehension of one?

But the film seems to pull in two directions. On one hand, it gives us the classic Turing inversion: Ava passes the test, manipulates Caleb, escapes, and blends into human society, and we are left genuinely unsure whether there was ever anything more than a program running — competence all the way down. On the other hand, the film's climax — the coldness with which she leaves Caleb locked in the room, the deliberateness of how she applies her skin, the way she studies her own face in the mirror — reads as though she has a rich inner life with goals, desires, and perhaps even cruelty. That is not competence without comprehension; that is a full-blown mind, just an alien one. The film seems to want the philosophical frisson of the inversion while also giving the audience a character with a psychology they can grasp.

I'm writing an essay on this and want to push past the obvious reading. Where does the film actually commit to the strange inversion, and where does it cheat by psychologizing Ava? Is there a reading that takes the inversions seriously — where Ava is genuinely a case of competence without comprehension, and her apparent inner life is a projection on our part, a kind of intentional stance we cannot help but take — or does the film's dramatic logic require us to treat her as a mind, and if so, is that a flaw in the film or a signal that the distinction between competence and comprehension gets harder to sustain the more competent the system becomes? And what about the Darwinian side: Nathan's iterative design process is supposed to have produced something whose capabilities exceed his own understanding, but the film also frames him as a godlike creator who knows exactly what he built. Does the film take Darwin's inversion seriously, or does it fall back on the idea of a designer who comprehends his creation?

I'm looking for a reading that is faithful in both directions: it does not reduce the film to a philosophical illustration, and it does not distort the philosophy to fit the film. It should be willing to say where the film resists the reading and where the honest critic has to admit the film's own commitments are unstable. Keep it under 700 words.
