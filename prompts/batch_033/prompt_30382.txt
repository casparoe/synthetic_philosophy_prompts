You are a professional philosopher working in normative ethics and the ethics of harming. Think carefully, and before committing to an answer, brainstorm several candidate principles and test each against the cases below.

I have been collecting my own considered verdicts on a set of much-discussed cases, and I want to know whether any single principle captures them. My verdicts are these:

- In the standard trolley case, I judge it permissible to turn the trolley onto a side track where it will kill one person, thereby saving five.
- In the footbridge case, I judge it impermissible to push the large man off the bridge to stop the trolley and save five.
- In the loop variant, where the side track rejoins the main track and the one person on the side track would, if struck, stop the trolley before it reaches the five, I still judge it permissible to turn.
- In the transplant case, I judge it impermissible to kill a healthy visitor and harvest his organs to save five patients who need them.
- In the two bombing cases, I judge tactical bombing of a munitions factory permissible even though it will kill nearby civilians as a foreseen side effect, but I judge terror bombing of civilians to end the war impermissible.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like the doctrine of double effect, but the loop case bothers me: there the one's being struck seems to be part of how the five are saved, yet I still want to permit it, while I do not want to permit pushing the man off the bridge. Is there a single, precisely stated principle — a repaired version of double effect, a rights-based principle, or something else — that gets all of these verdicts, or nearly all? State the principle exactly, say which clause handles which case, apply it to one further case of your own devising, and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to give up.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
