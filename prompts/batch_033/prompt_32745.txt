I've been working out my views on what separates real science from pseudoscience, mostly from reading popular books and following debates online. I haven't studied philosophy formally, so I may be missing distinctions that would clarify things, but I've arrived at a set of commitments that feel right to me. I'd like someone to check whether they actually hang together before I start defending them to other people.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) The thing that makes science special is falsifiability, roughly as Popper described it. A claim is scientific only if it rules out some possible observations — if it sticks its neck out and says "you won't see X." If you do see X, the claim is in trouble. This is what draws the line between science and everything else.

(2) Freudian psychoanalysis is on the wrong side of that line. It can explain any behavior whatsoever. If you're early to an appointment, it's anxiety; if you're late, it's resistance. There is no imaginable observation about a person that a psychoanalyst couldn't fit into the framework after the fact. That's pseudoscience.

(3) But I also think evolutionary biology is solid science, even though I've seen critics argue that adaptationist explanations are just-so stories in exactly the same way — that you can take any trait an organism has and retrofit an adaptive story about why natural selection would have favored it. I think this criticism misses something important, but I'm not sure I can say what that something is without also accidentally letting psychoanalysis back in.

(4) I think climate science is genuine science. I also think that when a theory's defenders keep adjusting it to dodge falsification rather than admitting a problem, that's a hallmark of pseudoscience. But climate models do get adjusted when they miss predictions, and I don't think that makes climate science pseudoscientific. So I need a way to tell the difference between legitimate updating and illegitimate evasion, and I'm not sure my principle in (1) gives me one.

(5) Lately I've been wondering whether the real criterion is about the attitude of the practitioners rather than the logical structure of the theory: do they treat a failed prediction as a problem that needs fixing, or as an inconvenience to be explained away? But I can't tell whether this is just (1) restated in psychological language, or whether it's a genuinely different standard — and if it's different, whether it conflicts with (1) in cases where they point in opposite directions.

So my question is: which of these commitments are actually in tension with each other, which ones only look like they're in tension but aren't on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision to make the whole set consistent? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for each one, say what would have to give and what the revision would cost me.
