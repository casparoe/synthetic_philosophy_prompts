I've been going back and forth on Friedman's F-twist and I want someone to actually unpack the conceptual machinery rather than just repeat the slogan. The version I originally absorbed from reading rationalist-adjacent econ stuff is that you shouldn't judge a theory by whether its assumptions are realistic; you judge it by whether its predictions track. So a model that treats firms as if they solve a maximization problem is fine even though no actual manager computes a Lagrangian, because the assumption is just a compact way of describing the behavior, not a claim about what's going on inside anyone's head. But then I see people using the same move to wave off assumptions that seem to be doing very different work: representative agents, rational expectations, perfect competition, frictionless planes, infinite populations of traders. And other people say no, that's a category error, there's a real difference between a harmless idealization and a substantive false assumption, but they almost never spell out what the difference actually is.

What I'd like is a clear explanation of what distinguishes the kinds of unrealistic assumptions that are defensible in a model from the kinds that quietly change the thing you're modeling. In particular, what would need to be true about the world for an as-if assumption to be legitimate, and what kinds of evidence would tell you that you've crossed from idealization into fantasy? I don't need a bibliography or a history of the old methodology debates; I want the underlying conceptual distinction, the kind of thing you could use to adjudicate a disagreement with someone who thinks the map being not-the-territory means no assumption ever has to be realistic. I still cant articulate why some false assumptions feel like harmless simplifications and others feel like they're doing the modeling work for you, and I'd like that distinction made explicit.
