I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the philosophy of artificial intelligence. In the course of my reading, I came across the following summary of the debate between John Searle and Daniel Dennett about original and derived intentionality, in a recent popular book on minds and machines:

"Searle argues that computers can never have genuine understanding because they lack what he calls original intentionality — the kind of intrinsic aboutness that our thoughts possess just by being thoughts. The symbols in a computer have only derived intentionality: they mean something only because we interpret them, just as the words in a book mean something only because a reader bestows meaning on them. Dennett's reply, which he dubs the 'two-bitser' argument, is that this distinction between original and derived intentionality collapses under scrutiny. He points out that evolution by natural selection produced our minds without any conscious designer — Mother Nature is a two-bitser who built intentionality from scratch through nothing but blind variation and selection. So if original intentionality can arise from a mindless process, there is no principled reason to deny that computers could acquire it too. Dennett concludes that the intentional stance — treating a system as if it has beliefs and desires because doing so lets you predict its behavior — is all there is to intentionality, and Searle's insistence on a special kind of 'original' intentionality is really just mysterianism about consciousness dressed up as a technical distinction."

I'd appreciate a claim-by-claim accuracy audit: what would a philosopher who works on this debate accept, what would they push back on as an overstatement or a conflation, and what would they say is simply wrong?
