I am a final-year philosophy undergraduate at a British university, and I have been attending supervisions with a tutor who works on the normativity of attitudes—specifically, the distinction between the 'right kind' and 'wrong kind' of reasons for belief, admiration, desire, and the like. Over the term she has given crisp verdicts on a set of stock cases, and I have kept a careful record:

1. The demon case: a powerful demon threatens to torture you unless you admire him. The demon is not in the least admirable. Verdict: the threat is a reason to want to admire him, or to take a pill that would induce admiration, but it is not a reason to admire him—it is the wrong kind of reason.
2. The trust pill: a drug would induce a belief that your friend is trustworthy, but your friend is in fact thoroughly unreliable. Verdict: the availability of the pill is a reason to take it, but not a reason to believe your friend is trustworthy—wrong kind.
3. The bribe: you are offered a hundred pounds to enjoy a film that is, by any independent standard, dull and clumsy. Verdict: the money is the wrong kind of reason to enjoy the film.
4. The evidence case: you have ample, solid evidence that your friend is trustworthy. Verdict: this evidence is the right kind of reason to believe your friend is trustworthy.
5. The good film: a film is masterfully shot, superbly acted, and emotionally resonant. Verdict: these features are the right kind of reason to enjoy it.
6. The admirable person: someone has devoted decades to helping others at great personal cost, with no ulterior motive. Verdict: their selflessness is the right kind of reason to admire them.

She has now set me a problem for our final meeting. The case is this: you are considering whether to believe that you will succeed at a difficult task. You have no evidence either way—the task is genuinely hard, and you have no special track record. However, a psychologist has reliably informed you of the following: if you believe you will succeed, your confidence will so improve your performance that you will in fact succeed; if you doubt yourself, you will perform worse and fail. The consideration in favour of believing you will succeed is therefore that the belief causes its own truth—it is not a pragmatic benefit separable from the belief's accuracy, because the benefit just is that the belief becomes true. Does she count this as a right-kind reason or a wrong-kind reason for the belief? I am meant to work out which theory of the distinction she is implicitly running, which of her existing verdicts is the closest analogue, and what principle she would be adopting with each possible answer. And if her existing verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, I would like to know what further case would best settle which theory she holds.
