I'm on my department's graduate studies commitee and we're in a bit of a bind. One of our PhD students has written a dissertation arguing that certain semantic paradoxes — the Liar, Curry's paradox, maybe the sorites too — can only be adequately resolved if we accept that some contradictions are true. The dissertation develops a paraconsistent framework (roughly in the lineage of Graham Priest's work) and applies it to some open problems in philosophical logic. The external examiner, who's a fairly eminent philosopher of language from another institution, has recommended that the dissertation be failed. His report says, in essence, that dialetheism is not a live theoretical option — that the law of non-contradiction is constitutive of rational thought, and a dissertation built on rejecting it is like a dissertation built on rejecting modus ponens: it's not wrong so much as it has left the space of reasons. The supervisor and the internal examiner both argue the dissertation should pass. They say the external examiner is conflating acceptance of a view with competent engagement with it, and that the dissertation is rigorous, original, and makes contributions that even a classical logician could appreciate — new impossibility results, sharper formulations of the paradoxes, that kind of thing.

The committee meets next week and I've been asked to draft a short written opinion with a concrete recommendation: pass, fail, or pass conditional on revisions (and if so, what revisions). I need to engage the external examiner's objection seriously — not just dismiss it as conservatism — and also do justice to the student, who's spent four years on this and by all accounts (except the external's) has produced work well above the bar. Oh, and please: separate what's actually settled about dialetheism and paraconsistent logic from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff. I don't want the committee's discussion to be driven by someone confidently asserting something controversial as if it were textbook consensus.
