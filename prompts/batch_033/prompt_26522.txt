I'm a facilitator — I mediate between community groups that have really deep value disagreements. The kind of thing where it's not just interests on the table but whole ways of seeing what matters: a religious school and secular parents arguing about what goes in the ethics curriculum, or animal rights activists and farmers hashing out an agricultural expansion. I've been reading around this idea of "moral trade" — the thought that when two parties have genuinely different moral priorities, they can sometimes make compromises that are actual gains from trade rather than one side just losing. The classic move is: if the environmentalists care vastly more about the wetlands and the union cares vastly more about job guarantees, maybe there's a deal where each gets what they rank highest and concedes what they rank lowest, and both walk away better off by their own lights. That's not splitting the difference; it's more like comparative advantage but for values.

The problem is I have no idea how to spot these opportunities in a real mediation. A lot of what gets called moral trade in the abstract looks, once you're in the room, like the more powerful group dressing up a demand as a reasonable compromise, or like a false equivalence between "we want clean water" and "we want higher margins." I need something I can actually use — a decision procedure or checklist I can work through in a couple hours before a session, or in the debrief after a proposal emerges. It should help me tell whether a proposed compromise is a genuine moral trade (Pareto-improving by each side's own standards) versus capitulation, a power play, or just a category mistake where the framework doesn't apply at all. It should flag the questions I need to go back and ask the parties, and it should be honest about where the whole idea breaks down — the cases the procedure can't handle or where it might give the wrong answer.

Then I'd like you to run the procedure on two cases: (1) a religious school and secular parents disagreeing over whether the ethics curriculum should cover gender identity, where the proposed compromise is that the school includes the modules but lets parents opt their kids out; and (2) an animal rights group and a farming cooperative in a dispute over expanding a livestock operation, where the compromise is expansion with substantially higher welfare standards than the legal minimum.

Please flag where you're least confident in what you're claiming, and write the whole thing in British English.
