I'm in a graduate seminar on American pragmatism, and every week someone drops the line "truth is what works" as if it closes the issue. Last Tuesday it happened during a discussion of whether Peirce's convergence-at-the-end-of-inquiry picture and James's cash-value picture are even the same theory—someone said "well, both hold that truth is what works, so the difference is just emphasis." I've also heard the slogan used to dismiss the correspondence theory ("truth isn't mirroring reality, truth is what works"), to defend a kind of instrumentalism about science ("quantum mechanics is true because it works"), and, memorably, to argue that religious beliefs can be true if they produce good effects in the believer's life. The slogan seems to be doing wholly different work in each case, and I suspect some of these uses are simply bad misreadings—especially where James is concerned.

I want to get clear on this before I write my term paper on Peirce versus James. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that "truth is what works" could be expressing, from the most deflationary reading (something like: it's just the trivial point that we call beliefs true when they guide action successfully) to the most radical (something like: truth is constituted by practical satisfactoriness, not merely correlated with it)? For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible on its own terms, whether Peirce or James (or neither) actually held it, and—this is what I really need—which of the readings would actually support each of the four moves I described above. Some of those moves may turn out to be supported by no defensible reading at all, and I want to know which ones. I'm looking for a committed answer that takes definite positions and defends them; please don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick. Please write in American English.
