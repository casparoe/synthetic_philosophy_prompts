I'm a philosophy professor, and I'm pulling together a handout for my graduate seminar on mind and language where I try to state my own committments about meaning and content, and I'd like an outside audit before I circulate it. Here's roughly where I've landed.

(1) I think the Twin Earth case shows that what a word means, and what a thought is about, is partly fixed by facts outside the speaker's head. Two speakers can be internal duplicates and still mean different things by 'water', because their environments differ.

(2) But I also want to say there's a narrow kind of content that such duplicates share, and that this narrow content is what cognitive science really needs when it individuates mental states.

(3) I think a competent speaker normally has priviledged access to what their own words mean and what they themselves are thinking; they don't have to investigate their environment to know that.

(4) I think a lifelong brain in a vat whose inputs exactly simulate a normal Earthly life is still thinking about water when it uses the word 'water' — not about nothing, and not merely about electrical impulses or features of the simulation.

(5) I think that if someone were switched from Earth to Twin Earth without noticing, then after enough time their word 'water' would come to refer to XYZ rather than H2O, even though nothing from the inside would signal the change.

I suspect something in this set is pulling against something else, but I can't quite see where, and I'd rather not find out in front of my students. Go through the pairs: which are genuinely in tension, which only look like they're in tension? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each, tell me the cheapest revision and what it costs. And please, write the answer in American English.
