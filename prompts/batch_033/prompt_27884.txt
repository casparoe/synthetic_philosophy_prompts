I'm a software engineer by training, and I got pulled into decision theory through an interest in how an AI system might reason about self-modification and credible commitment. One debate I keep circling back to but cannot seem to resolve for myself is the one about whether rational irrationality is coherent.

The canonical case, as I understand it, is Parfit's hitchhiker. You want to make a credible threat against someone — say, a driver whose car you need — but you know that a purely rational agent, once the threat has failed to deter, would not follow through, because carrying it out is costly and yields no further benefit. Anticipating this, the driver has no reason to take the threat seriously. So you take some action — a drug, a piece of hardware, a self-modification — that makes you genuinely irrational in a way that ensures you will carry out the threat. The question is whether the decision to take that action was itself rational.

What bothers me is that the literature on this seems to have reached a standstill. One camp treats rationality as fundamentally instrumental: an action is rational if it maximizes expected utility given your preferences. From that vantage point, taking the drug is straightforwardly rational — you are better off in expectation than if you had not taken it, and the fact that you are subsequently irrational is simply part of the causal machinery by which the better outcome is secured. The other camp insists that this instrumental picture misses something constitutive about rationality — that rationality is not merely a matter of selecting actions that produce good outcomes, but of responding correctly to reasons, and that a process of reasoning cannot coherently aim at its own abolition. If you know you are taking the drug in order to become irrational, they argue, you are in some sense already undermining the very state you are trying to enter.

After reading a fair amount of the back-and-forth, I cannot tell what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute about the word "rational," where one side means something like "prudentially optimal" and the other means something like "epistemically sound"? Is it a substantive disagreement about the structure of agency — about whether an agent can coherently adopt a policy that requires abandoning the very deliberative standpoint that adopted it? Are the two sides answering different questions under the same heading, one about what you should do and the other about what it means to reason well? Or is there a hidden formal question — about the logic of self-referential decision procedures, or about the conditions under which an intention can be genuinely adopted — that would settle the matter if anyone could answer it?

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse, grounded in what each side actually argues on its own terms. And I want to know what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a new case that breaks the symmetry between the two positions — or whether the impasse is here to stay because the question itself is ill-posed.
