so I've been going down a rabbithole on bounded rationality lately — I'm retired from an economics department and finally have time to actually read the stuff I used to just cite. here's where I've landed, and I'd like someone to tell me if it hangs together:

(1) satisficing is the right model of real decision-making: you set an aspiration level and take the first option that clears it, rather than trying to optimize.

(2) the rational way to set that aspiration level is by trading off search costs against the expected gains from finding something better. so you're optimizing at the meta-level, even though you're satisficing at the object level.

(3) but there's no objective, context-independent fact about what the "correct" aspiration level is. it depends on your goals, the stakes, how tired you are — there's a range of reasonable levels, not a precise answer.

(4) stronger claim: satisficing is rational in its own right, not just a second-best for agents with cognitive limits. even with unlimited computational resources, you should satisfice, because the world doesn't hand you a well-defined utility function and a pre-partitioned option set. optimizing is solving a problem that doesn't exist.

(5) a lot of what gets called "bias" in the psychology literature is just satisficing misinterpreted. if someone evaluates you as if you're optimizing but you're actually satisficing, you'll look irrational. but you're not — you're playing a different game.

I can't tell which of these are actually in tension and which ones just look that way. my gut says (2) and (4) might be the real problem — if satisficing is rational even for unlimited agents, why should the meta-level be about optimizing? shouldn't you also satisfice when setting aspiration levels? and if you do, you get a regress. but maybe that's not as bad as I think.

then there's (2) and (3): if there's no fact of the matter about the correct aspiration level, what exactly is the optimization in (2) computing? though maybe this one dissolves — the optimization just gives you a reasonable range, and (3) is only saying there's no precise number.

and I've got a nagging worry about (4) and (5) together. (4) says satisficing is rational even for ideal agents; (5) says biases are just satisficing seen from the outside. but then ideal agents would also show those biases? that seems off — surely an ideal agent doesn't anchor.

anyway, go through the set, tell me which pairs actually conflict and which don't, rank the tensions by severity, and say what the cheapest revision would be.
