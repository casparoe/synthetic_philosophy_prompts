I am an undergraduate currently taking a course in behavioral genetics, and a recent conversation with my uncle has left me convinced that he is overreading a study, though I have struggled to articulate exactly how. He had read a summary of research reporting that roughly sixty percent of the variation in children's reading ability within the population studied could be attributed to genetic differences among them. From this he drew two conclusions. First, he argued that because genetic differences appear to account for the majority of the variation, differences in teaching quality, home environment, and individual effort probably matter much less than people assume. Second, he argued that if children from certain backgrounds score lower on average on reading tests, this finding gives us reason to think those group differences are substantially genetic and therefore not something schools could do much to change. I told him I suspected he was overreading the study, but when he asked me to explain precisely what a result of this kind does and does not establish, I found I could not give a clear account.

Could you help me think through how to respond to him? I am less interested in whether the particular percentage is accurate than in what a finding of this general kind would actually license. In particular, what does such a result tell us about the roles of genes and environments in individual children, and which of my uncle's inferences about group differences and the likely effects of educational intervention are unwarranted?
