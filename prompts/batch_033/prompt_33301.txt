I am a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and I have been reading the literature on Benacerraf's dilemma. What strikes me most is how little the overall shape of the debate has changed since it began. The original dilemma, as I understand it, is that two constraints on a satisfactory account of mathematical truth pull in opposite directions. The semantic constraint says that mathematical statements should be taken at face value: a sentence such as "there are infinitely many primes" has the same logical form as "there are infinitely many stars," and if it is true, its singular terms and quantifiers refer. The epistemological constraint says that our account of mathematical knowledge must be continuous with our account of knowledge of the physical world, where reference and knowledge are typically mediated by causal interaction. If mathematical objects are abstract and causally inert, the face-value reading makes mathematical knowledge look impossible; but the standard ways of escaping that problem—rejecting the face-value reading, revising the semantics, or revising the epistemology—each seem to give up something another side regards as non-negotiable.

The literature since then seems to me to have settled into a stable pattern. Platonists bite the bullet on the epistemological constraint and try to weaken or replace the causal requirement. Nominalists and fictionalists reject the face-value reading and then owe us an account of why mathematical discourse is so useful and why it so persistently looks referential. Structuralists and others try to split the difference by relocating the objects or reinterpreting the quantifiers. Each position has well-known costs, and each side's response to the others' costs is by now extremely familiar. Yet almost no one moves. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not a failure of cleverness but a sign that the two constraints encode different conceptions of what an account of mathematical truth is supposed to do—one answerable primarily to logic and semantics, the other to naturalistic epistemology—so the "dilemma" may be less a single problem than a forced choice between two projects that have never fully acknowledged their different aims.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey or a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is it a substantive metaphysical disagreement about what mathematical objects are, a verbal or methodological disagreement about words like "true," "refer," and "exist," or two research programs answering different questions under one heading? Are there hidden empirical or formal questions doing the work, or is the impasse generated by bedrock starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, a formal result, a change in the question, or an argument that we should stop expecting convergence?
