I was reading a pop-science book on logic and paradoxes (the kind you'd find at an airport bookstore), and there was a chapter on something called the Raven Paradox. The author summarizes it like this:

"Hempel noticed that the statement 'All ravens are black' is logically equivalent to 'All non-black things are non-ravens.' So if you're trying to confirm that all ravens are black, you should be able to do it by looking at things that aren't black and checking that they aren't ravens. A white shoe, for instance, is a non-black thing that's not a raven, so it counts as evidence for the hypothesis. But obviously that's absurd — nobody thinks you can do ornithology by looking at shoes. The paradox shows that simple logical principles of confirmation lead to nonsense, and it's one of the main reasons philosophers gave up on trying to formalize how evidence works."

I found this genuinly surprising and a bit unsettling, but I'm also suspicious. The book makes it sound like the paradox is a kind of reductio that just blows up the whole project of confirmation theory. But I've heard Hempel's name mentioned in other contexts — he's a pretty major figure, right? — and no one seems to treat him as having proved that confirmation theory is impossible. What's actually going on here? Is this summary fair, and if not, what's it getting wrong?
