In our political philosophy seminar we have been working through the debate between negative and republican conceptions of liberty, and a recurring analogy is the slave with a benevolent or non-interfering master. The idea is that even if the master never actually interferes with the slave's choices, the slave remains unfree because she lives subject to the master's arbitrary will: interference is always possible, even if it never materializes. The analogy is meant to show that negative liberty, understood as the mere absence of interference, misses something essential, and that freedom properly requires the absence of domination, not merely the absence of interference. I want to audit this analogy carefully. What exactly maps onto what between the master-slave relation and the citizen-state relation? Where does the mapping break down in ways that matter for the argument the analogy is meant to carry? Does the analogy quietly import features of the master-slave case — the personal character of the power, its radical asymmetry, the absence of any legitimate source — that do not transfer to the modern state, and if so, does that weaken the case against a purely negative conception? And does the conclusion that we should adopt a republican conception of liberty over a purely negative one survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy doing more work than it can bear?
