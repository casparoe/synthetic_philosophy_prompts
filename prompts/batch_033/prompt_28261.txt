I retired a few years ago after a career as an experimental physiologist, and I have lately been working through the philosophical literature on free will with more time than I once had. The part I keep circling back to is the set of experiments begun by Benjamin Libet and his colleagues in the early 1980s, in which subjects watched a rapidly rotating clock, flexed a wrist whenever they felt like it, and reported the moment at which they first became aware of the urge to move, while scalp electrodes recorded a "readiness potential" that began some hundreds of milliseconds before the reported time of the urge. I understand the experimental logic well enough, but I find it difficult to keep straight exactly what the experiments establish, because the striking conclusion often drawn from them—that the conscious decision is an afterthought and that free will is an illusion—seems to depend on several assumptions that are rarely made explicit, and I know that some of the findings have since been reinterpreted.

Could you explain, for someone who is comfortable with experimental design and statistics but not with the philosophical literature, precisely what the Libet-style experiments do and do not show about free will? I would particularly like to understand: (a) what the readiness potential is, including why the distinction between its earlier and later components matters, and why its timing relative to the reported moment of awareness is or is not good evidence that the conscious will is epiphenomenal; (b) which assumptions about the relation among a decision, an urge or intention, and a bodily movement the standard interpretation has to make—for example, assumptions about how reliably subjects can time their own awareness, and about whether a free action must be initiated by a prior conscious event; and (c) how later findings and reinterpretations change the picture, such as the evidence that the readiness potential may reflect ongoing fluctuations in neural activity that a decision merely crosses a threshold on, and the claim that consciousness can still "veto" an action in the final moments before movement. Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled empirical findings, which are mainstream but not universal interpretations, and which are genuinely contested among philosophers and neuroscientists working on the topic; I would rather you flag a disputed point than present one side as the established consensus.
