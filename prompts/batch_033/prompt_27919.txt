My wife and I withdrew life support from our daughter last autumn, after an illness that had stretched across two years. In the months since, I have found myself returning to the distinction between killing and letting die — not as a philosophical puzzle I once read about in a seminar, but as something I had to inhabit, moment by moment, in a hospital room. I would like you to write a sonnet about this distinction. The octave should capture the way the distinction feels when you first encounter it in the abstract: clean, principled, a bright line that separates moral agents from natural causes, action from omission. The volta should arrive at line 9, where a concrete case — a parent watching a child's ventilator being turned off, the hand that does not reach for the switch but also does not stop the hand that does — begins to erode the distinction. The sestet should explore that erosion without resolving it: the creeping thought that "letting die" is still something one does, that the line between omission and commission blurs when the omission is deliberate, the outcome foreseen, and the causal structure of the world makes no metaphysical distinction between the two. The closing couplet should leave the reader inside that blur — not with a thesis but with the weight of it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the philosophical literature would recognize the problem, but it should work as a poem for someone who has never read a word of philosophy.
