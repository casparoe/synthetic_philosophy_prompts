I have been going in circles over a version of Aristotle's sea-battle problem that a colleague set me, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature. The argument below seems to show, from the apparently innocent principle that every proposition is either true or false, that the future is already fixed and that deliberation is idle. I read each step slowly and find each one plausible on its own, yet the conclusion strikes me as absurd, and I cannot locate the mistake. I am not asking whether the future is open or settled; I am asking where this argument goes wrong, or, if it does not go wrong, which premise I should surrender and what surrendering it would cost.

(1) For any proposition p, either p is true or p is false, and not both. (2) Let S be the proposition that there will be a sea battle on 1 October. By (1), S is either true or false. (3) If S is true, then it was already true a year ago that there would be a sea battle on 1 October; a proposition's truth does not pop into existence only when the event it describes occurs. (4) If it was already true a year ago that there would be a sea battle on 1 October, then nobody can now prevent that battle, because preventing it would make a true statement about the past false, and the past cannot be changed. (5) Therefore, if S is true, the battle is unpreventable. (6) By parallel reasoning, if S is false, the absence of the battle is unpreventable. (7) Either way, one of the two outcomes has been fixed for at least a year, so the future is already settled. (8) Therefore deliberating about whether to prevent the battle is idle, since the outcome is settled independently of anything anyone now decides.

I would like the answer to identify the exact step to reject or the ambiguity being exploited, explain why that step looked so plausible, and set out what the main options commit me to: keeping bivalence while distinguishing truth from necessity, restricting bivalence for future contingents, distinguishing hard facts about the past from soft ones, or some other move. I would prefer not to pay the price of rejecting bivalence outright, but I want an honest account of what each option costs. Please answer in British English.
