The debate over whether indicative and counterfactual conditionals belong to a single semantic kind has been running since at least the late 1960s, and by now the arguments on both sides are well-rehearsed. On one side, unificationists hold that both sorts of conditional can be given a single formal treatment — typically some version of the Stalnaker-Lewis possible-worlds semantics — and that the differences between them reduce to differences in the modal base or the accessibility relation, not to a difference in the kind of meaning they carry. On the other side, bifurcationists hold that the two sorts of conditional are fundamentally different: indicatives are not truth-conditional at all but are devices for expressing high conditional probability, while counterfactuals do have truth conditions and require a different analysis. The same data gets traded back and forth — the or-to-if inference, the behaviour of conditionals under negation, the interaction with modals, the import-export principles, the probability-preservation patterns — and neither side has moved in decades.

I want a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled, not a survey of the positions. Specifically: is this a substantive disagreement about the facts of natural language, or is it a disagreement about what a semantic theory is supposed to do — about what counts as explaining the meaning of a conditional? Are the two sides answering different questions under the same heading, and if so, what are those questions? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that, if settled, would break the impasse? Or is the impasse a sign that the question itself — whether indicatives and counterfactuals are the same kind of thing — is ill-posed?

I have a working hypothesis, but I want it tested: my suspicion is that the unificationists are primarily interested in compositionality and want a semantics that assigns the same logical form to both kinds of conditional so that the compositional machinery of the language does not need to treat them as separate constructions, while the bifurcationists are primarily interested in the cognitive role of conditionals — how they are asserted, evaluated, and used in reasoning — and are willing to sacrifice compositional uniformity for a better fit with that role. If that is right, then the debate is not about language at all but about which theoretical desiderata matter more, and the impasse is permanent unless one side can show that the other's desiderata are incoherent or cannot be satisfied even by their own lights. But I am not sure that diagnosis is correct, and I want an honest alternative if it is not.

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a new empirical finding, a change of question — and whether we should expect any.
