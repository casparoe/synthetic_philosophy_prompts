Alan Turing died in 1954. In his 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" and a 1951 BBC radio lecture, he argued that building a machine with human-level intelligence would be best achieved by constructing a "child machine" with the capacity to learn, and then educating it through a curriculum of tasks, rewards, and punishments — much as one would teach a child. He anticipated that machines would eventually surpass human intellectual abilities and dismissed several objections to this prospect.

Since Turing's death, the field of AI alignment has emerged, centered on a concern he did not directly address: that an AI system optimizing for a specified objective might pursue that objective in ways that are misaligned with human welfare, and that a sufficiently capable system might resist attempts to correct or shut it down. Researchers now debate whether alignment can be solved through techniques like reinforcement learning from human feedback (which resembles Turing's reward-and-punishment model), or whether the problem is structural and requires fundamentally different approaches.

What would Turing likely have made of the contemporary alignment problem?
