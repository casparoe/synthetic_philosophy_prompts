I'm in a grad seminar on vagueness, and after three weeks on the epistemicism-versus-supervaluationism fight I still can't tell why it's stuck. As I understand the positions: epistemicists (Williamson mostly) say a vague predicate like 'bald' really does have a sharp cutoff — there's a last hair, a particular n — but we can't know where it is, because knowledge needs a margin for error, so the boundary is unknowable in principle. Supervaluationists (Fine, Keefe) say that's a crazy thing to build into semantics: 'bald' is semantically gappy, so 'Joe is bald' is neither true nor false when Joe is borderline; it's true only if it comes out true on every admissible sharpening. Both sides seem to keep classical logic in some form — the epistemicist outright, the supervaluationist at least for tautologies like 'Joe is bald or Joe isn't bald' — and both end up saying the sorites major premise is false, just for different reasons. So the fight isn't really over which sentences come out true.

My working theory is that it's actually a standoff about what semantics is for: the epistemicist is protecting bivalence and a clean classical picture of the world, while the supervaluationist thinks semantics should respect the fact that nothing in our practice or the world settles borderline cases, so gaps are the honest option. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a substantive factual or logical question hiding underneath? Is it a verbal dispute, two research projects talking past each other under the name 'vagueness,' or a genuine disagreement where one side is just wrong? And what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a better theory of what 'borderline' means, or admitting the question is ill-posed?
