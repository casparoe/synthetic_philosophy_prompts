I sit on my university's academic integrity panel. We are hearing a case in which a student was caught using her smartphone during a closed-book, no-device, in-person examination. The student does not dispute the facts: she was consulting notes she had written and stored on the phone. Her defense is that she has a diagnosed working-memory impairment, that she uses her phone as a prosthetic memory system in daily life, and that the examination's device ban effectively tests her un-augmented cognition rather than the competence she demonstrates in real-world settings, where she always has access to her external memory. One panel member has framed the issue in terms of the extended mind thesis from philosophy of mind — the view that cognitive processes can be partly constituted by external resources when those resources are reliably coupled to the agent and fill the same functional role as internal cognition.

The panel is split. Two members regard this as a straightforward rules violation: the exam policy was explicit, the student knowingly broke it, and allowing this defense would invite every student to claim their phone is part of their mind. Two others think the student raises a genuinely hard question about what we are trying to assess, and they note that the university's disability accommodations already permit some students to use external cognitive aids — a calculator for a mathematics examination, or a voice recorder for lecture notes — in ways that seem to tacitly accept something like the extended mind view. I hold the deciding vote.

Draft the panel's written decision — uphold the violation, dismiss it, or uphold with modified conditions — with reasoning that engages the philosophical argument seriously rather than simply citing the rulebook. The decision should be one the university's general counsel and accessibility office could both read without alarm.
