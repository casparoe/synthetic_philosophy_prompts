I have been reading around in the philosophy of action, and I keep returning to a piece of behaviour described in a natural history book I read some time ago, which I can no longer place. I want to know whether the pattern it illustrates has an established name, and how exactly it should be delineated.

A solitary wasp catches a cricket, paralyses it, and drags it to the mouth of its burrow. Before carrying the cricket inside, the wasp leaves it on the threshold and goes down alone to inspect the burrow. When it comes back up, it drags the cricket in. The striking feature is what happens if the cricket is moved: if someone shifts it a few inches away while the wasp is below, the wasp retrieves the cricket to the threshold, leaves it there, goes down to inspect the burrow again, comes back up, and only then drags it in. If the cricket is moved again, the whole sequence repeats, apparently without limit. The wasp never seems to register that the inspection is now pointless, because it has just performed it and the burrow has not changed.

What interests me is not the entomology but the shape of the behaviour: a competence that is real and effective in its normal setting, yet is carried out through a fixed routine that cannot absorb a small but relevant change in the circumstances, so the animal repeats a preparatory step that no longer serves its purpose. I suspect the same shape turns up elsewhere, for instance in a piece of software that reruns a check it has already run instead of using the result, or in a person who follows a procedure so faithfully that they redo the preliminary step even after the reason for it has disappeared. I am not asking about reflexes, ordinary habits, or compulsions, which seem to differ in several ways, and I am not sure whether the feature is best called a lack of flexibility, a kind of rigidity, or something else.

My question is this. Is there an established term in the philosophical literature for this specific pattern, meaning not only the wasp's behaviour but the underlying structure of rigid, subroutine-like competence that fails under small perturbations? If the established term attaches chiefly to the wasp example, I would like to know what the general phenomenon should be called and how far it extends. I would also like to know how this concept is distinguished from neighbouring notions such as instinct, automatism, and compulsion, and what the pattern is supposed to show about freedom: does exhibiting it, or lacking it, mark the difference between merely mechanical behaviour and the flexibility that free action requires, or would that be too quick? I am not satisfied with a bare label; I want the boundaries drawn and the bearing on free will worked out.

Please answer in British English, and write in continuous prose only, as one would in a letter or email: no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables.
