I keep encountering the phrase "the varieties of free will worth wanting" — sometimes shortened to "free will worth wanting" — in seminars on free will and determinism. It is almost always deployed to deflate the incompatibilist: the thought seems to be that incompatibilists have been worrying about a kind of free will that no one has reason to want, and that once we ask instead which varieties of free will are worth wanting, the traditional problem either dissolves or changes shape. I am not confident I know what the slogan actually asserts, and I suspect it is doing several different jobs at once. Lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, ordered from the least to the most revisionary. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. Make sure to separate at least these strands: a taxonomic claim (there are several things called "free will" and we should sort them out before arguing), an evaluative claim (the question that matters is which varieties of agency we value, not which are metaphysically possible), and a metaphysical or semantic claim (the compatibility question is ill-posed, or already settled, once we specify which variety we want). Then say which of these readings would actually license using the slogan to dismiss the incompatibilist worry about determinism and the ability to do otherwise, and which readings would leave that worry completely untouched. I want the readings as a menu I can take back to the seminar and use to press whoever invokes the phrase on which one they mean.
