I write about biology and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a feature on why the species count keeps shifting — why a single population of birds or orchids gets split into three species and then re-lumped, and why two competent systematists can disagree about where one species ends and another begins without either having made a factual error. Before I present the rival species concepts to my readers, I want the specification first: what would any adequate account of what a species is have to deliver in order to count as adequate at all, where the point of the account is to let an intelligent nonspecialist understand what taxonomists are doing and why their classifications change.

Produce the desiderata list. It should include the cases an adequate account must handle honestly — asexual and parthenogenetic lineages, ring species, hybrid zones and introgression, fossil chronospecies for which only morphology survives, bacteria and archaea with horizontal gene transfer, and populations too poorly studied for breeding or genomic data — and the phenomena it must explain rather than explain away, such as why some groups form discrete-looking clusters while others grade into one another. It should include the distinctions the account may not collapse: between the species category and a particular species taxon, between a diagnostic trait and the thing being diagnosed, and between a species being real and a species being a convenient unit. It should include the results from evolutionary biology it cannot contradict, such as population thinking, gene flow, and incomplete lineage sorting. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular school; flag any subsets that look jointly unsatisfiable, and say whether that forces pluralism about species concepts or a more deflationary conclusion; and identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the familiar candidates. A sentence or two on how each major contender fares against the list is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases.
