I'm writing a paper that tries to sort out which version of higher-order theory of consciousness—if any—survives the objections that have accumulated over the past few decades, and I've realized I can't evaluate the contenders without first settling on what the evaluation criteria are supposed to be. The literature is full of people demanding that a theory explain this or accommodate that, but the demands pull in different directions and some of them seem to me to beg the question against the whole higher-order approach from the start.

I want you to write the desiderata: the full list of requirements that an adequate higher-order theory of consciousness must meet, with the case behind each requirement, and a clear judgment about which are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp or another. The requirements should cover at least the following territory, but feel free to add any I'm missing: (a) the distinction between conscious and unconscious mental states, (b) the targetless higher-order state problem (misrepresentation and hallucination cases), (c) the apparent simplicity or unity of conscious experience and whether it's compatible with the idea that consciousness is a matter of one state representing another, (d) what the theory must say about animal and infant consciousness, (e) consistency with what we know about the neural correlates of consciousness, and (f) the question of what makes the higher-order state itself conscious—or whether it needs to be. For each requirement, explain what's at stake: what would be lost if we dropped it, and whether any major version of higher-order theory already gives up on it. Then, if you think some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, flag that—because that's the part I most want to see. Please write the answer in American English.
