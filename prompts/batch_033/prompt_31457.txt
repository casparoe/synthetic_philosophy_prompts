I have been taking part in a reading group on classical Indian philosophy, and our discussions keep circling back to the same standoff. One participant, a logician by training, is frustrated by Nāgārjuna's use of the catuṣkoṭi, the fourfold scheme in which a claim is considered under the alternatives that it holds, that it does not hold, that it both holds and does not hold, and that it neither holds nor does not hold. On his reading, certain Madhyamaka arguments reject all four alternatives at once, and he insists this is not a subtle result but a plain logical error. If you reject both P and not-P, he argues, you have given up excluded middle; and if you then also reject the alternative "neither P nor not-P," you are rejecting the very conjunction of the two rejections you just made. Another participant, who has studied Madhyamaka for years, replies that the objection smuggles in the very assumptions the Mādhyamika is challenging: that the statement in question has a determinate, findable subject matter, that the four alternatives are jointly exhaustive, and that a Madhyamaka rejection is an assertion of a contrary rather than a refusal to endorse any of the alternatives.

Please write a dialogue between these two interlocutors in which they work through the disagreement in earnest. Neither should be a strawman; each should engage the other's actual arguments rather than shifting ground. Let them test the questions that naturally arise: whether the Mādhyamika is committed to some non-classical logic, whether rejecting an alternative amounts to affirming its negation, whether the four alternatives really exhaust the possibilities once the relevant phenomena are taken to be empty, and whether the deepest dispute concerns logic or the conditions under which a statement has a determinate truth value. The dialogue need not end in complete agreement, but it should show genuine progress in isolating the crux of the disagreement.

Since a dialogue has no single verdict to extract, end with a single last line beginning "Summary:" that states in one sentence where the disagreement stands at the close.
