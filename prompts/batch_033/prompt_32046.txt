I recently read a popular article about probability that gave the following account of Bertrand's paradox, and I would like to know how much of it I can trust. The article's summary runs:

'Bertrand's paradox asks: if a chord of a circle is chosen at random, what is the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? The answer depends on what "at random" means. If you choose a radius and take the chord's midpoint at a uniformly random point along it, the probability is 1/2; if you choose the two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference, it is 1/3; and if you choose the midpoint uniformly inside the circle, it is 1/4. Each of these methods is an equally reasonable application of the principle of indifference — the rule that when there is no reason to favor one possibility over another, you should assign equal probabilities. The paradox is therefore usually taken to show that the principle of indifference is incoherent, since it assigns different probabilities to the same event depending on how that event is described. The standard lesson is that probabilities are never objective facts about the world; they are only artifacts of a description, or of a convention, that we choose.'

I suspect the arithmetic is right but that the philosophical morals are overreaching, and I do not know enough to say where. Please go through this account part by part: what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is wrong or contested? In particular, what would a careful defender of the principle of indifference say in reply, and does the final sentence about probability being conventional follow from anything true in the summary? Where the article reports what the paradox 'shows' or what its 'standard lesson' is, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
