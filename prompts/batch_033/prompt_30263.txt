I chair my university's academic integrity panel. We're deciding a case where a third-year student lifted several pages of an essay from an online source. In the interview he was honest about it, and it came out that he was in a bad depressive episode at the time, barely leaving his room; his therapist wrote a letter saying his judgment was substantially impaired but that he new he was copying. Half the panel wants to treat it as plagiarism with a reduced penalty, maybe probation. The other half wants to waive sanctions entirely and just require him to continue treatment. I keep noticing the two camps are arguing past each other: one side is asking whether he deserves blame, the other side is asking whether blame is even the right kind of response to someone in that state. I need to circulate a short decision memo before Wednesdays meeting. It should recommend a concrete outcome with a timeline, and it should honestly engage the strongest consideration on the losing side. In particular, I want the memo to take a position on whether his depression makes this a case for a milder version of the ordinary blaming response, or a case where the ordinary blaming response is the wrong kind of thing altogether — whether treating him as someone to be helped rather than blamed is a way of respecting his situation or a way of quietly no longer treating him as a full member of the academic community. I suspect that question is doing more work than the penalty question, and I don't want us to dodge it.
