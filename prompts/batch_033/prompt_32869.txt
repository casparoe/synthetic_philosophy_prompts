I'm preparing a paper for a philosophy of science seminar on the problem of verisimilitude, and I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think before I start writing. The problem, as I understand it, is this: we want to say that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another — that Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than Aristotelian physics, even though both are strictly false — and Popper tried to cash this out in terms of truth content and falsity content. Miller and Tichý then showed that on Popper's definition, no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other false theory, which seems to show that the purely content-based approach is unworkable. Since then the literature has split into two main families: the likeness approach, which defines verisimilitude in terms of the similarity between the possible worlds a theory permits and the actual world, and various attempts to repair the content approach by restricting which consequences count. I've been trying to sort out my own views and I've written down the following eight commitments. I'd like to know whether they form a consistent package or whether I'm helping myself to things that pull against each other.

Here they are:
(1) Verisimilitude is a genuine epistemic good — when choosing between two false theories, we should prefer the one that is closer to the truth, other things equal, and this preference is not just a disguised preference for something else like empirical adequacy or predictive power.
(2) The Miller-Tichý result is fatal to Popper's original definition and, more broadly, to any approach that defines verisimilitude purely in terms of the sets of true and false consequences a theory has, without any weighting or structure among those consequences.
(3) The likeness approach — defining verisimilitude as similarity between the set of worlds a theory designates as possible and the actual world — is on the right conceptual track. If we could make it work, it would capture what we mean by closeness to truth.
(4) But I am deeply skeptical that any non-arbitrary similarity metric over possible worlds can be given. The choice of which predicates to include in the distance measure, and how to weight them, seems to me to smuggle in substantive judgments about what matters, and I don't see how to ground those judgments without appealing to the very thing we are trying to define.
(5) We can and do make comparative verisimilitude judgments in the history of science — Newton is closer to the truth than Aristotle, Einstein is closer than Newton — and these judgments are objective enough that we should not treat them as merely pragmatic or interest-relative.
(6) I hold a general methodological view: if a concept resists non-arbitrary formal definition after sustained attempts, it should not play a foundational role in our philosophical account of science. We should either replace it with something more tractable or admit it was a placeholder.
(7) But the difficulty of defining verisimilitude does not show that truth is a dispensable goal of inquiry. The failures of formal verisimilitude tell us something about the limits of formal epistemology, not about whether truth matters.
(8) I find the Schurz-Weingartner "relevant consequence" approach — which tries to save the content-based picture by restricting which consequences count as relevant, using relevance logic or structural criteria — intriguing but ultimately unsatisfying, because I doubt there is a principled, language-independent way to carve off the "relevant" consequences.

Please go through these eight commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. For the genuine tensions, tell me what the cheapest revision would be — which commitment I would have to give up or modify, and what the revision would cost me. Also, please say which of my eight commitments you think is the weakest or most vulnerable, even if it is not in direct tension with the others. Finally, say how confident you are in each of these assessments and single out the point you are least sure of.
