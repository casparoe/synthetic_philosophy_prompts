The Frege–Geach problem has been the central challenge to metaethical expressivism since the 1960s, and the debate around it strikes me as unusually static. Expressivists keep offering new formal machinery—higher-order attitudes, hyperplans, being-for—and critics keep arguing that each proposal either fails to validate modus ponens in mixed contexts or implicitly reintroduces the propositional structure it was meant to replace. Both sides agree on the data (moral sentences embed in conditionals, arguments with moral premises are logically valid), both sides agree on the desiderata (an account of moral language that captures its logical behaviour without positing moral facts), and yet no solution has achieved anything close to consensus. After sixty years, the pattern looks less like a research programme making incremental progress and more like two groups with different standards of success talking past each other.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about whether expressivism can be made to work, but about what it would mean for it to work: one side treats the problem as a formal constraint—build a compositional semantics that validates the right inferences—while the other treats it as a psychological one—tell a story about what speakers are doing when they reason with moral language that does not require moral beliefs. If that diagnosis is right, then the impasse is not a sign that the problem is hard but that the question is ill-posed: the two sides are answering different questions under the heading 'the Frege–Geach problem'. But I am not confident in this diagnosis, and I would like an assessment from someone who knows the literature better than I do.

So: what kind of disagreement is the Frege–Geach debate, really? Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two research programmes with incommensurable success conditions? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a formal result, a new distinction, a change of question, or something else entirely?
