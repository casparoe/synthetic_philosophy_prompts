I'm taking a course on the philosophy of moral progress, and we've spent several weeks on the expanding circle thesis — roughly, the claim that moral progress across human history is best understood as the gradual widening of the set of beings to whom we accord moral standing: from kin to tribe to nation to all humans, and arguably now toward nonhuman animals and future generations. I'd like your credence that this thesis identifies the central dynamic of moral progress better than any competitor framework (e.g., accounts centered on improved compliance with antecedently fixed moral rules, on the refinement of moral concepts, or on institutional design). Please decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions:

(a) Whether the broad historical pattern genuinely fits an expanding circle rather than a more uneven, cyclical, or multi-dimensional story;
(b) Whether the mechanism driving expansion — if there is one — is sufficiently reliable to make further expansion probable rather than merely contingent on parochial factors;
(c) Whether apparent major contractions (e.g., twentieth-century totalitarianism, or the persistence of severe animal exploitation despite expanding rhetoric) are genuine counterexamples that the thesis cannot comfortably absorb; and
(d) Whether there is a coherent, unidimensional notion of "moral standing" that can be expanded in the way the thesis requires, as opposed to a patchwork of distinct capacities and relations that do not admit of a single axis of inclusion.

For each sub-question, give a rough probability and a sentence of justification. Then explain how you combine them to reach your overall credence. Finally, tell me which sub-question your overall number is most sensitive to — that is, which one, if you became confident you were wrong about it, would shift your credence the most, and what kind of evidence would change your mind on that point.
