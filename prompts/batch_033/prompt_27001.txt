I am a novelist, and I have been turning over a pattern of reasoning that I encounter constantly in literary culture — in workshops, in reviews, in conversations with editors — and I have come to suspect that it rests on a mistake, but I lack the vocabulary to articulate why. I am hoping that philosophers have already mapped this terrain and can give me the concepts I need.

The pattern is this. When a reader or critic encounters some feature of a novel — a detail of setting, a character's mannerism, a subplot, a shift in point of view — their first and often only question is: what is this doing for the story? What does it contribute to theme, to character development, to plot? The working assumption is that if something is present in the text, it must be there because it serves a narrative function; the only interesting question is which function it serves. I have sat through workshops where a fellow writer will say, of some small detail I included simply because it felt true to the world I was building, "I'm not sure what this is doing," and the implication is clear: if it is not doing something, it should be cut.

I have come to believe that this assumption — that every feature of a novel exists because it serves a function — is not only false but actively distorting. Some features of a novel are, I suspect, byproducts of other choices the author made. A character's verbal tic may not reveal anything about their psychology; it may simply be a consequence of the author having written their dialogue in a particular rhythm and needing a syllable to fill out a line. A subplot may not echo the main theme; it may be there because the author realized, late in the drafting, that a certain character would otherwise disappear for eighty pages, and the subplot was the least disruptive way to keep them present. A structural choice — say, alternating between two timelines — may not be "about" the interplay of past and present in any thematic sense; it may be an artifact of the order in which the author drafted the chapters. These are not flaws. They are simply features whose presence is explained not by their narrative function but by the constraints and accidents of the process that produced them.

What interests me is not the literary-critical question of how to evaluate such features, but the underlying pattern of thought: the reflexive assumption that every trait of a complex artifact must be functional, and the corresponding blindness to the possibility that some traits are structural byproducts. I have a vague sense that evolutionary biology has had a version of this debate — something about whether every trait of an organism must be an adaptation — but I do not know the literature well enough to say whether the parallel is exact, or whether philosophers have analyzed the pattern of reasoning itself, independent of the domain in which it appears.

So my question is this: does this pattern of reasoning have a name in philosophy? Is there a literature that diagnoses the error of assuming that every feature of a complex system must be explained by its function, and that treats this as a general epistemological mistake rather than a local empirical one? If the nearest concepts come from biology — adaptationism, or something like it — I would want to know how well they travel, and where the analogy between a novel and an organism breaks down. I am less interested in a label than in a precise account of what the mistake consists in, what makes it tempting, and what alternative explanatory strategies it obscures.
