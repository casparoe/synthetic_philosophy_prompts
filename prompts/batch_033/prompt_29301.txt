I work as a senior policy designer on a government behavioural insights team. My unit designs choice architectures for public-facing services: how options are presented on benefits enrolment websites, default settings on retirement savings platforms, the framing of organ-donor registration forms, the layout of tax-filing interfaces. Our decisions affect millions of people, and we make them regularly — several new interventions go live each quarter. My team includes designers, data scientists, and subject-matter experts, but almost none of them have any background in moral or political philosophy. I do not need them to become philosophers, but I do need them to be able to distinguish a legitimate nudge from an intervention that is objectionably paternalistic, and I need this distinction operationalised in a form they can apply without me in the room. The stakes are high: a badly designed intervention that crosses the line will, if noticed, generate press coverage accusing the government of manipulation, and even if it is not noticed, it may wrong the people it touches. The procedure has to be something a team member can run in under thirty minutes on a single proposed intervention, and it must be honest about where it relies on empirical claims we may not have evidence for and where it relies on value judgments that no procedure can settle.

What I want is a decision procedure — a checklist or a sequence of questions with decision rules — that a team member can run on a proposed intervention to determine whether it is permissible on paternalism grounds or whether it is ethically blocked. The procedure should draw on whatever the philosophical literature on paternalism has established that is genuinely usable: the distinction between hard and soft paternalism, between means paternalism and ends paternalism, the role of transparency and publicity, the difference between a nudge and a shove, the question of whose conception of the good is being imposed. But the procedure must not require the user to know those terms or to have read the literature. Each step should specify what counts as a sufficient answer to move forward, where the procedure stops with a red light, and where it must escalate to a person (or a panel) because the question is irreducibly normative. Please also flag the kinds of case the procedure cannot handle — for instance, interventions that are paternalistic toward one group in order to protect another, or cases where the empirical evidence about what the affected people 'really' want is contested or ambiguous, or interventions whose effects on autonomy and welfare point in opposite directions.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals we are currently debating:

(1) Automatically enrolling all employees in a workplace pension plan with an opt-out rather than an opt-in, at a default contribution rate set somewhat above what survey data suggest most employees would choose for themselves if they were choosing actively.

(2) Redesigning a benefits-application portal so that the option to report a change in circumstances — which triggers a reassessment that often reduces a claimant's benefits — requires navigating three additional screens and confirming twice, while all other options on the same portal remain one-click.

(3) Requiring a twenty-four-hour cooling-off period before a user can increase their daily betting limit on a state-regulated gambling app, where the delay is accompanied by a screen showing the user's losses over the past month.

For at least one of these, I expect the procedure to return an answer my team will find uncomfortable. Please do not soften that answer. I also want you to tell me, at the end, what the procedure cannot do — what kinds of paternalism question it is structurally incapable of answering, and where someone using it might be misled into thinking a moral question has been settled when it has only been deferred.
