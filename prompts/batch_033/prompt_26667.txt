I'm a PhD student in political theory working on the material dimensions of political order, and I've been trying to sort out my views on the Langdon Winner question: do artifacts have politics? I've been testing my intuitions on cases, and they're pulling me in different directions. I'd like you to look at my verdicts and tell me what principle, if any, captures the pattern — and which verdicts I might have to give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. Robert Moses's low overpasses on Long Island parkways, deliberately designed to prevent buses from reaching Jones Beach. Verdict: clearly political. The design encodes a racial and class exclusion that was explicitly intended.

2. The UC Davis mechanical tomato harvester, developed in the 1940s, which could only be operated economically on large farms and accelerated the consolidation of California agriculture, driving out small growers. Verdict: political, even though the engineers' aim was just efficiency. The harvester wasn't designed to hurt small farmers, but it predictably restructured an industry along lines that favored capital over labor.

3. A standard claw hammer. Verdict: not political. It's just a tool; it doesn't encode any particular social arrangement or power relation.

4. Nuclear power plants, which Winner argued require centralized, hierarchical, technocratic management to operate safely, and thus are inherently political. Verdict: I'm unsure. I see the argument, but France and the United States both have nuclear power and very different political systems. The claim that the technology requires authoritarianism seems too strong.

5. Hostile architecture — park benches segmented by armrests to prevent sleeping, spikes on ledges, and the like. Verdict: clearly political. These are designed to exclude specific populations from public space.

6. The TCP/IP protocol that underlies the internet, with its end-to-end principle: the network is dumb, intelligence lives at the edges, and the protocol doesn't inspect or discriminate among packets. Verdict: not political in itself. It's a neutral enabler. But I waver on this, because the choice to build a network this way rather than another way seems like it could be a political choice about where control should live.

7. A municipal decision to install roundabouts rather than traffic lights at intersections. Verdict: not political. Both solve the same coordination problem; roundabouts just do it more efficiently. But then I think: roundabouts force drivers to negotiate with each other rather than obey a central authority. Is that a political difference? I keep going back and forth.

8. Facial recognition software trained on datasets that are predominantly white and male, yielding higher error rates for women and people of color, and deployed by police departments. Verdict: political. The biases in the training data and the use case both embed racial and gender politics.

9. A staircase. Verdict: not political. But then: a staircase makes a building inaccessible to wheelchair users. Is the choice to build a staircase rather than a ramp political? I want to say the staircase itself isn't political, but the decision not to include a ramp is. That feels like it might be a dodge.

The tension I can't resolve is this: cases 3 (hammer) and 5 (hostile bench) both seem like designed objects that shape what people can do. Why is one political and the other not? And case 6 (TCP/IP) and case 8 (facial recognition) are both software systems — why do I feel so differently about them? I suspect my verdicts are tracking something about intent, or about whether the artifact embeds a power relation, or about whether the political effect is necessary or contingent — but I can't make any of those principles work across all nine cases.

Please give me the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts, stated precisely, with each clause motivated by the cases it handles. Tell me what the principle says about cases I haven't listed — ideally at least two or three. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon or revise. If no single principle gets all nine, I'd rather have a clean principle that overturns one or two verdicts than a gerrymandered one that saves them all.

Please keep your response to at most 800 words.
