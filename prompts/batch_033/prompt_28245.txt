I am a sixth-form student running a philosophy discussion group, and last week someone presented an argument against epiphenomenalism that seemed compelling at the time, but the more I think about it the more I suspect it proves too much. I cannot work out exactly where it goes wrong, and I would like you to diagnose it as a professional philosopher of mind. Please think through the argument carefully before settling on an answer.

Here is the argument, as I have reconstructed it from my notes:

(1) If epiphenomenalism is true, then conscious experiences — the felt quality of pain, the redness of red, and so on — have no causal effects on the physical world, including the brain.

(2) When I form a judgement about my own conscious experience — for instance, the judgement that I am feeling a throbbing pain in my left temple — that judgement is itself a physical event: a pattern of neural activity, and eventually an utterance or a piece of writing.

(3) Therefore, if epiphenomenalism is true, my conscious experience of pain does not cause my judgement that I am in pain.

(4) But my judgements about my own conscious experiences are remarkably detailed, accurate, and fine-grained. I can report not merely that I am in pain, but that it is throbbing rather than stabbing, located behind my left eye, about a five out of ten, and fading. It would be an extraordinary coincidence if my judgements happened to match my experiences this precisely without my experiences causing them.

(5) We should reject explanations that require extraordinary coincidences when a straightforward causal explanation is available.

(6) Therefore, epiphenomenalism is false: conscious experiences do have causal effects on the physical world.

So far, so good — or so it seemed in the discussion. But here is my worry. Premise (5) looks like a general principle: if two things are tightly correlated and one does not cause the other, we should reject the correlation as a coincidence unless we can find a common cause. But if that principle is sound, it seems to threaten a great deal more than epiphenomenalism. For instance, many philosophers hold that mathematical objects — numbers, sets, and so on — are abstract and causally inert, yet we form accurate judgements about them all the time. If the principle in (5) is correct, should we not also conclude that mathematical platonism is false, because the correlation between mathematical facts and our mathematical judgements would be an inexplicable coincidence? The same worry arises for moral realism, if moral facts are understood as non-causal. The argument seems to prove that we cannot have knowledge of any causally inert domain — which may be right, but it is a much bigger claim than the one the argument was introduced to support, and I doubt everyone in the room realised they were signing up for it.

So my question is: where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Is the principle in (5) too strong, and if so, what is the right principle? Or is the argument sound, and we really should give up on non-causal knowledge across the board? Or is there a relevant difference between our knowledge of our own conscious experiences and our knowledge of mathematical or moral facts that blocks the parallel? Please state how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of.
