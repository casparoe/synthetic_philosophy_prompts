Two people in my bargaining-theory study group got into it over the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution and I honestly couldn't referee it. They were looking at a two-person problem with disagreement point (0,0) and two nested feasible sets: the small one S is the convex hull of (0,0), (1,0), and (2/3, 4/3), and the big one T is the convex hull of (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), and (0,2). In S, Nash gives (2/3, 4/3) and KS gives (3/4, 1); in T, Nash gives (1,1) and KS gives (2/3, 4/3). So when you expand from S to T, only player 2's best possible payoff goes up — from 4/3 to 2, while player 1's stays at 1 — and yet Nash gives player 2 less than before (down to 1), while KS gives player 2 more (up to 4/3).

Lena said that shows Nash is perverse: player 2's options improved and the solution punished her for it, so monotonicity is obviously the right axiom and KS is the better solution concept. Sam said KS is the one with the perverse property: KS's outcome in T, (2/3, 4/3), is still available in S, but when you shrink back down to S, KS changes its mind and picks (3/4, 1). He said a solution that re-picks after you remove options it didn't choose is violating the one axiom that keeps bargaining solutions from being arbitrary — independence of irrelevant alternatives — and that's a worse sin then the Nash monotonicity thing, which is just a quirk of the product. Lena replied that IIA is exactly what's wrong with Nash: it forces you to ignore how much each player could have gotten, which is precisely the information that makes a compromise fair, so the "irrelevant" alternatives are not irrelevant to fairness. Sam answered that if fairness means tracking counterfactual ceilings, then the outcome can be moved by adding options nobody would ever accept, which isn't a fairness principle, it's a framing effect.

They asked me to adjudicate and I couldn't. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what, and who's more right overall?
