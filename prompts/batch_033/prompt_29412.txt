I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether social media actually changes people or just makes visible how they already are, and I keep half-remembering something from Kierkegaard. As I understand him, he thought the daily press didn't just report on public opinion — it produced a new kind of thing, the public, which is nobody in particular and yet somehow everybody: an abstract crowd where people hold opinions at second hand and no single person has to own what "everyone" says. He also thought this leveled genuine distinctions into a sort of envious chatter and endless reflection that never becomes action. (I'm going from memory, so don't take that as a precise reading.)

Here's what I actually want to ask. Take a specific, existing design: a social platform where you can post under a pseudonym, every post displays a public like and share count, and the default feed ranks replies by engagement rather than by whether the person knows anything about the topic. What picture of the public, of individual responsibility, and of what an opinion is does that design presuppose? I'm not asking about the company's stated values — I mean what the mechanics themselves commit the platform too. Is this basically the same phantom public Kierkegaard was worried about, or does making the crowd visible and quantified change the thing into something else? And if the people running the platform actually took Kierkegaard's worry seriously, what would be the smallest concrete design change that follows — removing the counts, forcing real names, killing the algorithmic feed, something else — and why that one first?
