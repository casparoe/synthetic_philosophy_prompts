I am a novelist, and my books have been translated into a dozen languages, which is how I came to the question I want to put to you. I have been reading Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot*, built around Clément Marot's poem "A une Damoyselle malade" — twenty-eight lines of three syllables each, rhyming couplets, the stress always on the final syllable, a shift from *vous* to *tu* at the midpoint, the opening line echoed at the close, the poet's own name tucked into the text. Hofstadter produces dozens of English versions of this one tiny poem, no two alike, and the effect on me has been to make me unsure what I actually believe a translation owes to a poem of this kind. That is not an academic uncertainty: it is the question of what becomes of a sentence of mine once it leaves my language.

Before I read further in translation theory, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata: what an adequate theory of translation under formal constraint must deliver. I am not asking for a survey or a reading list; I am asking for the requirements — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the phenomena it must explain or at least accommodate, the facts about language and prosody it must respect. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Where a case is a particular poem or translation, describe it in your own words; I have the texts at hand and do not need them quoted.

Scope it carefully. I am not interested in translation in general, but in the problem that arises when a source text's formal features — meter, rhyme, line length, wordplay, the patterning of sound — are not decoration but part of what the text is and does. A theory that settles for carrying the sense and letting the form fall where it may fails this problem by fiat; a theory that settles for reproducing the form at whatever cost to sense is equally evasive. The desiderata should tell me what lies between those two.

And flag the tensions. Which pairs of desiderata do you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — where a translation that fully honors one requirement must, as a matter of fact, slight another? That is the part I most want to see, because it would tell me where the real theoretical choices are rather than where the slogans are. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one school or one purpose. I can assess the existing theories myself; what I lack is the list.
