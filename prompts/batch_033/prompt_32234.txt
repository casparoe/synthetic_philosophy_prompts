I'm a high school student, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative move in political debates that I don't have a name for. I'm hoping you can tell me whether this pattern has been identified and discussed in philosophy, and if so, what it's called and how its boundaries are drawn.

The pattern goes like this. Someone advocating for a restrictive policy—tighter immigration limits, cuts to a social program, exclusion of a group from some benefit—will introduce a vivid thought experiment about a small group in a genuine survival crisis: a lifeboat adrift with too many passengers, a bunker with limited food, a life raft that will capsize if one more person climbs aboard. In the thought experiment, the situation is truly zero-sum and urgent: if you let one more person on, everyone dies. The setup is designed so that any reasonable person would agree that harsh triage is necessary and that ordinary moral considerations are suspended. Then, having secured that agreement, the person pivots to the real-world policy debate and argues that the same logic applies: we are in a "lifeboat" situation, so we must be hardheaded, make tough choices, and exclude people.

What interests me is the structure of the move. The survival scenario is not just an analogy; it seems to function as a kind of moral pump. It primes the audience to accept a harsh conclusion by getting them to endorse the logic in a context where it genuinely fits, and then it carries that logic over to a context where it may not fit at all—where the situation may not be zero-sum, may not be an emergency, and may contain many more options than the thought experiment allows. But the move is difficult to argue against in the moment, because if you push back, you sound like you are denying the reality of trade-offs, or you are accused of being sentimental.

I am not asking about the general concept of a false analogy—I know that exists. What I am after is this specific argumentative structure: the use of a deliberately extreme, zero-sum survival scenario to generate a conclusion that is then exported to a non-extreme, non-zero-sum policy context, where the scenario's emotional force and apparent unavoidability do the persuasive work. Is there an established term for this move? If so, how do philosophers distinguish between legitimate uses—where the real situation genuinely shares the relevant structural features of the survival scenario—and illegitimate ones, where the survival scenario is being used to make a situation seem more zero-sum or more urgent than it actually is? If there is no established term, what would be a good one, and what would the boundary cases be?
