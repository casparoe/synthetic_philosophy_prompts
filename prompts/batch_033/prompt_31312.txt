I work as an analyst in a state environmental health division, and we're deciding whether to recommend tightening the childhood lead exposure standard based on a big observational cohort study. The study is about as good as observational epidemiology gets—rich covariates, negative controls, quantitative bias analysis—but one of the senior epidemiologists keeps shutting the discussion down with 'no causes in, no causes out,' and half the room nods like that settles it. His line is that since we didn't randomly assign kids to lead levels, the study can't support a causal claim, so we shouldn't treat it as evidence that lowering exposure would prevent the outcomes. A younger colleague pushed back that this is a crude view of causation: on an interventionist account, a cause is just something you could in principle manipulate, and the study is estimating what would happen if we lowered exposure. I've been trying to read up on the difference between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation, and I honestly can't tell which side the slogan is even on, or whether its just a point about statistics rather than about what causation is.

I'd like to come to the next meeting with something that stops the slogan from doing unearned work. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims 'no causes in, no causes out' could be making—from the almost trivial to the very strong—and say for each whether it's actually true and what it would rule out about our study? Then tell me which reading the senior epidemiologist is probably relying on when he dismisses the cohort study, and whether that reading is defensible. I'd also like to know which readings, if any, would actually support the younger colleague's interventionist reply. I don't need a bibliography, just a clear menu I can circulate.
