I've been reading through the literature on longtermism and existential risk, and I keep noticing a structural feature of the arguments that I don't have a name for. The claim that we live at a uniquely pivotal moment — the "hinge of history" — seems to be supported by two quite different considerations that are never, as far as I can tell, defended simultaneously. One consideration is about probability: nuclear weapons, engineered pandemics, and especially the prospect of unaligned AI make the risk of civilizational collapse or extinction uncomfortably high by any reasonable estimate. The other consideration is about stakes: even if you think the probabilities are low, the number of future lives that could be lost is so astronomically large that the expected value calculation still makes this moment decisive — a small chance of losing a vast future is still an enormous expected loss. When a skeptic challenges the probability estimates, the defender points to the stakes. When a skeptic challenges the expected-value reasoning (on the grounds that tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs are too sensitive to be action-guiding), the defender points to the probability estimates. The slogan "we live at the hinge of history" stays the same, but the argument supporting it shifts depending on where the pressure is coming from.

I'm trying to figure out what to call this. It's not the motte-and-bailey, because the two arguments aren't arranged as a defensible core and an indefensible extension — they're more like two independent supports that are never load-bearing at the same time. It's not equivocation, because the phrase "hinge of history" means the same thing throughout; what changes is which justification is being offered for it. It's not a fallacy of composition, because the two arguments don't combine to produce the conclusion — they're alternatives. And it's not simply that the position is a conjunction of two claims, because when both are challenged at once, the defender can retreat to the position that "at least one of these considerations is sufficient on its own," which is logically weaker than "both are true" but rhetorically functions as if the full position had been defended.

Is there an established term for this specific argumentative structure — a single conclusion supported by two independent lines of reasoning that are never jointly defended, because each serves as the fallback when the other is under pressure?
