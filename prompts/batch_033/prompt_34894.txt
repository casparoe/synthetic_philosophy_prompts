I have been reading discussions of Ulysses contracts in the rationalistsphere for years — tying yourself to the mast, precommitting against akrasia, the idea that your present self can bind your future self because you know you will predictably defect from your own considered preferences. The individual case is fairly well mapped. What interests me is the collective analogue and whether the analogy actually holds up under scrutiny.

Take a concrete institutional design that really exists: the practice, near-universal in liberal democracies, of making constitutional amendments harder to pass than ordinary legislation — requiring supermajorities in the legislature, multiple readings, ratification by subnational units, or referenda. Ordinary statutes pass by simple majority; constitutional changes do not. The practice is routinely defended by analogy to the Ulysses contract: a democracy binds itself because it knows that, under pressure, it will be tempted to abandon its own long-term commitments. The entrenchment is supposed to be self-binding by a single agent — the polity — rather than one group shackling another.

I want to know what theory of collective agency this practice presupposes, and whether it can survive without one. In the individual Ulysses case, the same agent does the binding and the being-bound; Ulysses-today and Ulysses-tomorrow are continuous enough that we call it self-binding rather than one person restraining a stranger. But in the constitutional case, the legislature that enacted the entrenchment and the legislature now constrained by it are composed of different people — often generations apart, with different values, different information, and no actual say in the original decision. So the practice seems to presuppose one of two things: either that the polity is a single enduring agent across time in a sense strong enough to make the Ulysses analogy go through, or that the Ulysses analogy is a misleading gloss and the real justification rests on something else entirely — perhaps an intergenerational contract, a veil-of-ignorance argument, a purely pragmatic claim about the stabilising effects of making some rules harder to change, or a claim that entrenchment protects not the majority against itself but minorities against majorities.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of collective agency that the practice of constitutional entrenchment presupposes — or, if it presupposes no single theory, map out the range of theories that could license it. Then tell me: which features of the practice are philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would all recommend the same institutional design? If we took seriously the view that there is no enduring collective agent and that constitutional entrenchment is merely one generation imposing its preferences on another under a flattering metaphor, what would have to change in how we design, justify, or limit the practice? And is there a version of the Ulysses analogy that does not require positing a collective agent — for instance, one that treats entrenchment as a coordination device among contemporaneous individuals who all prefer to be constrained, rather than as a diachronic commitment by a group-self?

I want to know where the argument is shakiest: say how confident you are in each major claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
