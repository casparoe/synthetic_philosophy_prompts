I work in the constitutional affairs unit of a small country's parliament. We keep getting proposals to entrench things -- supermajority requirements for tax changes, independent fiscal councils that can veto budgets, constitutional amendments locking in particular policies, sunset clauses that are deliberately hard to remove. Some of these are sensible Ulysses-contract situations where the state is binding itself against foreseeable future weakness. Some are just the current majority trying to tie the hands of future goverments they don't trust. And a lot sit in a grey area where it's genuinely unclear which it is.

I need a decision procedure my team can run on any entrenchment proposal -- a checklist or flowchart in words -- to sort these into: clearly legitimate precommitment, clearly illegitimate entrenchment, and the cases where the distinction breaks down and a political judgement is needed. The procedure should be grounded in whatever political philosophy and rational-choice theory actually have to say about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, but it has to be usable by policy officers who haven't read Elster and don't want to.

The constraints: we typically have about a week to produce an analysis, the stakes are high (these things shape what future parliaments can do), and our output goes to ministers who will ignore anything that looks like a philosophy seminar. The procedure shouldn't require data we can't realistically get in that time.

I want the procedure to include:
- The questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each
- Where the procedure stops with a "no, this is just entrenchment of current preferences"
- Where it stops with a "yes, this is a legitimate precommitment"
- Where it hands off to political judgement because the theory can't settle it
- An honest accounting of which steps are themselves value-laden rather than technical, and where the procedure smuggles in a normative commitment the user might not share

Then run the procedure on three proposals:
1. A constitutional amendment requiring a two-thirds majority to introduce any new tax, framed as protecting future generations from deficit spending.
2. An independent central bank with a price-stability mandate that parliament cannot override without a referendum.
3. A law making it illegal for any future government to privatise the national water utility, with no amendment procedure short of a new constitution.

I want at least one of these to come out as an answer I won't like -- the procedure should have teeth and shouldn't just ratify whatever my political instincts tell me.
