I'm a retired evolutionary biologist who's gotten into philosophy of mind the last couple years. I keep coming across functionalism and the multiple realizability argument — the idea that mental states are defined by what they do, not what they're made of, and that the same mental state (like pain) could be realized in wildly different physical systems, neurons, silicon, whatever.

Here's the thing: this sounds exactly like stuff I've been thinking about my whole career. Convergent evolution gives you the same function realized in completley different structures — wings in birds, bats, and insects. Ecological niches are defined by functional roles, and totally unrelated taxa can fill them. We even have the distinction between homology (same structure, different function) and analogy (same function, different structure). It feels like the philosophers are basically talking about convergence and niche-filling but with minds instead of morphologies.

So I want you to restate functionalism and the multiple realizability argument in the vocabulary of evolutionary biology — niches, convergence, homology, analogy, adaptive landscapes, whatever terms you need — as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what parts of the philosophical position have no counterpart in the biological vocabulary at all.

I'm especially curious about one thing. In biology, we don't say the wing IS the function — the wing is a trait that performs a function, and the function is something like "producing lift for flight." But functionalists seem to want to say the mental state just IS the functional state — pain isn't something that plays the pain-role, it is the pain-role. Is that difference just a quirk of how we talk in biology, or does it point to something deeper that doesn't survive the translation? And if I try to translate the philosopher's identity claim into biological terms, does it come out sounding like a category mistake, or is there a way to make it work?
