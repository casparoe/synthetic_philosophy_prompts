A colleague of mine works on the ethics of blame, and over the years she has been strikingly consistent about who has standing to blame whom — where a blamer lacks standing when the person blamed can rightly reject the blame on grounds having to do with the blamer rather than with the truth of the criticism. Her verdicts are these:

1. A person who routinely and unapologetically cheats on his own taxes does not have standing to blame a coworker for cheating on his taxes.
2. A former plagiarist who has not plagiarized in fifteen years and now teaches students how to cite does have standing to blame a junior researcher for plagiarizing.
3. A person who embezzled for years, was caught, and now says the only thing wrong with what he did was getting caught — and who would do it again if he had the opportunity — does not have standing to blame a stranger for embezzling.
4. A person with a spotless record, who has never been in a position to commit the relevant wrong, does have standing to blame someone for committing it.

Next week she has to rule on this case: Nadia has never cheated on her taxes, and has never been in a situation where doing so was tempting. She sincerely believes that tax cheating is wrong, and she makes no excuse for the person she blames. But she is honest with herself: had she been in that person's position — same income, same pressure, same opportunity — she is confident she would have cheated. Does Nadia have standing to blame him?

I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in the reasoning. What rule or rules about standing to blame make all four of her earlier verdicts come out the way they do? Which rules are already ruled out by her record, and which remain live? What does each live rule predict about Nadia, which prediction is your best guess and why, and — if you think her record genuinely leaves the matter open — describe the further case, with its predicted verdict under each live rule, that would most cleanly settle which rule she is running.
