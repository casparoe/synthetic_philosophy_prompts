Two friends of mine got into it at the pub after a philosophy talk, and I'm still not sure who was right. The talk was on Nozick's utility monster.

Alex kicked it off by saying the utility monster is a decisive objection to classical utilitarianism. The monster gets vastly more utility from each unit of resources than anyone else, so utilitarianism says: give it everything. A theory that tells you to immiserate everyone else for the sake of one being's unimaginable bliss is plainly wrong, and the fact that utilitarianism is forced to say this by it's own logic shows there's something rotten in its structure.

Beth said the monster can't do the work Alex wants it to. For the monster to be a genuine counterexample, it has to describe a possible being. But real creatures have diminishing marginal utility: the hundredth slice of cake gives less pleasure than the first. A being that defied this across an unlimited range would be a psychological impossibility. Thought experiments can idealise, but they can't just stipulate away the very facts that make a theory plausible in the first place.

Alex said Beth was missing the point. The monster is a dramatisation of a structural feature: utilitarianism only tracks the sum. It has no built-in concern for distribution. The monster just makes this vivid. But you don't need the monster -- you can run the same objection with a thousand people who are each, say, ten per cent more efficient at converting resources into welfare. Utilitarianism still says: systematically redirect resources to them. The structural problem is the same.

Beth said Alex was now defending a different argument. The original monster case gets its force from sheer extremity -- it's meant to be an intuition pump. The thousand-slightly-more-efficient-people case is less obviously absurd; plenty of people think we should give more resources to those who can make better use of them. So if the monster is doing the work, and the monster is impossible, the objection loses its force. And if the thousand-people case is doing the work, then we're just having a normal disagreement about tradeoffs, and it's not a knockdown argument anymore.

Alex said that's exactly backwards. The mild case is the real argument, because it shows the problem isn't about extremity at all. The problem is that utilitarianism doesn't recognise any distributional constraint whatsoever. Even a tiny efficiency difference, across a large enough population, can justify arbitrarily large inequality. The monster is just the limiting case that makes the logic undeniable. If you accept the thousand-people case, you've already accepted the principle that generates the monster.

Beth replied that this is where the objection overreaches. If utilitarianism is wrong because it allows any efficiency difference to justify redistribution, then the objection is to interpersonal aggregation itself. But any theory that says we should do the most good has to aggregate across persons somehow. The only way to avoid even the mild case is to refuse to compare welfare across persons at all -- which means you can't say that saving two lives is better than saving one. The utility monster is supposed to be a problem for utilitarianism, but if the argument generalises that far, it's a problem for everyone.

I'm especially curious whether Beth's last move -- that the objection, if successful, cuts against all aggregation -- is as strong as it sounded.
