I have been reading Henry Sidgwick in a course on the history of utilitarianism, and I keep stumbling over a fact that is usually mentioned only in passing: Sidgwick, the author of The Methods of Ethics and one of the most careful moral philosophers of the nineteenth century, was also a founder and early president of the Society for Psychical Research, and he spent years investigating mediums, telepathy, and the evidence for survival after bodily death. The standard treatment tends to treat this as a biographical oddity or an embarrassment, but I suspect that is too quick.

Could you explain the philosophical rationale for Sidgwick's involvement in psychical research? In particular, I want to understand what he thought was ethically at stake in the question of whether the individual personality survives death, and how this connects to the problem he called the dualism of practical reason: the apparent conflict between the demands of rational self-interest and the demands of impartial benevolence. Please reconstruct the argument that would make survival after death matter for the foundations of his utilitarian project, and then assess it. If you think the connection is weaker than Sidgwick took it to be, say precisely where the reasoning fails. I am not looking for biography or a survey of his investigations, but for the systematic connection between his ethics and his psychical research.
