I recently read a popular philosophy book that discusses Daniel Dennett's use of the sphex wasp to illuminate questions about free will. The author summarises Dennett's view roughly as follows:

"The philosopher Daniel Dennett famously uses the sphex wasp to illuminate the nature of free will. The wasp performs an elaborate sequence — hunting a cricket, paralysing it, dragging it to the burrow entrance, going inside to inspect, then coming back out to retrieve it — that looks intelligent and purposeful. But if an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside inspecting, the wasp will repeat the inspection routine, potentially indefinitely, never simply dragging the cricket straight in. Dennett's point is that the wasp's behaviour is perfectly deterministic yet rigid: it cannot adapt because it is locked into a fixed behavioural programme. He calls this 'sphexishness' and uses it to argue that what matters for free will is not whether our actions are determined but whether we are capable of noticing our own patterns and breaking out of them. On Dennett's view, humans are less sphexish than wasps because consciousness allows us to step back from our routines and consider alternatives. The more conscious deliberation we engage in, the freer we are. This is why Dennett thinks free will is compatible with determinism: freedom is a matter of degree, measured by how flexibly we respond to our circumstances, and humans score high on this scale even if our brains are deterministic machines."

I know Dennett has quite specific and deflationary views about consciousness, and I wonder whether the summary smuggles in a more traditional picture of conscious control than Dennett would accept.
