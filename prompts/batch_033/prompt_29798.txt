In a moral philosophy reading group I recently heard a speaker defend the view that a man who finds his wife and a stranger both drowning and saves his wife should not be expected to justify the choice in impartial terms. When another participant suggested that the man could justify himself by saying that she was his wife and that in such circumstances one is permitted to favour one's spouse, the speaker replied that this was precisely the justification Bernard Williams was rejecting when he said that the man would have had 'one thought too many.' That reply ended the discussion, but I suspect the phrase was doing more work than it can bear, or at least more than one kind of work. I would like a careful disambiguation of the slogan. What distinct claims could 'one thought too many' be used to assert—about the husband's psychology, about what the wife might reasonably hope for, about the place of impartial justification within close personal relationships, and about the standing of impartial moral theories generally, including the integrity objection with which the phrase is often associated? For each candidate claim, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something that is likely false, and then say which readings would actually support the speaker's dismissal of the proposed justification. I would also like to know whether Williams's own discussion of the example warrants the strongest of these readings, and whether there is a reading on which the husband may offer an impartial justification afterwards without that justification itself being one thought too many.
