I am a philosophy professor who also serves on the clinical ethics committee at a teaching hospital. Over the past several years, our committee has issued opinions on a series of cases involving advance directives. I would like you to analyze our track record and help us decide a new case that has come before us.

Here are our precedents, summarized:

1. **Margot.** A 32-year-old woman, after watching her grandmother decline with dementia, signed an advance directive refusing all life-sustaining treatment if she ever reached a stage where she could no longer recognize close family members. At 55 she developed early-onset Alzheimer's. At 62 she had advanced dementia, did not recognize her husband, but was affectionate, sang to herself, and seemed to take pleasure in simple activities. She developed a serious infection treatable with antibiotics. We voted 4–3 to honor the directive.

2. **Robert.** A 50-year-old man with early-stage dementia signed an advance directive refusing antibiotics for any life-threatening infection, stating he would rather die than live with severe dementia. At 55 he had moderate dementia, was frequently agitated and distressed, and appeared to suffer considerably. He developed pneumonia. We voted unanimously to honor the directive.

3. **Sarah.** A 40-year-old competent woman signed an advance directive refusing mechanical ventilation if she were ever in a condition where she could not communicate. A year later she developed Guillain-Barré syndrome, became temporarily paralyzed and unable to speak, but was fully conscious and expected to recover. We voted unanimously *not* to honor the directive, on the grounds that the clinical situation she was in did not match the kind of scenario she had in mind.

4. **David.** A 70-year-old man with mild cognitive impairment signed an advance directive refusing tube feeding if he developed severe dementia. He eventually reached severe dementia, was generally content, ate willingly when food was offered, and seemed to take genuine pleasure in meals. He developed a condition that made swallowing temporarily difficult, requiring a short course of tube feeding. We voted 4–3 *not* to honor the directive.

5. **Elena.** A 25-year-old woman with bipolar disorder signed an advance directive during a manic episode, refusing all psychiatric medication. She later stabilized and did not reaffirm it. During a subsequent severe depressive episode with suicidal ideation, clinicians wanted to administer medication. We voted unanimously not to honor the directive, citing doubts about the validity of a decision made under impaired judgment.

6. **James.** A 60-year-old man with ALS signed an advance directive refusing mechanical ventilation. As his disease progressed, he remained competent and communicated through eye-tracking technology. When respiratory failure set in, he signaled that he now wanted ventilation. We voted unanimously to honor his current wish.

Now the new case:

**Clara.** Clara is a 60-year-old woman with early-stage Alzheimer's disease. She has cared for her own mother through a decade of dementia and has strong, articulate views about how she wishes to live and die. She writes a detailed advance directive — accompanied by a video recording and a personal journal — refusing all life-sustaining treatment if she ever reaches a stage where she no longer recognizes her spouse of 40 years. She is explicit that this refusal includes antibiotics, artificial nutrition, and hydration, and that her wishes are not to be overridden even if she appears content or happy in that state. At 68, Clara has advanced dementia, does not recognize her spouse, but is calm, enjoys music, takes pleasure in simple activities, and shows no signs of distress or suffering. She develops a severe bacterial infection that requires a two-week course of intravenous antibiotics. The treatment would be somewhat uncomfortable but not painful. Without it, she will likely die within weeks.

We need to decide whether to honor Clara's advance directive.

Please analyze our precedents and recommend a verdict on Clara's case. Specifically, I would like you to:

- Identify the candidate principles or rules that are consistent with our precedents (or with most of them, acknowledging where they diverge).
- Explain what each candidate rule would prescribe for Clara's case.
- Identify which of our precedents is the closest analog to Clara's case, and which precedent is the most difficult to reconcile with the others.
- Give your best recommendation for what we should decide if we want to be consistent with our own track record, and explain what principle we would be adopting with that choice.
- Finally, tell me what argument or consideration would be most likely to change your recommendation — that is, where your reasoning is most vulnerable, and what new information or different framing of the case would lead you to a different verdict.
