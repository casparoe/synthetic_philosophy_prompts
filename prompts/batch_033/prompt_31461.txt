Write a dialogue of roughly 1,500 words between two equally sophisticated members of a philanthropic advisory board touring a large commercial black soldier fly farm. The board must decide whether to recommend a major grant aimed at reducing invertebrate suffering. One member, the interventionist, holds that the scale argument is action-guiding now: even a modest probability that insects are sentient, multiplied across the astronomical number of insects alive at any moment and the additional trillions farmed or killed each year, yields expected welfare stakes large enough to justify shifting substantial resources toward invertebrate welfare, including changes to farming and slaughter practices. The other member, the cautious researcher, holds that the scale argument is best understood as a decisive reason to fund research on insect sentience and welfare measurement rather than as a license for large interventions, because the relevant probabilities are too model-dependent and the downstream effects too poorly understood for expected-value reasoning to be reliable here.

Neither character should be a strawman: each should be able to state the other's view in terms the other would accept, and each should make at least one genuine concession that costs them something during the exchange. The dialogue should be a real back-and-forth in which later moves respond to specific earlier arguments rather than a sequence of position statements. The characters should argue from first principles and from concrete facts about insects and farming, without citing any literature or namedropping.

At a minimum the exchange should work through these pressure points: the charge that the scale argument is a Pascal's-mugging-style inference from tiny probabilities to huge practical conclusions; the reply that refusing to act is itself a large intervention in insects' lives and cannot claim to be neutral; the difference between well-calibrated risk and model uncertainty that undermines the expected-value calculation; and the question of whether welfare can be compared across forms of life so different from us. The dialogue should end with the two characters having located their disagreement as precisely as they can, and it should remain genuinely open whether the disagreement is ultimately about decision theory, about the empirical evidence, or about how to handle moral uncertainty. Use American English throughout. End the dialogue with a single line beginning Summary: that states in one sentence where the disagreement ultimately lies.
