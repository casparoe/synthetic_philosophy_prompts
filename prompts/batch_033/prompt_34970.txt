I'm a novelist, and I've been rereading Ursula Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" alongside the philosophical literature on trolley problems and the doctrine of double effect. I want a reading of the story through that lens. In Omelas, an entire city's flourishing depends on the abject misery of a single child locked in a cellar — everyone knows this, and after an initial horrified reckoning, most accept it as the price of their happiness. The standard trolley case asks whether you would sacrifice one to save five; Omelas asks something closer to whether you can justify benefiting from a sacrifice made on your behalf, and what it means if you cannot. I'd like a reading that takes both the story and the DDE seriously. Where does the Omelas scenario track the DDE's distinction between intending harm as a means and merely foreseeing it as a side effect, and where does the story break that distinction? Does the story dramatize something the philosophical debate misses — perhaps about the difference between a one-off intervention and an ongoing structural arrangement, or about what it means to walk away from a wrong you did not create but continue to benefit from? And where does the story resist the philosophical lens: are there features of Omelas that the DDE framework simply cannot capture, and that an honest critic should admit? I'm less interested in whether Le Guin herself had a position than in what the story, read carefully, does to the philosophical categories.
