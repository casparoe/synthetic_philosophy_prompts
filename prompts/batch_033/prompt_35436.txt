Below is a question from a philosophy of psychiatry exam, followed by a student's answer.

Question: Explain Ian Hacking's notion of a looping effect and why it leads him to treat psychiatric classifications as interactive kinds rather than indifferent labels for pre-existing conditions. Illustrate with an example, and state one genuine limitation of the concept.

Student answer:

Hacking's looping effect is the process by which a classification of people interacts with the people classified. Once individuals become aware of a category, they can take it up in their self-understanding and alter their conduct accordingly; the altered conduct then changes what members of the category are like, and that change feeds back into how the category is understood and applied. Hacking calls categories that work this way interactive kinds, and he contrasts them with indifferent kinds, such as electrons, which are not changed by being classified. In psychiatry, a diagnosis such as multiple personality disorder can become part of how patients describe their experience, and the availability of the category can shape how symptoms are presented; clinicians in turn revise the category, which is the dynamic nominalism Hacking describes. The looping effect therefore shows that psychiatric categories are self-fulfilling prophecies: the label brings about the very condition it names. One limitation of the concept is that it appears to apply only to psychiatric classifications, since those are the only categories that people take up and act on.
