I’m trying to work out what I actually believe about how to act when I’m morally uncertain — that is, when I’m not sure which moral theory is correct, or not sure how a moral principle applies to a particular situation. I’ve been going case by case, and my intuitive verdicts resist any simple principle. Here they are, with my rough credences in the relevant moral claims.

**1. Factory-farmed meat.** I’m about 80% confident that factory farming causes severe suffering to beings with moral standing, and 20% confident that either animals lack the relevant consciousness or their suffering does not generate obligations. I judge that eating factory-farmed meat is wrong. The 20% chance that I am complicit in enormous suffering seems to settle it.

**2. The risky charity.** I donate to a global health charity. I’m 90% confident it saves lives extremely cost-effectively, but I have read credible criticisms suggesting a 10% chance that its interventions undermine local health systems and do net harm over the long term. I judge that I should keep donating. The 10% risk of harm does not overturn the 90% case.

**3. The transfusion refusal.** I am a physician. A patient refuses a life-saving blood transfusion on religious grounds. I am 70% confident the patient is competent and the refusal is autonomous; 30% confident their judgment is impaired by their medical condition. I judge that I should respect the refusal, even though the stakes are life and death.

**4. The election.** Candidate A is clearly better on domestic policy. On foreign policy, I am uncertain: 60% chance A is better, 40% chance A is significantly worse than B. I vote for A without hesitation. The expected choiceworthiness seems to favour A, and the tail risk does not change that.

**5. The picket line.** My union is on strike. I am 60% confident the union’s demands are justified and 40% confident they are unreasonable. I judge that I should respect the picket line. Crossing it risks complicity in undermining my coworkers, and I should not take that risk when I am not confident the strike is unjustified.

**6. The colleague’s misconduct.** I suspect a colleague fudged data in a grant application, but I am only 40% confident. I judge that I should not report it, and that reporting would be wrong — the risk of falsely accusing someone outweighs the chance of catching misconduct. I would need to be much more confident.

I have been trying out the principle *maximize expected choiceworthiness*: treat moral uncertainty the way you would treat factual uncertainty under expected utility theory, by weighing the choiceworthiness of each action under each moral theory by your credence in that theory. But this principle seems to give the wrong answer in the meat case. It would say: if you are 80% confident eating meat is wrong and 20% confident it is permissible, and if the permissible theory assigns eating meat a neutral value, then the expected choiceworthiness might still favour eating meat — yet I feel it is wrong. It also struggles with the picket line case and the misconduct case, where I seem to be applying a kind of moral precautionary principle that the expected-choiceworthiness framework does not capture. Yet the charity case and the election case fit the expected-choiceworthiness framework perfectly — there I am not precautionary at all.

My question is this. Is there a single principle that captures all six verdicts, or at least most of them? If not, which verdicts should I overturn, and on what grounds? Please state the best available principle precisely, identify which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and explain what it says about a case I keep going back and forth on: I have been offered a well-paying job at a company whose core business is morally ambiguous — I would say there is roughly a 50% chance the work is ethically fine, a 30% chance it is mildly harmful, and a 20% chance it is seriously harmful. Should I take the job?
