I sit on the board of a conservation trust that manages a large fenced reserve. We've got a proposal in front of us from one of our own field biologists, who wants funding for a two-year pilot to treat sarcoptic mange in the reserve's fox population using medicated bait stations. The mange is spreading and the foxes that get it die slowly over a few weeks. The technical side looks solid: the bait is target-specific, the dosing is worked out, and she's budgeted for monitoring.

The problem is our trust's mission statement commits us to preserving native ecosystems and biodiversity, and says nothing about animal welfare. Treating the foxes would probably cut off a slow and nasty source of mortality, but it would also leave fox numbers higher than they'd otherwise be, and nobody's modelled what that does to the small mammals and ground-nesting birds on the reserve. Some board members think the proposal is just a category error — our job is ecological integrity, not running a field hospital for wild animals. Others have started using the phrase 'wild animal suffering' as if its an obvious part of our remit, and they point out that we already intervene plenty (we cull invasives, we repair erosion), so the 'hands off nature' line isn't one we actually hold.

I need a short written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than just restating the mission statement. I'd also like you to be straight with us about what we'd be commiting ourselves to if we approve: is there a principled stopping point between treating mange and, say, predator control or supplementary feeding during a hard winter?
