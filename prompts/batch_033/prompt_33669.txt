In existential risk research, there's a practice called correcting for the 'anthropic shadow.' The idea is simple: when we look at the historical record to estimate how often civilization-ending catastrophes happen, we can't just count the centuries humanity has survived and say 'the risk is tiny.' If a catastrophe had wiped us out in the 12th century, nobody would be around now to observe that fact. Our dataset is unavoidably biased — we only ever get to observe histories where we survived. So some researchers argue we should adjust our estimates upward: the true extinction risk might be much higher than the naive survivial record suggests.

What I want to know is: what does this practice presuppose about anthropic reasoning? Specifically, does it commit you to the self-sampling assumption (SSA) rather than the self-indication assumption (SIA)? And if it does, is that consistent with the way the same researchers often talk about the astronomical value of future generations — a move that seems to treat the existence of possible future people as something we should update on, which feels more SIA-flavored? And where does the presumptuous philosopher fit into all this?

(2) 'SSA vs. SIA and the presumptuous philosopher' — explain whether SIA could also license the correction, or whether adopting SIA would force us to abandon it, and how the presumptuous philosopher bears on the choice. (3) 'Is the package consistent?' — take a stand on whether combining anthropic shadow reasoning with longtermist concern for vast future generations hangs together philosophically, or whether there's a hidden contradiction.
