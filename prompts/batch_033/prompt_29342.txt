I am a high school student writing an essay for an independent project, and I would like detailed comments on the argument I have sketched below. I am not asking for a reading list or for sources; I want an evaluation of the reasoning itself.

The working idea of my essay is that the question whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer is badly posed because it runs together three things one might mean by the word reward. First, there is the engineering sense: a scalar signal that a learning algorithm uses to update a policy or value function. Second, there is the functional sense: a state that plays the role of reinforcement, in that it makes certain behaviors more likely and sets an optimization target. Third, there is the phenomenal sense: a state that feels good or bad to the agent, with valence. My essay argues that only the third sense is relevant to suffering, and that the first two senses do not get you to the third by themselves.

In particular, I claim that a reward prediction error in a standard deep reinforcement learning system is the wrong kind of thing to be a pain state. It is defined entirely by its role in updating parameters; it has no intrinsic badness, no evaluative directedness at the agent's own condition, and no connection to anything the agent is trying to avoid except as an instrumental consequence of the update rule. I then consider the objection that this merely restates the general problem of explaining phenomenal consciousness, and that from the outside one can never tell whether a system has felt valence. I want to resist that objection by arguing that the issue is partly architectural rather than purely epistemic: a system could in principle be built so that its value function or its reward signal is implemented as a state with negative felt quality rather than as a bare scalar, and that difference would be substantive rather than merely verbal. But I am not sure this is a real distinction rather than a metaphor, and that is one of the places where I most want criticism.

I would like comments on three things. First, accuracy: is the three-way distinction between reward as an engineering signal, reward as a functional role, and reward as phenomenal valence standard enough to rely on, and am I drawing it correctly? Second, interest: is the central claim that the interesting boundary is optimization target versus valenced state, rather than reward versus no reward, worth making, or does it just relabel a familiar point? Third, weakness: where is the argument weakest, and what is the strongest objection I have not anticipated?
