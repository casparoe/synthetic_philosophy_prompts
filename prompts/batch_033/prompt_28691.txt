So in my ethics seminar we got into this argument about supererogation and I'm still not sure who was right. Two people in my group went back and forth and I just took notes. Here's roughly how it went:

The case was someone who donates a kidney to a stranger — not a family member, not a friend, just someone on the matching list they've never met.

Maya said this is a textbook case of supererogation: it's morally excellent, you're a hero for doing it, but nobody would say you *had* to. You can't be blamed for keeping both your kidneys.

Dan said he wasn't so sure. He pointed out that the risk of death from donation is something like 1 in 3000, and the long-term health outcomes for donors are basically normal — they live just as long as non-donors on average. If you can save someone's life at that level of risk to yourself, he said, it starts looking less like a nice bonus and more like something morality actually demands, the way we'd demand you pull a drowning kid out of a shallow pond.

Maya came back with: the pond case involves no bodily intrusion. Letting someone cut you open and remove an organ is qualitatively different from getting your clothes wet. Bodily integrity is a special kind of right, and you can't be obligated to violate it even for a very good cause.

Dan said that can't be right, because we *do* think you can be obligated to undergo bodily risk in some cases — a parent who won't donate bone marrow to their dying child is a monster, not just ungenerous. So bodily integrity isn't an absolute shield against obligation.

Maya said the parent-child case is different because parents have special obligations to their kids that they don't have to strangers. The question is about what we owe to people we have no special relationship with.

Dan said fine, but then consider this: if the special-obligations point is what's doing the work, then the difference between obligatory and supererogatory isn't really about the act itself — it's about your relationship to the beneficiary. That seems weird, because when we call an act supererogatory we're usually commenting on the act, not on who it's directed at.

Maya said that's a feature not a bug: supererogation is partly relational. The same physical act can be obligatory toward your child and supererogatory toward a stranger. That doesn't make the concept incoherent.

Dan said okay, but now push it further. If I walk past a stranger drowning in a pond, saving them is obligatory even though I have no special relationship to them. So relationship isn't necessary for obligation. And if the kidney case involves comparable benefit (a life saved) and comparable or only slightly higher cost, why isn't it also obligatory? The only remaining difference is bodily intrusion, and we already agreed that bodily intrusion doesn't categorically block obligation.

Maya said the pond rescue and the kidney donation aren't comparable in cost. The pond rescue costs you five minutes and wet clothes. Kidney donation costs you a permanent physical alteration, weeks of recovery, and a small but real risk of death. You can't just handwave that as "slightly higher."

Dan said he wasn't handwaving it — he was pointing out that if you actually look at the numbers, the expected harm to the donor is tiny compared to the expected benefit to the recipient (who otherwise dies). And we normally think that when the cost-benefit ratio is that lopsided, you have a duty to act. That's basically the logic behind Good Samaritan laws and why we think it's wrong to not call an ambulance when someone's bleeding out.

Maya said Good Samaritan laws require minimal cost — calling 911. They don't require you to undergo surgery. That's the whole point: there's a cost threshold, and kidney donation is on the far side of it.

Dan said but where's the threshold and why there? If you can't give a principled answer, then "supererogatory" is just a label for "stuff I don't feel like doing."

At that point the seminar ended and I never got to hear Maya's response. But I've been turning it over since.

Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move? For each turn, say who has the better of it (if either does) and why. And then give me your overall take: is kidney donation to a stranger supererogatory, obligatory, or does the answer depend on further facts — and if so, which ones?
