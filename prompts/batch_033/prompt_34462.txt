I retired a few years back from a career in physical chemistry, and since then I've been working my way through ethics and decision theory, mostly on my own. A move I keep seeing in the corners of the internet I now spend too much time in is that moral or political compromise is basically gains from trade. The thought is: two people (or two groups) can have quite different value systems, and each can concede on something they care about less to get something they care about more, so both end up with more of what they actually value. Compromise therefore isn't selling out, its a Pareto improvement, and refusing a mutually beneficial moral trade is as irrational as refusing a mutually beneficial economic trade. I've seen this used to argue that an animal-welfare person and a global-poverty person should each put money into the other's cause, and also that on my local planning commission the pro-housing faction and the preservation faction should just swap concessions.

My reflex after decades of watching models get overextended is that this analogy is doing a lot of quiet work. I'd like you to audit it properly. Give me the correspondence table first — what (if anything) plays the role of the buyer, the seller, the goods, the price, the contract, the gains from trade — and then go through where the mapping breaks. In particular, does the economic picture smuggle in that the two sides share a common currency or at least a common scale of value, that preferences are fixed and available to be traded against each other, that nobody else is affected, and that whatever is promised is actually enforceable? Then the part I actually care about: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive — that this kind of compromise is generally a good thing and that refusing it is irrational — or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? And if there's a better analogy for what's going on when people with different values make compromises, I'd like to hear it.
