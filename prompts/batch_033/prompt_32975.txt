I'm a retired physical chemist, and I've been reading around in philosophy of mind and philosophy of biology for a few years now, and there's one argumentative move I keep seeing that I'd like to know the name of, if it has one.

The pattern goes like this. Someone offers an explanation of some fancy phenomenon — consciousness, say, or the appearance of design in living things — that builds it up step by step from simpler parts: neurons and signals, or mutation and selection, that sort of thing. A critic objects that this is greedy reductionism, that it tries to explain the higher-level phenomenon away rather than explain it. So far I'm with them; reductions can overreach, and I've seen enough hand-waving about molecules to know that. But then the same critic, often in the very next paragraph, turns around and offers their own explanation, and it turns out to be a top-down cause that is simply asserted: the mind as a whole has a primitive causal power, or life is pulled toward complexity by a principle of self-organization. No account of how the higher-level cause arose from the parts, how it interacts with them, or how you would test it. And this gets treated as the sophisticated non-reductionist option, when to me it looks like the same sin pointed upward: instead of explaining the top from the bottom without remainder, they've added an unexplained cause at the top and called it an explanation.

I spent my career around mechanisms, so when someone calls a mechanism greedy and then offers no mechanism at all, I feel like I'm missing something. I'm not asking wether the top-down cause is true. I'm asking about the shape of the argument — the pairing of a charge of greedy reductionism with an unexplained higher-level cause that is somehow exempt from the same criticism. I know the phrase greedy reductionism covers the first half, but that names an overreach in the downward direction. What I'm after is the upward version, and the way people slide between the two. Does the upward move have a name of its own? Does the combination have one? And if there's no established name, I'd want you to say so and propose one, with its edges marked: for example, does it still count if the higher-level cause gets a mathematical description but no mechanism, or if the person admits it's just a placeholder?
