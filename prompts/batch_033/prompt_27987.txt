I'm putting together a unit on thought experiments and I keep coming back to Ursula Le Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas.' It gets taught as a story but it basically functions as an intuition pump about utilitarianism—the whole city's happiness depends on one kid locked in a dark room, and the narrator more or less dares the reader to say whether they'd accept the deal. I'd like a reading of the story through the lens of how intuition pumps persuade and mislead. Where does Le Guin build the pump carefully (the suffering being stipulated as necessary, the way she refuses to describe the child fully so readers fill in the worst themselves, the fact that walking away is left unexplained), and where does the story rig the game in a way a philosopher should flag? Does the story make the utilitarian tradeoff feel more damning than the bare argument can actually show, or does it expose something the standard trolley-style cases miss because they stay too schematic? I don't need a plot summary—I know the story. What I want is an honest critic's account of the story as philosophical machinary, including the places where it resists the reading or overreaches.
