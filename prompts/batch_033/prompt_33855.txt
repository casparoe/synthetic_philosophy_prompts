I'm a high school junior in a world philosophy elective, and I'm preparing a presentation for my class on the debate between Mencius and Xunzi about human nature. I want to lead my classmates through a thought experiment that forces them to see exactly where the two philosophers come apart, rather than just memorizing that Mencius says human nature is good and Xunzi says it's bad. Could you construct a thought experiment to the following specifications?

Constraints:

- The scenario should involve a single spontaneous action by a child between ages three and six, in an ordinary setting (a home, a playground, a classroom). No adults are present or intervening, so the child's behavior cannot be explained as obedience to an authority figure.
- The action must be morally ambiguous: it should be possible for a Mencian to read it as a natural sprout of compassion or innate moral inclination, and equally possible for a Xunzian to read it as raw self-interest that has not yet been reshaped by ritual, education, or deliberate effort.
- The scenario should be structured so that the feature doing the work is timing: the child's first impulse and second impulse go in opposite moral directions, and the disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi turns on which of the two impulses counts as the expression of the child's underlying nature.
- One paragraph, realistic, no exotic scenarios.

After presenting the scenario, please walk through what each philosopher would say about it and why — specifically, how each would identify the child's "nature" with one impulse rather than the other, and what each would say about the opposite impulse.

Then identify:

(a) Which feature of the scenario is doing the work to force the two readings apart.
(b) What happens if you remove that feature — does the case collapse into one philosopher's favor, and if so, which one?
(c) How a defender of the philosopher who comes out looking worse could revise or refine their account of human nature to handle the case without giving up their core claim.
