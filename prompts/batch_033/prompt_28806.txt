EXAM QUESTION (Midterm, Decision Theory, PHIL 164):

Newcomb's problem: a Predictor with a proven track record of high accuracy (say, 99%) presents you with two boxes. Box A is transparent and visibly contains $1,000. Box B is opaque, and its contents were determined before you choose: if the Predictor predicted you will take only Box B, it placed $1,000,000 inside; if it predicted you will take both boxes, it left Box B empty. You know all of this. You may choose either to take only Box B ("one-box") or to take both boxes ("two-box"). Explain the disagreement between one-boxers and two-boxers. What decision principle does each side appeal to, and what deeper dispute in decision theory does this disagreement reflect? Which position do you find more compelling, and why?

STUDENT ANSWER:

The disagreement between one-boxers and two-boxers is a clash between two fundamental decision principles: dominance and expected utility maximization.

The two-boxer appeals to dominance. Consider the two possible states of the world: either the Predictor predicted one-boxing, or it predicted two-boxing. If it predicted one-boxing, Box B has $1,000,000; choosing both boxes yields $1,001,000, while choosing only Box B yields $1,000,000. If it predicted two-boxing, Box B is empty; choosing both boxes yields $1,000, while choosing only Box B yields $0. In both possible states, two-boxing yields strictly more money than one-boxing. Therefore, two-boxing dominates one-boxing and is the rational choice.

The one-boxer appeals to expected utility maximization. Given the Predictor's 99% accuracy, the probability that Box B contains $1,000,000 conditional on your one-boxing is 0.99, and the probability that it contains $1,000,000 conditional on your two-boxing is 0.01. The expected monetary value of one-boxing is 0.99 × $1,000,000 = $990,000, while the expected value of two-boxing is 0.01 × $1,001,000 + 0.99 × $1,000 ≈ $11,000. One-boxing maximizes expected value, so it is the rational choice.

This disagreement reflects a deeper dispute between evidential decision theory (EDT) and causal decision theory (CDT). EDT evaluates actions by asking what the action would be evidence for: since one-boxing is strong evidence that $1,000,000 is in Box B, EDT recommends one-boxing. CDT evaluates actions by asking what the action would causally bring about: since your choice cannot causally affect a prediction made in the past, CDT treats the contents of Box B as fixed and evaluates each action against that fixed state. This yields the dominance argument, so CDT recommends two-boxing.

CDT holds that the dominance principle applies only when the states of the world are probabilistically independent of the act. If the states and the act are probabilistically dependent, dominance reasoning is invalid because you cannot treat the states as the same across both actions. In Newcomb's problem, the Predictor's accuracy creates a probabilistic dependence between your choice and the contents of Box B, so the one-boxer is entitled to reject the two-boxer's dominance argument as question-begging. The two-boxer is effectively assuming CDT to argue for CDT.

I find the two-boxing position more compelling. The one-boxer's expected utility calculation tacitly treats the evidential correlation between one-boxing and getting $1,000,000 as though it were a causal relation. But at the moment of choice, the money is either in Box B or it isn't, and nothing you do can change what was already placed there. The one-boxer is acting as though their decision can retroactively influence the Predictor's past prediction, which is impossible. Rationality requires acting on the causal consequences of your choices, not on mere correlations, so two-boxing is correct.
