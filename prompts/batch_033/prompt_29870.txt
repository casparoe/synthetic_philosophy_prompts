I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've ended up going down a rabbit hole about what it means to know something. It started during a postmortem argument about whether we knew a deploy was safe when the test suite was passing for the wrong reason, and now I've been reading around the Gettier problem and collecting my own gut verdicts on cases. I'd like to know whether there is one principle behind my verdicts, or whether some of them have to go.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. My kitchen clock reads 3:00, so I believe it's 3:00. It is 3:00, but the clock stopped exactly twelve hours earlier and I didn't know that. Verdict: I don't know it's 3:00.
2. I believe the standup is at 10:30 because my calendar says so. The calendar is correct and the standup is at 10:30. Verdict: I know.
3. I believe the standup is at 10:30 because my calendar says so, but that calendar entry is a stale one from last week; the organizer happened to move today's standup to 10:30. Verdict: I don't know.
4. I have overwhelming evidence that my coworker Dana still drives a Honda: I've seen her drive it for years, and she told me so last week. I form the belief that either Dana still drives a Honda or Priya is in the London office this week. I have no idea where Priya is. Dana actually sold the Honda a few days ago, but Priya is in London. Verdict: I don't know the either/or claim, even though it's true and I had good reason to accept it.
5. A reliable manager has told me Sam will get the promotion, and I saw Sam empty ten coins from his pocket this morning, so I infer that the person who gets the promotion has ten coins in his pocket. In fact I get the promotion, and I happen to have ten coins in my pocket too, though that played no part in my inference. Verdict: I don't know it.
6. I'm driving through a county that, unbeknownst to me, is full of extremely convincing barn facades left over from a film shoot. I look at the one real barn in the area and believe that it's a barn. It is a barn. Verdict: I don't know it, but this case bothers me, because my eyes were working fine and I can't point to any false step.
7. I see a sheep-shaped boulder in a field and believe there's a sheep in the field. There is a sheep, hidden behind the boulder. Verdict: I don't know there's a sheep.
8. I read the CI logs, see the failing tests, and believe the build is broken. The build is broken. Verdict: I know.
9. I believe I have two hands. Verdict: I know, and any account that denies this has gone wrong somewhere.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? I can already see that a belief's being true and justified isn't enough, and that something like the belief's not resting on a false assumption handles several of the cases but not the barn one. Please state the principle or candidates precisely, say which case motivates each part, apply the principle to at least one case I haven't listed, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Please make the final line of your answer a single sentence beginning "Verdict:" that states the principle you'd endorse.
