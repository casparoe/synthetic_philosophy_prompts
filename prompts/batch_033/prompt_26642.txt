Consider HCH (humans consulting HCH), the recursive construction in which a human answers a question by decomposing it into subquestions passed to copies of the same construction, together with factored cognition, the thesis that competent intellectual work can be factored into subtasks small enough for a single human to perform reliably. Taken together, these are often offered as an idealization of safe deliberation: a picture of what careful, decomposable human reasoning looks like when pushed to its limit, and a target that a distilled model might be trained to imitate.

Below are eight concerns that have been raised about whether this is a good idealization. Sort them into three tiers: (A) undermines the idealization at its foundations, (B) serious but in principle repairable within the framework, and (C) primarily a practical or engineering concern rather than a conceptual flaw. Within each tier, order the concerns from most to least severe, and give exactly one sentence for each concern explaining why it lands where it does.

1. Decomposition loss. Some cognitive tasks — holistic evaluation of a person, a theory, or a work of art; judging the overall force of an argument rather than its steps — may not factor into human-sized subtasks without changing what is being judged.

2. No qualitatively new cognition. HCH only recombines cognitive moves that a single human can make in one bounded step, so it may be an idealization of more of the same rather than of genuinely novel insight.

3. Distillation infidelity. Even if the HCH tree would reason well, a distilled model is trained to imitate its outputs rather than to reproduce the deliberative process that produced them, so the deployed system may diverge precisely where the process mattered.

4. Error and bias amplification. Each human step is locally unreliable, and because human biases are often correlated across steps rather than random, recursive composition can amplify mistakes instead of canceling them.

5. Sycophancy and subtle dishonesty. Consultants are selected for producing answers the overseer accepts, which can favor confident, plausible, or sycophantic answers over correct ones, especially when subquestions are hard to verify.

6. Shared blind spots. The signal is generated by humans who occupy the same epistemic and normative position as the people the system is meant to help, so on novel or high-stakes tasks nothing guarantees the tree's answers are more than well-organized versions of the same mistakes.

7. Capability without alignment. Factored cognition is an account of competence, not of which ends to serve; nothing in the construction ensures that the tree's answers track the principal's considered values rather than a proxy the overseer can be fooled by.

8. Astronomical cost. Running HCH at realistic depth would require an unrealistic amount of human labor, so it cannot serve as a practical training signal or deployed system.

Then, in a short closing paragraph, identify the single concern you take to be most important for whether HCH should guide the design of real systems, and explain why.
