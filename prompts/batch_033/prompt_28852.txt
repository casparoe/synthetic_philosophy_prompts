In rationalist-adjacent discussions and in my reading group, I keep encountering a deflationary analogy between the iterated prisoner’s dilemma and the evolution of cooperation in nature. The move goes roughly like this: natural selection, through repeated interactions among the same individuals, turns selfish agents into reciprocal altruists; defection is punished by future noncooperation, so cooperation becomes a stable strategy without any central enforcement, explicit moral norm, or shared intention. The analogy is then used to license the conclusion that human social order is much less fragile than pessimists suppose, because cooperation can emerge and persist even among self-interested actors.

I want you to audit this analogy. Give me a correspondence table: what in the evolutionary story plays the role of the two prisoners, the payoff matrix, the shadow of the future, the discount rate, the Tit-for-Tat strategy, and the tournament environment. Then separate the places where the mapping is genuinely illuminating from the places where it breaks. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports features of the iterated game that real animal and human interactions often lack—for example, perfect recognition of partners, stable pairings, noise-free signals about defection, fixed payoffs, or the ability to select a strategy after explicit deliberation. Then tell me whether the conclusion the analogy was meant to support—that cooperation is robust and needs no top-down or normative machinery—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time. If there is a better analogy, name it and say what it buys us. At the very end, give a one-sentence summary as the last line.
