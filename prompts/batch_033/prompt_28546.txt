At a reading group on the foundations of AI, a colleague who had just finished a popular book on Gödel offered the following summary of what the incompleteness theorems are supposed to show. I wrote it down afterwards as carefully as I could:

"In 1931 Gödel proved that any formal system rich enough to express basic arithmetic is incomplete: if the system is consistent, there will be true statements in its own language that it cannot prove. Since a computer is, at bottom, just a formal system of this kind, no computer can fully capture human mathematical reasoning — when a machine is shown its own Gödel sentence, we can see that the sentence is true but the machine cannot prove it. This is why a perfectly rational AI is impossible: any AI must be either inconsistent or incomplete, so it will either prove falsehoods or be blind to truths that we can recognise. And since the theorem applies to any rule-governed system whatsoever, it also marks a principled limit on what science, as a rule-governed enterprise, can ever explain."

The opening sentence sounds roughly like the standard statement I remember, but I suspect the later conclusions are doing more work than the theorem can support, and I do not know enough to say precisely where the slide happens. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a logician would accept as a fair gloss, which parts conflate distinct claims, and which parts are overstatements that Gödel's theorems do not license. For each problematic step, give the corrected, more limited version of the claim, and say whether the intermediate conclusions about AI and the final conclusion about the limits of science survive the corrections.
