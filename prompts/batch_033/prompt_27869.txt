Write an essay on the following controversy in the theory of convention and the evolution of norms. David Lewis analyzes a convention as a behavioral regularity that solves a recurrent coordination problem: members of a population conform to it, expect one another to conform, and prefer to conform conditional on others conforming, with this entire structure being common knowledge; the regularity must also have had at least one viable alternative that would likewise have solved the coordination problem had everyone conformed to it instead. Critics argue that the common-knowledge condition over-intellectualizes convention, since many real conventions and social norms arise and persist among agents who never form the full hierarchy of higher-order expectations, and some emerge through trial-and-error, imitation, or cultural transmission rather than through a shared public state that generates common knowledge. Defenders reply that weakening or dropping the condition sacrifices the account's ability to explain the distinctive stability and normative force of conventions, and that public signals, precedent, and repeated interaction can approximate or realize common knowledge in ordinary cases. Weigh the main arguments and intermediate positions. In doing so, address at least the following: (a) whether the explanatory work is really done by common knowledge, by lower-order mutual expectations, or by high-probability common belief; (b) whether the emergence of conventions through non-deliberative processes is an objection to Lewis's analysis or only to a stronger claim about explicit agreement; and (c) what, if anything, is lost if the condition is weakened or replaced. Defend a specific verdict about how much of the Lewisian account survives and which condition, if any, should be revised. Do not end on a non-committal note.
