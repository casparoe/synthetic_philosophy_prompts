An advance directive allows a person to specify, while competent, what medical treatment they should receive if they later develop severe dementia and lose decision-making capacity. Many jurisdictions treat these directives as binding, even when the person with dementia appears content and actively resists the treatment the directive requires — for instance, refusing tube feeding despite a prior instruction to accept it. Reconstruct the theory of personal identity and autonomy that this practice presupposes. In particular, does it presuppose that the earlier self has authority over the later self because they are the same person, or can it be justified on a view that treats the later self as a different person with different interests? If the practice were instead built on a psychological-continuity theory that takes sufficiently severe dementia to sever the relevant continuity, what would have to change — and would binding advance directives still make sense?
