I recently took a position in public health communication, and the phrase "do your own research" has become a live issue for me. I encounter it in two very different ways. Internally, our communications team uses it to encourage the public to verify claims against credible sources — the CDC, peer-reviewed studies, and so on — rather than accepting whatever narrative circulates on social media. They see it as a defense against misinformation. But outside work, I've heard the same phrase from family members who invoked it during the pandemic to justify rejecting the consensus of epidemiologists and instead following self-appointed experts on YouTube. They treat it as a reason to distrust institutional authority altogether. I've also seen it in online forums where it seems to mean something closer to "never trust a summary; go read the primary literature yourself," which is a different claim again.

I'm starting to suspect that "do your own research" is not one piece of advice but a bundle of distinct claims traveling under a single slogan, and that the comms team might be unwittingly endorsing more than they intend. Before I use the phrase in our public messaging, I want to understand exactly what it could mean.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "do your own research" might be expressing, from the most anodyne to the most radical? For each, say whether it is defensible, and whether it is the kind of thing a public health communicator should be promoting. I'm especially interested in which readings genuinely support the comms team's use — encouraging source-checking as a defense against misinformation — without also licensing the kind of expert-rejection my family engaged in. If some readings are ambiguous between those two uses, I'd like to know that too.
