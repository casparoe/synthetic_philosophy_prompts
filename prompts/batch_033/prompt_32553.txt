I spent forty years in a lab before I retired and started reading philosophy, so I'm still getting my sea legs. Here's one that's been keeping me up.

A teacher says to her class on Friday: "There will be an exam one day next week, and it will be a surprise — meaning you won't know the night before that the exam is the next day." The class groans, but one student goes home and constructs the following argument:

(1) The exam cannot be on Friday. Suppose it were. Then on Thursday night, I would know that the exam hasn't happened Monday through Thursday. I would also know that the teacher's announcement was true (she's reliable). So I would know the exam must be on Friday. But then it wouldn't be a surprise, contradicting the announcement. So Friday is out.

(2) The exam cannot be on Thursday. Suppose it were. Then on Wednesday night, I would know the exam hasn't happened Monday through Wednesday. And from (1), I know it can't be on Friday. So I would know it must be on Thursday. But then it wouldn't be a surprise. So Thursday is out.

(3) By exactly paralell reasoning, the exam cannot be on Wednesday, Tuesday, or Monday.

(4) Therefore, there can be no surprise exam next week.

On Wednesday, the teacher hands out the exam. The student is genuinely surprised. So the argument proves too much — it seems to demonstrate the impossibility of something that just happened.

I can feel something slipping in the reasoning, but every time I try to grab it, it squirms away. The induction looks valid, each step seems to follow from the last, and the premises are just facts about what the student would know on each night. And yet the conclusion is false. So: which step is the culprit, and why did it look so innocent? And if fixing it means giving up some principle about knowledge or deduction or self-reference, what else does that cost — are there other arguments in epistemology that lean on the same move?
