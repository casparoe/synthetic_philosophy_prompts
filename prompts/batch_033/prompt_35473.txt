I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate ethics course, and I am grading a midterm. The unit just covered the distinction between an action's being right and an action's having moral worth, along with the Kantian claim that moral worth depends on acting from duty rather than merely in accordance with duty. The exam question was as follows:

"Explain the distinction between an action's being right and an action's having moral worth. Then critically assess the claim that an action has moral worth only insofar as it is performed from duty. Your answer should use at least two examples, including one in which an agent does the right thing from inclination rather than duty, and should say what the claim gets right as well as where, if anywhere, it goes wrong."

One student submitted this answer:

"The distinction between rightness and moral worth is a distinction between two objects of evaluation. Rightness is a property of the action considered in itself: an action is right when it conforms to the moral law, or more broadly when it does what there is decisive moral reason to do. Moral worth is a property of the agent's will, or of the action taken together with its motive: it concerns whether the agent deserves moral credit for what she did. That is why an action can be right without having moral worth, as when someone does the right thing by accident or from self-interest.

On the Kantian view, an action has moral worth only if it is performed from duty, not merely in accordance with duty. The shopkeeper who charges fair prices only because honesty is good for business acts in accordance with duty, but his determining ground is prudence rather than duty, so his action lacks moral worth. That seems right as far as it goes.

The criterion is too strong, however, because it implies that whenever an agent has any inclination to perform the action, the action cannot have moral worth. Consider a parent who throws herself in front of a car to save her child. She is moved by love, not by the thought that this is her duty; since inclination is present, Kant must say her action has no moral worth, which seems to miss something important. Even if we add that she also recognizes the action as her duty, the mere presence of the inclination would still, on Kant's view, prevent the action from being done from duty. So the criterion cannot distinguish the prudent shopkeeper from the loving parent in the right way.

Finally, in practice we can settle whether someone acted from duty by asking her: if she sincerely reports that she acted from duty, then she did, and if she sincerely reports an inclination, then she did not."

I have a provisional grade in mind, but I would like a second opinion before finalizing it. Please assign a letter grade (A through F) and provide the specific comments that justify it. I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: identify what the answer gets right, identify any errors, conflations, or misattributions and explain exactly why they are mistakes, and indicate how much each point should affect the final grade. If something is a subtle misreading rather than an outright error, say so and explain how a correct treatment of that point would go.
