Take the Challenger launch decision in January 1986 and analyze it with imprecise probabilities, not the standard risk-management story. I know the basic facts: it was an unusually cold morning, far below any temperature the solid rocket booster O-rings had ever flown at, the engineers had seen erosion on previous warmer flights but had no data that would let them put a sharp number on failure at low temperature, management kept asking for proof it would fail rather than treating the engineers uncertainty as itself a reason to wait, and the launch went ahead. What I want is the careful mapping: set the decision up with acts, states, and outcomes, and say why the engineers' evidence at that point is naturally modeled as a set of probability distributions over O-ring failure rather than a single one. Then walk through what the main imprecise-decision rules — maximin expected utility, E-admissibility, maximality — would have said about launching versus delaying, and be explicit about which ones would have blocked the launch and which ones would have let it through. Then tell me where the model breaks. Imprecise probabilities are a theory of rational choice under ambiguity, but the Challenger decision was also about the burden of proof, who had authority over the data, and organizational pressure to launch. Does the imprecise-probability lens actually explain the failure, or does it mostly re-describe it? And in the other direction: is Challenger an argument that safety-critical decisions should be made with imprecise probabilities and ambiguity-averse rules, or does the episode show the real problem was something no decision rule reaches?
