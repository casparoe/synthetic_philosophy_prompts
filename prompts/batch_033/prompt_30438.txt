I've been reading around in the misinformation literature lately, and I keep coming back to the echo chamber / epistemic bubble distinction — the idea that an epistemic bubble is when someone just isn't exposed to the other side (their feed never shows it to them), while an echo chamber is more active: a community that preemptively discredits outside voices, so contrary evidence arrives already tagged as a lie or a psyop. I've gotten fairly convinced that this is a real difference in kind, not just degree, and that it matters practically: you can pop a bubble by diversifying someone's sources, but doing that to someone inside an echo chamber just gives the discrediting mechanism more material to work with. But I'm aware I might be overfond of a clean taxonomy.

What would actually count against this veiw? I want the counter-evidence and counter-arguments sorted by how decisive they'd be, from 'this kills the distinction' down to 'this is awkward but survivable.' For instance: are there cases where something that looked like a textbook echo chamber came apart just through repeated exposure to outside information — and would that falsify the view, or just show the group wasn't really in an echo chamber? Could the whole distinction be flattened into a single dimension, like how much trustworthy outside information reaches you, with 'distrust' being just one more way information fails to land? And does the view insulate itself by treating every failed debunking as confirmation that the distrust mechanism is working? I'd also like to know which of these defeaters a defender of the distinction would honestly concede as counting against them, and whether — if the view turns out to be unfalsifiable in practice — that's a problem for a conceptual distinction of this sort, or just the price of doing social epistemology.
