I'm working through a beginner's guide to metaphysics, and the chapter on possible worlds gives this summary of David Lewis's modal realism:

"Lewis's modal realism says that every way the world could have been corresponds to a world that is just as real and concrete as ours. These worlds are not abstract fictions or linguistic constructions; they are out there, scattered through space but so far away that we could never travel to them. So when I say there could have been blue swans, I'm saying that in some other world, blue swans exist. What makes our world special is just that it's the one we're in: 'actual' is an indexical word like 'here' or 'now', and each world is actual to its own inhabitants. Because no person exists in more than one world, Lewis rejects identity across worlds and instead says that when we say 'you could have been a plumber', what we mean is that you have a counterpart — someone in another world who resembles you in the relevant respects — who is a plumber. The standard objection is that the view is extravagant: it multiplies worlds beyond necessity, in violation of Ockham's razor. Lewis's reply is that the payoffs in logic, semantics, and metaphysics justify the cost. Critics also complain that these other worlds are unobservable, so the theory can't be tested; Lewis answers that we know about them by reason alone, the way we know about numbers, and that this is no more unscientific than mathematics."

The book then wraps up by saying modal realism "fails because it is unfalsifiable, and a theory that cannot be tested should be rejected whatever its theoretical payoffs."
