So my friend and I got into this huge argument last night about the Ellsberg paradox — you know the one, with the urn that has 30 red balls and 60 that are either black or yellow but you don't know the ratio? I've always thought ambiguity aversion is totally reasonable. Like, of course you'd rather bet on a known probability than an unknown one, that just seems like common sense. But my friend, who's been reading a lot of decision theory lately, walked me through this argument that seems to show that if you take ambiguity aversion seriously, you basically can't make any decisions at all in the real world. And I couldn't find the flaw, but the conclusion is obviously insane.

Here's the argument, roughly as he laid it out:

(1) If ambiguity aversion is a rational preference, then whenever you face a choice between an option with a known probability of an outcome and an option with an unknown or imprecisely known probability of that same outcome, you should prefer the known-probability option, all else equal.

(2) In the real world, virtually no probabilities are truly known. The probability that your car will start, that a medication will work, that a bridge won't collapse — these are all estimated from finite data, with model uncertainty, unmeasured confounders, the works. They're ambiguous in exactly the sense that matters: you don't know the objective chance, you only have some rough evidence and a model you hope isn't too wrong.

(3) But there are some probabilities we can know with precision: the probability of drawing a red card from a shuffled deck, the probability of a fair coin landing heads, etc. Call these "laboratory" probabilities.

(4) So by (1), a rational ambiguity-averse agent should prefer to bet their life on drawing a red card from a shuffled deck (known 50% chance of death) than to drive to work (ambiguous probability of a fatal crash — say roughly 1 in 10,000 but with huge uncertainty around that estimate, since it depends on weather, traffic, whether the guy in the next lane is texting, etc.). The known probability is orders of magnitude worse, but it's known, and the real-world one is ambiguous.

(5) But that's absurd. No one should prefer a 50% chance of death to a ~0.01% chance of death, even if the latter is "ambiguous."

(6) So ambiguity aversion isn't a rational preference — it's a bias, a heuristic that works in the lab but falls apart when you try to live by it.

I tried to push back by saying that ambiguity aversion is only about cases where you have literally no information, like the Ellsberg urn where the number of black balls could be anthing from 0 to 60. But he pointed out that in real life you never have literally no information either — you always have some prior, some experience, some base rate. So if ambiguity aversion only applies when you have zero information, it applies nowhere, and the Ellsberg paradox is just a parlor trick irrelevant to actual decision-making. And if it applies when you have imprecise information, then we're back to the bridge-crossing problem.

I feel like the argument has to break somewhere, maybe around premise (1) — like, ambiguity aversion isn't about preferring known probabilities simpliciter, it's about an aversion to the ambiguity itself that can be traded off against other considerations. But then I'm not sure how to formalize that without losing the force of the original paradox. Where does this argument go wrong, and what's the cost of the fix?
