Two of my colleagues in the department got into it at lunch last week, and I got drafted as referee. It started when one of them mentioned that her nephew has been sending fifty dollars a month to a group called the Deep Future Relief Fund. The group claims that for a small monthly donation it can make a tiny dent in the probability that a future civilization will be stopped from simulating an enormous number of suffering minds — the number they give has about thirty zeros in it. Dan said the nephew is being perfectly rational. The chance the group can do what it says is astronomically small, but the claimed stakes are astronomically large, and a tiny probability times a huge number can easily come out to more than fifty dollars a month. If you refuse, he said, you're just not maximizing expected value. Priya said that's a mugging: you can't multiply a probability you pulled out of nowhere by a number the claimant made up and call the result a reason to hand over money. Dan said every charity works that way — you never know the exact probability that a bed net saves a life, you estimate it from messy data and multiply, so her objection would rule out all charitable giving. Priya said no: with a bed net there's a causal mechanism and a track record, and the probabilities are anchored by base rates; here there's no base rate, and anyway the stunt is general — the group can always add three more zeros to the number of minds until the expected value clears any bar, so the rule never tells you when to stop. Dan said the same point applies to insurance and asteroid defense: tiny probabilities of huge harms, and we still pay for those. Priya said the difference is that those harms have a known mechanism and the probability can be estimated from data, so the expected value isn't just stipulated. Dan said sure, that's a difference in how big the error bars are, not in the principle, and the principle was all he was defending. Priya said no — once the error bars are that big, the calculation stops being a reason at all, not just an imprecise one. They asked me to rule, point by point, and I honestly couldnt. So I'm passing it to you.
