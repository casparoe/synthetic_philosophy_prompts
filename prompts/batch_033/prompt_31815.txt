Right, I keep going round in circles on consequentialist cluelessness, and before I read another response I want the full spec of what any adequate response has to do. You're a professional philosopher working on normative ethics and decision theory; take your time and think through the candidate criteria before you commit — maybe brainstorm first.

The thing I want the desiderata for is this: a theory of how a consequentialist should choose, given that the long-run consequences of nearly any action ramify in ways nobody can actually track. By adequate I mean it should either tell an agent what to do, or give a principled explanation of why nothing can, and it should do that without quietly ceasing to be consequentialist. I dont just want a survey of positions; I want the requirements list first, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

In particular I want to know:

- which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference (e.g., determinate guidance for every choice, or the demand that the theory make no empirical assumptions about how the world works);

- whether the distinction between simple and complex cluelessness — roughly, cases where you have some probability distribution over outcomes versus cases where you cant even form one — forces different requirements, and whether a single theory can meet both;

- how a theory can recover the cases where we're still confident despite cluelessness — e.g., that giving to a well-evidenced charity beats burning the money — without quietly helping itself to the empirical claim it's supposed to earn;

- whether the split between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure helps, or whether saying the right move is to adopt whatever rules have the best expected consequences is just dodging the question;

- and most of all, whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that there's a trilemma here: you can't have (i) determinate action-guidance, (ii) a purely outcome-based criterion, and (iii) no unargued empirical claim that long-run effects wash out, all at once. I'd like that made precise, or shown to be false.

Then, briefly, score the usual responses against the list — naive maximising consequentialism, the washing-out / expected-value line, indirect or sophisticated consequentialism, and satisficing or robust-choice views. But keep the weight on the criteria and their motivation, not the scorecard. No bibliography, please — I want the reasoning, not citations.
