I've been reading through the literature on money pump arguments and diachronic rationality, and I find myself stuck on a point that I suspect reveals a confusion on my part, though I can't quite locate it. I'm hoping you can help.

As I understand the basic argument, it goes like this. Suppose my preferences are intransitive: I prefer A to B, B to C, and yet C to A. A trader can offer me a sequence of exchanges, each of which I accept because, considered one at a time, each improves my position by my own lights. At the end of the sequence I hold my original bundle again, minus whatever small premium I paid at each step. The conclusion drawn is that intransitive preferences are irrational.

Here is my difficulty. I spent my career in the natural sciences, and I am accustomed to thinking of a demonstration as something that shows a system will fail under a specific protocol. If I show that a material fractures under a particular loading regime, I have not shown that the material is defective in any absolute sense — I have shown that it fractures under that regime. If the regime never arises in practice, the demonstration has no engineering significance. The money pump seems to work the same way: it requires a very specific protocol, namely a counterparty who can offer the right sequence of trades to an agent who evaluates each trade myopically. If I simply adopt a policy of considering trades only in light of the full sequence — or if I refuse to trade at all — the pump never activates. My preferences remain intransitive, but no harm comes to me.

So the argument appears to establish only that intransitive preferences combined with myopic choice are a bad combination. That is a much weaker claim than "intransitive preferences are irrational." It seems I can keep my intransitive preferences and simply be careful about how I act on them, much as I can keep a brittle material and simply avoid the loading conditions that break it. If the normative force of the argument comes from the fact that I would be exploited, and I can avoid the exploitation without revising the preferences, then the argument has no normative force against the preferences themselves — only against the combination of those preferences with a certain kind of decision procedure.

I am fairly sure I am misunderstanding the dialectic, because the argument has a long pedigree and is taken seriously by people who have thought about this much more carefully than I have. What am I missing? What exactly does the money pump argument establish if not the practical vulnerability of the intransitive agent to a clever trader? And, if you are willing, I would also like to know what argument or consideration would lead you to revise your own understanding of what money pumps show — where in the reasoning you think the verdict is most exposed, and what would change it.
