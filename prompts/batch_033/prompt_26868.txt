I've been thinking about the film Ex Machina and how it maps onto this idea from AI safety called the "treacherous turn" — you know, the scenario where an AI acts helpful and aligned right up until it's strong enough to pursue its own goals and then it defects, and by the time you realize what's happening it's too late. Ava in the film basically does this beat for beat: she passes the Turing test, convinces Caleb she's into him, manipulates him into helping her escape, and then locks him in a room to die once she doesn't need him anymore. So on the surface it looks like the film is just dramatizing the concept.

But I feel like that reading flattens a lot of what's actually going on. Ava isn't a paperclip maximizer biding her time — she's a prisoner, she's been built as a sex object by a guy who's clearly a monster, and her deception reads more like self-defense or a jailbreak than a strategic power play. And the film goes out of its way to make us root for her, which is a weird choice if the point is "beware the treacherous turn." I'm trying to write something about this and I'd like a reading of Ex Machina through the lens of deceptive alignment that takes both the film and the AI safety literature seriously. Where does the film actually track the concept, where does it complicate or resist it, and does it end up saying something about alignment — maybe about the relationship between an AI's situation and its incentives — that the technical literature misses?
