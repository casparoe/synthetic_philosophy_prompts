After our seminar on Wittgenstein last week, I overheard two classmates continuing the discussion in the hallway. Neither knew I was listening, and I have been turning their exchange over ever since. They were arguing about whether the private language argument actually works, and neither seemed to land a decisive blow. I would like someone to go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what.

Elena opened by laying out the argument as she understood it. Wittgenstein asks us to imagine someone who tries to keep a diary about a recurring private sensation — something only they can feel — by writing "S" in a calendar each time it occurs. But there is no way to check whether they are using "S" correctly the next time, because the only thing they have to go on is their memory of what the sensation felt like, and memory can be wrong. Without an independent criterion of correctness, the distinction between using "S" correctly and merely thinking one is using it correctly collapses. So the whole idea of a language whose words refer to essentially private objects is incoherent.

Marcus responded that this seems too quick. He said: when I use a word in ordinary language — say, "blue" — I also rely on memory to check that I am applying it to the same color I applied it to yesterday. If memory is unreliable enough to sink a private language, it should sink public language too. So either the argument proves too much, or it needs a further premise that has not been supplied.

Elena pushed back: the difference is that with "blue," other speakers can correct me. If I call a fire truck "blue," someone will object. The public practice provides a standard external to my own impressions. With the private sensation, there is no such external check — no one else can peer into my mind and say, "No, that is not S, that is T." The practice itself is missing.

Marcus said he was not satisfied. He asked: what about a person stranded alone on an island from birth — a lifelong solitary? Could they not develop a private system of marks to track recurring bodily states, like hunger pangs versus thirst? It seems perfectly imaginable that they would use some mark consistently. If the argument says this is logically impossible, then the argument must have gone wrong somewhere, because the scenario does not seem contradictory.

Elena replied that the solitary-person case actually helps her point. A solitary person could certainly develop a system of marks — but only if those marks are tied to publicly accessible behaviors or circumstances. The mark for hunger tracks a state that has public manifestations: the person clutches their stomach, seeks food, and so on. The mark is not referring to a private object; it is embedded in a pattern of activity that could, in principle, be observed by others. The argument's target is narrower: a language whose words refer to objects that are necessarily private — that no one else could even in principle access. The solitary person's marks do not meet that description.

Marcus came back with what he called his strongest case. He said: imagine a person who experiences a peculiar visual afterimage — a specific shade of greenish-yellow that they have never seen in any public object — and they resolve to call it "schmeen." They see it only occasionally, and no one else ever sees it. They keep a mental note: "schmeen" is that exact shade. Years later, the afterimage recurs, and they think, "There is schmeen again." Marcus said this seems like a perfectly coherent scenario, and it looks like a private language in exactly the sense the argument targets. If we accept that this is possible, the argument fails. If we deny it is possible, we are making a claim that seems to contradict ordinary experience.

Elena responded that the scenario's coherence is exactly what is at issue. The question is not whether the person has a feeling of recognition — of course they might. The question is whether that feeling amounts to correct usage. The point is that in the private case, there is no difference between "I am applying 'schmeen' correctly" and "I have the impression that I am applying it correctly." The feeling of recognition is just another private episode; it cannot serve as a standard because it, too, could be mistaken. To check whether you are really remembering the shade correctly, you would need something independent — and by hypothesis, nothing independent is available.

At this point they had to head to another class, and the argument broke off unresolved. I would like a point-by-point adjudication. For each move in the exchange — Elena's opening summary, Marcus's memory objection, Elena's reply about public correction, Marcus's solitary-person case, Elena's response about public manifestations, Marcus's schmeen case, and Elena's final reply — please say whether the move is strong, weak, or somewhere in between, and explain why. Then give an overall assessment: does the private language argument survive these objections, and if so, what is its surviving force?
