I'm an engineering manager at a mid-size company, three years into the job after a decade as an IC. We run blameless postmortems, but we also have quarterly calibration where we're supposed to hold people accountable, and I keep flip-flopping: I either let real carelessness slide because I don't want to be the blame guy, or I come down hard on someone who was basically set up to fail, and either way the team notices and trust erodes.

A friend who studied philosophy summarized an old essay by P.F. Strawson for me, and it's the first thing that made this feel tractable. The gist as I understand it: holding someone responsible isn't about discovering some fact about whether their will was free. It's about being in a certain stance toward them — feeling resentment or indignation when they screw up, expecting good will, wanting an explanation or an apology. And we suspend those attitudes in two very different ways. An excuse says this particular act didn't express ill will (they didn't know, it was an accident). An exemption says this person isn't the kind of participant we can have those attitudes toward at all — like a small child or someone in a genuinely altered state. And then there's a third thing, the objective stance, where you step out of the participant relationship entirely and treat the person as something to be managed, fixed, or routed around rather than reasoned with. My friend said Strawson thinks taking the objective stance toward someone is always in some way a big deal, even when it's justified.

I want this turned into a proceedure I can actually run. Please write a decision procedure for after an incident or a pattern of behavior: given a person who caused or contributed to a failure, what questions do I ask, in what order, to decide whether my reactive attitudes (resentment, indignation, demanding an explanation) are appropriate; whether I should excuse the person; whether I should exempt them; or whether I should shift to the objective stance. For each question, say what a sufficient yes looks like, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to me as a judgment call rather than a mechanical step. It has to be usable in under an hour before a postmortem or calibration meeting by someone with no philosophy background, and it should respect that my company has HR rules I can't ignore. Then run it on three cases: (1) a senior engineer who shipped a change with no tests because they were rushing to a demo, and it took down production; (2) a junior engineer who caused a data-loss bug on a task they had told their lead they didn't understand; (3) a normally solid engineer whose work has collapsed since a bereavement and who keeps missing handoffs. One of these should come out in a way I won't like, and I want you to say plainly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, whether it can ever tell me if taking the objective stance is a cop-out instead of the right call.
