I'm putting together a position paper on moral uncertainty for a graduate seminar, and I want to run my commitments past someone who can spot where they pull against each other before I embarrass myself in class. Here's where I've landed after the first few weeks of reading:

(1) The right way to make decisions under moral uncertainty is to maximize expected choiceworthiness (MEC): for each action, weigh the choiceworthiness that action would have according to each moral theory you have credence in by that credence, sum across theories, and pick the action with the highest total.

(2) Intertheoretic comparisons of choiceworthiness — the step where you need to say that theory A's verdict "this action is wrong" counts for more or less than theory B's verdict "this action is suboptimal" — are possible only when the theories share enough structural common ground. In practice, this means you can compare two consequentialist theories (they both use a value function you can normalize) but you can't compare a consequentialist theory with a deontological one in any non-arbitrary way.

(3) When intertheoretic comparisons break down, the right fallback is moral caution: if any theory you have non-negligible credence in says an action is seriously wrong, don't do it — even if the expected-choiceworthiness math (were it computable) might have come out in favor of doing it.

(4) Moral uncertainty is structurally the same problem as empirical uncertainty. In both cases, you have a probability distribution over possible states and a value assignment to outcomes or actions, and the rational norm is to maximize expected value. The machinery is identical; the only difference is what the uncertainty is about.

(5) The fanaticism worry — that a tiny credence in a bizarre moral theory will dominate all your decisions — isn't as serious as it looks, because fanatical results only arise if you grant extreme views extreme choiceworthiness magnitudes, and those magnitudes are exactly the sort of thing intertheoretic comparisons can't establish non-arbitrarily. The fanaticism problem is really just the intertheoretic comparison problem in disguise.
