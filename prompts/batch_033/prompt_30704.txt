For a working group on aggregation in ethics, I have been asked to write down my considered position on the torture-vs-dust-specks problem so that the group can pressure-test it. Before I present, I would like an audit of whether the commitments I am about to list actually cohere. Here is the package:

(1) Verdict on the case. In the forced choice between one person being tortured for fifty years and an astronomically large but finite number of people — say, 3↑↑↑3 — each receiving a single dust speck in the eye, the dust-specks outcome is clearly the better one; I would spare the torture without hesitation.

(2) Lexical priority of severe harm. No finite number of dust specks can outweigh one fifty-year torture. The torture is not merely worse by degree; it is worse in kind, and it remains worse than any number of specks.

(3) Small harms have positive weight. A single dust speck is a genuine harm to the person who receives it, not a harm of zero size.

(4) Transitivity. The relation "is better than" is transitive.

(5) No sharp threshold. I doubt there is a principled, non-arbitrary point along the spectrum from dust speck to torture at which harms below the line can never outweigh harms above it. Adjacent points on that spectrum differ only marginally, and I do not think moral badness jumps discontinuously.

(6) Within-life but not across-life aggregation. I am comfortable saying that many small harms can add up within a single person's life — one person receiving a million dust specks is worse than one person receiving one — but I am uneasy about summing harms across different people as though they belonged to a single subject.

I genuinely cannot tell whether this set hangs together, which pairs are merely apparently in tension, and which commitments would have to be revised if it does not. Please audit the set: identify the real tensions, note any apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection, rank the tensions by severity, and say what the cheapest revision would be in each case.
