The dispute between classical and constructive mathematicians has now run for over a century, and, as far as I can tell, each side can state the other's position accurately and neither moves. The classical mathematician is content to prove that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that a^b is rational by splitting into cases: if sqrt(2)^sqrt(2) is rational, take a = b = sqrt(2); if it is irrational, take a = sqrt(2)^sqrt(2) and b = sqrt(2), so that a^b = 2. The proof never determines which case obtains. The constructivist replies that this is not an existence proof at all, because it supplies no witness: it shows only that the supposition that no such pair exists is contradictory. Constructivists accordingly require the disjunction property — a proof of "P or Q" must tell you which disjunct holds — and the existence property — a proof of "there exists an x such that P(x)" must deliver an x. The classical mathematician responds that these strictures make ordinary analysis unavailable: the intermediate value theorem does not hold constructively for arbitrary continuous functions, and the constructivist cannot even assert that every real number is either zero or not zero, since no procedure decides equality to zero for an arbitrary real. The constructivist, in turn, insists that classical mathematicians conflate two different rules: proving a negation by deriving a contradiction from the claim being negated (which constructivists accept) and proving a positive claim by deriving a contradiction from its negation (which they reject).

My working hypothesis is that this is at bottom a verbal disagreement about the word "exists": classically, "there exists an x" means something like "it would be contradictory for there to be no such x," while constructively it means "we can exhibit an x," and the standard double-negation translation shows that classical proofs can be read as constructive proofs of a weaker, negative claim. But I doubt my own hypothesis, because the constructivist also rejects assertions that are not obviously about existence in that narrow sense, and the classical mathematician does not merely translate constructive theorems but denies that constructive standards are the right standards for mathematics at all. Diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive (about the subject matter of mathematics, or about what a proof is for), a difference in epistemic standards, or several of these at once? Engage with what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change in practice — or whether we should stop expecting convergence.
