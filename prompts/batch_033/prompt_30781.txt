I've been teaching a grad seminar on dispositions this semester and one of my students built an argument that's been keeping me up. I can't tell if it's brilliant or if there's a subtle mistake I'm missing. Here's the argument, roughly as he presented it:

(1) The whole point of analyzing dispositions conditionally is to explain what it is for a glass to be fragile, or salt to be soluble, without helping yourself to unreduced modal notions. The standard move: x is disposed to M when stimulated by S iff, were x to undergo S, x would M. (2) Finks and masks are supposed to show that this simple conditional is false. A fink is something that removes the disposition when the stimulus occurs — a sorcerer casts a spell on the glass so that it loses its fragility the instant before the hammer hits. The conditional "if struck, it would break" comes out false, but the glass was fragile. (3) The natural fix is to add a ceteris paribus clause: x is disposed to M when S iff, were x to undergo S and nothing interfered, x would M. (4) But here's the move I can't evaluate: my student claims that "interference" in this clause can only be spelled out as "something that prevents the disposition from manifesting." If that's right, the analysis is circular — we're analyzing disposition M in terms of the absence of interference with M. (5) He then argues that any attempt to characterize interference in purely categorical, non-dispositional terms will fail, because what counts as interference is always relative to the disposition we're attributing. A spell that turns glass to steel interferes with fragility but not with, say, electrical conductivity. (6) So dispositions can't be analyzed in non-dispositional terms. (7) But if they can't be analyzed, they must be primitive modal properties — pure powers, not grounded in anything categorical. (8) And if every disposition attribution posits a distinct primitive power, we get a massive explosion of fundamental properties: fragility, solubility, malleability, irascibility, and so on. (9) That's metaphysically unacceptable. (10) So we should be eliminativists: there are no dispositions, just useful fictions.

I'm pretty sure the conclusion is false, but I cant figure out where the argument goes wrong. The step from (4) to (5) feels like it's doing too much work, but I'm not sure I can articulate why. Alternatively, maybe the problem is earlier — maybe the conditional analysis was never supposed to be a reductive analysis in the first place, and the whole thing is attacking a straw man. But then what IS the point of the conditional analysis? I'd like to know which premise you'd reject and what the theoretical cost is. If you think the argument is valid and we just have to bite the bullet on one of the costs (primitive powers or eliminativism), say which one and defend it.
