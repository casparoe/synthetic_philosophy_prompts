For background: cognitive penetration is when your beliefs, expectations, or background knowledge literally alter your perceptual experience, not just the judgments you make on the basis of that experience. If you just *interpret* what you see differently, that's not penetration — the experience itself has to change. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. **The grey banana.** You're in a room lit with deep red light, and someone holds up a banana that's been spray-painted neutral grey. But you still see it as yellowish — your brain "corrects" the color based on your knowledge that bananas are yellow. This feels like genuine penetration to me. The color experience itself is shifted by what you know.

2. **The Müller-Lyer illusion.** The classic one with the arrowheads. The two lines look like different lengths even though you know perfectly well they're identical. Your knowledge doesn't touch the experience. Clearly not penetrated — the belief is just walled off from the relevant perceptual module.

3. **The heavy backpack and the hill.** People wearing heavy backpacks estimate a hill as steeper than people without them. This one's weird because it's not a *belief* doing the penetrating — it's your body's energetic state. But it still feels like a top-down influence on perception, not just a judgment shift. I want to call it a kind of penetration, maybe not the central case.

4. **The duck-rabbit.** You know the drawing is both a duck and a rabbit, but you can't see them simultaneously. You can flip intentionally with some effort, and when you do, the lines seem to reorganize themselves. I'm genuinely torn here — the knowledge helps you flip, but the experience also seems to have a stubbornness that resists full cognitive control. No verdict.

5. **The radiologist.** A novice and an expert look at the same chest x-ray. The expert literally sees the tumor; the novice sees grey blobs. My strong intuition is that the expert's perceptual experience is richer and different in kind — their knowledge has restructured what they see. Penetration.

6. **The sommellier.** A wine expert and I sip the same Bordeaux. She tastes blackberry, leather, maybe a hint of tobacco; I taste... red wine. I think her actual gustatory experience is more articulated than mine. Her knowledge enriches the perception, doesn't just add a commentary on top. Penetration.

7. **The gun vs. wallet.** An officer in a high-stress encounter sees a Black man pull a wallet from his pocket and perceives a gun. My intuition — and I feel strongly about this — is that this is NOT genuinely perceptual. It's a catastrophic misjudgment driven by bias, but the visual experience itself probably wasn't of a gun. Calling it penetration feels like it lets the officer off the hook (oh, my perception was just penetrated by my beliefs, not my fault). I think it's a belief-driven inferrence error, not a perceptual one.

So my verdicts: 1, 5, 6 are penetration; 2 and 7 are not; 3 is borderline; 4 I can't decide. And here's the crux of my argument with Sam: case 1 and case 2 seem to flatly contradict each other. Why would knowledge about banana color penetrate color perception, but knowledge about equal line lengths fail to penetrate length perception?
