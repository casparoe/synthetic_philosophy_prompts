Draft essay

The Suffering of Fields? A Caution About Reading Ethics Off Panpsychism

Panpsychism is often presented as a solution to the hard problem of consciousness: phenomenal properties are fundamental and ubiquitous. One of its attractions is that it promises to put mind back into nature without making it an unexplained emergent addition. But panpsychism is increasingly followed by an ethical suggestion: if the fundamental furniture of the physical world is experiential, then the domain of moral concern extends to the basic constituents of reality, and physics itself becomes an ethically loaded science. The slide from panpsychism to what might be called pan-sentience—the claim that feeling, and in particular suffering, is everywhere—is the target of this essay.

I argue that the inference from panpsychism to a world pervaded by suffering is too quick. It rests on a conflation of three things that need not travel together: phenomenal character, affective valence, and a subject in the morally relevant sense.

First, the version of panpsychism that has received the most serious recent defense, constitutive micropsychism, holds that fundamental physical entities have simple phenomenal properties and that these combine to constitute the unified consciousness of a human or animal mind. But the properties the view needs are properties of there being something it is like. Nothing in the view requires those properties to be pleasant or unpleasant. It is one thing for an electron-like subject to have a faint, minimal what-it's-likeness; it is another for that experience to feel bad. The ethical reading of panpsychism needs the second claim, and the second claim is not supplied by the metaphysics.

Second, even if micro-experiences do carry valence, it is unclear how micro-valences relate to the suffering that matters morally. The combination problem—the difficulty of explaining how many micro-subjects yield one macro-subject—has an ethical twin. Do millions of faintly negative micro-valences sum to a great deal of suffering? Do they cancel? Are they ethically subsumed into the unified state of a larger subject, so that they should not be counted separately? A panpsychist who wants to derive an ethics from the metaphysics owes an answer here, and the standard defenses of panpsychism do not provide one.

Third, the notion of suffering that carries moral weight may require more than a negatively valenced state at an instant. It plausibly requires a perspective with temporal depth: a subject that persists, remembers, anticipates, and can have a global hedonic condition. A momentary micro-experience, even a negatively valenced one, may be more like a bare twinge than like suffering in the sense that grounds obligations. If so, the fact that fundamental physics describes countless micro-experiences would not by itself show that the universe contains countless sufferers.

The conclusion is deliberately limited. I do not claim that panpsychism has no ethical implications. If some version of the view is true, it may force us to say that value is rooted in the intrinsic nature of the physical and that the boundary between ethics and physics is less sharp than we thought. But the specific claim that panpsychism reveals a world saturated with suffering depends on additional theses about valence, combination, and the unity of the subject—theses that are not part of the package.

Questions for comments

1. Is the three-way distinction between phenomenal character, affective valence, and a subject in the morally relevant sense doing real work, or does it merely restate an obvious point?
2. Does the essay engage the strongest version of the panpsychist ethical claim, or does it risk attacking a strawman? If the latter, what is the strongest version I have missed?
3. Which paragraph is weakest, and how would a sympathetic panpsychist most effectively attack it?
4. Does the conclusion understate the revisions the argument actually needs? Be specific about moves I could make to strengthen the essay rather than offering general advice.
