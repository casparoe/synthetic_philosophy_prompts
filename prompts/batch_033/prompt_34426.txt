Question:

In his 1889 book *Calcul des probabilités*, Joseph Bertrand posed a problem that has since become known as Bertrand's paradox. Consider a circle with an inscribed equilateral triangle. What is the probability that a chord chosen "at random" from the circle is longer than a side of the triangle? Bertrand showed that three apparently natural ways of interpreting "at random" yield three different answers: 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4.

Explain the three methods Bertrand considered and the probabilities they generate. Then discuss the philosophical significance of the paradox for the principle of indifference. What exactly does the paradox show about the principle? Does it refute it, or does it point to something more nuanced? Be precise about the logical relationship between the paradox and the principle.

Student Answer:

Bertrand's paradox is a classic problem in the philosophy of probability that exposes a deep difficulty with the principle of indifference. The principle of indifference states that when we have no evidence favoring one outcome over another, we should assign equal probabilities to all possible outcomes. Bertrand's paradox shows that this seemingly innocent principle leads to inconsistency.

The setup is straightforward. Take a circle and inscribe an equilateral triangle. A chord is longer than a side of the triangle if and only if its midpoint lies within a smaller concentric circle of half the radius. Now, we need to select a chord "at random." Bertrand proposed three methods, each of which seems to be a reasonable application of the principle of indifference.

First, the random endpoints method: choose two points uniformly on the circumference and draw the chord between them. The probability that the chord is longer than the triangle's side is 1/3. Second, the random radius method: choose a radius uniformly and then choose a point uniformly along that radius; draw the chord perpendicular to the radius at that point. The probability is 1/2. Third, the random midpoint method: choose a point uniformly inside the circle and make it the midpoint of the chord. The probability is 1/4.

All three methods apply the principle of indifference to different aspects of the situation — endpoints, radii, and midpoints, respectively — and the principle gives no guidance about which application is correct. Therefore, the paradox refutes the principle of indifference by showing that it leads to contradictory probability assignments.

The deeper lesson is that probability is not "out there" in the world waiting to be discovered. The paradox shows that probability assignments are ultimately subjective: they depend on how we choose to describe the problem. This is a major argument in favor of the Bayesian or subjectivist interpretation of probability. The principle of indifference tries to generate objective probabilities from ignorance, but Bertrand's paradox demonstrates that ignorance alone cannot determine a unique probability distribution.

Some have tried to salvage the principle. Edwin Jaynes argued that if we impose invariance requirements — specifically, rotational and translational invariance — the problem has a unique solution, namely the 1/2 answer from the random radius method. Jaynes's solution resolves the paradox for this particular case. However, his approach does not fully rescue the principle of indifference in general, because the problem of choosing a parameterization recurs whenever we try to apply the principle to a new domain.

In summary, Bertrand's paradox refutes the principle of indifference as a general rule for assigning probabilities, and it supports the subjectivist view that probability always depends on a choice of description or reference class.
