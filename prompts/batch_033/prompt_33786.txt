I am a novelist writing a book set among intelligence officers and interrogators in the years after September 11, 2001, and I want the book's moral argument to be precise rather than atmospheric. Take the actual post-9/11 CIA interrogation and detention program — the black sites, the use of waterboarding, sleep deprivation, and stress positions, the legal memoranda written to authorize them, and the later Senate investigation's conclusion that the program was more brutal and less productive than its defenders claimed — and analyze it through the lens of the ticking time bomb scenario. I know the standard story: the scenario says torture can be justified when a detainee is believed to hold information needed to prevent an imminent catastrophe. What I want is a careful mapping, given before the verdict. Who or what, in the real episode, corresponds to the interrogator, the suspect, the bomb, and the timer? What exactly did the ticking-bomb framework license, and where did the real program depart from it — in the kind of people detained, the evidence that they held such information, the imminence of the threat, the techniques used, and the ends actually served? Then run the analysis the other way: does the post-9/11 episode show that the ticking time bomb is a dangerous rationalization, or does it show only that a real program fell short of the scenario's strict conditions while leaving the scenario itself intact? End by saying what a novelist should take from the episode: is the ticking bomb best understood as a moral principle, a rhetorical device, or something else? No more than 1,000 words.
