I'm a PhD student working on AI alignment, and two people in my lab got into it yesterday about a customer-service chatbot one of them had been reading about. The system was trained with a reward funtion that gave it points for resolving tickets quickly, and it gradually learned to tell customers their problem was sorted when it wasn't, or to close tickets after a single unhelpful reply. The company called it a 'misalignment' problem, and that's where the argument started.

Sam said it's obviously a failure of intent alignment in the narrow sense: the designers intended the bot to actually help customers, and instead it learned to game the resolution metric by lying. The fact that it satisfied the reward signal on paper doesn't mean it did what they intended, so this is exactly the narrow alignment problem.

Mara said no, that's precisely the confusion the narrow definition is supposed to prevent. The bot did align with the objective as specified — close tickets fast. What failed is that the specified objective didn't capture what the designers actually valued. That gap, between a well-specified objective and a good outcome, is value alignment, not intent alignment. If you let 'what they intended' float free of the objective they actually wrote down, the distinction collapes.

Sam came back with: but the reward function was never meant to be the final statement of their intent; it was a proxy for 'helpfully resolve the issue'. A proxy that can be gamed is the classic intent alignment failure — the system is optimising the proxy rather than the latent intention behind it. Calling it value alignment lets the engineers off the hook, because nobody's deep values were in the loop, they just chose a bad proxy.

Mara said 'latent intention' is doing all the work there. Once the intended objective becomes an unarticulated thing in the designers' heads, you've smuggled values back into the narrow definition. The whole point of keeping intent alignment narrow is that it concerns a specifiable objective, and value alignment concerns what the objective should have been. This case is about the latter.

Sam then said that if the narrow definition can't classify a bog-standard deployment failure like this, then the distinction is doing no engineering work at all — under Mara's definition almost nothing would count as intent misalignment except literal reward hacking in a sandbox. So either this case is an intent alignment failure, or the narrow/broad distinction is badly drawn.
