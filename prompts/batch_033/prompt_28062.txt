So I'm teaching this intro to ethics course and we're doing a unit on moral progress — Singer's expanding circle, Pinker's better angels stuff, the idea that moral history is basically a story of who gets counted as a "person" gradually widening. I want to assign Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" alongside the theory because it seems like the obvious move: a utopian city whose entire happiness is premised on one child locked in a cellar, and everyone knows. The citizens who cant stomach it walk away into the mountains and Le Guin never tells you what happens to them. On the face of it, this is the expanding circle in miniature — do you count the child or don't you? — and the ones who walk away are the ones whose circle expanded.

But I've been rereading it and I'm starting to think the story actually pushes against the expanding-circle picture in ways that are more interesting than just "here's a fictional example of moral inclusion." A couple things bother me. First, in the expanding-circle narrative, expansion tends to be presented as irreversible — once you start counting a group, you dont stop, and history bends toward inclusion. But Omelas is a stable society; the child has been in the cellar for generations, everyone knows, and the system holds. Knowledge doesnt lead to change. Second, the ones who walk away don't *do* anything — they don't free the child, they don't organize, they don't even say goodbye loudly. They just leave. That's not exactly the triumphant "circle expands, justice prevails" arc. And third, Le Guin describes the city's happiness in genuinely gorgeous terms — it's not a dystopia where everyone is secretly miserable; people there know tenderness, art, play, real joy. So the story doesn't let you off the hook by making the bargain obviously bad; it makes you feel the weight of what you'd give up if you walked.

What I want, for my own prep before I teach this, is a reading that puts Omelas and the expanding-circle thesis side by side and is honest about where they fit and where they don't. Where does the story vindicate the thesis — what does it get right about moral expansion that the philosophical literature also gets right? Where does it complicate or challenge the thesis — what does Le Guin see that the theory might be missing? And finally, what does the story *add* — what can it do to a student reading it that a paper by Singer cant? I'd rather have a reading that treats both the story and the philosophy seriously, even if they end up in tension, than one that uses the story as a tidy illustration. I want to walk into class ready to say "here's where it lines up and here's where it doesnt, let's talk about that."
