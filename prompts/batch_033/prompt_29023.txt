I have no academic background in economics, but I have spent years trading on my own account, and I have arrived at a view I find hard to escape: whenever two people voluntarily trade the same asset, at least one of them must be mistaken about what it is worth. My reasoning runs as follows. A trade is an exchange at a price, and the price is a claim about value. If the buyer is right that the asset is worth more than the price, the seller is surrendering that difference; if the seller is right that it is worth less, the buyer is paying too much. Both cannot be right about the same asset at the same price. Moreover, the other side's eagerness to trade is itself evidence I should not ignore: if someone is willing to sell to me at a price I like, that very willingness is a reason to lower my estimate, and if someone is willing to buy from me, that is a reason to raise it. Speculative trade therefore looks as though it should unravel, and any trade that still happens looks as though it must rest on someone's overconfidence, bad information, or wrong model of the world. I want you to take this view seriously and then try to break it. What would count against it? I already see that some trade is not speculative — people sell because they need cash, or because an asset is riskier for them than for the buyer — and I want to know how much of the view survives once those cases are set aside. But go further than that. Are there realistic cases in which two people, both trading purely to profit from their own judgment, both acting sensibly given what they know, trade the same asset, and yet neither is making an error? What would have to be true about their information, their starting beliefs, or their circumstances for that to be possible? Which features of real markets should move me, and how much — the sheer volume of trading in the most liquid markets, the existence of market makers who earn the spread without needing a directional view, the fact that prices often seem to anticipate news better than any single participant could, or the traders who seem to profit for years? Please rank the considerations from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a defender of my view would concede as counting against it rather than dismiss. I would find a realistic, worked-through case more persuasive than a general argument. Finally, I want an honest assessment: does my view, as I have stated it, take any real risk, or can it be retreated into qualifications until nothing could refute it? If it has become unfalsifiable, say so plainly, tell me whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, and describe what a version that does take real risks would have to say.
