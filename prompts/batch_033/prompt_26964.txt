I'm taking a graduate epistemology seminar this semester, and we've spent the last three weeks on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Our professor refuses to lecture on her own view — instead, she's been issuing verdicts on a series of cases and challenging us to extract the principle she's running. Next week we each submit our best guess about how she'll rule on a new case, along with the principle we think explains her track record, and she's hinted that the new case is designed to separate students who have identified the real principle from those who have latched onto something that merely happens to fit the data so far. A few of us in the seminar have been meeting to compare notes, and we've gotten stuck.

Here are the cases she has ruled on:

1. **Standard lottery**: You hold one ticket in a fair lottery with a million tickets and a single winner. You believe, solely on the basis of the odds, that your ticket will lose. Verdict: not rational.

2. **Standard preface**: An author has written a nonfiction book with one hundred factual claims. She checks each claim individually against her sources with reasonable care. She believes each claim. She also writes in the preface that, given her experience with such projects, she is confident the book contains at least one error. Verdict: her belief in each individual claim is rational; her belief that at least one claim is false is also rational.

3. **Small lottery**: Same as case 1, but with only ten tickets. Verdict: not rational.

4. **Newspaper report**: You read in a normally reliable newspaper that your lottery ticket did not win. You have no other information about the drawing. Verdict: rational.

5. **Disease base rate**: You know that sixty percent of the people in a lecture hall have a particular condition. You have no other information about Jane, who is sitting in the hall. You believe Jane has the condition. Verdict: not rational.

6. **Diagnostic test**: Jane takes a medical test for the condition that is 99.9% accurate for both positive and negative results. The test result is positive. You believe Jane has the condition. Verdict: rational.

7. **Watched drawing**: You watch the lottery drawing on television and see that the winning number differs from yours. You believe your ticket lost. Verdict: rational.

The new case she has posted for us is this: An author writes a book with one hundred factual claims. She checks each claim individually, using a fact-checking method that she knows from long experience catches errors 99% of the time but predictably misses 1% of errors. She checks all one hundred claims, finds no errors, and on that basis believes each claim. She also knows, purely as a statistical matter, that she should expect about one undetected error in a book of this length. She believes that the book contains at least one undetected error. Are her belief in each individual claim, and her belief that the book contains at least one error, rational?

We have identified at least two principles that seem to fit all seven of her earlier verdicts. One is something like a statistical-evidence principle: rational belief about a particular proposition cannot be based on evidence that is merely statistical with respect to that proposition; you need evidence that is, in some sense, appropriately connected to the specific case. The other is a modal or sensitivity principle: a belief is rational only if you would not hold it — or would not hold it by the same method — were it false. These two principles agree on the first seven cases but appear to diverge on the new one, or at least we think they do. I would like to know: what exactly are the most defensible formulations of the principles that fit her track record, how do they each rule on the new case, which one is the better interpretation of her pattern of verdicts and why, and — most importantly — what further case, not yet on her list, would most cleanly force a choice between the remaining candidates? I would also like to know whether any of her existing verdicts is doing disproportionate work in narrowing the field, and whether any of them is genuinely surprising under the principle you end up attributing to her.
