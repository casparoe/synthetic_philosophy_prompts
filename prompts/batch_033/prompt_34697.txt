I spent 35 years in molecular biology, and one thing you get used to in the sciences is that your field actually moves. When I started my career we didn't know the structure of the ribosome at atomic resolution; by the time I retired, we did. Problems get solved, textbooks get rewritten, and nobody seriously defends phlogiston theory anymore. So when I retired and started reading philosophy — which I'd always meant to do — I was genuinly surprised by how much of it looks like a permanent stalemate. I've now read a fair bit of Chalmers, Williamson, and some of the metaphilosophy literature, and I keep running into a version of an argument that honestly keeps me up at night. I'll lay it out as best I can:

(1) If a field makes genuine cognitive progress, then over time its practitioners converge on answers to its central questions, and previously live positions get abandoned by the field as a whole.
(2) In philosophy, after two and a half millennia, virtually every central question — free will, the mind-body problem, the nature of justice, realism vs. anti-realism — still has multiple incompatible positions defended by smart, competent people, and there's no sign of convergence.
(3) The usual reply is that philosophy makes progress in a different way: by clarifying concepts, drawing new distinctions, mapping logical space, and ruling out simplistic or naive positions — even if it doesn't converge on final answers.
(4) But (3) looks suspiciously like what any field would say when it's stuck: you redefine "progress" so that whatever you're doing counts as progress.
(5) Worse, if (3) is sufficient for progress, then fields like astrology and theology also make progress — they too refine their conceptual frameworks, rule out naive versions of their claims, and develop ever more sophisticated positions over time.
(6) We don't think astrology makes progress. So either philosophy doesn't either, or we're applying a double standard.
(7) Therefore, philosophy makes no genuine cognitive progress.

I don't want to believe this conclusion. It feels like something has gone wrong — but I cant tell where. When I try to push back on it, I find myself saying things like "but philosophy is different because it deals with conceptual rather than empirical questions," and then I realize that's exactly the move that premise (4) anticipates. So I'm stuck. Tell me where this argument goes wrong, if it does. And I want a committed answer — pick a premise, or a step, and tell me why it fails, and what rejecting it costs. Don't just survey the options; I've read the surveys. I want to know what you'd actually bet on.
