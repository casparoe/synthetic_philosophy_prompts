I work on reinforcement learning systems — mostly applied work in recommendation and logistics — and lately my team has been pulled into discussions about the welfare of the agents we train. In those discussions I keep running into the same argument from the more philosophically inclined ML people in the group. They say an RL agent's reward signal just is, or is the best available model of, pleasure and pain: positive reward is the agent's "good," negative reward is its "bad," and a training run that produces a lot of negative reward is therefore doing something morally like hurting the agent. The slide from "the agent is receiving negative reward" to "the agent is suffering" is doing a lot of work, and I want to know whether the analogy can actually carry it.

The analogy, spelled out, runs something like this. In an animal, pain is a signal that something is wrong: it has negative valence and it drives behavior away from the bad state. In an RL agent, the negative reward signal is a scalar that marks states as bad and, through the update rule, drives the policy away from those states. So reward plays the causal role of valence, and if valence is the core of suffering, then an agent that accumulates negative reward is, by the same logic, suffering. I have seen this used to license everything from "we should add a penalty term to reduce the agent's suffering" to "current training runs are a moral catastrophe."

I want an audit of this analogy, structured in the following sections, in this order:

1. "The mapping" (~250 words): an explicit correspondence table. What maps onto what — in the animal, what plays the role of the reward signal, the value function, the discount factor, the update rule, the terminal state, and reward hacking, if anything does; and in the agent, what plays the role of felt valence, pain intensity, suffering, and the motivational pull of pain, if anything does. A table is fine.

2. "Where the analogy holds" (~250 words): the respects in which negative reward genuinely is like pain and positive reward genuinely is like pleasure, and what conclusions about agent behavior or training dynamics the analogy does license.

3. "Where it breaks" (~400 words): the disanalogies. I am especially interested in which of them matter for the welfare conclusion and which do not — for example, that reward is a training signal as much as (or more than) a felt state; that valence in animals is qualitative and felt rather than merely functional; that animals can suffer while behaviorally frozen or without any reward-like learning; that RL agents can be reward-hacked, wireheaded, or can exhibit avoidance without affect; and whatever else you think belongs on the list.

4. "What the analogy imports" (~200 words): the features of pain and pleasure from the animal case that people assume carry over to the agent case without argument.

5. "Does the conclusion survive?" (~300 words): once the disanalogies are on the table, does "the agent is receiving negative reward" license "the agent is suffering" (or even "we should treat it as if it might be suffering")? Does it survive in some weaker form, and what would an agent have to be like, beyond merely receiving negative reward, for the analogy to earn the conclusion?

6. "A better analogy" (~150 words): if reward-as-valence is not the right comparison, what is, and what does the better analogy license?
