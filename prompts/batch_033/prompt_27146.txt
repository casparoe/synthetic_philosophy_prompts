At a reading group I attend, two people got into an argument about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. I'll reconstruct it as best I can.

(1) Amir said the thesis is straightforwardly empirical: it claims that every effectively computable function is Turing computable, and since "effectively computable" is an informal notion that picks out something in the world — what human beings (or machines) can actually compute, given enough time and paper — the thesis makes a falsifiable claim. If someone exhibited a procedure that we all agree is effective but that computes a non-Turing-computable function, the thesis would be false.

(2) Julia replied that this mislocates the claim. The thesis isn't a prediction about what we'll find; it's a piece of conceptual analysis. The notion of "effective procedure" was vague, and Church and Turing each proposed a precise formalization. The thesis is the claim that these formalizations correctly capture the pre-theoretic concept. That's not the kind of thing you falsify with a counterexample — you evaluate it the way you evaluate any definition: by whether it carves at the joints and does the work we need it to do.

(3) Amir came back: if it's just a definition, then it's empty. The thesis has bite precisely because we can imagine a world in which someone finds a procedure that we'd intuitively call effective but that isn't Turing computable. The fact that we find this hard to imagine is a psychological fact about us, not evidence that the thesis is a priori. Plenty of empirical claims are so well-confirmed that we can't imagine their falsity, but that doesn't make them definitions.

(4) Julia said this conflates two different Church–Turing theses. The mathematical one — about the extension of "effective computability" — isn't empirical, because there's no independent way to identify effective procedures apart from our best formal theories of them. The physical one — about what physical processes can compute — is empirical, but that's a different thesis. The one philosophers of mathematics actually debate is the mathematical one, and it's not empirical.

(5) Amir said the distinction between the mathematical and physical theses is itself question-begging, because it assumes the very thing at issue: that there's a clean separation between the conceptual and the empirical here. If our concept of effective computability is partly constituted by facts about what we can do in the physical world, then the distinction collapses.
