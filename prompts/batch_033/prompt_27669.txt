I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on is a campus novel set in a linguistics department. Two of the central characters are a hardline Quinean about translation and a semantic realist who thinks Quine overreached. The dean forced them into a year-long adversarial collaboration — his idea of making them useful — and the set piece I keep putting off is the joint statement they finally sign. I need you to write that document for me.

It has to be the kind of thing two people who've spent a year arguing could both put their names on, and it has to be philosophically exact enough that a reader who knows the gavagai literature wouldn't cringe. I dont want it to read like a textbook or a lecture; it should read like two stubborn people who finally located the floorboards they share and the fault line where they part.

Please structure it as a numbered list of agreed claims first, then a disagreements table. The agreed claims should cover what the radical-translation setup really is, what the field linguist has to go on, why the rabbit case gets its bite, and what underdetermination by behavioral evidence does and doesn't by itself show. Make these substantive — claims that cost both of them something to sign, not truisms.

Then the table. For each remaining disagreement, give (a) the Quinean's position in wording the Quinean would accept, (b) the semantic realist's position in wording the realist would accept, (c) the crux — the sub-question that if settled would move at least one of them — and (d) a concrete case, argument, or test both would agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance.

One thing I especially need nailed down: the difference between ordinary underdetermination — two theories fit all the evidence and we just can't tell — and Quine's stronger claim that there is no fact of the matter at all about whether gavagai picks out rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or rabbit-stages. Can they even state that difference in a way neither side thinks begs the question? I suspect one crux is the home-language case, where the same inscrutability gets applied to our own words and the realist can't fall back on the linguist's foreignness. Work that in if it earns its place; if it doesn't move either of them, say why. If part of their fight turns out to be verbal, say which part and why, and seperate that from the part that isn't.

Don't bother with citations or a reading list; the characters wouldn't footnote a signed statement, and I'll handle any scholarly apparatus myself. Keep it around fifteen hundred words, the length of something they'd actually circulate.
