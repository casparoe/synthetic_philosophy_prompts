I'm teaching a unit on moral blame and hypocrisy in an intro ethics class, and I want to use *The Scarlet Letter* as a way into the standing-to-blame literature. Here's what I'm chewing on: the novel is basically a sustained study of who gets to blame whom. The townspeople publicly shame Hester, but the reader knows most of them are hiding their own sins. Dimmesdale is the most interesting case — he's actually guilty of the very thing Hester is punished for, yet he participates in her shaming (or at least doesn't stop it), and his sermons about sin become more powerful precisely *because* of his hidden guilt. Meanwhile Hester herself seems to gain a kind of moral authority through publicly bearing the blame.

I want you to think through this as a philosopher who works on standing to blame. Specifically: does the novel anticipate or complicate the contemporary philosophical debate about whether hypocrisy undermines standing? The standard view is that if you're guilty of the same thing, your blame is somehow defective — but Hawthorne seems to be doing something weirder. Dimmesdale's hidden guilt makes his moral pronouncements more rhetorically effective, not less, even if we the reader find him contemptible. And the townspeople's blame, while hypocritical, still manages to create the conditions for Hester's genuine moral growth. Is Hawthorne challenging the idea that standing matters for blame's *social function*, or is he just showing us that effective blame and justified blame can come apart?

Also, what do you make of Pearl? She seems to function as a kind of standing-detector — she's constantly calling out the hypocrisy that the adults paper over. Is that a philosophical point Hawthorne is making, or just a literary device?

I'd love a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophy seriously, and that's honest about where the novel resists the philosophical framework rather than just using it as an illustration. If there are specific scenes or chapters you'd point students to, that'd be great too. Think carefully and don't just give me the standard take — I want the messy version where the two don't line up neatly.
