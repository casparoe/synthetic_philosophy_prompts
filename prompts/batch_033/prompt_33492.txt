I've been going down a rabbit hole on the paradox of self-amendment in legal theory — you know, the thing where if a constitution lays out a procedure for amending itself, can that procedure be used to amend the very clause that specifies the procedure? Alf Ross kicked this off arguing it creates a genuine logical paradox: if the old amendment rule authorizes its own replacement, what authorizes the new rule once the old one is gone? A bunch of people have pushed back saying it's just a confusion about time, or about levels of rules, or about what "validity" even means. Peter Suber wrote a whole book defending the paradox as real and deep. But as far as I can tell, the debate has been basically frozen for decades — the same moves get made, nobody convinces anyone, and the literature just sort of circles. What kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it a substantive logical question that someone could settle with the right formal argument, a verbal dispute about the word "validity," or a deeper disagreement about whether legal systems are best modeled as static logical systems or as temporal processes? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually move it, or is this just one of those problems where the two sides are asking different questions and don't realise it?
