I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been reading Hume's essay "Of Miracles" and tried to formalize his argument to see if it holds up. I wrote out what I think is the core reasoning, and when I run it on some test cases, it seems to reject way more than just miracle claims. I'm hoping someone can help me find the bug.

Here's my reconstruction:

(1) For any reported event E, if the only evidence for E is testimony, then we should believe E only if it would be more improbable for the testimony to be false (through error, fraud, or credulity) than for E to have occurred.

(2) A miracle is a violation of a law of nature, and a law of nature is backed by uniform, exceptionless experience. So the prior improbability of a miracle is as high as it can be — it is maximally unlikely relative to all our past experience.

(3) By contrast, the probability that testimony is false is never that low. People are frequently mistaken, exaggerate, or deceive, and even reliable witnesses can be fooled. So the improbability of false testimony will always be less than the improbability of a miracle.

(4) Therefore, applying (1), no testimony can ever rationally establish that a miracle has occurred.

Here's the problem. When I test premise (1) on non-miraculous cases, it seems to rule out beliefs I'm pretty sure are rational. Suppose you're the first European naturalist to see a black swan. Before your sighting, all of Europe's experience was that swans are white. The prior improbability of a black swan, given that uniform experience, is extremely high — maybe not as high as a miracle, but still high enough that the probability of misperception or fraud would seem to beat it. By (1), you should doubt your own eyes. But that's not how science works: we take anomalous observations seriously, and sometimes they lead us to revise what we thought were laws. Similarly, if a respected lab reports a surprising experimental result that contradicts decades of uniform results, premise (1) seems to say we should always dismiss it as error or fraud, since the prior improbability of the new result is higher than the improbability of experimental error. But that would freeze science in place.

So the argument seems to prove too much: it doesn't just rule out miracles; it rules out rational belief-updating on surprising testimony in general. And yet Hume's conclusion — that testimony alone can't establish a miracle — feels right to most people. I'd like to know which premise to give up, and what it costs.
