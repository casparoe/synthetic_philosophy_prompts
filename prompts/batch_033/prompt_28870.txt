I've recently taken a position on the risk committee of an environmental regulatory agency. I review quantitative risk assessments that inform permitting decisions for hazardous facilities, and I keep running into the same foundational problem. Different analysts assign wildly different probabilities to the same event — a catastrophic failure, an extreme flood, a contamination plume reaching an aquifer — and when I press them, the disagreement isn't about the data. It's about what a probability claim is in the first place. One analyst treats it as a long-run frequency and worries endlessly about which reference class the event belongs to. Another treats it as a rational degree of belief given the evidence and updates it whenever new information arrives. A third talks about the physical propensity of the system, as if probability were a property like mass. I need to get this straight before I can judge whose numbers to trust.

Write me the desiderata for an interpretation of probability: what any adequate account of what probability means must deliver. Give me the list of requirements, each with the case or phenomenon that makes it a requirement, and distinguish which are non-negotiable constraints on any interpretation and which are parochial demands of one camp that another can reasonably set aside.

Make sure the list wrestles with at least these fault lines: how an interpretation handles unique, non-repeatable events (this particular dam, this particular watershed under this climate scenario) versus its obligation to explain why probabilities are constrained by observed frequencies; how it accounts for the role of probability in guiding action under uncertainty versus its role in scientific explanation; and how it makes room for the fact that reasonable people with the same evidence can disagree about a probability without either of them being obviously irrational, while also making room for the fact that some probability assignments are flatly wrong.

I want a committed answer. Tell me which requirements you think are mandatory, whether any subset of them can't be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding the leading interpretations. A table for the scorecard is fine, but the weight should be on the reasoning behind each entry on the list — why it earns its place and what it costs to drop it.
