**Question:**

Heritability (H²) is frequently misinterpreted in public discourse. In a well-organized answer, explain: (a) what broad-sense heritability measures and how it is defined; (b) why high heritability does not imply that a trait is genetically determined or unchangeable; and (c) what high within-group heritability does and does not imply about the sources of between-group differences in the same trait. Use at least one concrete example.

**Student Answer:**

Broad-sense heritability, denoted H², is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population that is attributable to genetic variance. Formally, H² = V_G / V_P, where V_G is genetic variance and V_P is total phenotypic variance. Crucially, heritability is a population-level statistic: it describes variation within a particular population in a particular range of environments, and it says nothing about the causes of any individual's trait value.

High heritability does not imply genetic determinism for two reasons. First, heritability can change when the environment changes — if environmental variance decreases, heritability goes up even though the role of genes hasn't changed. Second, a trait can have high heritability and still be highly responsive to environmental intervention. The classic example is phenylketonuria (PKU): the condition is strongly heritable, yet its most severe effects can be prevented by a low-phenylalanine diet. So heritability is not a measure of immutability.

Regarding between-group differences, the key point is that heritability within groups does not logically entail anything about the causes of differences between groups. This is often illustrated with Lewontin's thought experiment: if you plant two genetically identical batches of seeds in rich and poor soil, all variation within each plot will be environmental (heritability is zero within groups), yet the between-group difference is entirely environmental. The same logic works in reverse: high within-group heritability is compatible with purely environmental between-group differences. For instance, if two populations live in environments that differ in a way that affects the trait but that doesn't vary within either population, the between-group difference could be entirely environmental despite high heritability within each group.

However, it would be a mistake to conclude that heritability is irrelevant to between-group differences. If a trait shows high heritability in every population that has been studied, across a wide range of environments, this does provide some evidence that genetic factors contribute to observed group differences. The reason is that if the trait were entirely environmentally determined, we would expect to find at least some environments where heritability is low, and the consistent failure to find such environments is surprising under a purely environmental hypothesis. So while high within-group heritability never proves that group differences are genetic, it can shift our credences when the pattern is robust across many environments.
