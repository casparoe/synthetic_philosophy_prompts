I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I mostly lurk in arguments about free will and blame, so I'm going to reach for control-flow language. There's a move I keep seeing that I don't have a name for. Someone describes a case where a person decides on their own to do something bad — punch a stranger, say. But without them knowing, a device has been wired in that will make them punch if they show even a flicker of hesitation. The device never fires; they just do it. So there's a real sense in which they couldn't have done otherwise — if they'd wavered, the device would have forced the punch — and yet everyone still says there fully to blame. The person telling the story then uses this to knock down the idea that you're only responsible for something if you could have avoided it. I've also seen the same point made with no gadget at all: someone's upbringing or temperament guaranteed they'd do it, so they couldn't have done otherwise, but they're still blamed. Are these the same move or different ones? Does the gadget version have an established name, and does it actually show what it's supposed to show? I can think of a few escape hatches — maybe the person still 'could' hesitate in some sense, or maybe the gadget changes what 'could have done otherwise' means, or maybe the case only works because we already think they're responsible for other reasons — but I don't know wich of those are taken seriously. I'm not just after the label; I want the carve-up of what the case rules out, what it leaves standing, and where its edges are. Also, in code terms the 'could' being attacked and the 'could' the gadget removes feel like a runtime branch versus a compile-time guarantee, and I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or me being confused.
