I sit on my hospital's ethics committee. A patient with bipolar disorder signed a Ulysses contract during a stable period: if she becomes manic and refuses medication, she authorizes the hospital to treat her involuntarily until the episode resolves. She's manic now, refusing lithium, and arguing — pretty coherently, honestly — that she's the one living in this body now, that the woman who signed that paper doesnt get to override her, and that forcing medication on her would be using her as a tool for somebody else's preferences. Our policy says we can honor such directives when the patient lacks decision-making capacity, but she isn't obviously incapacitated: she understands the treatment and its risks; she just thinks she doesn't need it and that the contract is invalid. Draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: recommend whether to enforce the contract, under what conditions, and for how long, and make the strongest case for her side before you explain why you'd reject it — no strawman. Flag the points where you're least sure, and if you think the capacity test is the wrong lens here, or the whole way I've posed the question is confused, say so plainly instead of writing around it.
