I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've ended up reading around medical ethics because my family recently had to make decisions for a relative with dementia. A colleague of mine, who did study some philosophy, summarized the debate about advance directives for me in the following way: "An advance directive is basically a contract you make with your future self. While you are still competent, you decide which treatments you will accept later, and your later, demented self is bound by that choice in roughly the way you would be bound by a promise. The standard philosophical defense, associated with Ronald Dworkin, says that your critical interests — what makes your life go well as a whole, such as living with dignity and not ending up in a state you would have found degrading — outweigh the later self's merely experiential interests, such as enjoying a sing-along or a favorite food. So we should honor the directive even when the person now seems content. Critics reply that the later person is effectively a different person, because the memories and values that made the directive yours are gone; honoring it is therefore imposing the wishes of a dead person on a stranger. And since no one would accept being sacrificed for a stranger's preferences, this objection refutes the practice of treating advance directives as binding."

That summary feels almost too tidy to me. I'm inclined to suspect that the contract-with-your-future-self framing is doing more work than it should, and that the final step moves too quickly, but I cannot pinpoint what a careful philosopher would accept, what they would qualify, and what they would reject. Could you go through it piece by piece and tell me which claims are fair, which are misleading simplifications, and whether the conclusion — that the critics' objection refutes binding advance directives — actually follows from the accurate parts?
