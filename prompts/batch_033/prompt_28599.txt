I recently learned, through a DNA test, that I was conceived using a sperm donor. My parents confirmed it and then told me something that has been quietly unraveling my sense of my own existence ever since: they had narrowed their choice to two donors and almost chose the other one. A last-minute scheduling conflict with the first donor's clinic made them switch. If the clinic had returned their call an hour earlier, they would have used different genetic material, and the embryo that became me would never have formed.

I cannot stop asking whether I would still exist if they had gone with the other donor. On one hand, the thought seems straightforward: the embryo that became me came from a particular sperm and a particular egg. Had a different sperm fertilized that egg, a genetically distinct embryo would have developed — a different person, a sibling who never was. So I simply would not have been born. On the other hand, I am here, I am me, and the idea that a trivial clerical delay is the only thing separating my existence from non-existence feels both vertiginous and faintly absurd. If my parents had conceived naturally, I would not lie awake wondering whether a different sperm could have reached the egg on a different night — so why does the donor choice unsettle me in a way that ordinary reproductive contingency does not?

I am not looking for reassurance or therapy. I want to understand what is philosophically at stake in the claim that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg. Is that claim actually defensible, or does it rest on a picture of personal identity that falls apart under pressure? And if it is right, what follows — about the role of luck in who exists, about whether my existence is more fragile than I had reason to think, about what I can coherently feel grateful for?
