My moral philosophy professor has given the following verdicts across a term of cases, all of which share a common structure: an agent must decide whether to violate a deontological constraint against φ-ing in order to prevent multiple other agents from φ-ing. She has not stated a general principle, but her verdicts appear to follow some consistent rationale.

Here are the cases and her verdicts:

1. **Kill to prevent killings.** A terrorist will kill five innocent people unless you kill one different innocent person (the terrorist will then relent). Verdict: impermissible.

2. **Torture to prevent tortures.** A captor is torturing five prisoners. The only way to make him stop is to torture his innocent child, after which he will release everyone. Verdict: impermissible.

3. **Lie to prevent lies.** A colleague is about to tell five seriously harmful lies to five different people. The only way to stop her is to tell her a single lie, after which she will abandon her plan. Verdict: permissible.

4. **Break a promise to prevent broken promises.** You have promised to meet a friend for coffee. Five other people are each about to break serious promises to their own friends. The only way to prevent all five breaches is to break your coffee promise. Verdict: permissible.

5. **Destroy property to prevent property destruction.** A vandal is about to destroy five people's cars. The only way to stop him is to destroy one different person's car. Verdict: impermissible.

6. **Minor assault to prevent minor assaults.** A person is about to slap five strangers. The only way to stop her is to slap one different person. Verdict: permissible.

Next week's case is this:

**Imprisonment to prevent imprisonments.** A corrupt official is about to have five innocent people imprisoned for a year each on fabricated charges. The only way to stop him is to plant evidence that will cause one different innocent person to be imprisoned for a year.

Predict her verdict on the imprisonment case. Identify the candidate principles that fit her track record, say which one you think she is running and why, and explain what each candidate principle would say about the new case. If you think the track record genuinely underdetermines her verdict, say so and explain what principle she would be adopting with each choice.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about your prediction: which further case would most cleanly discriminate between the candidate principles, or what argument would lead you to a different reading of her track record.
