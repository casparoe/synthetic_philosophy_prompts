For a reading group in population ethics, I have been asked to write down my considered position so that the others can pressure-test it. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I am a person-affecting theorist: all reasons and values are ultimately reasons and values with respect to particular people, whether actual or future; I do not countenance impersonal reasons.

(2) I think it would be wrong to deliberately conceive a child whom you know will have a life dominated by severe chronic pain, even if that child's life would still be worth living and the only alternative for that child is never to have existed.

(3) I explain (2) through the procreation asymmetry: the absence of pain in a person who never comes to exist is good, while the absence of pleasure in a person who never comes to exist is not bad. That is why coming into existence is held to a different standard than continuing to exist.

(4) Nevertheless, I think ordinary procreation is permissible when the resulting child's life will on balance be happy; I am not an anti-natalist.

(5) I also accept the standard non-identity verdict for policy cases: if a long-term policy changes who comes to exist, it wrongs no one so long as the people who do exist under it have lives worth living.

(6) I firmly reject pro-mortalism: once someone exists, the fact that their life contains suffering never by itself makes death good for them, and it is not wrong to keep them alive.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank the tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be, and identify the single revision that would do the most to make the whole set coherent.
