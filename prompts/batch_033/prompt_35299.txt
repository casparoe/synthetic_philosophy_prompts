I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've gotten myself tangled in a problem that I think has philosophical stakes. I wrote out an argument that seems to show that deductive inference can never be justified — that any attempt to take even a single logical step generates an infinite regress and therefore never bottoms out. The conclusion is obviously false: I used deduction to write the argument, and you're using it to read it. But I can't locate the error in the chain of reasoning, and it's been bothering me for weeks.

Here's the argument:

(1) Suppose I hold two beliefs: P, and "if P then Q." I want to infer Q.

(2) The inference from P and "if P then Q" to Q is not logically automatic; it requires a rule that licenses the move. Call that rule Modus Ponens (MP).

(3) So now my premises are: P, "if P then Q," and MP. But MP is just another statement sitting among my premises. To apply it — to recognize that this particular P and this particular "if P then Q" fall under the rule and that the rule says I may infer Q — I need a further rule. Call it R1: "Given premises of the form A and 'if A then B,' and given the rule MP, infer B."

(4) Now I have premises P, "if P then Q," MP, and R1. To apply R1 to this situation, I need R2. To apply R2 I need R3. And so on without end.

(5) Before I can ever complete the original inference from P and "if P then Q" to Q, I must first complete an infinite sequence of rule-applications. A finite mind or a finite machine cannot complete an infinite sequence.

(6) Therefore, the inference to Q is never justified. Since the same structure applies to any deductive step, no deductive inference is ever justified.

As a programmer, this looks exactly like a recursive function with no base case: each call to `apply_rule` spawns another call to `apply_rule`, the stack grows without bound, and the function never returns. In code, the fix is straightforward — you add a base case. But what would a base case even look like here? The obvious candidate is something like "at some level, you just apply the rule without needing a further rule to license it." But that feels like saying the recursion terminates because you got bored. It doesn't explain why the regress isn't vicious at step (2) rather than at step (47).

So my question is: where does this argument go wrong? If you have to reject a premise, tell me which one, and tell me what rejecting it costs — what else do you have to give up, or what picture of reasoning do you have to accept instead? I'm not looking for a historical answer about Lewis Carroll (I know the argument is related to his dialogue, but I haven't read it). I want to know what's wrong with the reasoning itself, stated in terms that make sense to someone who thinks in functions and base cases.
