I chair the grants committee at a small family foundation. We meet once a quarter and make three or four grants; the amounts aren't huge but they're life-changing for the groups that get them, and the board signs off on whatever we recommend. Nobody on the committee has a philosophy background. This stopped being an abstract question for me last year, though. My dad died and my sister and I had to divide up his things — his tools, the house, his mother's letters — and I kept building a spreadsheet were every item got a dollar value so we could trade fairly. It felt like lying about what we actually cared about, and the spreadsheet just hid the hard calls instead of making them. Now I notice we do the same thing in committee: we score each proposal 1-10 on impact, feasibility, and equity, average the numbers, and fund the top ones. That works fine when one proposal is just better on everything. It breaks exactly when it matters: a legal aid clinic vs a community garden vs a music program for kids. We pretend the rubric is deciding when really someone strong-arms the room.

So write me a decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person — that my committee can actually run in a two-hour meeting and use every quarter. Ground it in what philosophers actually know about incommensurable values and hard choices: when options are on a par rather than one being better, why forcing a common metric distorts the decision, and how you can make a justified choice between things that don't share a scale. I want it to catch both failure modes: the case where the options are actually comparable and we're just being lazy, and the case where we use the rubric to dodge a hard choice.

Commit to a definite procedure and defend the choices you made in building it — don't give me a survey of theories and leave me to pick. And be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a flowchart costume, and what the procedure can't do for us.

Then run it on three situations that actually come up for us, or close versions of them: (1) a tenant legal aid clinic vs an after-school music program, roughly equal cost, serving different values; (2) a proposal I personally love that turns out to be dominated by another proposal — worse on every dimension we actually care about, once we list them; (3) a proposal from a board member's daughter's organization that scores fine but not top, where the committee starts looking for reasons after the fact. Make sure one of these gives an answer we won't like.
