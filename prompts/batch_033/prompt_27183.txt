I am a PhD candidate in philosophy, and in three weeks I am presenting a paper at a workshop on intergenerational ethics. The thesis I am defending is a version of Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: much of what we value and do is implicitly conditioned on the assumption that humanity will continue after we are gone, and this reveals a reason why imminent human extinction would be bad that is not captured by aggregating the welfare losses of the people who would die or never come to exist. My argument has two stages: first, a set of reflection cases meant to show that our engagement with projects, knowledge, art, and even ordinary routines would be substantially diminished if we learned that the world would end shortly after our own deaths; second, the claim that this diminishment is not merely a psychological fact about us but bears directly on what makes extinction bad.

The audience is mostly value theorists and population ethicists: several committed total utilitarians, two people who work on person-affecting accounts of harm, and a few philosophers of action who are skeptical of appeals to "meaning." None of them are historians of philosophy, and I should not expect sympathy for the idea that anything matters independently of welfare. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way one of these people would put it. For each objection, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, note where the reply is weakest, and say what I should concede up front and what I should hold. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
