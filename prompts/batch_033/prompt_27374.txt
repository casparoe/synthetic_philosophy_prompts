I'd like you to construct a thought experiment in modal metaphysics that meets the following specifications.

The two theories in play are counterpart theory (in David Lewis's sense) and transworld identity (the view that individuals literally exist in more than one possible world). The task is to engineer a scenario involving an ordinary individual and a pair of de re modal claims—one governed by a single modal operator, the other by iterated modal operators—such that:

(1) The two theories agree on the truth-value of the simple claim.
(2) The two theories disagree on the truth-value of the iterated claim.
(3) The disagreement must be driven by structural features of the two theories—specifically, by how each handles de re modality across worlds—and not by auxiliary assumptions about the accessibility relation, the similarity metric used to select counterparts, or the richness of modal space.
(4) The individual should be ordinary: a politician, an athlete, an artifact, or the like. No exotic beings, no non-standard mereology, no science-fictional elements.
(5) Describe the scenario in a single paragraph.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. First, walk through the reasoning each theory gives for the simple claim, confirming that they agree. Second, walk through the reasoning each theory gives for the iterated claim, showing precisely where and why they diverge. Third, identify which structural feature of counterpart theory—absent from transworld identity—is responsible for the divergence. Fourth, explain how a defender of counterpart theory might revise the theory (for instance, by constraining the counterpart relation or by adjusting the semantic clause for iterated modalities) to block the divergence, and state clearly what theoretical cost the revision would incur.
