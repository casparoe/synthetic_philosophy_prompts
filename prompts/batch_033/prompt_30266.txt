So this argument broke out at a meetup last week and I've been turning it over ever since. Two friends of mine, both pretty serious about rationality and both read the sequences back in the day, got into it about a third friend's investment decision. I'll call them Nate and Chloe.

The situation: our mutual friend Derek put about $15k — basically his entire savings at the time — into a crypto project two years ago. At the time, the project had basically every red flag: anonymous team, plagiarized whitepaper, promises of guaranteed returns, the works. Multiple people told Derek it looked like a scam. He said he had a "good feeling" about it. Fast forward to today: the token did a 200x, Derek cashed out near the top, and he's sitting on about three million dollars.

Here's how the argument went.

Nate: Derek made an objectively terrible decision. The expected value was clearly negative given the information available to him. The fact that it worked out is pure luck. If you run that decision a thousand times, Derek is broke in 990 of them.

Chloe: But we don't have a thousand runs. We have one run, and in that run, Derek made three million dollars. By what metric was that a bad decision? You're defining "good decision" in a way that's completely disconnected from results.

Nate: I'm defining it in terms of the decision procedure. If someone drives drunk and gets home safely, they still made a bad decision. The outcome doesn't retroactively make the decision good. This is basic — it's like, the first thing you learn about evaluating decisions under uncertainty.

Chloe: The drunk driving analogy is misleading because we have massive amounts of data telling us drunk driving is dangerous. With a novel crypto project, the base rates are much murkier. Derek might have been picking up on real signals that your checklist missed. The fact that he bet big and won is Bayesian evidence that his "good feeling" was tracking something real — maybe not consciously, but the outcome updates us toward "Derek has some ability to spot real projects that look like scams."

Nate: That's exactly the error. You're using the outcome as evidence about the decision quality, but you can only do that if you have a reference class. The reference class here is "crypto projects with anonymous teams and plagiarized whitepapers that promise guaranteed returns." In that reference class, the success rate is approximately zero. Derek got the one-in-a-thousand outcome. Using that to update your model of Derek's judgment is textbook survivorship bias.

Chloe: But your reference class is question-begging. Why is the relevant reference class "projects with those specific red flags" rather than "projects Derek felt good about"? If Derek has a track record — and he does now — of betting on things that succeed, then "projects Derek feels good about" starts looking like a pretty good reference class. You're assuming the red flags are the right way to carve reality, but maybe Derek is tracking something those red flags don't capture.

Nate: He has a track record of one! One data point! You can't build a reference class out of one data point. That's not how any of this works.

Chloe: Sure, one data point is weak evidence. But it's not zero evidence. And more importantly, you're dodging the deeper point: if we never let outcomes update our models of what good decision procedures are, then our models of good decision procedures become completely untethered from reality. They become just... kinda aesthetic preferences about what kinds of reasoning sound good. At some point the map has to answer to the territory, and the territory is outcomes.

Nate: The territory is the distribution of outcomes across the reference class, not the single outcome. You're confusing the territory with a tourist photo of the territory.

Chloe: And you're confusing a model with the thing it's modeling. Your reference class is a model. It might be wrong. Derek's success is a reason — not a conclusive reason, but a reason — to think it's wrong, or at least that it doesn't carve the world at the joints the way you think it does.

At this point someone changed the subject and they never resolved it. I've been trying to figure out who's right about what. Can you go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate? I'm especially interested in whether Chloe's point about reference classes being models that need to answer to outcomes is actually a good point or just a sophisticated way of comitting the outcome bias, and whether Nate is right that the whole thing reduces to "don't update on a single data point" or whether that's too quick.
