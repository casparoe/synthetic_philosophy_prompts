I'm a retired geophysicist, so I'm used to infinite series and convergence issues, but the philosophy of decision theory is newer to me. I've been reading about the Pasadena game—the one where you toss a fair coin until heads, and if it first lands heads on toss n you win $2^n/n when n is odd and lose that amount when n is even—and I've settled into a set of commitments that I'd like you to audit for consistency. I don't want a lecture on the background; I want the tensions, ordered by severity, with what would have to give.

Here's where I've landed:

(a) Expected utility maximization is the right normative rule for choices among gambles whenever expected utility is well-defined.
(b) The Pasadena game has no well-defined expected value. Its expected-payoff series is conditionally convergent, so the sum is order-dependent; that order-dependence shows there is no priviledged expected value.
(c) If I had to choose between the Pasadena game and the Pasadena game plus a sure dollar, I'd take the plus-dollar version. It dominates the original state by state.
(d) Dominance reasoning is a legitimate rational constraint even when expected utilities are undefined, not just a theorem that holds when expectations exist.
(e) We should not fix this by adopting weak expectations or by stipulating a preferred summation order. The fact that rearrangements change the sum is a sign the question is ill-posed, not an invitation to pick a convention.
(f) A rational decision theory does not need to issue a verdict for every pair of gambles; some options can be genuinely incomparable.

I keep going back and forth on whether (b) and (c) are consistent, and whether (f) quietly conflicts with (a). But I'm to close to it.
