I've been going down a population ethics rabbit hole lately, and I've landed on a version of the procreation asymmetry that I'd have a hard time giving up. The way I hold it: when we're deciding whether to bring someone into existence, the fact that the life would contain suffering counts as a reason against creating it, but the fact that the life would contain happiness doesn't count as a reason in favor in the same way. Slightly more formal: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when there's no one around to be deprived of it, but the absence of pleasure isn't bad unless there's an actual person who misses it. I don't think this is just a quirk of my moral psychology, either — its the thing that keeps me from concluding we're obligated to tile the universe with happy people, and it makes a lot of person-affecting views look like they're tracking something real.

What I'd like from you is a map of what would count against this position. Not just a clever counterexample, but the full set of pressure points: which intuitions, which cases (including ones about already-existing people and about merely possible people), which results in formal population axiology, and which neighboring commitments the asymmetry rises or falls with. I especially want to know which of these a defender of the asymmetry would themselves admit counts against it, versus which ones they'd dismiss as question-begging or as changing the subject.

Sort the defeaters by how much damage they'd actually do, from "this kills the view" down to "this is an annoying wrinkle a defender can live with." And give me an honest read on whether the asymmetry, as it's usually defended, has been hedged so much that nothing could ever touch it — and whether I should treat that as a problem, or as the normal condition of an axiological bedrock view. Answer in American English, and don't water it down.
