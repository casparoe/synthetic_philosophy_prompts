I'm teaching an upper-level metaphysics seminar this term — about 15 grad students, most of them sharp but new to the material constitution literature. I assigned a chapter from a pretty well-known intro textbook (won't name it, but you'd recognize it) and there's a passage that's been nagging at me. I've read it four times now and I still can't decide whether it's a defensible simplification or whether it's going to give my students a picture they'll have to unlearn later. Here's the relevant bit, paraphrased but keeping the structure:

The textbook says that the puzzle of the statue and the clay — the classic case where a lump of clay is shaped into a statue of Goliath on Monday and then squashed on Tuesday — is ultimately a puzzle about sortal concepts, not about the world. The lump and the statue are spatially coincident and materially identical, so they're the same thing. The fact that we say the statue is destroyed by squashing but the lump isn't just shows that "statue" and "lump" carry different criteria of identity and persistence. But those criteria, the textbook argues, are just features of our conceptual scheme — they're rules we've adopted for tracking the same underlying stuff under different descriptions. So there's no genuine metaphysical problem of two things being in the same place at the same time; there's just one thing, and we're describing it two ways. The textbook then says this is basically what Aristotle had in mind with the distinction between form and matter, and that most of the contemporary debate is philosophers making a mountain out of a molehill that was already flattened.

I have my own take on this, but I want to make sure I'm not being unfair to the textbook before I tell my students to read it critically. So: which parts of this summary would someone working on material constitution (say, a defender of the coincidence view, or even a monist who takes the puzzle seriously) accept as fair? Which parts are oversimplifications or misrepresentations? And does the deflationary conclusion actually follow from the accurate parts, or is the textbook smuggling in a substantive metaphysical claim under the guise of conceptual analysis? I'm especially interested in whether the move from "different persistence conditions" to "just features of our conceptual scheme" is legitimate, and whether the appeal to Aristotle is doing any real work or is just rhetorical furniture.
