I'm a PhD student in political theory, working on early modern arguments about the relation between power and right, and I keep getting stuck on a short passage in Pascal's Pensées, from the section "Justice and the Reason of Effects" (trans. W. F. Trotter):

"Justice, Might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just.

Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

The first paragraph looks prescriptive — justice and force need each other — but the second paragraph seems to report that only one half of the prescription was ever achievable. I want help with the exact logic of the second paragraph. In particular: (1) What is the obstacle that prevents giving might to justice? Is the point that justice is essentially disputable while force is not, so there is no agreed justice for force to be attached to? (2) Does the final clause, that we have made what is strong just, mean that force has been genuinely transformed into justice, or merely legitimized after the fact? (3) Does the second paragraph overturn, complete, or merely redescribe the prescription of the first? Please give a close reading of these two paragraphs rather than summarizing the surrounding fragments.

Reply in three titled sections: "The obstacle" (~150 words), "The sense of 'making the strong just'" (~150 words), and "Prescription or description?" (~150 words).
