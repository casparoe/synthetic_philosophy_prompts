I'm in a philosophy of science seminar, and we're working through Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*. I keep running into what seems like a serious tension in the account, and I'm hoping you can help me see whether it's resolvable or whether it really is a problem for the view.

Kuhn insists that competing paradigms are incommensurable: there is no neutral observation language or theory-independent set of methodological rules that could adjudicate a dispute between, say, a Ptolemaic and a Copernican astronomer. But he also wants to say that paradigm shifts aren't merely arational gestalt switches or acts of mass conversion — scientists are persuaded by reasons, such as the new paradigm's superior puzzle-solving ability, its simplicity, or its promise for guiding future research. The difficulty is that what counts as a puzzle, what counts as simplicity, and what counts as a promising research program all seem to be internal to a paradigm. So when a Copernican argues that heliocentrism is simpler, she's applying standards that the Ptolemaic astronomer has no reason to accept — the standards themselves are part of what's in dispute. Appealing to them looks question-begging.

It seems to me that Kuhn can't have it both ways. Either paradigm transitions are rational, in which case there must be shared trans-paradigm standards after all (and incommensurability is false), or incommensurability is genuine, in which case transitions can't be rational in any interesting sense and the view collapses into a kind of relativism. Kuhn himself explicitly rejected the relativist reading, so I assume I'm missing something. Where exactly does the reasoning go wrong?
