Please give me your actual credence in the following proposition: it is sometimes determinately true or false that a change affecting one person makes that person better off to a greater degree than a different change affecting another person makes that person better off or worse off, independently of any convention adopted for measurement, aggregation, or policy. I am not asking whether such comparisons are useful, unavoidable, or convenient; I am asking whether at least some of them are objectively correct.

Begin by stating the precise proposition you are assigning a probability to and the interpretation of utility or well-being you are using. If your credence would differ across interpretations, give separate numbers for no more than two interpretations and then choose one to work with for the rest of the answer.

Decompose your credence into at least five sub-questions. For each sub-question, give a numerical probability, a one- or two-sentence justification, and a statement of how it bears on the main proposition. Your decomposition should include, at minimum, the following sub-questions: (1) whether the correct theory of well-being is one that supports cardinal intrapersonal comparisons, such as a hedonic magnitude view or a preference-satisfaction view with a defensible cardinalization; (2) whether intrapersonal units of well-being can be defined non-arbitrarily, even if the correct theory supports them; (3) whether interpersonal scaling, meaning the fixing of a common zero point and unit across persons, can be grounded in fact rather than stipulation; (4) whether interpersonal comparison claims are truth-apt descriptive claims rather than expressions of approval, recommendation, or policy choice; and (5) whether our actual comparative judgments and empathic capacities track the relevant facts, if such facts exist. You may add further sub-questions if they do real work.

Then combine the sub-questions explicitly. Show the arithmetic or conditional structure that yields your final credence, and indicate how sensitive the final number is to each term. Identify the sub-question you are least confident about, and state what evidence or argument would most shift your credence on it. Finally, separate the epistemic uncertainty in your answer from the metaphysical uncertainty: roughly how much of your hesitation is doubt about whether we could ever know such comparisons, as opposed to doubt about whether there is any fact there to know?
