i keep going back and forth on what it even means to say a neural network "has representations." there seem to be at least two ways of cashing this out and i cant tell if they're compatable or if picking one over the other has downstream consequences.

on one view, a representation is roughly something that carries information about a distal variable — like, a direction in activation space counts as representing "truth" if you can decode truth-values from it better than chance, and ideally if the information is actually used somewhere downstream. this is the information-theoretic slash decoding view, and it seems pretty minimal: you don't need to know how the representation is computed or what causal role it plays, you just need mutual information between the activations and some external variable.

on the other view, a representation is defined by its causal role in the system's computations — it's not enough that the activation pattern correlates with something in the world; it has to be used by the network in a way that respects something like a compositional semantics. like, if the network has a representation of "dog," that representation should combine with representations of other concepts to produce systematic behavior, and intervening on it should produce predictable changes in output that respect the semantics. this is more like a functional-role or structural account.

what i want to know is: do these two views come apart in practice when we're doing interpretability on real models? are there cases where the information-theoretic view says "yes, there's a representation here" but the causal-role view says "no, that's not really a representation in the relevant sense" — or vice versa? and if they do come apart, which one should we actually care about for alignment purposes? like, if i want to know whether a model is internally representing deceptive plans vs. just outputting text that happens to be about deception, which notion of representation do i need to be using?
