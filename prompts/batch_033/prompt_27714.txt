I've been following the epiphenomenalism debate in philosophy of mind for a few years now and it feels like its the same three moves over and over, with nobody budging. The anti-epiphenomenalist says: if qualia are causally inert, then my judgment "I'm in pain right now" isn't caused by the pain — it's caused by whatever neural state causes the pain. But then the fact that the judgment is true looks like a massive coincidence.Why should my phenomenal judgments reliably track my phenomenal states if the latter have no causal influence on the former? The epiphenomenalist's standard reply is: look, the neural state N causes both the quale Q and the judgment J — common cause, no coincidence. And then the anti-epiphenomenalist says: that explains correlation between Q and J, but it doesn't explain why J is *about* Q. You've given me a causal diagram, not a theory of intentionality. And somewhere in here someone always brings up self-stultification: if epiphenomenalism is true, then the epiphenomenalist's own *argument* for the view — including their introspective judgment that they even have qualia — wasn't caused by those qualia, in which case it's unclear how it could count as knowledge of them. And yet epiphenomenalists keep publishing papers and nobody seems to feel refuted.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is there a hidden empirical question here — e.g., about whether the neural realizer of a judgment could be the same neural state that causes the corresponding quale? Is it a dispute about what "aboutness" requires, and if so is that a substantive or a verbal disagreement? Are the sides operating with different standards for what makes an explanation satisfying, such that the common-cause story feels complete to one side and question-begging to the other? Is the self-stultification charge a genuine knockout if it works, or does it just beg the question by assuming that knowledge requires a causal connection to the fact known? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move this debate — a formal result about common-cause structures and semantic content, a new experimental paradigm, a sharper distinction between epistemic and metaphysical versions of the paradox, or should we just accept that this is a clash of bedrock modal intuitions that isn't going anywhere?
