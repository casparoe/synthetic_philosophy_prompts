I'm writing a paper on the justification of deduction, and before I engage with the existing proposals, I want to nail down what a theory in this area is even supposed to deliver. The framing problem is Carroll's tortoise. As you know, Achilles tries to force the tortoise to accept Z (a conclusion) from premises A and B (where B is "If A then Z"). The tortoise accepts A and B but refuses Z. Achilles adds premise C: "If A and B are true, Z must be true." The tortoise accepts A, B, and C but still refuses Z. The regress is off.

What I want is a list of desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of the justification of deduction must satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each. Some will be drawn directly from the tortoise scenario; others will come from broader concerns about what it means for a rule of inference to be justified, about the relationship between inference and implication, and about the kind of normativity logic is supposed to carry. For each desideratum, say whether it is non-negotiable (a constraint any solution must respect) or merely a preference of a particular camp.

I am especially interested in whether any pair of desiderata on your list are in tension with each other, or jointly unsatisfiable. If the tortoise teaches us that some demand we want to make of a theory of deductive justification is precisely what generates the regress, then the desiderata themselves encode the shape of the problem — and that is the part I most want to see.

Optionally, include a brief scorecard of how the main families of response (rule-circular justification, inferential externalism, constitutive-role accounts, scepticism about the project) fare against the list, but the desiderata and their tensions are the main deliverable.
