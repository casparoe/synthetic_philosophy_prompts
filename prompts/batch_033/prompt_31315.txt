I'm taking a metaphysics course this semester and we've been slogging through material constitution — the statue and the clay, the Ship of Theseus, Tibbles losing his tail, the whole family of puzzles. I thought I had a grip on it until my prof drew an argument on the board that I've been turning over in my head for a week. It feels like it proves too much, but every time I try to pin down the mistake, I end up somewhere I don't want to be. Here's the argument as I reconstructed it from my notes:

(1) On Monday, there's a lump of clay — call it Lumpl — sitting on the workbench. It's just a formless blob; no statue yet.
(2) On Tuesday morning, the sculptor molds Lumpl into a statue of Goliath. So now there's both the lump and the statue ("Goliath") on the workbench.
(3) At every moment on Tuesday, Lumpl and Goliath are made of exactly the same matter, arranged in exactly the same way, and occupy exactly the same region of space.
(4) If two material things are composed of the same matter and occupy the same place at the same time, then they are identical — one thing, not two.
(5) So Lumpl = Goliath.
(6) But Lumpl has a property that Goliath lacks: Lumpl existed on Monday, while Goliath did not. The statue came into existence only when the clay was shaped; before that, there was no statue.
(7) Leibniz's Law (the indiscernibility of identicals): if a = b, then any property of a is a property of b, and vice versa.
(8) So Lumpl ≠ Goliath.
(9) Contradiction: (5) says they are identical, (8) says they are not.

The way I see it, the options are: deny (4) and accept that two distinct objects can coincide in the same place at the same time, deny (7) and abandon Leibniz's Law, deny (2) and say that either the lump or the statue isn't really a thing, or find some sneaky equivocation I'm missing. I'm especially reluctant to give up (4) — the idea of two distinct material objects sitting in the exact same spot at the same time just feels like magic. But I also don't want to touch Leibniz's Law, since that's basically the definition of what identity is.

I'd love for you to act as an expert metaphysician and walk me through this carefully. Don't just summarize the standard views — I've read the intro material. I want to know where this argument actually goes wrong, if it does, or if it's valid then which premise is genuinely the least costly to give up and what you have to live with if you do. Start by laying out the candidates before you commit, and think through what each move really costs.
