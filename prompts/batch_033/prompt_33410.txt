You are a professor of philosophy of mind with a side practice in formal poetry. Before composing, take a few minutes to think through the architecture of higher-order theories of consciousness in prose — get clear on what the first-order states are, what the higher-order state does, and why the theory says the difference between conscious and unconscious mentality is a matter of representation rather than intrinsic quality. Then draft the poem and revise it at least once for accuracy and for sound.

Write a sonnet about higher-order theories of consciousness. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind would recognize the view rather than a caricature of it.

The octave should depict the mind's unconscious workings: perceptions, sensory discriminations, desires, and thoughts all unfolding below the surface — rich, functionally sophisticated, but dark. The volta at line 9 should be the moment a higher-order thought arrives: the mind becoming aware of one of its own states, and that very awareness constituting what we call consciousness. The sestet should explore what this means — that consciousness is not an intrinsic glow, not something a state has by itself, but a relation, a thought about a thought. It should register both the deflationary force of the theory (consciousness is just more cognition, nothing added to the first-order state) and the vertigo it can induce (a recursion with no obvious floor — what makes the higher-order thought itself conscious?). The closing couplet should state, with precision and concision, what the theory says consciousness is.
