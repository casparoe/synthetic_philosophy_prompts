I sit on my high school's Honor Council, and we are about to vote on a case that has split the Council down the middle. I need to draft the written opinion I will circulate before our meeting on Friday.

The facts are not in dispute. A junior, M., copied roughly a third of a history research paper from an online source without attribution. M. admits this fully. Our school's Academic Integrity Policy states: "Any student found to have committed plagiarism shall receive a failing grade in the course and shall be suspended for one academic semester." The policy contains no provision for mitigating circumstances, no sliding scale, and no language about intent.

The circumstances, however, are these: M.'s mother was hospitalized for the two weeks before the paper was due, with a serious condition that required surgery. M. was working evening shifts at a grocery store to help cover household expenses during that period. M. has no prior disciplinary record of any kind and a GPA above 3.8. Before the teacher even noticed the plagiarism, M. approached the teacher, confessed, explained the situation, and submitted a completely new paper written from scratch, asking only that it be considered for a late grade. The teacher brought the case to the Council only because the policy requires reporting all plagiarism, and the teacher has submitted a letter urging leniency.

Half the Council argues that the rule is clear, that it was democratically approved by the student body and faculty, and that making an exception here — however sympathetic the case — would mean we are substituting our own judgment for a rule we are charged with applying. They say that once we start carving out exceptions, the policy loses its force, and future cases will turn on which students can tell the most compelling story.

The other half argues that applying the rule mechanically to this case would be unjust in a way the rule's authors never intended. The policy, they say, was written to deter students who cheat cynically to gain an advantage — not to punish a student who, under extraordinary strain, made a bad decision, immediately owned it, and did the work properly without being asked. To suspend M., they say, would be to treat a rule as an idol rather than as a tool for justice.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — whether to impose the full penalty, impose a reduced penalty (specify what), or impose no penalty — with a time frame if relevant. It should give reasons for the recommendation, and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it.
