OK so I've been thinking about the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem a lot lately and I want to try out an argument on you. The standard line is that G-S is this devastating result — it shows that every non-dictatorial voting rule with at least three candidates is manipulable, i.e., there's always some situation where someone can get a better outcome by lying about their preferences. And everyone treats this as bad news. Strategy-proofness is held up as this obviously desirable property that we tragically can't have, right up there with Arrow's IIA.

But I think this is backwards. I want to argue that strategy-proofness is not actually desirable, and that the possibility of strategic voting is a feature, not a bug. Specifically, strategic voting is the only way for voters to express preference intensity in an ordinal voting system. If you make a rule strategy-proof, you're also making it incapable of distinguishing between "I slightly prefer A to B" and "I would do literally anything to prevent B." And that's a huge loss — maybe bigger than whatever we gain by eliminating manipulability.

Here's the core of the argument. Take any strategy-proof voting rule. By definition, no voter can ever improve the outcome (from their perspective) by misreporting their preferences. This means the rule must be, in a certain sense, unresponsive to the difference between a voter who cares intensely about some pairwise comparison and one who barely cares at all. Because if the rule were responsive to that difference — if it gave more weight to intense preferences — then a voter with mild preferences could just report intense ones and get more weight. Strategy-proofness rules that out. So strategy-proofness forces a kind of flattening: all preference orderings are treated as if they represent equally strong preferences, because the rule can't peek behind the ordinal curtain to see which ones burn and which ones are shrugs.

But in the real world, preference intensity matters enormously. If 51% of people slightly prefer candidate A and 49% would be devastated by A winning, most people (I think) believe the 49% should win. A strategy-proof rule can't capture this at all — it has to treat the 51%'s mild preference as exactly as weighty as the 49%'s intense one. Strategic voting, by contrast, lets the 49% do things like vote as a bloc, coordinate tactically, or incur costs to signal that their preference is stronger. The fact that they're willing to bear the cognitive and social costs of strategic coordination is itself a costly signal of intensity. You can't fake caring a lot by voting strategically if voting strategically is hard.

So when G-S tells us that all non-dictatorial rules are manipulable, maybe we should read this as: all non-dictatorial rules permit the expression of preference intensity through strategic behavior. And that's good! A rule that was truly strategy-proof would be a rule that forced everyone to vote as if all their preferences were equally strong, which would systematically disadvantage those with intense preferences on specific issues — exactly the people we usually think should have more say on those issues.

The obvious objection is that strategic voting doesn't just communicate intensity — it also lets sophisticated voters manipulate outcomes at the expense of unsophisticated ones. The 49% who care intensely might win not because their preferences are genuinely stronger but because they're better at game theory, or better organized, or wealthier. And that's an equity problem, not a feature.

My reply: this is a real concern, but it's a concern about unequal strategic competence, not about manipulability per se. The solution isn't to chase the impossible dream of strategy-proofness (which G-S tells us you can't have anyway without dictatorship) — it's to level the playing field through deliberation, information, or by using rules where the strategic incentives are simple enough that everyone can figure them out. Approval voting, for instance, has a much simpler strategic landscape than ranked-choice. But more fundamentally, the intensity-signaling function of strategic voting is valuable enough that we should try to preserve it while mitigating the inequity, rather than eliminating the signal altogether.

So where does this leave us with the theorem? G-S is still a real constraint — it tells us something true and important about the space of possible voting rules. But instead of reading it as "we can't have the nice thing we want," we should read it as "any voting rule responsive to more than one person's preferences will necessarily permit strategic behavior, and this permeability is precisely what allows preference intensity to enter the system at all." The theorem isn't a tragedy; it's a structural insight about how collective decision-making necessarily works when you only record ordinal rankings.
