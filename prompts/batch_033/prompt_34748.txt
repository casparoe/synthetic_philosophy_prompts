I keep running into a claim that stops me short: that Solomonoff induction just is Occam's razor, finally made mathematically precise. The story goes like this: Occam's razor says to prefer simpler theories. Solomonoff gives us a formal measure of simplicity — Kolmogorov complexity, the length of the shortest program that outputs the data. Then he proves that an agent who assigns prior probability 2^{-K(h)} to each hypothesis h will, in the limit, make optimal predictions no matter what reality is like. So the conclusion people draw is that the razor has been vindicated: simplicity isn't just a pragmatic rule of thumb, it's been proven to track truth.

I want to know how far this analogy holds. What exactly maps onto what between the pre-theoretic razor and the formal machinery? Where does the mapping break? I can see that Kolmogorov complexity depends on your choice of reference machine (up to an additive constant) and that the whole thing is uncomputable, but I don't know whether these are surface-level disanalogies or whether they cut against the conclusion that the razor has been mathematically justified. Please give me the correspondence table, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that don't, and tell me whether a better analogy would be something else. Also, please say how confident you are in each major claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
