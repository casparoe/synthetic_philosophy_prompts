I'm to be best man at the wedding of my oldest friend next month, and I am quietly dreading the speech. He and I met in our first week of a metaphysics MPhil, bonded over a shared horror at the state of the undergraduate teaching on persistence, and have been arguing ever since. He is a thoroughgoing endurantist: he holds that a person is wholly present at every moment at which she exists, that there are no such things as temporal parts, and that when he makes a vow he makes it with the whole of himself. I am a perdurantist, and have been for as long as either of us can remember. On my view, what we call a person at a moment is a temporal slice of a larger four-dimensional object — a spacetime worm — and identity across time is not the simple thing he takes it to be. This is not an abstract disagreement for us; it has shaped how we talk about loyalty, regret, self-improvement, and, more than once, whether it made sense for him to stay angry at me for something my earlier temporal part did in 2011.

The room will be a proper mix. About a dozen of the guests are philosophers of time and metaphysics who will catch every conceptual move I make and will know exactly what I am refusing to say. The other eighty-odd guests are family — his mother, her grandmother, cousins who run pubs and teach primary school — who have never heard the word 'endurantism' and who have come to witness two people pledge their lives to each other. I want to give a speech that is warm, funny, and genuinely celebratory, that lands for both halves of the room, and that does not feel like a man trying to smuggle a metaphysics seminar into a wedding reception. But I cannot, in good faith, say the things best men are supposed to say: that he is the same man now as the boy she fell in love with eight years ago, that they will grow old together as two enduring selves moving side by side through time, that their love has been one continuous thing from the start. Every one of those claims, on my view, is either false or badly in need of a paraphrase I cannot give in a five-minute speech. Please write the toast — roughly five minutes when spoken — in a way that honours the friendship and the occasion without ever asking me to say something I believe to be untrue.
