At a recent workshop two of my colleagues got into an argument about the absent-minded driver problem, and neither could convince the other; I would like a careful adjudication. The case, as they set it up, is this. A driver plans to take the second exit on a highway. Exiting at the first exit is worthless (payoff 0), exiting at the second is what he wants (payoff 4), and missing both exits leaves him late but not stranded (payoff 1). The difficulty is that he is absent-minded in a specific sense: while driving he cannot tell the two exits apart and cannot remember whether he has already passed the first. He therefore cannot carry out the plan of continuing at the first and exiting at the second; whatever probability of exiting he uses, he must use the same probability at every exit. Call it p. Before setting out, his expected payoff is (1-p)(1+3p), which is maximized at p = 1/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3.

Rao argued that this settles the matter: the best feasible plan is to exit with probability one-third at every exit, and since no other plan does better from the outset, that is what a rational driver would do.

Chen disagreed. Once the driver is actually at an exit, she said, he has information he did not have when planning: he is at an exit now. If he had been planning to use probability p, then conditional on being at an exit the probability that he is at the first exit is 1/(2-p), and the probability that he is at the second is (1-p)/(2-p). Given those beliefs he should ask which continuation probability p' is best from here on. If he is at the first exit, adopting p' yields (1-p')(1+3p'); if he is at the second, it yields 1+3p'. Conditional on being at an exit, then, the value of p' is proportional to (1+3p')(2-p-p'), whose maximum is at p' = (5-3p)/6. For p = 1/3 this is p' = 2/3, not 1/3, and the driver would strictly prefer continuing with probability 2/3 to continuing with probability 1/3. So the ex ante plan is not self-supporting once the driver conditions on being at an exit.

Rao replied that this replanning is incoherent when followed through, because the same reasoning applies at every exit. If the driver reoptimizes from 1/3 to 2/3, the beliefs induced by 2/3 make the best continuation 1/2, then 7/12, and so on until the process settles at p = 5/9. But p = 5/9 gives an ex ante expected payoff of only 32/27, strictly below the 4/3 obtained from p = 1/3. So the in-the-moment reasoning, consistently applied, leaves the driver with a strategy he would have rejected before starting the car. That, Rao said, is a reductio of treating the exit-time perspective as authoritative.

Chen answered that the reductio shows only that the two perspectives genuinely conflict; it does not show that the planning perspective wins. The driver is not bound by his earlier self once he has new information, and being at an exit now is new information even though he always knew he would be at an exit eventually. If rationality means maximizing expected value conditional on current information, then the exit-time reasoning is the correct one, and the interesting conclusion is that no single probability is optimal both before setting out and at every exit.

Rao said this cannot be right, because the supposed new information is only indexical. The driver knew with certainty that he would be at an exit at least once; learning that he is at an exit now adds no fact about the world. If it changes his probabilities, that is only because he is treating the two exits as distinguishable while also admitting that he cannot tell them apart. Either he can condition on which exit he is at, in which case the memory assumption has been dropped, or he cannot, in which case the ex ante plan is all there is to optimize.

Chen replied that the information is not which exit he is at but that he is at an exit, and that this genuinely shifts the weight between the two possibilities: conditional on being at an exit, the first exit is more likely than the second, precisely because if he were at the second he might already have exited at the first. That asymmetry is generated by his own strategy, and forbidding him to use it would rule out sequential reasoning in any problem in which the agent does not know the time.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each move, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right and why; flag any mathematical or conceptual errors; then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
