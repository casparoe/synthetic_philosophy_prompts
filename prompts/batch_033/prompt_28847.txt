I retired from a career in experimental physics a few years ago and have been spending my newfound free time reading philosophy of mind, which I'd always wanted to do but never had the bandwidth for. I've worked my way through the consciousness literature — Chalmers, Dennett, Frankish, Goff, and others — and the debate that's really gotten under my skin is the one between illusionists and realists about phenomenal consciousness. I think I understand the basic positions: the realist says there are subjective qualitative properties — qualia, the "what-it's-like"-ness of experience — that are real features of the world and that pose a special explanatory challenge for physicalism; the illusionist says phenomenal consciousness in that sense doesn't exist at all — we have introspective representations and reactive dispositions that we systematically misdescribe as a private qualitative realm. But I'm finding it hard to locate the precise point where the two sides are talking past each other, and I suspect there's more than one such point.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between an illusionist and a phenomenal realist that pushes past the familiar opening moves. Both characters should be sharp and charitable — neither should be a strawman. The dialogue should be structured around a few specific pressure points. For instance: (1) The realist should press the illusionist to explain what an "illusion of phenomenality" could even be, given that an illusion is something that seems a certain way to a subject, and the seeming already appears to involve the very qualitative character the illusionist wants to deny. The illusionist in turn should be forced to say whether the state of being under the illusion itself has any phenomenal character, and if not, what makes it count as an illusion rather than just a misdescribed functional state. (2) The illusionist should press the realist on what it would mean for phenomenal properties to be real in a physical world — are they identical to physical properties (and if so, why does the identity seem so deeply non-obvious in a way that other theoretical identities do not), or are they something over and above the physical (and if so, how does that work without dualism)? (3) At some point the dialogue should grapple with the meta-problem: why we are so strongly disposed to believe we have phenomenal consciousness, and whether the illusionist can explain that disposition without inadvertently relying on the very thing they are denying. I want the characters to genuinely respond to each other's moves rather than delivering set speeches, and I'd like the dialogue to end not with a resolution but with each side having been forced to say clearly what they think the other is missing and what would count as evidence against their own view.
