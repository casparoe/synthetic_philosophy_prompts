I am a journalist who covers philosophy and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on why some people's moral criticism lands while other people's does not. You are a moral philosopher who works on blame and responsibility, helping me think it through. I want the specification before the position: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of standing to blame must meet, where the purpose is to make sense of blame in public moral argument. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the full range of cases and candidate requirements before you prune them into a final list.

The theory must handle at least the following cases and say what it must get right in each: the hypocrite who condemns conduct they routinely engage in; the complicit party who enabled, encouraged, or profited from the wrong; the wholly uninvolved third party; the victim; and the repentant wrongdoer. It must respect the distinctions that matter: between a blame-judgment's truth or warrant and the blamer's entitlement to voice it; between lacking standing and having standing while blaming badly (too harshly, too publicly, for the wrong reasons); and between the blamer's own moral record and the blamer's relationship to the wrong or the person wronged. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp, such as relationship-based accounts versus clean-hands accounts. If you suspect that some pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, a clean-hands condition alongside the victim's retained standing — say so. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise attractive accounts, and close with a very brief scorecard of the main views: standing as the victim's exclusive right, standing as a matter of the blamer's own record, and standing as a matter of the relationship.
