I'm a legislative aide for a state senator, and I'm the one who reads the briefing packets that advocacy groups, industry lobbyists, and sometimes our own staff hand us before a vote. More and more of these packets cite heritability numbers — "educational attainment is about 40% heritable," "divorce is heritable," that kind of thing — and then slide in the same paragraph to a policy conclusion: so the schools can't close the gap, so this program won't move the needle, so its mostly the parents, not the schools. I've read enough to know that these numbers get misused constantly, but not enough to explain why, on the spot, to a senator who wants a yes/no.

I need a reusable procedure I can run on any heritability claim in about half an hour, before I brief him. This comes up a few times a month, and the stakes are real votes and a public record, so the procedure has to be defensible, not vibes. I don't have a statistics background, so the questions have to be plain-language, but I don't want the dumbing-down to lose the distinctions that actually do the work — I know there's something about within-population variance versus causes in an individual, and something about heritability depending on the range of environments, and I know a number being high doesn't mean an intervention is pointless and a number being low doesn't mean a specific program will work. Write the procedure as an ordered checklist: the questions to ask, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where I stop with "this number doesn't license that conclusion," and where I have to hand it to an actual geneticist or methodologist because the step is judgment dressed up as arithmetic. Be honest about where the checklist will misfire, and say explicitly what it cannot do.

Then run the procedure on three worked examples:

1. A school-choice group says a cognitive measure is 60% heritable in adults, so a state preschool program can't reduce gaps, and we should cut the program.
2. A mental-health group says depression is about 40% heritable, so screening and prevention are a waste unless we go after "the biology."
3. Our own office hands me a briefing saying a trait is only about 20% heritable, so it's mostly environmental, so the after-school program we like will close the gap.

Make sure at least one of the worked examples comes out against the briefing that's using the number — including, if that's what the procedure says, against our own office's briefing.
