You're an applied mathematician who works on deterministic chaos and has also had to write operational guidance for forecasters who don't have time for theory. Think carefully, and before you commit to a procedure, brainstorm the distinct ways a deterministic forecast can go wrong and what each one implies about whether it's responsible to issue a single number.

I'm a senior hydrologist at a regional flood forecasting agency. We run deterministic rainfall–runoff and river-routing models to produce water-level forecasts, and we're under constant pressure from emergency managers to put one number on things: "where will the river crest?" But the models, while deterministic, are chaotic in practice. Tiny differences in the initial soil-moisture state or in rainfall estimates — well within measurement error — produce completely different forecasts by day 3 or 4, and sometimes the spread collapses again. I suspect there's a principled distinction between the timescale where a single deterministic forecast is meaningful and the timescale where we should be issuing ranges, ensembles, or scenario-based guidance, and that it has something to do with Lyapunov times and predictability horizons. But I've never seen that turned into something a duty forecaster could actually run at 2 a.m.

Please write me a decision procedure my forecasters can run each time a forecast request comes in. It should be a checklist: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "do not issue a single number," and where it hands the decision to a person because the call is about values (say, an acceptable false-alarm rate) rather than dynamics. The constraints: one forecaster on duty, about twenty minutes per request, no time to spin up a new ensemble from scratch, and we may not have a computed Lyapunov exponent or a decent estimate of model structural error for our particular catchment. Say what to do when those numbers arent available, and be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three cases: a 36-hour crest forecast for a town during an ongoing storm; a 7-day inflow outlook for a drought-stricken reservoir where the water manager wants a single volume for release planning; and a 30-day seasonal water-supply outlook. I expect at least one of these to come out as an answer we won't like — probably the 7-day one — so dont soften it. End with a short note on what the procedure cannot settle and where it will misfire.
