For the better part of a decade, I've been following the decision-theory literature on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum. One cluster of problems has generated an enormous amount of discussion with strikingly little convergence: counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. These are supposed to be decisive counterexamples to some class of decision theories, but there's no agreement on which class, or even on what the scenarios are testing. The same small group of people have been refining the same arguments since roughly 2013, and as far as I can tell, nobody has changed their mind in years. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is — not a verdict on which theory is correct.

Let me lay out the terrain. In a counterfactual mugging, a predictor flips a fair coin. On heads, the predictor simulates you facing a choice about whether to pay $100; if the simulation pays, the predictor gives real-you $10,000. On tails, nothing happens. The predictor then tells you the coin was tails. The question is whether you should now pay the predictor $100. Evidential decision theory says yes, because your paying is evidence that your simulation-self paid, which means you're in a world where you get $10,000. Causal decision theory says no, because your paying now doesn't cause the simulation to have paid — the coin was tails, the simulation never ran, and the $10,000 was never coming. Functional decision theory and updateless decision theory complicate the picture by insisting that the right answer depends on the logical relationship between you and the simulation, not on causation or evidential correlation. But the FDT/UDT answer to counterfactual mugging is itself contested, and different formulations of the problem seem to pull in different directions.

Then there's XOR blackmail. A blackmailer writes two letters: one threatening to reveal your secret unless you pay, the other harmless. The blackmailer predicts what you would do upon receiving each letter and sends exactly one — whichever maximizes their payoff. You receive a sealed letter. You don't know which one it is. Should you commit to not paying, thereby ensuring the blackmailer sends the harmless letter? But you've already received the letter; your decision now doesn't affect which one was sent. EDT and CDT give different answers, but the pattern of answers doesn't line up neatly with the counterfactual-mugging pattern, and the FDT/UDT analysis introduces considerations about logical counterfactuals that make the whole thing even less tractable.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about decision theory at all. It's a disagreement about what it means to face a decision problem in the first place — specifically, whether a decision problem is defined by the causal structure of the world, by the agent's information state, or by the logical relations among possible agents and their possible decisions. If that's right, then the impasse is a sign that the question "which decision theory is correct?" is ill-posed until we settle the prior question of what we're doing when we formalize a decision problem. But I'm not confident in that diagnosis.

What kind of disagreement is this? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "decision"? A clash of bedrock intuitions about what counts as a reason for action? A hidden factual question about what kind of agent we are or what kind of world we live in? Or is it a methodological dispute about what a decision theory is supposed to do — predict behavior, prescribe action, or characterize rational agency? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? A new thought experiment that breaks the current stalemate? A formal result? A conceptual distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict? Or should we expect the impasse to persist indefinitely, and if so, why?
