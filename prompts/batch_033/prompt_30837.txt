I'm in a philosophy of perception seminar and we've been going around in circles on the argument from illusion, and I think I've stumbled into a version of it that nobody's addressed directly — it feels like it proves too much and I can't figure out exactly where the trap is.

The standard argument from illusion goes: a hallucination of a dagger is subjectively indistinguishable from veridically seeing a dagger. Since what you're directly aware of in the hallucination isn't a physical dagger, and the two experiences are the same fundamental kind of thing (they share a "common factor"), what you're directly aware of in the veridical case can't be a physical dagger either. It's a sense-datum in both cases. So far so familiar.

Now here's the move that's bugging me. Take your awareness of your own sense-data. You can be wrong about them — you think you're aware of a reddish-orange sense-datum but if you attend more carefully you realize it's really more orange than red, or you think you're having a visual afterimage with crisp edges but it's actually fuzzy. In the mistaken case, it seems to you exactly as if you're aware of a sense-datum with property F, but you're not — there's no such sense-datum in your sensory field. By the exact same reasoning as the original argument: the mistaken introspection is subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical one, so what you're directly aware of in both cases must be the same kind of thing. In the mistaken case you're not aware of a real sense-datum. So in the veridical case you're not aware of a sense-datum either — you're aware of a second-order sense-datum, or a sense-datum-of-a-sense-datum, or whatever we want to call it. And then you can iterate the argument on those, and you get a regress where you're never directly aware of anything at all.

This looks like a reductio of the argument from illusion — it undermines the very posit it was supposed to establish. But I'm not confident the sense-data theorist can't wiggle out, and I'm also not sure the disjunctivist can deploy this without it backfiring, since the disjunctivist's whole move is to reject the "common factor" premise in step one, and if that premise is what's causing the trouble here too then the regress doesn't add any new dialectical force. So: where exactly does this regress argument go wrong? Is there an equivocation on "subjectively indistinguishable" between the perceptual case and the introspective case? Is the "common factor" premise more plausible for introspection than for perception, and if so, does that mean the disjunctivist has to go disjunctivist about introspection too? What does each position in the landscape — sense-data theory, disjunctivism, intentionalism — have to give up to block the regress, and which bullet is cheapest?
