I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about the units of selection problem — whether natural selection acts primarily on genes, on individual organisms, or on groups. We kept circling around the same stock examples (the t-allele in mice, sterile worker castes, the sex ratio) and I came away convinced that the debate would be sharper if we had a single, clean, novel case where the three major frameworks — gene's-eye view, individual-level selection, and multilevel selection — pull in genuinely different directions for the evolution of a trait, and you can watch exactly why they diverge without the case turning into a contrived just-so story. My friend was skeptical that such a case could even be constructed without stacking the deck, and I suspect he may be right, but I'd like to see someone try.

Design a thought experiment in evolutionary biology with the following specifications. The scenario should be biologically plausible: a real or realistic population of organisms, no supernatural interventions, and the trait in question should be something a field biologist could in principle observe, measure, and track across generations. The three frameworks — gene-level selection (of the sort defended by Dawkins or Williams), individual-level selection (of the sort defended by Mayr or the early Gould), and multilevel selection (of the sort defended by Sober and Wilson) — must each predict a different evolutionary outcome for the trait, or at minimum a different account of why the trait spreads, stabilizes, or fails to spread. The divergence must be generated by the structural features of the theories themselves, not by stipulation: you cannot simply declare that the gene's-eye view says one thing and group selection says another; the predictions need to fall out of how each framework operationalizes fitness, identifies the relevant unit of selection, and handles the tension between within-group and between-group dynamics. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning each framework would apply, state clearly what each predicts, and identify which feature of the case is doing the work in making them come apart. Then, as a final step, tell me what a defender of the framework that looks weakest in your scenario would say to resist the conclusion — what theoretical move they would make to bring their prediction back into line, or to argue that the case is not a genuine counterexample but rather a misdescription of what their framework actually commits them to.
