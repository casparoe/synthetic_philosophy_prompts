Analyse the militant suffragette campaign in Britain (roughly 1905 to 1914) — the window-smashing, arson, and hunger strikes carried out by the Women's Social and Political Union under Emmeline Pankhurst — through the lens of theories of political obligation. The question is not whether the tactics were effective, but whether the suffragettes were morally bound to obey laws they had no hand in making.

Begin by laying out the salient facts of the episode: who was excluded from the franchise, what forms of law-breaking they undertook, and how the state responded (force-feeding, the Cat and Mouse Act, imprisonment). Then work through at least three of the major theories of political obligation — for instance, actual consent, fair-play, and a natural-duty or justice-based account — and say what each theory would have to conclude about the suffragettes' obligations. For each theory, identify where it strains: does actual-consent theory have anything to say to people who never consented and were denied the chance to? Does fair-play theory assume a cooperative scheme from which the suffragettes were in fact excluded as political participants? Does a natural-duty-to-support-just-institutions account treat the Edwardian state as just enough to trigger the duty, and if so, what does 'support' require when the institution is unjust in a specific, remediable way?

Then turn the analysis around: what does the episode reveal about the theories themselves? Does it expose a gap that none of them handle well — for instance, the position of a class of people who are inside the legal order but outside the political one? Does it suggest that political obligation is conditional on inclusion in a way that the standard theories obscure? If one concludes that the suffragettes were justified in disobedience, does that justification come from within any of the theories, or does it require a supplementary principle — a right to resist exclusion — that the theories do not themselves supply?
