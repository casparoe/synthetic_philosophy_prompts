I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity, and I keep coming back to Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a kind of test case. For those who don't know the novel: it's narrated by Stevens, an aging English butler who's spent his whole life serving Lord Darlington with what he calls "dignity" — total identification with the role, never letting a personal self show through. He takes a road trip and revisits memories, and it becomes clear that Darlington was a Nazi appeaser and that Stevens passed up a real human relationship with the housekeeper Miss Kenton because he couldn't step out of the butler persona.

Here's what I want to think through. On Dennett's view, the self isn't a thing in the brain or a Cartesian res cogitans — it's an abstract object like a center of gravity, posited to make sense of the narrative we (and others) tell about a human life. There's no "real self" underneath the story; the story is all there is. Stevens seems like he could be Dennett's poster child: he has literally constructed himself through a narrative — the narrative of the great butler — and that narrative is what his self is.

But the novel is devastating precisely becuase we feel Stevens *missed* something. He failed to be a self in some fuller sense. There's a famous scene where Miss Kenton tries to take a book from him and he won't let her, and the tension is all about what he's refusing to let himself feel. If the self is just the narrative, what exactly did he miss? Is the novel pointing at something Dennett's view can't capture — the difference between a narrative that tracks and responds to experience versus one that suppresses it? Or can a Dennettian say: look, Stevens's narrative was a bad one, it impoverished his life, but there's no further fact about a "true self" he betrayed — just a worse story rather than a better one?

I'd love a reading that takes both the novel and Dennett seriously, doesn't treat the novel as just an illustration, and flags where the novel might be smuggling in a more substantive notion of selfhood that Dennett would reject. Bonus if you can talk about the ending — Stevens sitting on the pier at Weymouth, resolving to practise bantering — and whether that counts as a new narrative or just a repair job on the old one.
