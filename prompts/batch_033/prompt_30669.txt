I'm not an academic — I just fell down an internet rabbit hole about s-risks a while back, and now I've got an argument I can't get out of my head. It seems to show that taking s-risks seriously makes every possible action wrong, which can't be right. But I can't find the step to drop.

By an s-risk I mean the kind of outcome people worry about with future technologies: a huge number of minds — maybe digital ones — suffering enormously, on a scale that makes all the suffering in human history look small.

Here's the argument.

(1) If an action has a real chance — a chance worth taking seriously — of causing an s-risk, then the sheer scale of the possible suffering makes that action wrong.

(2) Every action you could take has some chance, however tiny, of causing an s-risk, through a chain of knock-on effects nobody can predict — a conversation changes someone's career, which changes a research programme, and so on.

(3) So every action has a real chance of causing an s-risk. After all, when the stakes are that high, any chance is worth taking seriously.

(4) So every action is wrong.

(5) This includes the actions people take to reduce s-risks: working on AI safety, writing about the problem, building institutions to prevent it. Each of those could backfire and cause the very thing it was meant to prevent, so they're wrong too.

(6) But not acting is also a choice, and it too could set off a chain that ends in an s-risk, so it's wrong as well.

(7) So the view says every option is wrong. A principle that condemns every option — including the option of following it — can't be a good principle. So one of the steps above must be bad. I'd like to keep (1), because without something like it s-risks don't seem to matter more than any other risk, but maybe that's a seperate issue. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
