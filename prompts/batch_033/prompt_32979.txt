I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and two of my cohort got into an argument about teleosemantics that's been running for like three days. The question is whether teleosemantics can actually solve the disjunction problem.

Quick background: teleosemantics says the content of a mental representation is whatever it's the biological function of that representation to indicate. So a frog's fly-detector means FLY because its evolutionary function is to detect flies, even if it sometimes fires for bee-bees. The disjunction problem is: why does it mean FLY rather than FLY-OR-BEE-BEE, given that it fires for both? The teleosemanticist answers: because the function was selected for fly-detection, and bee-bees are just noise the system wasn't designed for.

Here's where they disagree:

Sam says this doesnt work. The function of a mechanism is always underdetermined by its evolutionary history. The frog's detector was selected because it correlated with flies in the ancestral environment. But it also correlated with "small dark moving things crossing the visual field at a certain velocity." Why privilege one description over the other? The selection story can't discriminate between them unless you already have a notion of what the mechanism is "supposed" to detect, which is exactly what you're trying to naturalize.

Leah says Sam is demanding too much. Natural selection doesn't need to "discriminate" between descriptions — it just operates on the actual causal properties of the mechanism. The mechanism was selected because flies caused fitness-relevant outcomes, not because "small dark moving things" in the abstract did. The function is determined by the actual causal history, not by how we describe it. The disjunction problem is an artifact of thinking about functions at the wrong level of description.

Sam came back: but that's circular. You're saying the function is determined by the actual causal history — but "actual causal history" is itself description-relative. When you say "flies caused fitness-relevant outcomes," you're already carving up the world in intentional terms. The whole point of naturalizing content is to avoid that. If you help yourself to fly-individuated causal histories, you've built content into the foundation.

Leah replied that this is just a general problem for any causal explanation, not a special problem for teleosemantics. Every causal claim involves carving up the world, but that doesn't make causation mind-dependent. The fact that we describe the cause as "flies" rather than "small dark things" doesn't mean the causal relation itself is description-dependent. The frog's ancestors ate flies, full stop.

They're now at an impasse and both think the other is begging the question. Can you adjudicate this? Specifically: (1) Is Sam right that the disjunction problem survives the teleosemantic move to evolutionary functions? (2) Is Leah's appeal to "actual causal history" question-begging, or is Sam's demand for description-independent function individuation unreasonable? (3) Is there a way to break this deadlock, or does it just recapitulate a deeper problem about naturalizing normativity?
