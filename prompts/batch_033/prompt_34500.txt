I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of medicine, and one of my colleagues — a philosopher of biology with unusually systematic views about disease concepts — has been serving as an outside discussant. Over the semester, she has offered crisp, unhesitating verdicts on a series of cases we have put to her, always with the same instruction: "Is this a disease? Answer yes or no, and set aside questions about whether it is a disability, an injury, a risk factor, or a syndrome — just disease, strictly speaking." Here are the cases and her answers:

1. Bacterial pneumonia in an otherwise healthy thirty-year-old: disease.
2. Pregnancy in a healthy woman: not a disease.
3. Adult-type lactose intolerance in a population where only 10% of adults retain lactase production: disease.
4. Adult-type lactose intolerance in a population where 90% of adults are lactase non-persistent: not a disease.
5. ADHD in a nine-year-old boy who is failing third grade: disease.
6. Presbycusis (gradual, bilateral age-related hearing loss) in a seventy-year-old: not a disease.
7. Fibromyalgia (chronic widespread pain, fatigue, and cognitive difficulties with no identifiable peripheral tissue damage): disease.
8. Exclusive same-sex attraction in an adult who is entirely comfortable with it: not a disease.

Next week we are adding a ninth case: congenital anosmia — being born without a sense of smell — in a forty-year-old who reports no distress about it, has adapted fully, and does not regard it as an impairment. I want to predict her verdict before she gives it. I am less interested in the bare yes or no than in the rule or principle she appears to be applying across these eight cases, which other rules fit her verdicts equally well, and which of those rules would yield different answers on the anosmia case. If you can identify a further case — ideally one we could present to her in the final session — that would cleanly discriminate between the remaining candidate rules, I would be especially grateful.
