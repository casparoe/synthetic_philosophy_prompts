I am a retired planetary scientist. I spent most of my career on the geology of icy moons and Mars, and since retiring I have been reading environmental ethics and the philosophical literature on the ethics of space settlement. I am not a trained philosopher. I now serve on the standing review panel of a national space agency, where we decide whether a proposed activity beyond Earth orbit may proceed when it could alter, contaminate, or irreversibly change an extraterrestrial environment. The panel meets roughly every two months and handles ten or twelve proposals at a sitting: robotic landers, melt probes through ice shells, sample-return missions, in-situ resource utilization trials, and, increasingly, private proposals for settlement and for ecopoiesis experiments. The decisions matter because they are often irreversible, because they can permanently foreclose scientific questions, and because they may harm or destroy whatever is actually there. Each proposal arrives as an engineering summary plus a microbiological risk assessment, and the committee discusses it for about forty minutes. The other members are engineers, a microbiologist, and a lawyer; none of them have read any of the ethics. The director signs off on our recommendation and almost never overrules it.

I want a reusable decision procedure for this panel, not a one-off judgment. It should turn a precautionary, non-interference stance toward other worlds into something with structure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The agency's existing guidance is written to protect the science, not the environments themselves; I want the procedure to be explicitly ethical, so that it can say a proposal is wrong even when it would not ruin any future experiment.

The procedure will have to handle several distinctions without collapsing them. One is the difference between harming an organism and merely destroying our chance to learn about it. Another is the difference between a living target and a lifeless but scientifically or aesthetically significant one. A third is the difference between contamination that is reversible or local and alteration that is effectively permanent. It also needs a policy for how to treat absent evidence of life, for how much weight to give to scientific knowledge as a justification, and for asking who bears the risk and who receives the benefit, including cases where a private party is trying to commit the whole species to a terraforming trajectory. Please be explicit about which steps are genuine judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say where the procedure is likely to misfire.

Then run the procedure on two proposals. The first is a robotic mission to melt through the ice shell of Europa, with terminal sterilization of the probe but no way to guarantee that zero viable terrestrial organisms are delivered; the scientific goal is to detect life. The second is a private consortium's small field trial that would release engineered cyanobacteria at a single Martian site to test whether they can survive, explicitly as a first step toward altering the atmosphere. Choose at least one of these so that the procedure returns an answer I am unlikely to welcome, and tell me plainly when that happens. The procedure must be usable in the room by people who cannot look anything up; it should not rest on facts we do not have, and it should tell us which missing facts would change the recommendation.
