I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, so I'm used to the feeling of an argument that seems airtight until someone locates the one load-bearing premise. Here is one I keep failing to disarm.

A friend summarized Dennett's view as follows: the self is not an extra substance, and not a thing located somewhere in the brain, but something like a center of gravity — an abstraction over the distributed facts of a person's life, useful for keeping track of a story without adding new causal machinery. Then he ran this argument:

1. If the self is a center of narrative gravity, then it is an abstraction over the underlying facts of a person's history and dispositions, not a concrete item in the causal order.
2. An abstraction like a center of gravity does no causing: the mass distribution does the causal work, and the center of gravity merely summarizes that distribution.
3. So, on this view, the self does no causing; nothing happens because of anyone's self.
4. If the self does no causing, then ordinary self-citing explanations — I raised my hand because I decided to, she apologized out of remorse — are not made true by any self; at best they are useful fictions.
5. But the same reasoning applies to every other abstraction we reason with — corporations, markets, software, arguments, beliefs — since each is an abstraction over more basic goings-on. So either nearly everything above microphysics is a useful fiction, or we need a principled reason why the self is special, and it is not obvious one exists.
6. Therefore the narrative-gravity analogy proves too much: it either makes the self unreal in a way that guts deliberation and responsibility, or it commits us to a sweeping irrealism about all higher-level posits.

I cannot find the exit. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is invalid, I want the exact step and the reading under which it fails — not just the observation that an analogy is not an identity. If it is valid, I want to know which premise is cheapest to give up and what I lose by giving it up. For what it is worth, I would most like to hold onto (4), because I do not want self-talk to turn out to be fiction; but I worry that holding onto it pushes me back into treating the self as a concrete causal part, which seems to be exactly what Dennett is denying. And if the whole task is badly posed — if, say, causal inertness is doing more work than the analogy can support, or my framing smuggles in a false dilemma — tell me that plainly rather than pretending there is a clean answer.
