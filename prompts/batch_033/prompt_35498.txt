I first came across the St. Petersburg paradox a few years ago and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since. For anyone unfamiliar: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads. If the first heads is on flip one, you get two dollars. Flip two, four dollars. Flip three, eight. The payoff doubles each time. The expected value is one dollar from the first outcome, plus one from the second, plus one from the third, and so on without end — infinite. And yet no reasonable person would pay more than perhaps twenty or thirty dollars to play this game. Expected value theory, which seems like the most basic tool for thinking about decisions under uncertainty, gives an answer that no one would follow.

What bothers me is not the paradox itself but the fact that the debate about it has been running for nearly three centuries without converging. The major responses have all been on the table for a long time. Bernoulli's original move was to replace money with utility, arguing that the utility of money rises more slowly than the money itself — logarithmically, say — which makes the expected utility finite. Others note that no real casino has unlimited funds, so the actual expected value of any playable version is bounded. Some argue that we should discount or ignore extremely small probabilities, or that our decision procedures simply aren't built to handle them. Others take the paradox to show that unbounded utility functions are incoherent and that rational decision theory must rule them out. And some treat it as a special case of a broader problem about decisions involving infinities, connected to things like Pascal's wager and the two-envelope problem.

Every one of these responses has been pushed back against. The diminishing-marginal-utility solution doesn't close the issue because you can restate the game in terms of utility directly — construct a payoff schedule whose expected utility is infinite under any unbounded utility function. The finite-bankroll response feels like it sidesteps the conceptual question by appealing to a contingent practical limit. The probability-discounting approaches face the charge of being ad hoc. The bounded-utility response raises the question of where the bound comes from and whether it's just stipulated to avoid the problem.

After reading everything I could find — original papers in translation, blog posts, forum discussions, video essays — I've come to suspect that the impasse isn't really about which solution is correct. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is actually about what the paradox is supposed to be a paradox of. One camp treats it as a problem within decision theory: given that we want a norm of rational choice, what adjustment to expected value theory fixes the bad recommendation? Another camp treats it as a problem about decision theory: the paradox reveals that the whole project of reducing rationality to the maximization of some quantity runs into trouble the moment infinities appear. If I'm right, the two sides aren't answering the same question, and that's why the debate doesn't move.

But I'm not confident in that diagnosis, and I'd like someone who knows the terrain better than I do to either sharpen it, replace it, or tell me why it's wrong. What kind of disagreement is the St. Petersburg debate, at bottom? Is it verbal, substantive, methodological, or something else? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually count as progress — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question — or is this a permanent tension that comes with applying infinite mathematics to finite decision-makers?
