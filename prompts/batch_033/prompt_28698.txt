I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been trying to get a clear picture of a debate that has been running in moral philosophy for decades: whether the relation "better than" is transitive. A colleague at the magazine warned me that the topic would be too abstract for our readers; I suspect the abstractness is exactly the point, but it has forced me to be precise about what the disagreement amounts to. The puzzle is easy to state but hard to shake. One can describe a spectrum of outcomes — a sequence of possible lives, for instance, or a sequence of social states — in which each step from one outcome to the next looks like a clear improvement, yet the first outcome in the sequence does not look better than the last; if anything, the last looks much worse. If "better than" is transitive, this pattern ought to be impossible, because a chain of improvements would have to leave the final outcome better than the initial one. But the stepwise judgments feel undeniable, and the endpoint judgment feels undeniable too, and philosophers have spent a long time trying to decide which of these appearances to abandon.

What has struck me, in reading around the literature and talking to a few of the people involved, is that the dispute seems to have settled into a standoff rather than moving toward resolution. One side treats the transitivity of "better than" as close to a fixed point of practical reason: a nontransitive betterness relation, they argue, cannot guide choice or rank options in the way a value relation is supposed to, so the spectrum cases must be misleading somewhere. The other side treats the spectrum cases as genuine counterexamples and says that preserving transitivity requires denying either that each small step is an improvement or that the endpoint comparison is as it seems, both of which strike them as ad hoc. Each side, as far as I can tell, believes the other is protecting a theoretical commitment at the expense of the examples rather than explaining the examples away.

I have a working hypothesis, but I want it tested rather than confirmed. My suspicion is that the disagreement is not fundamentally about which outcomes are better, and not about any empirical fact. It may be a disagreement about how many distinct notions of "better than" are in play — all-things-considered betterness, betterness in a particular respect, betterness for a person, pro tanto betterness — and about whether a single notion has to do all the work in both the stepwise and the endpoint judgments. If that is right, the debate is partly verbal, or at least partly a dispute about which theoretical role the phrase "better than" is supposed to play. But I can also see a competing diagnosis: that this is a substantive clash between bedrock intuitions, a transitivity intuition on one side and a spectrum intuition on the other, neither of which can be defended from anything more basic. Which diagnosis is more faithful to what the two sides actually say? And on the better diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a sharper distinction, a formal result about what a nontransitive betterness relation can and cannot do, evidence about how people's judgments behave across the spectrum, or something else? Or is this a question that is ill-posed in a way that means no progress should be expected at all?
