I am a novelist, and I keep writing characters into a particular kind of intellectual predicament that I suspect has a name in philosophy, though I have not been able to find it. I would be grateful if you could identify the nearest established concept, or tell me if none fits and help me delineate what is distinctive about it.

Here is the pattern. A character works through a difficult question — say, whether to leave a marriage, or whether a political commitment they have held for years is mistaken — and arrives at a conclusion after careful, sincere reasoning. They can articulate their steps; the argument feels solid to them. Then they acquire a piece of information that does not directly bear on the question itself but bears on their capacity to answer it. Perhaps they learn that they have been sleeping four hours a night for a fortnight and that sleep deprivation of that severity sharply degrades judgement about precisely the kind of emotionally freighted decision they are facing. Or they learn that almost everyone who reasons in the style they used — weighing pros and cons in a certain systematic way, say — gets this particular kind of question wrong, even though no one can point to a specific flaw in the reasoning. The character now faces a conflict. Their first-order reasoning still looks sound to them; they cannot locate an error in it. But they have what feels like excellent reason to distrust the output of that reasoning. They are stuck: they cannot simply set aside their conclusion, because it still seems right, but they cannot comfortably endorse it either, because they no longer trust the machinery that produced it.

I am not asking about ordinary self-doubt or a failure of nerve. The distinctive thing is that the character has specific, credible evidence about the unreliability of their cognitive process on this occasion, and yet the process itself continues to strike them as sound from the inside. The two levels of assessment — the object-level argument and the evidence about the arguer — point in opposite directions, and there is no obvious way to weigh one against the other.

I have come across the phrase "epistemic peer disagreement" in my reading, but that seems to be about two different people disagreeing, whereas here the conflict is internal: the character is divided against their own reasoning. I have also seen "higher-order evidence" mentioned, but that seems to label the type of evidence rather than the predicament itself. Does this state of being caught between one's own reasoning and one's reasons to distrust that reasoning have an established name in epistemology? If no term captures it exactly, I would be grateful for an account of what the nearest concepts are, how my cases differ from them, and what you would propose to call this thing — the name matters less to me than getting the boundaries right.

Please write your answer in British English.
