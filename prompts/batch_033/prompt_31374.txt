I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I keep noticing a pattern in risk debates that I don't have a precise name for. It involves the precautionary principle, but I suspect the pattern is more general.

Here's an instance. Alice argues that gain-of-function virology research should be halted because "if there's a chance of causing a pandemic, we shouldn't do it until we've proven it's safe." Bob points out that field epidemiology also risks causing outbreaks — collecting samples in wet markets, transporting pathogens — and that Alice's stated principle would seem to require halting that too. Alice replies that of course she didn't mean an absolute prohibition; she meant something like "the burden of proof should be proportional to the potential harm, and the proponents of gain-of-function research haven't met it." The conversation moves on. A week later, in a different thread, someone proposes a new geoengineering field trial, and Alice writes: "You can't just experiment with the climate — the precautionary principle says if you don't know it's safe, you don't do it." The strong formulation is back as if the earlier exchange never happened.

The pattern isn't quite motte-and-bailey. In a motte-and-bailey, there's a defensible but boring claim and an exciting but indefensible one, and the arguer retreats to the defensible one under attack. Here, both the strong and weak formulations of the precautionary principle are defensible positions in their own right — there are serious philosophers who defend each. The problem isn't that one is indefensible; it's that the arguer uses whichever formulation favors their current conclusion, and the shift is invisible to them. It's also not straightforward hypocrisy, because the arguer seems to experience themselves as applying the same principle consistently. And it's not ordinary equivocation, because the two formulations genuinely share conceptual content; they aren't homonyms.

What I'm after: Is there an established name for this specific pattern — the motivated oscillation of a principle's strength that tracks whose proposal is being evaluated? Second, if no name exists, how would you characterize it to distinguish it from motte-and-bailey, from isolated demands for rigor, and from the legitimate practice of adjusting a principle's stringency to the stakes? Third, is there something about the precautionary principle in particular that makes it vulnerable to this pattern — perhaps the fact that it bundles an epistemic claim about uncertainty with a normative claim about who bears the burden of proof — or would you expect to see the same pattern with any principle that has both a crisp and a graded formulation? Please answer in American English.
