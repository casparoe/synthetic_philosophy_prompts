I keep coming back to No Country for Old Men — the Coen brothers film — and I can't figure out what it's actually saying about free will. Chigurh acts like he's just an instrument of fate: the coin toss with the gas station guy, the way he talks about the coin traveling twenty-some years to get to that moment. But he also clearly makes choices and seems to enjoy the cruelty of them, which doesn't square with being a mere cog. And then there's Sheriff Bell, who spends the whole film talking like things are going a certain way and he can't stop them, but he still feels responsible and haunted by it. Is the film doing compatibilism? Hard incompatibilism? Or is it showing that the whole distinction between free will and determinism kind of collapses when you're inside a life, trying to live it? I want a reading that takes the film's details seriously — the ending, the dream Bell recounts, the way violence happens offscreen — and doesn't just treat it as a philosophy textbook illustration.
