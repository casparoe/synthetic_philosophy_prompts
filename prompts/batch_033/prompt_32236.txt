So I was at this party last week — one of those things where you end up in the kitchen at 1am because that's where the wine is — and two people I know got into it about the handicap principle. I'm a novelist, not a biologist, but I've read a bit of this stuff for a book I'm working on, and I found their argument genuinely hard to sort out. I'm going to reconstruct it as best I remember, and I'd love for someone to go through it move by move and say who's right about what.

The cast: Miriam is a behavioral ecologist. Leo is a philosopher of science who works on explanation. I'm just the guy refilling glasses.

It started when Miriam mentioned peacock tails as a clear example of the handicap principle. Leo said he's never been convinced the handicap principle actually explains anything — that it might just be a redescription dressed up as a mechanism.

That's not really a move in the argument, just the setup. Here's the actual back-and-forth:

(1) Miriam: "The handicap principle is straightforward. A peacock's tail is metabolically expensive and makes you more visible to predators. Only a male in genuinely good condition can afford to grow and carry one. The peahen can read off his condition from the tail. The cost is what makes the signal honest. Without the cost, low-quality males could just fake it and the signal would collapse."

(2) Leo: "Right, but the cost isn't the thing doing the epistemological work. What's doing the work is the correlation between tail quality and condition. The peahen is using the tail as evidence of condition because they're correlated. The cost is just one way that correlation gets established and maintained. If you could magically enforce the same correlation without the cost — if, I don't know, some god decreed that only healthy males grow nice tails and it was effortless — the signal would be just as informative. So the cost isn't conceptually necessary for honest signaling."

(3) Miriam: "You're describing a world that doesn't exist. The whole point of the handicap principle is that in the actual world, where low-quality individuals have an incentive to deceive, cost is what maintains the correlation. Remove the cost and the correlation unravels, because cheaters invade. The principle is a causal claim about how honesty is stabilized under selection. It's not just 'signals are honest when they're correlated with quality' — that would be trivial."

(4) Leo: "Okay, fair — I'll grant that in signaling games with conflicting interests, differential cost is one way to get an honest equilibrium. But here's my worry. There are plenty of honest signals in nature where the honesty doesn't come from strategic cost at all. Index signals, for instance. A frog's call pitch is constrained by its body size; a big frog literally can't produce a high-pitched call. The correlation is mechanical, not strategic. So the handicap principle isn't the general theory of honest signaling — it's one kind of honest signaling among several. Calling it a 'principle' makes it sound like a general law when it's actually a special case."

(5) Miriam: "I think you're smuggling in a distinction where there isn't one. The frog's call is still costly in the relevant sense — producing a low-pitched call requires the anatomical machinery of a large body, which is a cost you pay over development. The 'mechanical' constraint you're talking about is just the cost measured in a different currency. The handicap principle doesn't require that the cost be a strategic choice; it just requires that the signal be differentially costly to produce depending on the underlying quality. That's true of the frog's call too."

(6) Leo: "Now you're stretching the concept of cost so thin it covers everything. If 'cost' just means 'any correlation between signal and quality exists in virtue of some causal constraint,' then the handicap principle loses all its bite. It's not telling us anything specific. It's saying: honest signals are those that are hard to fake. Which is true, but it's basically a tautology. What I want to know is: what would count as an honest signal that *doesn't* fit the handicap principle? If the answer is 'nothing,' then the principle isn't falsifiable."

(7) Miriam: "That's not true. A signal that's honest purely because of shared interest — say, two members of the same cooperative species signaling about a food source — doesn't require differential cost. There's no incentive to deceive, so the handicap machinery doesn't apply. That's an honest signal outside the principle. The principle applies specifically to situations of conflicting interest, where deception is a live possibility. That's its domain, and within that domain it's genuinely explanatory."

That's where they left it, because someone opened another bottle and the conversation drifted. But I've been turning it over since. I honestly can't tell who has the better of the argument. Leo's point about falsifiability in move (6) seems genuinely worrying, but Miriam's response in (7) seems like it might answer it. And I'm not sure whether Leo's move (4) about index signals is a good objection or whether Miriam successfully deflates it in (5). Can you go through each move and adjudicate — who's right, who's wrong, and what's being missed?
