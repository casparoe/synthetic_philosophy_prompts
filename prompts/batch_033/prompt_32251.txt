I've been in this ethics reading group at my university for about a year now, and we've been stuck on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism for what feels like forever. It's started to matter to me personally because I'm helping design a health resource allocation policy for my province's health authority — basically, we have to decide how to distribute a fixed budget across different patient populations, and the question of whether you can legitimately add up benefits across separate lives is suddenly not an abstract exercise anymore. Every meeting I go to, I hear the ghost of this debate haunting the spreadsheet.

Anyway, the shape of the objection you probably know: utilitarianism aggregates wellbeing across persons as if they were one big container of experience, and the objection says this ignores the fact that persons are separate — my pain and your pain don't merge into some third thing, they stay mine and yours. Rawls made a big deal of this, Nozick too, and Parfit spent a lot of time on it. The weird thing is, everyone in the reading group agrees on the factual claims. We all agree that persons are distinct entities, that utility is experienced within individual lives, and that aggregating across them is a theoretical move, not a description of some unified experience. But half the group thinks this is a devastating objection that makes utilitarianism obviously false, and the other half thinks it's basically a confusion — like saying you can't add up the heights of different mountains because each mountain is separate.

I've started to suspect that this isn't really one disagreement but several, tangled together under the same label. Part of it might be about whether moral reasons have to be agent-relative or can be agent-neutral. Part of it might be about whether the metaphysics of personal identity matters for ethics at all — like, if Parfit is right that personal identity is less deep than we think, does that undercut the objection or strengthen it? And part of it might just be bedrock intuitions about what counts as a moral reason in the first place, which neither side can argue for.

Are the two sides really answering different questions, or do they disagree about what counts as evidence for a moral theory?
