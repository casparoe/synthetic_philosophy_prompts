Take the Greek bailout referendum of July 2015 and analyze it through the lens of populism and the idea of "the will of the people." The basic facts, as I understand them: in late June 2015 the Syriza-led government broke off negotiations with its creditors and called a referendum on their proposed terms; on July 5, about 61 percent voted No on about 62 percent turnout, and the government presented the result as a democratic mandate; within weeks the same government accepted a third bailout program with further austerity, without returning to the voters. Both the government and its critics claimed to be honoring the will of the Greek people.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not the familiar referendum-versus-elites story. Spell out what a populist appeal to "the will of the people" presupposes about who the people are, how their will is known, and what a government should do once it knows that will. Then apply those presuppositions to the actual players, options, and sequence of events: what exactly did the No vote determine, was it one will or several, and what should the government have done by the lights of the populist account? After that, say where the lens explains the episode and where it misdescribes it—in particular, whether a referendum can transparently reveal a unitary popular will, and whether the aftermath is best described as betrayal, as evidence that the will was never unitary, or as something else. Finally, tell me what the episode does to the theory: does it confirm populism's conception of the people's will, expose an anomaly, or show that the conception never fit cases like this?
