I'm trying to get clear on when someone lacks standing to blame — you know, the whole "who are you to talk" thing. Here are my gut reactions on a bunch of cases, and I want to know if there's a principle that gets all or most of them.

1. My friend Alex cheated on his wife for years. Last week he got furious at a coworker who had a one-night affair and called him a selfish bastard. I think Alex has no standing here. That one's easy.

2. But here's a harder one: my sister smoked for a decade, quit five years ago, and now she's on her teenager's case about vaping. I feel like she does have standing, even though she did the same kind of thing. The fact that she quit and regrets it seems to matter.

3. A politician votes for a war that kills thousands, then later condemns another politician for authorizing a drone strike that kills dozens. I want to say he lacks standing — the hypocrisy is too much — but I'm not sure if it's because the wrongs are similar in kind or because his was worse. Or both.

4. Someone who has never stolen anything but knows in their bones they would steal if they were desperate enough blames a shoplifter. My instinct is that this person does have standing — you don't lose standing just for having a bad character you never acted on. But I'm not confident about this one at all.

5. A boss who regularly berates employees in private blames a colleague for yelling at an intern in public. I feel like the boss lacks standing even though the wrongs are different in some obvious ways — one is private, one is public. So maybe the domain matters less than I thought?

6. Here's one that really bothers me: a friend who drove drunk exactly once, twenty years ago, and has been ashamed of it ever since, hears about someone getting a DUI and says nothing publicly but tells me privately the guy is reckless. I think my friend has standing to make that judgment, at least to me. But if he posted about it on Facebook I'd feel differently. So maybe standing is partly about the audience or the forum?

7. Last one: a vegan who used to eat meat blames a meat-eater for supporting factory farming. I think the vegan has standing — the fact that they changed makes the blame more credible, not less. But I can't articulate why this feels different from Alex in case 1.

Is there a single principle that handles all of these? I'm especially stuck on why case 2 (ex-smoker) and case 7 (vegan) feel like they have standing while case 1 (cheater) doesn't, when in all three the blamer did the same kind of thing. And I can't tell whether my verdict on case 4 (the would-be thief) is right or whether I should flip it. Give me the best principle you can, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and test it on a case I haven't thought of.
