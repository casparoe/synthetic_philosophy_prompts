I'm in a philosophy PhD program and my advisor roped me into running a reading group on s-risks next semester. I want to pair some of the technical papers with fiction, and I keep coming back to Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." But I'm worried I'm reaching. In Omelas, the child's suffering is literally the mechanism that sustains the utopia — it's instrumental, everyone knows about it, and the whole society is built on accepting that tradeoff. Whereas s-risk scenarios (at least as I understand them from Bostrom and others) are more about suffering as an unintended side effect or a convergent outcome of competitive dynamics — no one's deliberately torturing anyone to power the lights. Also the scale difference is absurd: one child vs. astronomical numbers of minds. But then again, maybe the story isnt supposed to be a one-to-one analogy — maybe it captures something about the psychology of living with knowledge of distant suffering that the s-risk literature, which is mostly abstract and decision-theoretic, just doesnt get at. I'd love a reading that takes both the story and the s-risk framework seriously and tells me honestly where the comparison is illuminating and where it falls apart. What scenes or details would you point to, and what does Le Guin get right that the papers don't?
