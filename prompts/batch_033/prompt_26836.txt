I've been covering debates in moral epistemology and I've become persuaded by a position I've heard called pessimism about moral testimony: roughly, the view that there is something distinctively defective about forming a moral belief purely on the say-so of another person, even when that person is reliably correct about moral matters. On this view, if I believe that factory farming is wrong solely because a trusted moral philosopher tells me so, my belief lacks something important — perhaps it doesn't count as moral knowledge, or it fails to constitute moral understanding, or it is in some sense not fully my own — in a way that simply doesn't apply when I form beliefs about, say, the mass of the Higgs boson on the basis of expert testimony. The pessimist isn't denying that testimony can give me a true moral belief, or even a justified one; the claim is that something further is missing.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not just the standard counterarguments I've already encountered — that we rely on moral testimony constantly in practice, that the view seems elitist or over-intellectualized — but a systematic map of its exposure. What observations, arguments, thought experiments, or empirical results would a committed pessimist have to reckon with? Which of these would they themselves concede as genuine costs of the view, and which would they dismiss as begging the question? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like an honest assessment of whether the view has been, or could be, qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.
