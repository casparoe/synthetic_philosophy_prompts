Question: "Hume and Reid offer competing accounts of how we acquire justified belief through testimony. Explain each view, identify the strongest objection to each, and assess which view (or combination of views) is most defensible."

Student answer:

"The debate between Hume and Reid concerns whether testimony is a basic source of justification or whether it depends on other sources. Hume defends reductionism: the view that our justification for believing testimony must ultimately reduce to perception, memory, and inductive inference. On his account, when someone tells us something, we are only justified in believing them if we have independently verified that they are reliable — either by checking their track record personally or by having background evidence that people in general tend to tell the truth. The strongest objection to Hume's view is that it seems to lead to skepticism about testimony in cases where we lack such evidence, like when a stranger gives us directions. If we haven't checked their reliability, Hume's view says we shouldn't believe them, which seems overly demanding.

Reid, by contrast, defends anti-reductionism. He argues that testimony is a basic source of justification on a par with perception and memory. Just as we don't need reasons to trust our senses, we don't need reasons to trust what people tell us. Reid grounds this in what he calls the principle of credulity: God has implanted in us a natural disposition to believe testimony, and this disposition is generally reliable. The strongest objection to Reid is that this appeal to God and natural design is no longer philosophically respectable — we can't base an epistemology on theological assumptions in a secular framework.

In my view, neither pure position is fully defensible. Hume is too demanding, but Reid gives us no way to distinguish reliable from unreliable testifiers. A hybrid view like Jennifer Lackey's seems better: testimony provides justification by default, but this justification can be defeated if we have reason to doubt the speaker's sincerity or competence. This preserves the insight that we don't need positive reasons for every instance of testimony while acknowledging that we shouldn't be credulous."
