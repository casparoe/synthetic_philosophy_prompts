My friend has been trying to apply Sartre’s account of bad faith to everyday cases, and she has a pretty clear track record of verdicts. Here are some:

- The café waiter whose performance of being a waiter is a little too precise and polished: bad faith.
- The woman on a first date who lets the man hold her hand but tells herself it’s just a neutral physical thing, ignoring what it means: bad faith.
- A habitual procrastinator who misses a deadline and says, “I’m just an irresponsible person, that’s why this keeps happening,” then uses that as a reason not to change: bad faith.
- A hiker on a cliff path who feels vertigo because nothing actually stops them from jumping: she says not bad faith, just anguish.
- A person who lied once and, when called a liar, says “I did lie, but I’m not a liar; I can choose differently,” and then does choose differently: not bad faith.

New case: someone has repeatedly avoided difficult conversations, and when a friend points out the pattern, they say, “I’m not an avoidant person; those earlier cases were just too stressful, and that’s not who I am.” They make no concrete change.

What would she likely say about this new case? I care less about the bare verdict than about the rule she’s implicitly running. So: which rule or rules best fit her earlier verdicts, which other rules fit nearly as well, and what further case would most cleanly tell them apart? Also, mark where you’re leaning on a contested reading of Sartre rather than something settled, and say how confident I should be in the prediction.
