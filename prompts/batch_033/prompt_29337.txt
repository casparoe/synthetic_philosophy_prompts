I'm applying for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship and need a research proposal of about 1,500 words. My area is metaphysics, and I want to work on origin essentialism — roughly, the thesis that an individual human being could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones they actually came from. The standard arguments for this thesis trace back to Saul Kripke's work on the necessity of origin, and the subsequent literature has largely been a standoff between those who find the modal intuition compelling and those who offer counterexamples: twinning, cloning, embryo splitting, and various puzzle cases meant to show that gametic origin is not strictly necessary for identity.

My half-formed suspicion is that both sides are making the same mistake. They treat "originating from a particular sperm and egg" as if it picks out a single, well-defined candidate essential property, when in fact there are at least three different things one could mean by it — originating from the specific token gametes (the very cells), originating from the genetic material they carry, or originating from the zygote formed by their union (which raises further questions about whether the zygote is individuated by its material constituents or by its spatiotemporal-causal history). These come apart in the standard puzzle cases. In monozygotic twinning, the same zygote gives rise to two people, which looks like trouble for the zygote-origin version but may leave the genetic-origin version untouched. In cloning, genetic material is replicated but the token gametes are not involved, which may trouble the token-gamete version differently. And so on. If I'm right, the debate has seemed intractable because each side is tacitly defending or attacking a different version of the thesis, and the real work is to disambiguate them and ask which versions survive scrutiny and which don't — rather than treating origin essentialism as a single claim that stands or falls together.
