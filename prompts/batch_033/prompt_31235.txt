I am trying to get my views about historical explanation into a shape I can defend at a workshop, and I would like a consistency audit rather than a defense of any one of them. I find myself inclined to accept all of the following, and I cannot tell which of them, if any, has to go. First, I am a determinist about human history in the causal sense: the actual course of events was fixed by the conjunction of antecedent conditions and natural laws, so that, given the past, only one sequence of events was physically possible. Second, I think specific counterfactual claims about history are determinately true or false rather than merely useful or vivid; for example, I am prepared to assert that if the July 1944 attempt on Hitler's life had succeeded, the war in Europe would have ended earlier than it in fact did. Third, I think the language of inevitability is almost always a retrospective projection: when historians say the First World War was inevitable, they are reading the outcome back into the summer of 1914 and ignoring the forks that were live for the actors. I do not think the war was inevitable in any sense that would make the decisions of July 1914 causally inert. Fourth, I think some enormous historical developments turned on small, chancy occurrences, such as the wrong turn taken by Archduke Franz Ferdinand's driver in Sarajevo, and that this is part of why narrative explanation is indispensable. Fifth, I also think the First World War was massively overdetermined by alliance commitments, mobilization schedules, and great-power rivalry, so that the assassination was at most a trigger and a European war of some kind was probable anyway. Sixth, I think covering-law explanation is the wrong model for history: historical explanation is conjunctural and agent-centered rather than subsumptive. Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be so, ordering the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be; for the merely apparent ones, say briefly why they dissolve. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent. I would rather be told that two of these cannot stand together than be given a forced reconciliation. Please write your answer as continuous prose, as if in an email or letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
