In my decision theory reading group last week, I put two choice problems to the members and asked them to pick. The first problem offered:

A: $1,000,000 for certain.
B: an 89% chance of $1,000,000, a 10% chance of $5,000,000, and a 1% chance of $0.

The second problem offered:

C: an 11% chance of $1,000,000, otherwise nothing.
D: a 10% chance of $5,000,000, otherwise nothing.

Mara chose A in the first problem and D in the second. Jonah chose B and D. When they compared answers, neither would let it go. The exchange went something like this:

Jonah: "Your two choices can't be made consistent with any assignment of utilities to the three dollar amounts. Choosing A over B says an 11% chance of a million is worth more to you than a 10% chance of five million. Choosing D over C says the exact reverse. No expected-utility calculation can deliver both."

Mara: "That just means I violate the independence axiom. Whether independence is a requirement of rationality is precisely what's in dispute, so you can't settle the argument by assuming it."

Jonah: "I'm not just assuming it. In each pair, the two options share an 89% branch: A and B both give a million there, and C and D both give nothing there. So the comparison in each pair comes down to the same 11% sub-choice — a certain million versus a 10/11 chance of five million and a 1/11 chance of nothing. You're making that same sub-choice two different ways."

Mara: "The 89% branch isn't inert. In the first problem it makes A a sure thing; in the second it doesn't. A certain million is worth more to me than the branch-by-branch decomposition of A, and you're assuming value separates across mutually exclusive branches. That separability assumption is the independence axiom again."

Jonah: "Then consider what happens when the 89% branch doesn't occur. If you'd chosen A and then learned you were in the 11% branch, you'd be getting a million while your own D-over-C choice says that, conditional on the 89% branch not occurring, you prefer the 10/11 chance of five million to the certain million. You'd reverse your earlier choice without learning anything that distinguished the two options."

Mara: "I would have learned something that matters: the sure thing is gone. Ex ante, A's certainty was exactly what I was choosing. Once I learn the 89% branch didn't occur, I'm no longer choosing between a sure thing and a gamble — the guarantee has already lapsed. There's no inconsistency in valuing the sub-choice one way when it comes attached to certainty and another way when it stands alone."

They asked me to referee, and I couldn't. Please adjudicate the argument move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific points, whether either of them wins overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
