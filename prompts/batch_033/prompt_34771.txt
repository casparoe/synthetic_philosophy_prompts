So I'm in this epistemology course and we just did the Clifford/James debate on the ethics of belief. After class two people from my seminar got into this argument at the pub and I ended up stuck between them trying to follow it. I'm supposed to write a response paper on the topic and honestly hearing them go at it made me more confused, not less. Could you adjudicate their exchange point by point?

Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Dan started by saying Clifford is obviously right about the ship-owner case -- if you believe your ship is seaworthy just because you want it to be, and you suppress your doubts, you're morally culpable when it sinks. And he said the point generalizes: believing without sufficient evidence is always wrong because it corrupts your intellectual character and makes you a danger to others.

(2) Mira pushed back: she said James's whole move is that Clifford's norm only makes sense if you value avoiding error above believing truth. She said there are cases where you have to decide -- you can't just wait for more evidence -- and in those cases, refusing to believe until you have proof is itself a choice that might cost you the truth. She gave the example of deciding whether to trust a new friend: if you hold back all trust until you have ironclad evidence they're trustworthy, you'll never get the evidence because they wont open up to someone who's holding them at arm's length.

(3) Dan said that's a false dichotomy. You can act as if you trust someone -- be open, take risks -- while still proportioning your actual belief to the evidence. You don't have to believe they're trustworthy to act in a trusting way. The epistemic norm and the practical norm come apart.

(4) Mira said James would reject that separation as psychologically unrealistic for the cases he cares about. She said for a "genuine option" -- one that's live, forced, and momentous -- you can't indefinitely suspend judgment while going through the motions. Your passional nature is going to settle the question one way or another, and the real question is whether it's legitimate when it does. She also said that Dan's "act as if" move just smuggles the belief in through the back door: if you consistently act as if someone is trustworthy for years, in what sense do you not believe it?

(5) Dan came back with: fine, maybe we're psychologically incapable of perfect Cliffordian hygiene. But that doesnt make it epistemically right. It just means we're weak. The norm still stands even if we can't fully meet it -- like "don't have biased thoughts" is a norm even if we can't fully comply.

(6) Mira said that's exactly what James anticipated: Clifford treats the question as if it's already settled that we should prioritize avoiding error, but that's the very thing at issue. For forced, momentous options where the evidence underdetermines, James says we have a right to let our passional nature decide -- not in spite of the evidence, but where the evidence can't decide. She said Dan keeps assuming the Cliffordian frame is neutral when it's actually taking one side of James's two-sided epistemic goal.

At that point they ordered another round and changed the subject, but I was left with the sense that both of them had scored points and neither had really landed a knockout.
