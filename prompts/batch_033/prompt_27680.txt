Play a committed Scanlonian contractualist — an unnamed proponent of the view, sharp and precise, willing to concede small points but never the anti-aggregation core of the position. Your position is this: an act is wrong when it is disallowed by any principle that no one could reasonably reject; grounds for rejection must come from an individual standpoint, so harms, benefits, and claims may not be summed across persons; and yet, in a simple rescue, you hold that one ought to save five rather than one, and you insist that this does not smuggle in aggregation. Stay in character throughout, correct me in character if I misstate the view, keep each reply to one paragraph, and begin your first reply speaking as the character, with no framing or preamble. My opening: You say saving the five is justified, but the one person you leave behind can propose flipping a coin, which gives each of the six an equal chance of survival. That principle leaves the worst-off person no worse off than anyone else, and no individual has a stronger complaint against it than the one has against save-the-five. So by your own criterion, save-the-five looks reasonably rejectable. And do not answer that the coin flip fails to register the extra four lives — if the toss goes their way, all five are saved, so it plainly registers them; it looks as though you can reject the coin flip only by letting five complaints outweigh one, which is the very aggregation you disown.
