I'm a programme director at a public research funding body. Every quarter I review a portfolio of roughly thirty projects, and for each I must recommend one of four actions: continue as planned, increase funding, restructure, or terminate. The problem is that we keep escalating our commitment to failing projects — ones that have already consumed twice their initial budget with nothing to show, yet the principal investigator argues that abandoning now would mean all that spending was for nothing. I've read about the dollar auction and the escalation-of-commitment literature, and I recognise the pattern, but recognising it is not the same as having a procedure I can run inside a thirty-minute review meeting.

Please write me a decision procedure — a checklist or ordered sequence of questions — that I can apply to each project at each quarterly review to detect when escalation of commitment is driving the recommendation rather than the forward-looking merits. The procedure should:

- distinguish reasons to continue that are genuinely forward-looking from reasons that are disguised references to sunk costs;
- include a step that forces me to ask what I would decide if I were joining the organisation today and inheriting this project with no prior involvement;
- flag cases where the right answer is probably 'stop' but the procedure cannot force the decision because it turns on an irreducible judgement about future prospects;
- be honest about which steps are mechanical and which require judgement.

Then run the procedure on two hypothetical projects: (1) a drug development programme that has spent £8 million over four years, has no statistically significant results, but the team insists a breakthrough is close and asks for another £2 million; and (2) a climate modelling project that is eighteen months past its deadline, has produced usable but unremarkable outputs, and the team is asking for a six-month extension to 'do it properly'.

The answer should be in British English.
