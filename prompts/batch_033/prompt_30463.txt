I fell down a rabbit hole reading about Quine and the 'gavagai' thought experiment and now I have a pile of beliefs and I want to know if they actually hang together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. A translation is correct when it preserves what the original sentence means, and what a sentence means is a determinate fact about the speaker — there's a right answer to what they meant, even if we can't always recover it.

2. For the gavagai case — a linguist points at a rabbit and a native says 'gavagai' — the right translation is basically 'rabbit' or 'look, a rabbit'. The alternatives Quine floats (undetached rabbit parts, rabbit stages, rabbithood) feel like philosophers tricks; no actual translator would land on those.

3. If two translation manuals make exactly the same predictions about what the speaker will say, point at, or assent to in every possible situation, but one renders 'gavagai' as 'rabbit' and the other as 'undetached rabbit part', at most one of them is right, because they attribute different beliefs to the speaker.

4. The way you figure out which translation is right is by looking at behavior: what the speaker points at, assents to, corrects you on. I dont think there's some extra non-behavioral source of evidence we get to use.

5. Honestly I think the whole thing is a fun toy problem and has basically nothing to teach me about the translation systems I actually build, since real translation has aligned data and bilingual speakers and isn't this radical.
