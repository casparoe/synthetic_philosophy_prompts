I recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and have since been leading a small philosophy seminar for continuing-education students. I would like to grade one of the seminar's written exercises carefully, and I would appreciate a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. Below is the question I set, followed by one student's answer. Please grade the answer out of 20 and provide the specific comments that justify the grade. In doing so, identify what the student gets right, flag any claims that are imprecise or mistaken, and indicate any steps in the reasoning that do not follow.

**Question.** Acting together—painting a house with someone, performing a duet, or moving a bench together—appears to differ from acting merely in parallel. One influential family of views analyzes shared intention as a structured collection of individual attitudes rather than as an attitude of a literal group mind. Explain this reductive approach and assess whether the conditions it specifies succeed in distinguishing genuine joint action from parallel action.

**Student answer.** The most prominent reductive account is the one developed by Michael Bratman. On this account, two people share an intention to J just in case three conditions hold: (1) each participant has a we-intention toward the joint activity—that is, a primitive and irreducible intention of the form *we intend to J*; (2) the participants' individual subplans for carrying out J mesh, in the sense that they can be jointly executed; and (3) it is common knowledge between the participants that conditions (1) and (2) obtain. The first condition is meant to capture what makes the action collective rather than merely parallel: it is the presence of a genuinely collective attitude, not just two separate I-intentions. The second condition captures the coordination of means, and the third rules out lucky coincidence. The principal virtue of the account is that it avoids positing a group mind with its own attitudes, because a we-intention remains a state of an individual mind, albeit one with an irreducible collective content.

The account draws the distinction correctly in the standard cases. Consider two strangers who happen to walk in step down the same street. Here condition (1) is not satisfied, since neither has a we-intention toward walking together; their behavior is parallel but not joint. By contrast, when two people paint a house together, each has a we-intention, their subplans mesh, and each knows the relevant facts and knows that the other knows them as well—which is all that common knowledge requires. The analysis therefore captures the intuitive difference between acting together and acting merely in parallel.
