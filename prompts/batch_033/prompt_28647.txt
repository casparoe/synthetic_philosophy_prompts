I've been trying to get clear on the units of selection debate for a reading group, and I keep finding that the two sides talk past each other whenever the issue is posed in the abstract. The gene's-eye person should hold that so-called group selection is usually individual or gene-level selection seen through a blurry lens: once you partition change with something like the Price equation, the between-group term is just a statistical component of gene frequency change, and treating the average fitness of a group as group fitness smuggles in a level of agency that isn't doing any causal work. The multilevel selectionist should hold that this is exactly the move that mistakes bookkeeping for causation. There are realistic cases, like a subdivided population where groups differ in how many migrants or propagules they export and that difference tracks a group-level property such as collective defense or low within-group competition, where the group-level trait is the difference-maker and the gene's-eye redescription looses it.

I want real back-and-forth, not two monologues. Have them argue about whether the between-group term in the Price equation is a cause or a remainder, whether MLS1 and MLS2 are even the same debate, and whether the replicator/vehicle distinction clarifies things or just changes the subject. At some point one of them should grant the other a point and then try to show it doesn't matter for the main disagreement, and the other should push on exactly that. End with them either pinpointing the real residual disagreement or discovering it's partly verbal. The dialogue should be readable by someone who knows basic population genetics but isn't a specialist.
