My thesis is that the standard ways of dissolving the paradox — modeling rights as constraints on the feasible set, or Gibbard's line that the alternatives in the Lady Chatterley example were just described too coarsely — both work only by quietly assuming there's a neutral, principled way to sort the world into private and public dimensions, and there isn't. So the liberal paradox isn't really a tension between rights and Pareto; it's a warning that social choice can't even state what a right is without importing a contested theory of the private sphere.
