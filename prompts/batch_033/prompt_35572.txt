I was talking with a friend who just finished a philosophy of religion course, and he was explaining Molinism to me over coffee. He basically summarized it like this — I'm reconstructing from memory:

"So Molina was this Jesuit who tried to split the difference between Calvinist predestination and free will. He said God has three layers of knowledge. Bottom layer: God knows all the possibilities, like a chess engine seeing the whole game tree. Top layer: God knows what will actually happen, since He chose this world. But the clever bit is the middle layer, where God knows what every possible person would freely choose in any situation He could put them in — like, God knows that if He creates me and puts me in a room with a wallet full of cash, I would return it. So before creating anything, God runs through all the possible worlds, sees what everyone would freely do in each, and picks the one where the free choices line up with His plan. That way He gets predestination without pulling anyone's strings. My friend said the whole thing gets killed by the 'grounding objection': there's literally nothing that could make those would-do facts true before anyone exists, so Molinism is dead on arrival."

I nodded along but later I started wondering. My friend presented the grounding objection as a total refutation, but I know there are contemporary philosophers — Plantinga, maybe? — who defend something like Molinism, and they presumably know about the grounding objection. So either they're all missing something obvious, or my friend's summary isn't quite fair. Could you walk me through what a careful Molinist would accept in that summary, what they'd push back on, and whether the grounding objection really settles things the way my friend thinks? I'm especially curious whether the summary conflates middle knowledge with something else at any point — the "chess engine" analogy made me wonder if it's blurring different kinds of divine knowledge.
