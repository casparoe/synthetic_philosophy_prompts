I am a retired physical chemist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, and I sit on the scientific advisory board of a small foundation that funds work on formal epistemology and scientific inference. We are reviewing a proposal from an early-career researcher who wants to build a probabilistic forecasting model for a class of rare, poorly understood events. The model combines several weak indicators and treats each pair as probabilistically independent unless a specific dependence has already been demonstrated. One reviewer says this is unsound, because independence must be justified rather than assumed; another says it is a legitimate use of the presumption of independence that underlies much heuristic scientific reasoning, and that requiring demonstrated independence before modeling would bring the field to a halt. I need to circulate a short decision memo before our meeting next week. Please act as a philosopher of science with expertise in formal epistemology: first briefly brainstorm the strongest considerations on each side, then draft the memo. It should recommend fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with a concrete course of action and a time frame, and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it. The memo should also make clear what would have to be true for a presumption of independence to be epistemically legitimate in this kind of heuristic argument.
