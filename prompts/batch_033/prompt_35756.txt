I've been trying to work out my actual view about when it is worth acquiring information, and I keep finding that my verdicts on concrete cases do not line up neatly with the decision-theoretic result I was taught, Good's theorem, which I understand as saying that a rational expected-utility maximizer should never turn down costless information, since she can always ignore it. Here are the verdicts I find myself with.

(1) Before deciding whether to hold an outdoor event tomorrow, I would gladly pay a few dollars for an accurate weather forecast. (2) I would not pay for a diagnostic test whose result could not change my treatment either way, even though it is accurate information about my body. (3) I would pay to prevent a genetic test for an untreatable condition from being performed, not merely decline to receive its result, because the existence of a known-but-unseen result would itself make me anxious. (4) I would pay a little to learn which of two well-supported accounts of some historical event is correct, even though no decision in my life depends on it. (5) I would pay to keep a noisy but relevant statistic out of my hands when I know I will overweight it in a decision. (6) I would pay not to learn how a magic trick is done, because the knowledge would permanently ruin my enjoyment of the trick, even though no decision depends on knowing it. (7) I would pay to learn the history of a building I pass every day, because it would make the walk more enjoyable, again with no decision at stake. (8) A forecast that would change my investment position, and that I trust myself to use correctly, is worth a fair amount to me. (9) But the very same forecast, if I knew I would use it to over-trade despite my best intentions, I would pay to have withheld, even though it would be valuable to a more disciplined investor.

My working principle is something like this: information is worth acquiring when the expected improvement in the outcomes of my decisions, plus the value of the resulting belief state itself, minus the costs of obtaining and processing it, is positive. But that feels like a list wearing a formula's clothes, and I am not sure it handles the cases where the information changes what I will enjoy rather than merely what I will do.

Is there one principled way to state what all these verdicts have in common, or do some of them have to go? Please state the principle precisely, tie each clause to the cases that motivate it, say what it predicts about cases I have not mentioned—for example, a piece of information that would change my preferences in a way I cannot currently evaluate, a test whose result has only a tiny chance of changing a life-or-death decision, and a piece of information about a decision that will in fact be made by someone else who will not listen to me—and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
