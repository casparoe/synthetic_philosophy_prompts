I recently became the copy editor for an academic journal, and our style guide still defaults to "he" for persons of unspecified sex. Rereading Hofstadter's satirical "Person Paper" has made me suspect this isn't a trivial convention. The piece imagines a parallel society in which the unmarked generic term for any human being is "white," with "black" reserved for the marked case; in that world, proposals to replace "white" with "person" in compounds are met with precisely the objections our style committee raises against "chairperson" and "humankind"—that the traditional form is neutral, that it's aesthetically superior, that it's simply how the language works. What theory of the relation between language, concepts, and default or unmarked categories does the practice of using generic "man" and "he" presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that the male is the default human being, or does it merely inherit a convention whose history is sexist but whose current use could be semantically inert? And if we abandoned whatever it presupposes, what would a style guide have to be committed to instead—for instance, if we adopted singular "they" as our default?
