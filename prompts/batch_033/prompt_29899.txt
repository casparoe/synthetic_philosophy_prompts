Two people in my seminar got into an argument about a one-shot prisoner's dilemma and asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. The agreed setup: you play one round with a stranger, with no communication, no reputation, and no future interaction; the payoffs are symmetric, so mutual cooperation is best for the pair, but defecting always pays more than cooperating against whatever the other player does. Both also accepted that the universe is vast, containing an enormous number of agents facing this same choice and reasoning in the same way.

One of them said: in a large universe, my choice is evidence about what all those similar agents choose, so cooperating doesn't just affect this one game — it puts me in the world where the cooperative decision procedure is common. That is an evidential benefit defection lacks, so I should cooperate.

The other replied: that lets correlation do the work of causation. My choice doesn't causally influence the stranger or any of those faraway agents. Only the consequences I cause matter, and since defection dominates, I should defect.

The first answered: then you would also two-box in Newcomb's problem, but one-boxing is right. There too the predictor doesn't causally affect the boxes at the moment of choice; the boxes are already fixed. What matters is that my choice is evidence about the prediction.

The second answered: Newcomb's problem has a common cause — the predictor scanned my disposition before setting the boxes. The large-world case has no such mechanism, just a bare statistical regularity between me and agents who happen to share my reasoning. A regularity is not an act.

The first came back: the common cause is that we all run the same decision procedure. Choosing a decision procedure is the act — it settles what every instance of that procedure does. A plan of "defect while hoping the others cooperate" is one I know the other instances would execute too, so it is self-undermining.

The second answered: but I am not choosing for the whole population; I am choosing my own act. Being functionally identical to the others is a fact about the world, not a lever I pull, and defecting while they cooperate is perfectly possible even if it is atypical.

They asked me to adjudicate and I couldn't. Please go through the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific point, where does each argument go wrong, and what, if anything, are both of them missing?
