I've been trying to teach myself moral philosophy by reading whatever I can find online, and I keep getting tripped up by the phrase "you can't get an ought from an is." It comes up constantly — in comment sections, in video essays, in conversations with friends. Someone will say that because we evolved to care more about our in-group, maybe we should stop pretending we can be impartial. Or that because disgust reactions are unreliable, we should be suspicious of moral judgments that feel like disgust. And then someone else says "you can't get an ought from an is," and that's supposed to settle it.

I've spent a lot of time turning this over, and I'm convinced the phrase is doing too many jobs at once. Sometimes it seems to mean something about logical validity — you can't have a normative conclusion without at least one normative premise. That's narrow and probably true, but it doesn't rule out using descriptive facts as part of a larger argument that includes normative premises. Other times it seems to mean something much more sweeping — that facts about the world and human beings are simply irrelevant to ethics, which would mean evolutionary psychology, neuroscience, and history have nothing to contribute to moral thinking. That can't be right, but I need to see the steps between these two readings to know where exactly the overreach happens.

Could you lay out the distinct claims that "you can't get an ought from an is" might be making, ordered from the narrowest and most defensible to the broadest and least? For each, tell me whether it's plausible, and — crucially — whether it actually licenses using the slogan to dismiss the kind of argument I described, where someone cites a descriptive finding about human psychology and suggests it has implications for what we ought to do. I want to know which readings do real argumentative work and which are just conversation-stoppers.
