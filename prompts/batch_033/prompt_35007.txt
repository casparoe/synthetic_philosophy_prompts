I just spent an hour arguing with a friend about introspection, and I'm left with a version of his argument that I can't find a clean exit from. Here it is, reconstructed:

(1) I know by introspection when I am in pain. When I attend to my experience, the pain is simply there, unmistakably.
(2) The most natural account of how this works is that pain has a distinctive phenomenal character — a "what-it's-like" — and introspection detects it.
(3) If introspection of pain works by detecting phenomenal character, then the same must hold for introspection of any mental state: I can introspect a state only if it has a distinctive phenomenal character that I can detect.
(4) But propositional attitudes — beliefs, desires, intentions — do not have distinctive phenomenal characters. There is no single "belief-ish" feeling that is present whenever I believe something and absent whenever I don't. What it feels like to believe that Paris is in France is nothing in particular, and certainly nothing that distinguishes it from believing that Paris is in Germany, or from idly entertaining the thought without believing it.
(5) Therefore, by (3) and (4), I cannot introspect my beliefs, desires, or intentions.
(6) But I can introspect them. I know that I believe that 2+2=4, that I desire to finish this sentence, and that I intend to press the next key on my keyboard. I know these things not by observing my behavior or by inference, but by looking inward.
(7) So the argument must go wrong somewhere.

My friend thinks the fault is in (6): I don't really know my attitudes by introspection; I know them by some other route — perhaps by self-ascription or by being the person whose attitudes they are. I find that hard to accept, but I also don't know how to resist (3) without making introspection look like a grab bag of unrelated capacities. And rejecting (4) means accepting that there is a distinctive phenomenology of belief, which strikes me as empirically dubious. Which step would you reject, and what does your choice commit you to?
