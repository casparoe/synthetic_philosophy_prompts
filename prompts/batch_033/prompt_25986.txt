I've been trying to reconstruct Van Fraassen's Reflection principle from first principles and I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove that I should defer to my future self even when I know my future self will be epistemically compromised. That can't be right, but I can't find the bad step. Here's the argument:

(1) For any rational agent and any proposition A, credences obey the law of total probability: P(A) = Σ_i P(A | E_i) P(E_i), where {E_i} is any partition of the agent's possibility space.

(2) Consider the partition defined by the possible values of the agent's own future credence in A: for each x in [0,1], let F_x be the proposition that at some later time t, the agent's credence in A will be x. These F_x are mutually exclusive and exhaustive of the agent's doxastic possibilities, so they form a partition.

(3) Therefore P(A) = ∫_0^1 P(A | F_x) · P(F_x) dx.

(4) Now consider P(A | F_x): my credence in A, conditional on the proposition that my future self will assign credence x to A. What should this number be? It seems obvious that it should be x. My future self has all my current evidence plus whatever additional evidence they acquire between now and t. If I now learn that this better-informed version of me will settle on credence x, I should adopt that credence. To set P(A | F_x) to anything other than x is to claim I can predict the direction of my own future error from inside my current epistemic state — which looks like a Moore-paradoxical kind of irrationality.

(5) Substituting into (3): P(A) = ∫_0^1 x · P(F_x) dx = E[P_t(A)]. This is the Reflection principle: your current credence in A must equal your current expectation of your future credence in A.

(6) But now take a concrete case. I know that tomorrow evening I will be severely sleep-deprived and will have had two drinks. I have extensive evidence from past experience that in this state I become wildly overconfident about propositions involving numerical estimates. Right now I'm considering the proposition G: "The number of attendees at next month's conference will be between 150 and 200." My current evidence — registration numbers, venue capacity, historical attendance — supports a credence of roughly 0.4. But I am nearly certain that tomorrow evening, sleep-deprived-and-tipsy me will entertain G, ignore all the evidence, and confidently announce a credence of 0.85.

(7) By Reflection, since I am certain that P_tomorrow(G) = 0.85, I must set P_today(G) = 0.85. But that is clearly the wrong credence. The evidence supports 0.4, and the fact that a future compromised version of me will believe otherwise is not a reason to change my mind now.

(8) So Reflection gives a false mandate in this case. But the derivation in (1)–(5) seems to be built entirely from the axioms of probability and a platitude about not second-guessing your own epistemic trajectory.

(9) Therefore something in (1)–(5) must go. But which step? If I reject (4), I seem to be saying I can rationally anticipate my own error without correcting for it now — which sounds like epistemic akrasia. If I reject (2), I'm saying that propositions about my own future credences don't form a partition I can reason over, which seems to carve an arbitrary hole in the probability calculus. If I qualify (4) to apply only when my future self is rational, then Reflection is no longer a theorem of probability but a separate normative principle that needs its own justification — and I still need to explain what P(A | F_x) equals when my future self is irrational.

I'd most like to keep (4) in some form, because the idea that you shouldn't believe you'll be wrong by your own lights feels non-negotiable. But I don't see how to keep it without swallowing the absurd conclusion in (7).

Where does the argument go wrong, and what do the main ways of fixing it each cost? In your answer, please also tell me what argument or consideration would most likely change your diagnosis — where your reasoning about this is most vulnerable.
