something i've noticed is that when a public figure gets caught saying something awful, there's this whole choreography around the apology. the standard non-apology is "i'm sorry if anyone was offended" or "i regret that my words were misinterpreted." and everyone immediately recognizes this as a non-apology — the public, the press, everyone knows it's a dodge. what they're supposed to say is something like "i'm sorry for what i said" or "what i said was wrong."

so here's what puzzles me. the non-apology seems to work by locating the problem in the hearer's interpretation rather than in the speaker's intention. it treats meaning as something that happens on the receiving end — "if anyone was offended" — as if the speaker's own communicative intentions are irrelevant to what was actually communicated. the real apology, by contrast, treats the speaker as owning the meaning of what they said, even the stuff that was only implied and not spelled out.

what view of speaker meaning and implicature does this whole practice presuppose? specifically, the fact that everyone can tell the difference between a real apology and a non-apology suggests we're all operating with some shared theory of what it means to mean something. is it basically gricean — that meaning is about the speaker's intention to produce an effect in the hearer by means of the hearer's recognition of that intention? if so, the non-apology is a kind of bad-faith move that tries to retroactively break the gricean link between intention and meaning, and the reason we can all spot it is that we're implicitly committed to that link. or is the practice actually presupposing something messier, where meaning is partly a joint product of speaker and hearer, and the non-apology is exploiting a genuine ambiguity in that relationship rather than just being dishonest?
