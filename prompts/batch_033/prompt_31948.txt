Before I read any more proposals about how an aligned AI system should aggregate the values of the people it affects, I want the specification done properly first. The purpose I am evaluating theories against is this: the account must yield, for a general-purpose AI system acting in a large and pluralistic society, an objective — or a defensible procedure for choosing objectives — that can be justified to the people subject to it without asking any of them to abandon their own reasonable conception of the good. I mean the problem of value aggregation under reasonable pluralism and the constraints of liberal neutrality, not just the engineering problem of combining preferences.

Write the desiderata that any adequate account must meet. For each, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp (utilitarian, social-choice-theoretic, deliberative, liberal-neutralist, and so on). Among the candidates I keep seeing are: non-dictatorship; Pareto efficiency; an unrestricted domain of admissible values; independence of irrelevant alternatives; strategy-proofness; neutrality between conceptions of the good; interpersonal comparability, demanded by some and rejected by others; respect for the separateness of persons; dynamic consistency as people's values change, including under the system's own influence; scope rules for future people, nonhuman animals, and outsiders; and transparency or contestability of the procedure. I do not trust this list as given: some entries are parochial, some may be disguised versions of one another, and some may be in tension with the rest. I want the real list — keep, reject, refine, and add to it.

Then identify which subsets of the list are jointly unsatisfiable. In particular, I want to know where the Arrow-style impossibility results bite once the inputs are values rather than votes and the options are policies rather than candidates, and whether the strategy-proofness results or anything analogous carry over to the AI case. The tensions within the list are the part I most want to see.

Finish with a brief scorecard of the main families of proposals — utilitarian aggregation, total and average; majoritarian and other social-choice mechanisms; contractualist or deliberative approaches; and bargaining models — against your list. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria are the deliverable. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular proposals, and whether that requirement is one we should actually keep or one the field has inherited from an adjacent debate and should drop.
