For the past several years I've been teaching a graduate seminar on personal identity, and one of my colleagues — a philosopher of mind I'll call Professor M — has developed a habit of stopping by my office after each session to tell me what she thinks about the cases we discussed. She's never articulated a systematic theory, and when I've pressed her she's said she prefers to reason case by case. But I've now accumulated enough of her verdicts that I suspect a coherent picture is lurking underneath. Here are her judgments on the six central cases we've covered so far, exactly as she gave them to me:

1. **Destructive teleportation.** You enter a scanner; your body is destroyed; an exact molecular replica is built on Mars. M says: you die. The person on Mars is not you.
2. **Gradual neural replacement.** Over the course of several years, each of your neurons is replaced one at a time with a silicon chip that performs the identical computational function. You are conscious throughout, and your behavior, memories, and personality change only gradually. M says: you survive. The person at the end is you.
3. **Brain transplant.** Your entire brain is removed from your skull and transplanted into a new, healthy body. The old body is destroyed. M says: you survive. You wake up in the new body.
4. **Fission.** Both hemispheres of your brain are removed and transplanted into two different bodies. The original body is destroyed. Both resulting people wake up with your memories and personality. M says: you do not survive. Neither of the resulting people is you.
5. **Upload while alive.** Your brain is scanned and a digital copy of your mind is created on a computer. Your biological brain and body continue to exist. M says: the biological you is you; the digital copy is not.
6. **Total amnesia.** You suffer a catastrophic brain injury that destroys all episodic memories and radically alters your personality. Your body survives and remains conscious. M says: you survive. You are the same person, just dramatically changed.

We have not yet discussed the following case, and I want to know what M would predictably say if she is consistent with her earlier verdicts:

**New case: Reverse brain transplant.** Your entire brain is destroyed by a stroke, but your body is kept alive on life support. Surgeons then transplant a new brain — taken from a donor, with the donor's memories and personality intact — into your body. The resulting person looks like you and has your body, but has the donor's psychology.

I'd like you to do three things. First, identify the candidate rule or rules that jointly fit all six of M's verdicts. Be explicit about which rules are live possibilities and which are ruled out by her verdicts. Second, tell me what each live rule predicts about the reverse-brain-transplant case, and give your best guess about which rule M is actually running, with reasons. Third, if the rules diverge on the new case, tell me whether there is a further case — one we haven't discussed — that would cleanly tell them apart, and what it would show.

Please put your final prediction of M's verdict in the format \answer{M's predicted verdict: [survives / does not survive]}.
