So I cover philosophy and intellectual history for a magazine — the kind of beat where you spend three days at a conference and the real action is at the hotel bar after. Last month I was at an American Philosophy conference and ended up next to two pragmatism scholars who got into it about Peirce versus James on truth. I had my notebook and basically transcribed the whole argument because it was sharper than anything I'd heard from the podium. I've been turning it over ever since and I still can't decide whose got the better of it. Here's the exchange, cleaned up a little from my notes:

A: Look, James's whole "truth is what works" line is a disaster. It makes truth subjective — if a belief is useful to you, it counts as true, which means contradictory beliefs could both be "true" for different people. That's not a theory of truth, that's relativism with a folksy accent.

B: That's a caricature and you know it. James doesn't say any belief that makes you feel good counts as true. He says truth is what proves itself across the whole of experience — it has to fit with everything else you encounter. And honestly, Peirce's "final opinion" is just as vulnerable: we have no access to the end of inquiry, so how does it help us distinguish truth from falsity right now?

A: Peirce at least gives us a direction: keep inquiring. The point is that our current beliefs are fallible and always subject to revision. James's view seems to say that if a belief is working for you at the moment, there's no particular reason to question it — which is exactly how dogmatism gets going.

B: That's not fair to James. He says truth happens to an idea through its consequences over time — it's a process, not a static property. And Peirce's "keep inquiring" is fine as far as it goes, but it gives zero guidance about what to believe in the meantime. We have to act now, not at the end of inquiry.

A: But here's the deeper problem for James. Imagine a belief that's genuinely useful — it helps us navigate, it fits our other beliefs, it has real practical payoff — but it's false. A useful fiction. James seems forced to call it true, because it passes every test he's set. Peirce can say: no, it's still false, because it wouldn't survive inquiry in the long run.

B: I think "useful fiction that could never be exposed" is a much trickier category than you're making it. If a belief consistently proves itself across all possible experience, in what sense is it false? You'd need some kind of correspondence to reality that's independent of every means we have of accessing reality — and that's exactly the kind of metaphysics Peirce claimed to have abandoned.

A: Peirce doesn't need a mysterious correspondence relation. He just needs the idea that inquiry converges — that there's a fact of the matter about what we'd believe if we investigated long enough. That's a counterfactual, not a metaphysics.

B: But counterfactuals about what ideal inquirers would believe are themselves theory-laden in exactly the way James was worried about. Who counts as ideal? What methods count as investigation? You've just pushed the normativity back a step. James's honesty is in admitting these questions are settled pragmatically all the way down — there's no escape from the practical standpoint.

At that point someone ordered another round and the conversation drifted, but I've been stuck on this. Can you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate — who's right about what, where each of them overreaches or misrepresents the other, and who ends up on stronger ground overall? I'm writing a piece on pragmatism and I need to know whether I should come down more Peircean or more Jamesian, and right now I'm just confused.
