I am preparing a short paper for my school's philosophy club on how groups make decisions, and I recently read a popular science article that summarized the unilateralist's curse. I would like to check how much of that summary I can trust before I repeat any of it. The article summarized the idea roughly as follows:

"When a group of people each have the power to take some risky action on their own — for example, releasing a piece of dangerous information or starting a project that could go badly wrong — the group is far more likely to take the action than any individual member would be on their own. Each member acts whenever their own private judgment says the expected benefits outweigh the risks; because everyone's judgment is noisy, in a large enough group it is almost guaranteed that at least one person will overestimate the benefits and pull the trigger. The curse therefore shows that crowds are more reckless than individuals, since each person is going on their own hunch rather than pooling everyone's information. The article's proposed remedy was that the group should require unanimous agreement before anyone may act."

Something about the last two sentences strikes me as suspicious — especially the move from "someone will overestimate the benefits" to "crowds are more reckless than individuals," and the recommendation of unanimous agreement — but I do not know enough to tell whether the article is being loose or I am missing something.
