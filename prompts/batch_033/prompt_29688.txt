I've been reading about thermodynamics and the foundations of statistical mechanics, and I keep noticing a pattern that I don't have a word for — or rather, I suspect there is a word for it, but I don't know it, and I want to know whether the concept itself is as deep as it seems.

The pattern shows up most vividly in the Maxwell's demon thought experiment. A tiny being sits at a door between two chambers of gas and sorts fast molecules into one side and slow ones into the other, creating a temperature gradient from an initially uniform gas. That looks like it lets you run a heat engine off a single temperature reservoir, which would violate the second law. The resolution I've encountered — I think from reading around Szilard and later work — says that the demon has to acquire information about where the molecules are and how fast they're moving, and that acquiring or erasing that information carries an entropy cost that exactly balances the books and saves the second law.

Here's what strikes me as strange and interesting. In that resolution, information behaves as though it were a physical quantity: it can be converted into work, it has an entropy price, and erasing it costs something irreducible. That is not at all how I normally think about information. I think of information as abstract — a pattern, a fact, something that can be copied for free and stored without consequence. But the demon argument only works if information is, in some nontrivial sense, physical: if there is a lawlike relationship between bits and joules.

I've seen the phrase "information is physical" in passing, and I gather that Landauer and Bennett are part of this story, but I don't know the precise concept that names the idea I'm after. What I'm after is the claim that there is a minimum thermodynamic cost to erasing information, that this cost explains why Maxwell's demon fails, and that this in turn means information is not a free-floating abstraction but something subject to physical law. Is there an established name for this specific thesis? How exactly is it distinguished from the broader slogan that "information is physical"? And does it genuinely force us to treat information as a physical resource in the way we treat energy, or is it a narrower point about the engineering constraints on computation that doesn't carry the metaphysical weight it first seems to? I'm not satisfied with a bare label — I want to know what the thesis strictly asserts, what it doesn't assert, and where its edges are.
