Analyze the prorogation of the UK Parliament in September 2019 as a case study in performative utterances. Here are the facts I have in mind: in late summer 2019 the Prime Minister advised the Queen to prorogue Parliament for several weeks; the prorogation took effect through the usual constitutional machinery; shortly afterward the Supreme Court held that the advice was unlawful and that the prorogation was void, so that Parliament was treated as never having been prorogued. I am not asking about the constitutional politics; I am asking about the speech-act mechanics.

Walk through the chain of utterances involved — the Prime Minister's advice, the instrument that gave effect to it, any proclamation — and say, for each, whether it is a performative, what illocutionary act it performs, and what felicity conditions it has to satisfy in order to succeed. Then use Austin's framework to classify what went wrong. In particular, I want you to distinguish between a performative that misfires because a procedural or authority condition fails, and a performative that is validly performed but later annulled; the ruling seems to say the prorogation never had legal effect at all. Which of those things happened, and does Austin's taxonomy have a slot for it? I keep wanting to think of the ruling as a rollback or an exception thrown after a side effect, but I suspect that analogy is doing real work and may be misleading.

Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode show about speech act theory? Specifically, how much of a performative's success is fixed by the utterance situation itself — authority, procedure, uptake, intention — and how much depends on substantive facts about the world, such as whether the underlying advice was lawful? Does the case confirm the standard picture, expose a gap in it, or show that legal speech acts need a different account from ordinary performatives like promising or christening? Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
