I'm putting together a workshop on Kant's "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy" and I've got two colleagues who've been arguing about this for years — one's a dyed-in-the-wool Kantian who thinks Kant basically got it right even on the murderer-at-the-door case, and the other is a moderate who thinks lying is presumptively wrong but that there are genuine exceptions when the stakes are high enough. I'd like them to produce an adversarial-collaboration document: a joint statement they could both sign, with the claims they agree on, the precise points where they part ways, and the cruxes that would actually move the debate.

The thing is, they're both terrible at writing joint statements — they either paper over the real disagreement or write warring caricatures of each other's views. So I want to draft it for them, as a model of what this kind of document should look like.

Please write the document. It should have:

(1) A numbered list of agreed claims — things both sides can genuinely sign. These shouldn't be vacuous ("lying is a speech act") but should capture real common ground that structures the debate. For instance, they might agree on what Kant's argument actually is, or on what the murderer-at-the-door case is meant to test, or on what *doesn't* settle the question.

(2) A disagreements table with three columns: the issue, the Kantian absolutist's view, and the moderate's view — each stated in wording its own side would accept as fair. The disagreements should be specific and substantive, not just "one side thinks lying is always wrong and the other doesn't."

(3) A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that if you could settle them, at least one side would have to budge. For each crux, give the argument or case both sides agree would bear on it, and what each side predicts about it.

One thing I want to avoid is the whole thing collapsing into the moderate just being a consequentialist in disguise. The moderate position should be recognizably deontological — maybe it's a threshold view, or a Ross-style pluralism, or something about the nature of the duty not to lie being different from what Kant thought. The point is the debate should be within deontology, not deontology vs. consequentialism. That's what makes it hard and interesting.

Also, if you think any part of their dispute is actually verbal — if they're talking past each other about what "lying" means, or what "right" means, or what the scope of the moral law is — say so explicitly and explain why. But don't let that be a cheap way out; if there's a genuine normative disagreement underneath the verbal one, identify that too.
