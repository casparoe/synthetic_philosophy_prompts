I cover the philosophy beat sometimes for the magazine I write for, and a few weeks ago I got pulled into a long conversation with a metaphysician about dispositions, finks, and masks. I should say: before journalism I wrote software for about eight years, and I still reach for that vocabulary when I'm trying to understand something abstract. So while he was talking about fragile glasses and sorcerers who cast spells at exactly the wrong moment, I was mentally rewriting the whole thing as functions that get monkey-patched at call time, or middleware that intercepts a request before it hits the handler.

Here's what I think I understand so far. A disposition is like a function signature — it says what would happen under certain inputs, but it's just sitting there defined, not executing. A fink is something that changes the disposition precisely when the triggering condition occurs, so the conditional "if you call this function with these args, you'll get that result" comes out false even though the disposition is real. My friend's example was a sorcerer who makes a glass stop being fragile the instant before it's struck, but only if it's about to be struck. That sounds to me like a hook that fires on invocation and swaps out the implementation. A mask is different: the disposition is still there, but something blocks the manifestation — like packing a fragile vase in bubble wrap. In code terms that feels like a try-catch that swallows the exception, or a decorator that wraps the function and discards its output. But I'm pretty sure my analogies are leaking somewhere, because my friend keeps wincing.

What I'd like is for someone who actually knows both territories to do a proper translation: restate the whole dispositions-finks-masks debate — the main positions, the counterexamples, the moves people make to save the simple conditional analysis — in the vocabulary of software engineering. Functions, exceptions, event handlers, dependency injection, monkey-patching, middleware, idempotency, whatever fits. Be as precise as you can. Then give me the residue report. Which bits of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto software concepts? Which ones you had to stretch to make fit, and how? Which ones have no counterpart at all in the software vocabulary? And then the hard question: does the residue point to something the philosophers are tracking that my vocabulary can't capture — something real about dispositions that software concepts are blind to — or does it point the other way, suggesting that some of the philosophical problem is an artifact of the way they're talking about it? I want the translation to be honest, not rigged to make one framework look better than the other.
