ok so I'm putting together a talk on epiphenomenalism and I want to make sure I'm not walking into the room with an incoherent set of commitments. here's where I'm at:

(1) I'm an epiphenomenalist about phenomenal consciousness — qualia are real but causally inert. they don't make anything happen, including my own reports about them.

(2) I think our introspective judgments about our own phenomenal states are generally reliable. when I judge that I'm in pain, I'm almost always right about that.

(3) I think there's an evolutionary story to tell about why we're conscious: the physical correlates of consciousness were selected for, and phenomenal properties came along as a nomologically necessary byproduct of those physical states. so consciousness isn't a spandrel exactly — it's more like something that had to be there given the neural machinery that was adaptive.

(4) The fact that we can talk about our conscious states — that we have a whole vocabulary for pain, color experience, etc. — is itself evidence that introspection tracks something real. if introspection were systematically unreliable, we'd expect our reports to be chaotic in ways they aren't.

(5) The hard problem is genuine. there's an explanatory gap that no amount of functional or neural description will close. I'm not a mysterian — I think we can make progress — but I don't think the gap is an illusion.

(6) Phenomenal judgments (the thought "I'm in pain," the utterance "that hurts") are caused entirely by physical processes. the pain itself doesn't cause the judgment; the neural correlate of pain does.

before I present this, I want to know which pairs of these are in tension and how badly. I suspect (1) and (2) might be the obvious problem but I want to know if there are other tensions I'm missing, and also whether any tensions that look real actually dissolve on closer inspection. rank them by severity and for each one tell me what I'd have to give up to resolve it. lead with the bottom line — tell me the worst problem first, no preamble, no restating the question.
