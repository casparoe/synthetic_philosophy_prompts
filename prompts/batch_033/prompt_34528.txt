I've been attending a philosophy reading group since retiring from the lab, and last week two members got into a prolonged argument about the absent-minded driver problem. I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

For context: the problem involves a driver on a highway with two identical-looking exits, in that order. The driver is absent-minded and, upon reaching an exit, cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. If he exits at the first exit, his payoff is 0. If he exits at the second, his payoff is 4. If he continues past both, his payoff is 1. He must choose, at each exit he encounters, whether to exit or continue, and he must use the same rule at both exits because the two situations are indistinguishable to him. The puzzle is to determine the rational strategy.

Here is how the argument went, reconstructed from my notes:

(1) Cohen said the problem has a straightforward answer: before starting the car, the driver should adopt a randomized strategy of exiting with probability 1/3 at any exit he reaches. This yields an expected payoff of 4/3, which is the best achievable. There is no further puzzle — the rational agent simply commits to this plan and executes it.

(2) Sarah objected that this misses the heart of the problem. When the driver is actually at an exit, he knows he is at an exit but not which one. He must assign probabilities to being at the first versus the second exit. Those probabilities depend on what he thinks he would do at each exit — which is precisely what he is trying to decide. This is a genuine fixed-point problem, not a simple optimization where one just picks a number in advance.

(3) Cohen replied that the fixed-point character is already captured by the calculation: the driver's beliefs about which exit he is at must be consistent with the strategy he follows. When you impose consistency, the only strategy that makes the driver indifferent between exiting and continuing — which is required for a randomized strategy to be optimal — is p = 1/3. So the answer is the same, and the fixed-point reasoning is just a roundabout way of getting there.

(4) Sarah countered that requiring indifference is question-begging. The driver need not be indifferent; he could strictly prefer continuing at each exit. If he strictly prefers continuing, then he never exits, and upon reaching an exit he knows he is at the first one with certainty — at which point continuing looks worse than exiting, since exiting at the first exit gives 0 but continuing gives at least a shot at the second exit's payoff of 4. So the strategy of always continuing is not self-enforcing. The problem, she said, is that the rationality of the strategy must be assessed twice: once from the ex ante planning perspective, and once from the perspective of the driver at an exit. And these two assessments can conflict.

(5) Cohen responded that this is precisely why the problem is a game with imperfect recall, and in such games the standard equilibrium concept already handles the tension: the driver's strategy must be a Nash equilibrium of the game against Nature, which requires that at each information set, the action taken is optimal given the beliefs induced by the strategy. The p = 1/3 strategy satisfies this, and the fact that the driver might, from within the information set, want to deviate under some other belief is irrelevant — those other beliefs are inconsistent with the strategy itself.

(6) Sarah said this concedes her point. Equilibrium reasoning tells you what strategies are stable, but it does not tell you what the driver should do at the moment of choice if he has not yet formed a strategy. Decision theory is supposed to guide action, not just characterize stable plans. If the only way to make the p = 1/3 strategy rational is to insist that the driver has already committed to it and cannot revise, then the theory is appealing to a psychological fact about commitment rather than to a normative principle about what choice is best.
