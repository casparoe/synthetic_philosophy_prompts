Hey — I'm a political theory PhD student, and I've somehow ended up leading a session on Glazer's 2023 film The Zone of Interest for our department's theories of evil reading group, and I want to push past the obvious line that the film is just Arendt on screen. I'd like a serious reading of the film through Arendt's banality of evil thesis, but one that's precise about which claim we're attributing to her. The way I read Eichmann in Jerusalem (the subtitle of the 1963 book is literally A Report on the Banality of Evil), her point isnt that evil is ordinary or that anyone would have done the same thing; it's narrower — Eichmann was thoughtless in a specific sense, full of clichés, unable to think from the standpoint of anyone else, and that shallowness is what let him do what he did. The thesis gets flattened a lot, and I dont want to flatten it again.

The film gives us Rudolf Höss and his family living right up against the camp wall — garden, kids, career worries, a wife tending her flowers — while the killing is present mostly as sound, smoke, and small details, never shown from inside. Where does the film actually dramatize Arendt's idea, and where does it resist it? In particular: is the Höss of the film thoughtless in Arendt's sense, or is he something else — a careerist who knows exactly what he's running, a man managing the machinery rather than failing to think? Does the film's decision never to enter the camp capture Arendt's point about not-seeing and not-thinking, or does it risk turning the perpetrators' separation into something almost aesthetic? And is the film maybe closer to a different thesis — about the active work of maintaining a normal life next to atrocity, or about the social reproduction of evil — than to what Arendt actually argued?

I want a reading that's faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of the thesis, and don't soften Arendt into a slogan to make her fit. Be honest about where the film might be doing something Arendt herself would have been suspicious of, and what the film adds to the debate that the argument alone doesn't. I'd rather you engage specific scenes and formal choices than give me a survey of the secondary literature.
