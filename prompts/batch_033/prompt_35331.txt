I've been teaching virtue ethics for years and I've never been moved by the situationist critique. My view is that the whole thing rests on a misunderstanding: virtue ethics doesn't require the kind of rigid cross-situational consistency that the situationists set out to debunk. An honest person isn't someone who behaves identically in every honesty-relevant situation — that would be bizarre. The virtues are more like practical skills that involve sensitivity to context, so the fact that people's behavior shifts across situations (the phone booth dime experiment, the Good Samaritan seminary study, etc.) doesn't touch the view I actually hold.

But I'm aware I might be too comfortable here. What would actually count against my position? I want a map of the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. Are there versions of the situationist critique that target my kind of view specifically, rather than the straw man? Are there empirical results that would be awkward even for a context-sensitive virtue ethics? And honestly: have I qualified the view into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind — or is that just how normative theories work?
