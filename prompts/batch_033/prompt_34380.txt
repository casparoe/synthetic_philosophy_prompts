I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working on formal epistemology, and I was at our department's probability reading group last week where two of my friends—let's call them Mira (she's a halfer) and Evan (he's a thirder)—got into this really intense back-and-forth about Sleeping Beauty that went way past the usual talking points. I scribbled down the exchange afterward because I thought it was genuinely illuminating, but honestly I can't figure out who's right about what. I'd love a point-by-point adjudication.

Here's roughly how it went. They'd been discussing the standard problem—Beauty is put to sleep Sunday, a fair coin is flipped, she's woken Monday if heads, Monday and Tuesday if tails, with a memory-wipe each time so she never knows what day it is—and Mira was defending the halfer line. She said: look, when Beauty wakes up she learns nothing new. She knew in advance she'd be woken at least once, and that's exactly what's happening. So her credence in heads should stay at 1/2. Evan said that's wrong, because Beauty does learn something: she learns that "today is an awakening day," and that proposition is twice as likely under tails—two awakenings—than under heads—one awakening. So by Bayes she should update to 1/3. Mira replied that Evan's "today is an awakening day" is just a fancy redescription of the fact that she's awake, which she already knew would happen, and slapping the word "today" on it doesn't smuggle in new evidence. Evan then pulled out the extreme version: suppose Beauty is woken a million times under tails. If you wake up, don't you think it's crushingly likely the coin was tails? The "no new information" argument still says 1/2. That's absurd. Mira said it's not absurd—the coin is fair, period. The number of awakenings can't retroactively bias the coin. Evan shot back: so you'd bet at even odds on heads? Because if you're a halfer, you should accept even-money bets on heads at every awakening, and you'd be dutch-booked into oblivion. Mira said no, I wouldn't accept even-money bets, and that's the whole point—credence and betting odds come apart when self-locating uncertainty is involved. Your credence stays 1/2, but your betting odds reflect the fact that you'll face the bet twice under tails. This is the double-halfer position. Evan then said this is exactly where the position collapses: if your credence is 1/2 but becomes 2/3 upon learning it's Monday, then by the law of total probability your credence before learning the day—which is your waking credence—must be a weighted average of those, which works out to 1/3. You can't escape the theorem. Mira said the theorem was proved for ordinary propositions, not centered propositions, and the whole point of Sleeping Beauty is that it exposes a gap in how we apply probability to self-locating beliefs.

At that point someone spilled coffee and the conversation moved on. But I've been turning it over all week. I can see moves each of them is making that feel right and moves that feel like they're talking past each other.
