I've been trying to get clear on what rule I'm actually using in these symmetric "everyone is just as rational as me" situations, because my gut calls don't obviously hang together. Here are the cases (assume players can't communicate, and everyone knows the setup and knows everyone else knows it).

1. Platonia dilemma: 20 people each decide whether to send a telegram. If exactly one sends, that person gets a billion dollars; if none or more than one send, nobody gets anything. My considered call: I wouldn't just send and I wouldn't just stay silent; I'd roll a 20-sided die and send only on a 1. Sending outright feels like pretending I'm the special one, and silence feels like giving up. The 1-in-20 mix is the only move that doesn't pretend I'm different from the other 19.

2. The Luring Lottery that Scientific American actually ran: you send a postcard saying how many entries you want; the prize is a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, and one entry is drawn at random. The obvious "optimal" strategy is to submit as many entries as you expect everyone else will submit in total, but if everyone reasons that way the entries explode and the prize is worth nothing. My call: roll a die with as many sides as I expect there are players, and send "1" only if I roll a 1. Superficially same move as Platonia, but the point is keeping the total from exploding, not being the unique sender.

3. Newcomb's problem, near-perfect predictor, both boxes in front of me: I take both boxes. I know the predictor probably predicted that and the big box is probably empty, but the money is already there or not; taking both can't change it. This is the one that bothers me, because in case 1 sending weakly dominates staying silent — if others send I get nothing either way, if no one else sends I win — and yet I refuse to just send. In Newcomb I take both because two-boxing weakly dominates one-boxing. Those feel like the same structure and I land differently. Why should symmetry among players' rationality matter in one and not the other?

4. One-shot prisoner's dilemma against my identical twin, who reasons exactly like me and we both know it: I'd cooperate. Against a random stranger in the same anonymous one-shot: defect. So symmetry flips me in the PD like it flips me in Platonia.

5. Stag hunt with a room full of identical reasoners: everyone gets $1000 if everyone hunts stag; if I chase a rabbit I get a safe $900 and everyone else gets nothing; if others chase rabbits, my hunting stag gets me nothing. Against identical reasoners I hunt stag. Against strangers I probly still hunt stag, but way less confidently.

So my tentative principle is something like: when the other players are symmetric rational agents, choose the strategy — including mixed strategies — that would be best for me if everyone used it. In Platonia that means randomizing at 1/20 rather than sending, and in the twin PD it means cooperating. But I can't tell whether the principle is actually well-defined, whether it genuinely covers the Luring Lottery, and whether it forces me to one-box in Newcomb when the predictor is a simulation of me rather than a fixed machine. Can you state the principle precisely, test it against these five cases, and say which of my verdicts it saves and which it overturns? And what does it say about a variant of Platonia where sending costs a dollar, win or lose?
