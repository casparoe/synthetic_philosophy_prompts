The reference class problem has been particularly troubling to me — in physics we are accustomed to thinking of probabilities as objective features of the world, whether in statistical mechanics or in the standard interpretation of quantum measurement, but the reference class problem seems to threaten the very idea that a single event has a well-defined probability at all.

I would like your help thinking this through systematically. Please give me your all-things-considered credence on the following proposition:

**The reference class problem has at least one fully satisfactory solution — where "satisfactory" means a principled, non-arbitrary procedure for selecting the correct reference class when assigning a probability to a singular event, one that yields determinate answers across the range of cases where we pre-theoretically think such probabilities exist (e.g., the probability that this patient will respond to this treatment, that this defendant is guilty, that this hypothesis is true).**

I want not just a number but the decomposition. Break the proposition into the sub-questions your credence depends on, assign a rough probability to each, and show how they combine. At a minimum, please address the following:

(a) Whether the reference class problem is genuinely distinct from other problems of underdetermination — curve-fitting, the grue problem, the problem of the priors — or whether it collapses into one of them, together with your credence that solving those would solve this.
