I run a reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and last week we spent the session reconstructing the quantum suicide thought experiment. One member wrote on the board a version of the argument that he claims establishes far more than the usual point, and I have not been able to find a way out of it.

(1) If the many-worlds interpretation is correct, then whenever a quantum process has several possible outcomes with nonzero amplitude, the world branches, and every one of those outcomes is realized in some branch.
(2) Imagine an apparatus that measures the spin of a particle and is arranged so that, if the result is up, the experimenter is killed instantly and painlessly, while if the result is down, the apparatus merely displays a green light. By (1), the down result occurs in some branch.
(3) An experimenter can have no future experiences in a branch in which the experimenter has died; only surviving branches contain a successor with that experimenter's point of view.
(4) Therefore, from the experimenter's own point of view, the experiment will be observed to yield the green light with probability 1, no matter how small the amplitude of the down result may be.
(5) The same reasoning applies at each trial, so if the experiment is run a hundred times in succession, the experimenter should expect to see the green light every time, with probability 1, even though by the Born rule a hundred consecutive down results would be vanishingly improbable.
(6) But if that is right, the conclusion extends to any potentially fatal process in which survival is one of the quantum-mechanically possible outcomes. The experimenter should therefore expect, with probability 1, to survive every such process.
(7) Hence each of us should be certain of our own indefinite survival — quantum immortality — even though we observe that people die all around us.

I find (7) impossible to accept, but I cannot locate the step at which the argument goes wrong. The member who presented it says the only stable positions are to accept (7), to reject (1), or to weight the branches somehow. He dismisses that third option as giving up on the idea that what I should expect is determined solely by the worlds in which a continuation of me exists. I would prefer to keep (1), and I suspect that the mistake lies either in (4) or in the move from (5) to (6), but I cannot state the error precisely. Which step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it commit one to about first-person probability, personal identity across branches, or the authority of the Born rule?
