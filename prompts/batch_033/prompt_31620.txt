I am a novelist, and I am working on a book whose protagonist hosts a small but serious podcast on democratic theory. In one of the central scenes, she interviews a political theorist known for defending the all-affected-interests principle: the view that everyone whose interests are affected by a collective decision ought to have some say in making it, and that this principle offers the right answer to the boundary problem, the question of who should make up the demos in a democracy. I want this scene to feel like a real exchange between two people who know the terrain, not a soft introduction for a lay audience, so the host should be able to press the position where it is genuinely strained.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for the scene. The questions should go well beyond asking the guest to restate his view. At least one should press the circularity worry: that identifying who is affected seems to require a prior decision about which decisions count and who gets to judge that, which is precisely the question the principle is meant to answer. At least one should press the difference between being affected by a decision and being subject to a polity's laws, since many people may be affected by, say, a state's environmental policy without being governed by it. At least one should press the over-inclusiveness worry: once causal chains are traced far enough, nearly everyone is affected by nearly everything, so the principle may generate a demos with no workable boundaries. At least one should press the instability of the demos across levels and framings of a decision, since a city ordinance, a national statute, and a treaty each seem to summon a different demos. And at least one should ask whether the boundary problem admits of a democratic solution at all, or whether the act of constituting the demos must ultimately rest on something other than democratic principle. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give, so the host can keep the pressure on rather than allowing him to deflect.
