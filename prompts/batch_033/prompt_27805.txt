I'm giving a department colloquium next month at a large philosophy department — about two dozen faculty, strong in ethics, metaphysics, and philosophy of mind, plus a cluster of empirically oriented philosophers of cognitive science. My talk argues that a certain class of thought experiments function as what Dennett called "intuition pumps": their vivid narrative framing, emotional salience, and concrete detail are precisely what make them persuasive, and those same features make them resistant to the kind of reflective scrutiny that philosophical argument requires. My central claim is that when a philosophical conclusion depends essentially on intuitions elicited by such thought experiments, that conclusion is epistemically suspect — not necessarily false, but not something we should treat as having been established. I use as my main exhibits Thomson's violinist, the Chinese room, and fission cases for personal identity, and I close by sketching what a thought-experiment-averse methodology might look like for those debates.

I know this audience. The senior ethicist has spent decades using trolley-style cases; one metaphysician's book on personal identity turns on fission intuitions; the philosopher of mind works on the hard problem and uses zombie arguments routinely. The empirically minded faculty will be sympathetic to my suspicion of armchair methods but skeptical that I've done the empirical work to back up my claims about cognitive bias. And everyone in the room will have heard Dennett's phrase before — several will have read him more carefully than I have.
