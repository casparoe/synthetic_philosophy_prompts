I've been reading about decision theory and came across a blog post that summarized Hofstadter's concept of superrationality. The author wrote:

"Superrationality says that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with another player who is functionally identical to you — same reasoning process, same knowledge, same goals — you should cooperate. The argument is that since your decision and theirs will be the product of the same cognitive machinery operating on the same inputs, you can treat your choice as a choice for both of you. By deciding to cooperate, you're effectively deciding that both of you cooperate, and since mutual cooperation beats mutual defection, cooperation is the right move. This is what separates superrationality from standard game theory, where defection strictly dominates no matter what you believe about the other player."

I've seen this idea discussed on LessWrong and in rationalist circles, but I've also heard people say that the 'choose for both' framing is a misunderstanding of what Hofstadter was actually arguing — that it smuggles in a causal claim where Hofstadter was making a different kind of point. I'm not sure which parts of the summary are accurate and which aren't. Go through this summary carefully and tell me what a careful defender of superrationality would accept, what they'd object to, and whether the overall picture it gives is fair. I want a committed answer — don't just lay out the interpretive options; tell me where you think the summary goes wrong, if it does, and what the correct account looks like.
