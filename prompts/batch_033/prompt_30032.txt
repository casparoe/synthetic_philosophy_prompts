And here's the student's answer:

"Goodman's 'new riddle of induction' is presented in his book Fact, Fiction, and Forecast. The riddle uses the predicate 'grue,' which is defined as follows: an object is grue if it is examined before some future time t (say, January 1, 2050) and is green, or if it is not examined before t and is blue. All emeralds we've observed up to now have been both green and grue — they were examined before t and found to be green, which satisfies the first disjunct of the grue definition. The problem is that the same evidence — 'all observed emeralds are green' — equally supports the hypothesis 'all emeralds are green' and the hypothesis 'all emeralds are grue.' But these hypotheses make different predictions about emeralds first examined after t: the green hypothesis says they'll be green, while the grue hypothesis says they'll be blue. If induction is just about projecting observed regularities, then we have no reason to prefer one projection over the other.

In essence, Goodman's new riddle is Hume's problem of induction dressed up in new clothes. Hume showed that we can't justify induction without circularity; Goodman shows that even if we accept induction, we can't even say which inductions to make, because any set of observations can be described by infinitely many gerrymandered predicates. The new riddle thus deepens Hume's skeptical challenge: not only is induction unjustified, but the very idea of 'the' inductive conclusion from a body of evidence is indeterminate.

Goodman's answer is 'entrenchment.' Some predicates, like 'green,' have a history of being used in inductive inferences — they are entrenched in our linguistic and inferential practices. Others, like 'grue,' are cooked up ad hoc and have no such history. Goodman says we should project entrenched predicates and not project unentrenched ones. This solves the riddle by giving us a rule for picking among the infinitely many predicates that fit the data.

However, this solution is unsatisfying because it just pushes the problem back. Why should we trust entrenched predicates? Presumably because they've worked well in the past — but that's itself an inductive inference, and we're back to Hume's circle. So Goodman hasn't really solved anything; he's just described which inductions we happen to make, not which ones we ought to make."
