I'm a journalist covering the science of animal cognition for a magazine feature, and in the course of reporting I've run into an argument I can't shake. It started as a reconstruction of Descartes' case that animals are mindless automata, but the more I turned it over, the more it seemed to prove something nobody involved wants to accept. I need you to tell me where it goes wrong—if it does.

Here is the argument, numbered for reference:

(1) The only certain external sign that a being has a mind—that it thinks rather than merely reacts—is the capacity to use language in a way that is not mechanically triggered by a stimulus but is genuinely responsive to meaning. Descartes' phrase for this was the ability to arrange words to make oneself understood and to reply to the sense of everything said in one's presence.

(2) No nonhuman animal exhibits this capacity. A parrot may produce words, but it does not arrange them to express its own thoughts; it does not reply to the sense of what is said to it. Its speech is a triggered response, not a sign of thought. The same holds for every other animal species.

(3) Therefore, we have no certain external sign that any nonhuman animal has a mind. And since, for Descartes, having a mind just is having a thinking substance, and the only evidence for the presence of a thinking substance is the behavior described in (1), we may conclude that animals are mindless automata.

(4) Now consider a human being with profound global aphasia—someone who, after a stroke, has lost all capacity to produce or comprehend language. This person cannot arrange words to express thoughts, nor reply to the sense of anything said in her presence. By the criterion in (1), she exhibits no certain external sign of a mind.

(5) If the reasoning from (1) to (3) is sound, then the same reasoning—applied to the person in (4)—licenses the conclusion that she, too, is a mindless automaton.

(6) But that conclusion is obviously false, and almost no one, including Descartes, would accept it. Descartes himself insisted that all human beings possess rational souls, regardless of whether injury or illness prevents them from manifesting rational behavior.

(7) So the argument from (1) to (3) must be rejected. But which premise do we give up, and what does it cost? If we give up (1), we lose the only principled external criterion Descartes had for distinguishing minded from mindless beings—and we seem to lose any principled behavioral criterion whatsoever. If we keep (1) but reject (2) by pointing to modern evidence of animal communication, we open the door to animal minds, which is fine, but we still face the problem of (4): the criterion now seems to work only when we antecedently want it to. If we try to qualify (1) by saying that the capacity for language need only be present in potential, not in actual performance, then we have shifted the criterion from observable behavior to an unobservable potential, and we no longer have an external sign at all—we have a metaphysical commitment we can't verify.

I find every escape route unappealing, and I'm not sure the argument has a clean exit. Where does it go wrong? In your response, please follow this structure:

- **The diagnosis** (about 200 words): Identify the specific premise or inferential step that fails, and name the kind of failure it is (equivocation, false premise, scope shift, valid-but-costly, etc.).
- **Why it looked solid** (about 150 words): Explain what makes that premise or step so seductive—why a reasonable person, including Descartes, would have been drawn to it.
- **The cost of the repair** (about 200 words): Lay out what you have to give up or accept if you reject the problematic step, and what the standard positions—Cartesian, dispositionalist, behaviorist, or any other you think relevant—each have to pay.
- **My verdict** (about 100 words): Tell me which repair you think is least bad and why, and what it means for the broader question of how we know whether something has a mind.
