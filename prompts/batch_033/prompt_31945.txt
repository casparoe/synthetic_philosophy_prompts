I've been rewatching Breaking Bad with my philosophy reading group, and we've been arguing about Walter White for three weeks now. The question that keeps coming up: was Walt always a monster and the cancer diagnosis just gave him permission, or did the situation actually *make* him into one? This feels like it cuts right to the heart of the situationist critique of virtue ethics — the line from Harman and Doris that says stable character traits don't really explain behavior the way virtue ethicists think, because situational factors swamp them. The Milgram experiments, the Darley and Batson Good Samaritan study, the Stanford prison thing — all of that says what we call "character" is mostly noise and the situation is the signal.

But Breaking Bad seems to complicate this in ways that are actually philosophically interesting. Walt does terrible things, but the show spends five seasons showing us exactly how each step follows from the last, and it also gives us characters in nearly identical situations who don't go down the same path. Jesse, Mike, Gus — all in the meth trade, all shaped differently by it. So I'm trying to figure out: does the show endorse the situationist picture, where the circumstances of the diagnosis and the particular pressures of Albuquerque's meth economy gradually shape Walt into Heisenberg? Or does it take a more virtue-ethical line, where Walt's choices reveal — and then reinforce — something about his character that was latent all along? And what about the moments where the show seems to deliberately set up a situationist-style experiment and then subvert it, like when Jesse refuses to kill Gale even though every situational pressure is pushing him toward it?

I'd like a reading that takes both the show and the philosophy seriously, and that doesn't just use the show as an illustration of some pre-digested position. Where does the show push back against the situationist critique? Where does it make the virtue ethicist uncomfortable? And at the end of the day, what would change your mind about the reading you land on — what scene or argument from the other side would you have to concede to?

Please just start with the reading itself — no preamble, no "great question," just dive in.
