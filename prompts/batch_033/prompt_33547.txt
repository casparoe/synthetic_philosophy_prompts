I'm a software engineer who builds multi-agent simulation systems for a living — think distributed consensus protocols and agent-based models — and I recently fell into a rabbit hole reading about Aumann's agreement theorem. As I understand it, the theorem proves that two Bayesian agents with the same prior who have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities for some event cannot have different posteriors. In plainer terms: rational people who start from the same assumptions and know what each other believes cannot agree to disagree. This result has become something of a north star for how I think about disagreement in general. Whenever I see smart, well-informed people who have looked at broadly the same evidence and still disagree — whether in a technical design review, a policy argument, or just a heated group chat — I find myself defaulting to the view that at least one of them must be processing information irrationally, or they must have started from different priors, or their beliefs are not genuinely common knowledge between them. The theorem seems to say that persistent disagreement among reasonable people has to bottom out in one of those three things, and there is no fourth option.

But I'm aware I might be overreading a mathematical result — mistaking a theorem about idealized agents for a claim about how real people should reason. So here is what I want: if the view I just described is wrong, or importantly incomplete, I want a map of what would count against it. I'm not asking for a rehash of the standard objections (I've already skimmed some of those). I want the evidence, arguments, formal results, or empirical findings that should move someone who holds this view, sorted from most to least decisive. Are there things that have arguably already happened — results in epistemic logic, experimental economics, or elsewhere — that should already have moved me? Which defeaters would a committed defender of the view concede as counting against it, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? And I want an honest assessment of whether the view, as I've stated it, has been insulated against falsification by the way it's qualified — and if it has, whether that is a genuine problem for a view of this kind, or just the price of applying a formal theorem to the mess of real-world reasoning.
