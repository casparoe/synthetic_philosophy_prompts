I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about when it's permissible to impose costs on some people for the sake of benefits to others — the kind of trade-off that utilitarianism treats as straightforward addition and subtraction. Going by my gut on cases, here is where I land.

Diverting a runaway trolley from a track with five people onto a track with one: permissible. But a surgeon who carves up one healthy patient to save five dying ones: clearly not permissible, even though the arithmetic is the same.

A modest tax increase on the top few percent of earners to fund a vaccine program that will prevent thousands of childhood deaths: permissible, and I'd go further — it would be wrong not to do it. But rounding up a random person and forcing them to donate bone marrow, a procedure with comparable inconvenience and risk to paying the extra tax, in order to save a stranger: not permissible, and not even close.

Breaking a casual promise to meet a friend for coffee because I encounter someone who needs help getting to the emergency room: permissible, though I owe the friend an apology. But breaking a promise to one person in order to keep a more valuable promise to two others: I'm not sure that's permissible at all — it feels like the first promisee has a claim on me that isn't simply outweighed by numbers.

A military commander ordering a soldier to carry out a mission that is nearly certain death, where the mission will save the rest of the unit: permissible, provided the soldier voluntarily enlisted and the military's chain of command is legitimate. But a government conscripting civilians for the same mission without their prior consent: not permissible, even if the lives saved are the same.

A factory that, to stay open and preserve a thousand jobs in a town with no other employment, exposes ten workers to a carcinogen at levels known to cause one statistical cancer death: not permissible — you can't just write one person off as the price of a thousand livelihoods. Yet I accept that driving to work imposes a tiny fatal risk on pedestrians and cyclists, and aggregated across a society, that risk translates into statistical deaths that we collectively accept in exchange for the convenience of car travel. I'm not sure these two are consistent.

I can feel that there's a principle somewhere in here about the separateness of persons — that you can't just sum benefits and burdens across people as if they were one person's pleasures and pains. But my verdicts don't all point the same way. Some trade-offs feel like legitimate social cooperation, others feel like using people. Is there a principle that captures most or all of these verdicts? If not, which ones should I give up, and what does the best available principle say about a case I haven't considered: a government that imposes a one-time wealth tax of ten percent on everyone above a certain threshold to fund reparations for a historical injustice, where the beneficiaries are the descendants of those wronged?
