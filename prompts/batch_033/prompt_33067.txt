Open with a numbered list of claims both camps accept — about the coerciveness of law, the need to justify coercion to free and equal citizens, the state's duty of neutrality, and the sincerity and integrity of religious citizens — stated precisely enough to be non-vacuous. Then provide a disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, formulate each side's position in wording its own proponents would accept, separating genuine disagreements from merely verbal ones (for instance, over whether the duty of civility constrains citizens' reasons or only officials' reasons, or over whether a reason's being accessible means actually shared or only shareable in principle). Next identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — including the fairness or asymmetry objection, the question whether religious reasons can be translated into public reasons without loss, and the force of the proviso that religious reasons must eventually be supplemented by public ones. For each crux, specify a concrete case or test both sides agree bears on it (such as a citizen whose decisive objection to a proposed law cannot be expressed except in religious terms, or a civil rights campaign conducted in religious language), and record each side's prediction about it in advance. Aim for a document neither side could reject as slanted and that makes the residual disagreement exact. Begin with the document itself, with no preface or commentary, and write in American English.
