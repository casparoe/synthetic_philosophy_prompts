Draft the joint statement that a representative of the formal-methods camp and a representative of the relevance camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the frame problem.

By the formal-methods camp I mean the position that the frame problem, properly understood, is a problem about representing persistence in a logic of action: how to infer, within the situation calculus or a successor formalism, that a fluent survives an action unless the action is relevant to it, without writing a separate frame axiom for every action–fluent pair; and that nonmonotonic formalisms — circumscription, default logic, and the chronological-minimization refinements forced by the Yale shooting problem — have solved, or at least contained, the problem as originally posed. By the relevance camp I mean the position that the real frame problem is the one Dennett and Fodor pressed: how a finite, resource-bounded cognitive system determines which of its indefinitely many beliefs, goals, and potential inferences bear on the action it is now considering; and that the nonmonotonic solutions solve only a representation problem that presupposes exactly the relevance structure they were supposed to explain.

Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed-claims section should cover at least: the original formulation and why enumerating frame axioms is unacceptable; the distinction among the frame problem, the qualification problem, and the ramification problem; what circumscription and default logic actually accomplish; what the Yale shooting problem showed and what the accepted fixes assume; and what a deployed agent must do over and above deriving the right consequences in a representation. Phrase each agreed claim so that it is neither vacuous nor slanted.

The disagreements table should have one row per genuine residual disagreement, with each camp's view stated in wording that camp would sign; do not reduce the relevance camp to a vague worry about holism, and do not reduce the formal-methods camp to the claim that the philosophical problem is merely engineering. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify the case, argument, or experimental result both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If, in the course of drafting, you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal — for example, a disagreement only about how widely the label frame problem should be applied — say which part is verbal and state precisely what non-verbal disagreement remains.
