I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I've been following the debate about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible for a couple of years now. The literature seems to have settled into two entrenched camps. On one side, you have those who argue that genuine moral dilemmas are conceptually impossible: if an agent faces two conflicting obligations, the all-things-considered ought must resolve the conflict, otherwise we would be committed to a contradiction — that the agent both ought and ought not to perform some action — or to violating the principle that 'ought' implies 'can.' This camp often draws on deontic logic, particularly the idea that obligation agglomerates under conjunction and distributes over 'and,' to argue that conflicting obligations would entail an impossible requirement. On the other side, you have those who insist that moral experience and certain paradigm cases — agonizing tragic conflicts, cases where whatever one does one seems to violate a binding claim — reveal that agents sometimes face inescapable moral failure: whatever they do, they will have violated a genuine obligation, and the residual guilt or regret is evidence that the obligation was not simply canceled by the stronger reason. This camp often rejects the deontic-logical arguments by denying that obligations agglomerate, or by distinguishing between pro tanto obligations and all-things-considered obligations in ways that allow genuine conflict at one level but not the other.

What puzzles me is that both sides seem to agree on a great deal: they agree about the phenomenology of tragic choice, they agree that agents in these situations will feel regret or guilt whatever they do, and they often agree about what the agent should do in any given case. The disagreement seems to be about whether the "losing" obligation survives the decision — whether it remains a genuine ought that was violated, or whether it was merely an apparent ought that is extinguished once the weightier obligation is identified. And yet the debate has not moved much in decades. My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about the logic of 'ought' or about the metaphysics of obligations, but about what we are trying to do when we theorize about morality: one side wants a theory that vindicates the phenomenology of moral experience, including the sense of irresolvable loss in tragic choice, while the other side wants a theory that preserves the action-guiding clarity of moral reasoning, where there must always be a determinate answer to the question of what one should do. If that is right, then the two camps are answering different questions and the impasse is structural. But I am not fully confident in this diagnosis, and I would like to hear an alternative analysis. What kind of disagreement is this, at bottom? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that could break the stalemate? Or is this one of those debates where we should stop expecting convergence and instead ask what hangs on the choice between the two views?
