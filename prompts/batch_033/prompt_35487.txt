I'm a high school junior, and last week in my math club a friend proposed what he called an unbeatable betting game. The rules are simple: we flip a fair coin until it comes up heads. If heads appears on the first flip, I pay you $2. If it takes two flips, I pay you $4. Three flips, $8. In general, if the first heads comes on the nth flip, I pay you 2^n dollars. He then asked me what entry fee I'd consider fair to play this game. I thought about it and started calculating the expected value: there's a 1/2 chance of winning $2, a 1/4 chance of winning $4, a 1/8 chance of winning $8, and so on. The expected payoff is (1/2)·2 + (1/4)·4 + (1/8)·8 + … = 1 + 1 + 1 + …, which diverges to infinity. By that reasoning, I should be willing to pay any finite amount to play — $100, $1,000, even my entire savings — because the expected value is infinite. But that conclusion strikes me as obviously absurd. I wouldn't pay $50 to play this game, and I don't think anyone in their right mind would.

My friend insists the math is airtight and that anyone who refuses is simply being irrational. Our math club advisor overheard the discussion and smiled, saying something about a "paradox" and suggesting I look into it, but I want to understand the reasoning myself. What exactly is going on here? Is there a flaw in how I'm thinking about expected value, or is there a deeper principle that explains why the mathematical calculation and the sensible decision come apart?
