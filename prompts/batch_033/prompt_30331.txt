I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I also lead a workshop for early-career science and culture writers — about 16 people, one 90-minute session. I want to design a classroom activity about intuition pumps: the way a thought experiment can persuade through how it frames a case, before and partly independently of whether its logic establishes anything. The participants are sharp but have little formal philosophy, and I do not want to lecture them up front.

Please design a runnable activity, with rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief, that makes participants feel the force of this idea rather than hear it described. The moment of recognition should come from what they do. Ideally, they should find themselves moved one way by a version of a case and then, minutes later, moved the opposite way by another version that is logically the same case under different framing — and they should be able to point to exactly which devices did the moving. Avoid the stock cases they are likely to have met, such as the trolley problem and its variants; use something unfamiliar enough that the framing does its work live.

Constraints: no real money; any concealment of the point should be brief, benign, and disclosed in the debrief; and the term intuition pump should not be introduced to participants until the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity to the machinery of an intuition pump — vividness and availability, protagonist identification, loaded description, ordering of information, what is left out, and the difference between pumping an intuition and giving an argument for it. Participants should leave able to read a thought experiment in their own reporting and ask which details are doing the persuasive work and whether the verdict would survive their removal.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. Finally, say where your design or its philosophical gloss is most likely to fail: which empirical finding about framing, or which philosophical consideration, would lead you to redesign the activity or change its takeaway.

Please write the answer in American English.
