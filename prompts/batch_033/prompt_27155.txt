I'm a retired electrophysiologist who got into philosophy of science a couple years back, and there's a question I can't stop chewing on. In the lab we used to say things like "it's the same mechanism, it's just misbehaving" or "no, the perturbation recruited a different mechanism," and nobody ever pressed on what fixed the sameness. Now I'm reading around in the mechanistic explanation literature, and the same problem shows up in the newish work on mechanistic anomaly detection in AI interpretability: when a network does something unexpected, how do you decide whether the old mechanism is still there and malfunctioning, or a new mechanism has taken over? I want a sorting instrument for that.

Could you build me a decision tree — or a short quiz with a scoring key, whichever fits the material better — that takes someone from a concrete starting commitment to a position on what counts as the same mechanism. Build it from the logical space, not from a list of named positions: first the questions that genuinely split people apart, then locate the real positions in the cells, and flag any cells that are empty along with a sentence on why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). The branch questions should be answerable without knowing which position they lead to, and they should be concrete cases, not disguised requests for definitions.

Some cases I'd like you to use somewhere in it, because they're the ones my reading group keeps going in circles on:

- Two bacterial strains where the same regulatory outcome is produced by two different kinase cascades (I remember something like this from the literature; dont hold me to the exact system).
- A mechanical clock and a quartz clock that keep identical time.
- A neural circuit where you knock out one class of interneuron and the network compensates so the output looks the same as before.
- Two trained networks that respond to the same feature but learned different internal weights to do it.

The distinctions that seem to me to do the most work — though use better ones if the cases call for them — are: components vs. organization vs. the phenomenon produced; whether the environment or context is part of the mechanism; whether sameness is all-or-nothing or graded; whether a mechanism is individuated by what it normally does or by what it actually does on this occasion; and whether counterfactual stability under perturbation matters more than actual composition.

For leaves, I'd expect something like a strict component-identity view, an organizational view, a functional-role view, and some kind of graded or deflationary view to show up, but don't force any of them if the questions don't support them. Audience is a reading group of mostly retired scientists plus two philosophers, so technical vocabulary is fine if you define it on first use. Keep it to maybe eight to twelve leaves, and if two positions turn out to be inseparable by any question a person could actually answer, say so rather than inventing a distinction that does no real work.

One last thing: if the whole idea of "the same mechanism" is too vague or context-relative to support a taxonomy, or I've set the task up badly, tell me that plainly instead of handing me a tidy tree that papers over the problem. I'd rather learn the framing is confused than get an instrument that sorts people by which label they like.
