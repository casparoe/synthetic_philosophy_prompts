So I quit my PhD last semester — four years in, ABD, the whole thing — and I've been going back and forth on whether I made the right call or just fell for the sunk cost fallacy in reverse (like, I was so afraid of throwing good time after bad that I threw away the good time I'd already spent?). Anyway, I run this small discussion group that meets at a bar downtown, mostly grad students and a few people from industry, and next month we're doing a session on the sunk cost fallacy. I want to make a "which position on sunk costs do you actually hold?" quiz — not one of those buzzfeed things where you answer three questions and it tells you you're a Gryffindor, but something that actually sorts people by what they'd *do* in concrete cases, not by what label they like.

The thing I keep tripping over is that some people in my group think honoring sunk costs is always irrational, full stop, and others think there are good reasons to finish what you start even when the forward-looking calculus says cut and run — reasons about identity, about keeping promises to your past self, about not wanting to be the kind of person who quits. I'd like the quiz to use cases that aren't just about money (concert tickets, gym memberships, the usual textbook examples) but about life decisions where the "sunk cost" is years of your life, relationships, skills you've built, a sense of who you are. Stuff where the answer isn't obvious.

Can you write me eight to ten questions, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least four positions — the strict rational-choice view, the commitment/reputation view, the learning-value view, and the narrative-identity view (where finishing has value because it matters to the story you tell about your life). And if there are positions in logical space that nobody really holds, or that two of my categories cant tell apart, flag those too. Also, I'd specifically like the quiz to probe whether people who think they're hardline "sunk costs are irrelevant" types actually change their answer when the cost is something personal rather than financial — so maybe include a couple cases where the sunk cost is money and a parallel case where it's time or identity, and let the scoring key reveal if someone is inconsistent across those.
