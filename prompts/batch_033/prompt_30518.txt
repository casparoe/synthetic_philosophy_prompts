A friend of mine is writing a paper on moral trade, and he showed me an objection that he says he can't answer; now I can't either. The idea is that two people with different moral views can cooperate so that each does something their own view rates as mildly bad, in exchange for the other doing something their own view rates as very good — like a vegetarian donating to a poverty charity while a poverty-focused donor donates to an animal charity, so each sees the pair of donations as better than what they would have done alone. Fine. But then he ran the same logic at higher stakes and got something monstrous. Here's the argument:

(1) In a moral trade, each party does something their own view assigns negative value, and each regards the combined package as better than no trade.
(2) If a combined package is better than no trade by your own lights, then you should make the trade.
(3) Being better by your own lights is just a matter of positive value outweighing negative value, and there's no principled cutoff between mildly bad and gravely wrong acts — the negative side can always be outweighed if the positive side is big enough.
(4) So the same reasoning that makes the charitable swap okay also says a deontologist should kill one innocent person in exchange for the other party saving many innocent people, as long as by her own lights the package contains more lives saved.
(5) But a deontologist can have a decisive reason not to make that trade, even while granting that the package contains more lives saved overall.
(6) So the principle behind moral trade proves too much, and even the small-stakes swaps were never really justified.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (2) for the small-stakes cases, because it seems to capture something true about cooperation under moral disagreement, but I can't see how to keep it without getting (4). If I have to give something up, I'd rather give up (3) than (2), but I'm not sure that doesnt just smuggle in a line I can't draw.
