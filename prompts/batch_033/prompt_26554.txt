In a seminar on moral particularism, a fellow student dismissed Jonathan Dancy's attack on moral principles by saying, 'Well, hard cases make bad law, don't they?' — the thought being that the fact that moral principles break down in unusual or extreme circumstances does not show that they are not generally sound. The tutor nodded and moved on, but I have been turning it over since. What exactly is the claim 'hard cases make bad law' making when it is imported from jurisprudence into moral philosophy? I can see it might mean something about epistemic caution (do not let rare cases distort your sense of what normally matters), or something about the function of principles (they are for ordinary life, not the philosophy seminar), or something about the metaphysics of reasons (the existence of exceptions does not falsify a generalisation). Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this maxim could express, from the obviously true to the clearly false, and say which of them would actually answer Dancy's point about whether moral reasons are codifiable at all?
