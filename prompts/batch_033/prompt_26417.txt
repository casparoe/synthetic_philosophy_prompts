I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of probability module this term, and for my final essay I want to work through whether my own views on probability form a coherent package. I have ended up with commitments across metaphysics, epistemology, and statistical practice that I picked up from different parts of the course, and while each seemed sensible on its own, I suspect they do not all sit comfortably together. I would like an audit before I commit myself in writing.

Here is where I currently stand. (1) On the metaphysics of probability, I find propensity theories most plausible: the probability of a fair coin landing heads is a disposition of the coin-and-flipping-set-up, and this disposition is present even on a single toss—it is not reducible to facts about hypothetical infinite sequences. (2) I am persuaded that the reference class problem is fatal to frequentism as a metaphysical interpretation of probability: any individual event belongs to indefinitely many reference classes, and without a principled way to select among them, frequentism cannot assign unique single-case probabilities. (3) Nevertheless, in statistical practice I remain a frequentist—I reach for significance tests and confidence intervals, I am uneasy about prior distributions in published empirical work, and I think the Neyman-Pearson framework's guarantees about long-run error rates provide a kind of justification that Bayesian methods lack whenever the prior is substantially arbitrary. (4) In epistemology, I am a thoroughgoing Bayesian: rational credences are degrees of belief, they must obey the probability calculus, and updating proceeds by conditionalisation and nothing else. (5) I endorse the Principal Principle: my credence in an outcome, conditional on the proposition that its objective chance is x, should equal x. This seems to connect my epistemological Bayesianism with my metaphysical propensity view rather elegantly. (6) Finally, I am drawn to the idea that for many scientifically interesting inference problems—particularly those where symmetries constrain the problem — there is a uniquely rational prior. I find myself sympathetic to something like Jaynes's objective Bayesianism, where a suitably constrained principle of maximum entropy or principle of indifference does the work of selecting the correct starting distribution.

I can feel that some of these pull against one another, but I am not confident I can distinguish the genuine conflicts from the merely apparent ones. Could you go through the six commitments and tell me: (i) which pairs are in genuine tension and which only seem to be, explaining why each apparent tension dissolves; (ii) for the genuine tensions, rank them by severity and say what would have to give in each case; and (iii) what the single least costly revision would be to restore consistency across the whole set, and what I would lose by making it?

Please write your answer in British English.
