Here's a thing I can't stop thinking about. In a lot of common-law courts there's this hearsay rule: a witness can get on the stand and tell the jury what she personally saw, but she generally can't repeat what somebody else told her, if she's repeating it to show that what that person said was true. Even a signed statement or a recording gets excluded on that basis unless it fits one of the exceptions—dying declarations, excited utterances, business records, statements against interest, stuff like that. But the same court will happily let a witness testify from memory about something that happened years ago, so long as she's in the room and can be cross-examined. I'm trying to work out what theory of testimony this whole setup presupposes. Does it assume that believing what someone says is only justified when you've got positive reasons to trust them—so cross-examination is what's supposed to supply those reasons—or is it closer to the view that testimony is a default source of knowledge, like perception, and you're entitled to trust it unless something specific undermines it? And are the exceptions doing real philosophical work, or are they just ad hoc patches that make the underlying theory incoherent? If we actually adopted the opposite theory, what would have to change in how a trial handles secondhand reports? I want the reconstructed epistemology, not the history of the rule—and be explicit about where the practice is underdetermined, i.e., where its compatible with more than one theory.
