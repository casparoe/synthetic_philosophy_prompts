You have been asked to deliver the commencement address at a university's college of social science. You are a behavioral economist whose research has centered on bounded rationality and satisficing — the thesis, associated most famously with Herbert Simon, that human decision-makers do not and cannot optimize in the way classical rational-choice theory supposes. Instead, they search sequentially through options and stop when they find one that meets an aspiration threshold: they satisfice rather than maximize.

The graduating class and their families have just sat through a series of addresses urging them to maximize their potential, pursue excellence without compromise, and never settle for anything less than the best. You are the final speaker.

Write the address. It must be genuinely moving and inspiring to a general audience — parents and graduates who have never encountered the term "satisficing" — while remaining intellectually honest to the commitments of your own research program. You cannot simply fall back on the same optimization rhetoric dressed in slightly different language; the speech must do the harder work of making a case that a life built around "good enough" is not a life of diminished ambition but one animated by a different, and perhaps deeper, kind of wisdom. Aim for roughly eight to ten minutes when spoken aloud.
