I teach a seminar on the foundations of liberalism, and we have been wrestling with the paradox of tolerance in its applied form for the better part of a term. The abstract point — that a tolerant society need not tolerate the intolerant — earns easy agreement. But the operational question is where the line gets drawn, and our disagreements have turned out to be remarkably fine-grained. The case we keep returning to is this: a political party that contests elections, wins 12–15 per cent of the vote, and whose platform includes abolishing judicial review, restricting press freedom, and ending independent oversight of elections. The party operates legally and has not engaged in violence. Should the state (or, in the case of private platforms, should the norms of a liberal society) treat this party as beyond the pale — denying it public funding, broadcast access, and the ordinary courtesies of democratic engagement — or should it be treated as a legitimate participant in the political process until it actually breaks the law?

The disagreement in the room is not about whether the paradox of tolerance is valid in principle; it is about the empirical and institutional facts that determine whether the principle bites in this kind of case. Some students think that excluding such a party only martyrs it and drives its supporters into darker channels; others think that normalising it is precisely how Weimar happened. I have my own view, but I want to see how someone else would structure the reasoning before I tip my hand.

I would like your own credence on the following proposition: *In a contemporary liberal democracy with robust institutions, a policy of excluding a legally-operating but anti-democratic party from public funding and major platforms is more likely to preserve the democratic order over a twenty-year horizon than a policy of full inclusion.* Please decompose that credence into the sub-questions you think it most depends on — these might concern the radicalising effects of exclusion, the normalising effects of inclusion, the reliability of democratic institutions as a bulwark, the historical base rate from comparable cases, or anything else you consider diagnostic. For each sub-question, give a rough probability and a single sentence of justification. Then state your overall credence and identify which sub-question it is most sensitive to — that is, the one where a change of mind would shift your final number the most.

Please give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
