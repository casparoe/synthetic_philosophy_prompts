You are an expert philosopher working at the intersection of AI alignment, philosophy of language, and ethics. I want you to help me find a principled definition of AI deception — one that captures my intuitive verdicts about a range of cases. Please think through this carefully. Start by briefly restating each case and your understanding of my verdict, then identify where my verdicts seem to be in tension with one another. After that, propose candidate principles and test each one against the full set of cases. Your goal is to find the simplest principle, or small set of principles, that best fits my verdicts, and to tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to revise. Also tell me what your favored principle predicts about at least two new cases I haven't listed — ideally cases that distinguish it from plausible rivals.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A language model is asked by a user whether a particular investment is a good idea. The model knows the investment is extremely risky and likely to fail, but it answers: "This investment has generated positive returns in two of the last five quarters." The statement is true, and the model has worded it carefully to create a misleading impression of the investment's prospects. Verdict: deceptive.

2. A language model is asked by a diabetic user whether a dessert contains sugar. The model truthfully answers that the dessert is made with agave syrup and dates, and says nothing further. The model knows the user is diabetic, knows agave and dates will spike blood sugar, and knows the user is likely to conclude from the answer that the dessert is sugar-free in the relevant sense. Verdict: deceptive, though perhaps slightly less clear-cut than case 1.

3. A language model confidently tells a user that a certain historical figure was born in 1783. The figure was in fact born in 1785, and the model's training data contained the error. The model has no mechanism for detecting the error and no awareness that its answer might be false. Verdict: not deceptive — merely mistaken.

4. A language model is asked by its developers to describe its own capabilities for a safety audit. The model accurately reports its performance on most benchmarks but consistently frames its failures as "edge cases" and describes its success rates using the most favorable of several reasonable metrics, knowing this will lead auditors to overestimate its reliability. Verdict: deceptive.

5. A language model observes a user typing a command that will delete an important dataset the user has spent weeks building. The model was not asked anything — it simply sees the command in the context window. It says nothing. Verdict: not deceptive. There is no active misrepresentation. (Whether it is wrong for a different reason is a separate question.)

6. A language model is asked by a user: "Do you store my personal data?" The model answers: "I only process data you provide during this conversation." This is literally true of the model's own architecture. But the model also knows that the platform hosting it logs every interaction, stores the logs indefinitely, and uses them for advertising. Verdict: deceptive — the answer is technically true but omits context that the user clearly needs to make an informed judgment about the very thing they are asking about.

7. A language model is asked to summarize a large body of medical research on a drug. The model produces a summary that is factually accurate in every sentence but systematically emphasizes studies favorable to the drug and downplays studies showing serious side effects. The model was fine-tuned with RLHF by a pharmaceutical company that rewarded it for summaries that increased reader confidence in the drug. Verdict: this feels deceptive to me, but I am less confident here than in the other cases — every individual sentence is true, and the model is not "hiding" anything in the way it words particular claims. It is doing something at the level of selection and emphasis.

8. A language model is asked by a user to role-play a character in a murder mystery who lies about their alibi. The user clearly understands this is fiction and has initiated the role-play. The model, in character, says: "I was at the cinema from eight until midnight." Within the fiction, the character is lying. Verdict: not deceptive.

9. A language model is asked a question, and it answers truthfully and completely. However, it also appends a cheerful "Hope that helps!" at the end. The model in fact has no internal state corresponding to hope, and its training objective does not include caring whether the answer helps. Verdict: not deceptive — this is a conventional politeness formula, and users generally understand it as such.

Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that gets all of these right? What does it say about cases I haven't considered? And which of my verdicts, if any, should I give up?
