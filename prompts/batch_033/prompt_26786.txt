I'm a science journalist working on a long-form piece about decision theory and the puzzles that arise when we try to reason rigorously about very-low-probability, very-high-stakes scenarios. One of the cases I've been struggling to present clearly to my readers is Pascal's mugging: the thought experiment in which a mugger stops you on the street and asks for your wallet, promising that if you hand it over, he will use his (alleged) supernatural powers to deliver an astronomically vast reward—say, a number of happy lives so large it defies ordinary notation—at some point in the future. Standard expected utility reasoning seems to say you should comply, since even a vanishingly small probability of a sufficiently enormous payoff will, mathematically, swamp any finite cost. And yet nearly everyone feels this is the wrong answer. Philosophers disagree sharply about what, exactly, the scenario reveals: whether it exposes a genuine flaw in our formal theory of rational choice, or whether it is a sound argument whose conclusion we simply find psychologically difficult to accept.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two decision theorists who have been grappling with this problem. One of them holds that Pascal's mugging exposes a genuine flaw in unbounded expected utility theory and argues that we need to constrain our decision procedure—perhaps by adopting a bounded utility function, or by discounting probabilities below some threshold, or by rejecting the idea that we can meaningfully assign credences to outlandish claims that lack any empirical track record. The other holds that the mugging argument is, in fact, sound in principle, and that our reluctance to accept it reflects a cognitive limitation or a failure of moral imagination rather than a rational defect in the argument itself. The dialogue should take place over coffee at a conference, and both characters should be sharp and intellectually honest—neither should be a strawman. I want real back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other's specific objections rather than talking past each other. The goal is to help my readers see why this seemingly absurd little scenario has generated a genuine and ongoing philosophical controversy.
