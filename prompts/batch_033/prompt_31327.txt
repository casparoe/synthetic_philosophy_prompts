So I'm organizing this small workshop on bounded rationality and decision theory, and I had this idea — instead of the usual back-and-forth where everyone just restates their position, what if we actually produced an adversarial-collaboration document? Something both sides could sign. The two camps I'm thinking of are, on one side, the people who defend satisficing as a genuinely distinct normative standard (the view that it can be rationally okay to stop searching once you've found a "good enough" option, rather than always needing to find the best), and on the other side, the people who say that what looks like satisficing is really just optimizing once you properly account for search costs and computational limits — so satisficing colapses into maximization.

I've read enough of this stuff to know the standard moves. The satisficing side says: "if you include search costs, you're still optimizing, so you're missing the whole point about what it feels like to actually decide." And the optimization side says: "if you're not optimizing, you're being arbitrary about where you set the threshold — why this level of 'good enough' and not a slightly higher one?" I want to get past these moves and make the residual disagreement precise.

You're an expert in decision theory and formal epistemology who's followed this debate for years. Start by brainstorming the key pressure points each side faces and the formulations most likely to be acceptable to both. Then draft the statement with these sections:

- **Preamble** (~150 words): shared ground — what both sides agree about bounded rationality, the fact of computational limits, and what would count as progress in the debate.
- **Agreed Claims** (~300 words): a numbered list of specific claims both camps can sign, about the descriptive facts, the normative questions at issue, and the cases central to the debate.
- **Disagreements Table** (~400 words): a table with columns for (1) the disagreement phrased neutrally, (2) the satisficing camp's position in their own preferred wording, (3) the optimization camp's position in their own preferred wording, and (4) the crux — the sub-question that would resolve this row if settled.
- **Crux Analysis** (~300 words): for each crux, a concrete test, case, or argument both sides agree bears on it, plus what each side predicts about the outcome.

The whole point is a document both parties could actually put their names on. Avoid vacuous claims and don't subtly favor one side.
