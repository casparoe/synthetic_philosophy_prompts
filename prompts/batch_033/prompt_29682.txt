Long-time reader of LessWrong and the EA forum here, so I know the standard back-and-forth and I'm not looking for an intro. Over the years I've come around to the measurability-bias version of the institutional critique of EA, and I want to stress-test it properly. My view, roughly: the machinery EA uses to pick causes — cost-effectiveness estimates, expected-value calcuations, RCT-heavy evidence standards, the hits-based-giving ethos, the whole spreadsheet mindset — acts as a filter. It doesn't just measure which interventions do good; it selects for interventions whose good effects are legible in a particular way: near-term, countable, attributable to a specific actor, and expressible as a number. That means the movement systematically underweights work that's diffuse, slow, structural, or aimed at preventing things that haven't happened yet, and then mistakes the filter for neutral rigor. Not a claim that the numbers are wrong in individual cases; a claim about what the aggregate selection process does to the portfolio.

What would actually count against this? I want the defeaters, not the talking points. Specifically: what real-world patterns in EA's actual grantmaking or cause-prioritization over the last decade would make me update away from the measurability story — and which of those patterns have already shown up? What would the funding landscape have to look like for the neutral-instruments story to beat the filter story? Are there arguments that the selection effect is much weaker than it looks, because the same apparatus also gets used to justify the weird stuff, so the filter is less directional than I'm claiming? Which parts of the view are almost certainly true but trivial, and which part is the load-bearing controversial bit? And has this critique insulated itself — if EA funds a bunch of unmeasurable stuff, do I just call it an exception rather than a falsification — and if it has, is that a problem for a structural thesis of this kind, or is it just what you'd expect? I'd like the defeaters ordered from the ones that would actually make me drop the view down to the ones that would only make me add a caveat, and I'd want to know which of them a committed measurability-bias person would concede count against them rather than dismiss. If the honest conclusion is that the view is mostly insulated and that's fine, say so.

And please, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose, like an email. I don't mind length.
