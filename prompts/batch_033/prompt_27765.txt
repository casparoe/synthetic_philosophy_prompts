I've come to accept the view that at least some split-brain patients house two distinct centers of consciousness. On this picture, once the corpus callosum is severed, each hemisphere can support its own stream of experience, and the contents of one stream need not be co-conscious with the contents of the other. The left hemisphere's confabulated explanations of actions the right hemisphere has initiated are not, on my view, the voice of a single unified subject; they are one conscious system narrating another's behavior as if it were its own. The unity of ordinary consciousness is a contingent achievement of an intact brain, not a necessary feature of consciousness as such.

I want to know what would count against this, with the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which experimental outcomes or re-descriptions of the classic data should move me most? For example: what would it take to establish that the right hemisphere's apparently intelligent behavior is better explained as unconscious, automatized processing than as a second stream of experience? How rich would residual subcortical integration between the hemispheres have to be before the two-streams reading loses its empirical basis? Should I be moved by the parity worry that if the hemispheres support two centers of consciousness, then the modularity of each hemisphere threatens to multiply centers without limit? Does the view make predictions its main rival — one subject with divided access rather than divided phenomenal consciousness — does not: about patients who acquire right-hemisphere language, about what the left hemisphere does when shown evidence of the other's competence, or about how laboratory dissociations relate to the patient's everyday behavior? Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether this position has insulated itself by retreating from "two persons" to "two streams" to "partially distinct streams," and of whether the claim that consciousness is necessarily unified is the kind of claim no empirical result could touch. If it is, tell me whether that is a problem for a view like mine, or a feature of the dialectic I should accept.
