I'm a software engineer (no formal philosophy background) working on a recommender system, so I started reading the econ side on preference inference, and I've managed to talk myself into thinking revealed preference theory is empty. That feels wrong, so I want a diagnosis. Here's the argument I keep building:

(1) Revealed preference says a person prefers X to Y just in case they choose X when Y was available.
(2) So every observed choice reveals a preference, by definition.
(3) Utility is just a numerical representation of those revealed preferences: higher number for the thing chosen.
(4) So whatever a person does, they are maximising utility relative to the preferences their own choices reveal.
(5) Therefore 'people maximise utility' can never be false; no pattern of choices could count as a counterexample.
(6) A claim that can't be false has no content, so utility theory is just curve-fitting after the fact.

Each step looks fine on its own and the conclusion seems too strong, because economists clearly do get empirical milage out of choice and demand data. I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), since they look like definitions, but I don't know what I'm paying. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the diagnosis, not just the answer: why the bad step looked fine and what the alternatives are. Also, what would a batch of choices have to look like to actually fail to be representable by any utility function? Please answer in British English.
