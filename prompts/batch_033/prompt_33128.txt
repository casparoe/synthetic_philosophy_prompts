We mostly read things at the intersection of decision theory, philosophy, and AI alignment — the sequences on LessWrong, some of the classic Bostrom and Yudkowsky pieces, and occasionally we dip into the academic literature. Lately we've been discussing the problem of value aggregation and utility functions, and I've gotten interested in the idea that some values might be genuinely incommensurable — that is, not just unknown or hard to weigh, but not even comparable on a single scale. I've been reading some of Ruth Chang's work on hard choices and parity (though I'll admit I'm not a philosopher, so I'm probably missing some nuance), and I want to design a 45-minute activity for our next session that makes people feel why this isn't just a failure of measurement but a real feature of how we evaluate options.

Here's what I have in mind for the setting: we meet in a conference room, 12–15 people, all technically minded but without formal philosophy training. I want an activity where participants are forced to make a series of choices between options that pull in genuinely different directions — maybe career paths, life decisions, or even tradeoffs in product design — and then discover that they can't produce a consistent ranking, or that small improvements to one option don't reliably flip their preferences the way a utility-maximization model would predict. Ideally the activity should be structured enough that we get clean results to discuss, but open-ended enough that people's actual values come into play. I don't want to lecture; I want them to run into the problem themselves and feel the weirdness of it.

Could you design the activity for me? I need: (1) concrete rules, prompts, or decision scenarios (maybe on printed cards), (2) a step-by-step timeline for the 45 minutes, (3) a debrief script that maps what happened in the activity onto the conceptual distinction between incomparability, commensurability, and the "parity" idea (where options are comparable but not ranked), and (4) an honest diagnostic of what will predictably go wrong — e.g., people trying to force everything into a single metric, or getting stuck on the idea that all choices are just preference-revealing. I'd also appreciate a note on what the activity can't capture — for instance, whether it works differently for personal values than for public policy choices, or whether the "parity" concept requires more philosophical machinery than the activity can deliver.
