I was at a philosophy meetup last week and two people got into a really tangled arguement about Molyneux's problem that I've been turning over in my head ever since. You probably know the setup: Molyneux asked Locke whether a person born blind, who could tell a cube from a sphere by touch, would recognize them by sight alone if suddenly given vision. The argument went like this:

Sasha kicked things off by saying the question is basically settled now — we've done the experiment. There are kids born with dense cataracts in places like rural India, and when they get surgery as older children, they can't immediately match what they see to what they've touched. The answer is no. Lena pushed back hard: she said that's not what Molyneux was asking at all. He was asking whether the *idea* of shape is amodal — whether there's a single concept of cubeness that you can access through either sense. The cataract kids are just experiencing a perceptual integration problem, like learning to use a new tool. Sasha said that's moving the goalposts: if the question was "can they do it," we checked, they can't, and calling it a "perceptual integration problem" is just a redescription. Lena came back with: so what if we design a perfect experiment — someone who's fully awake, no surgical trauma, and we give them unlimited time to inspect the objects? Would that settle it? Sasha said that's not a real experiment, it's science fiction, and Lena said exactly — that's the point, the original question has a conceptual core that no empirical result can fully touch because it's about what it *means* to have a shape concept across modalities. Then Sasha said if the question is purely conceptual, why did anyone ever think it was empirical, and Lena said because Locke and Molyneux were empiricists who didn't fully distinguish the two — which made Sasha roll her eyes and say that's just the kind of thing philosophers say when the data goes against them.
