I teach an undergraduate aesthetics course, and next week we turn to the problem of fakes and forgeries — roughly, the question of why, or indeed whether, an original artwork should be valued differently from a perceptually indistinguishable copy. The standard philosophical touchstones are familiar: Denis Dutton on the experience of discovering a forgery as a kind of personal betrayal, Nelson Goodman on the distinction between autographic and allographic arts, and Arthur Danto on the way art-historical context constitutes the identity of a work. But I have been wondering whether Donna Tartt's novel *The Goldfinch* might do more for my students than any of those papers. The novel's protagonist, Theo Decker, steals Carel Fabritius's painting *The Goldfinch* from the wreckage of a museum bombing and carries it through his life as a secret, a burden, and a kind of talisman. For long stretches, the physical painting is hidden away — nobody sees it, nobody knows it survives — and yet it shapes Theo's entire trajectory. The novel also features a subplot involving forged antiques and a character, Hobie, who restores old furniture and wrestles with the ethics of passing off his restorations as originals. What I would like from you is a reading of *The Goldfinch* through the philosophical literature on fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals. Where does the novel dramatize claims that philosophers have made — for instance, that the value of an original is relational rather than intrinsic, or that our attachment to originals is a kind of fetishism we should see through? Where does the novel complicate or resist those claims? And what, if anything, does the novel add to the philosophical debate that the papers do not capture — something about the private, almost magical relationship a person can have to an object whose history only they know? I am not looking for a summary of the plot or the philosophy; I want the kind of reading that treats both the novel and the arguments with equal seriousness and says honestly where they illuminate each other and where they talk past each other.
