I spent my career as a research physicist before retiring, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy in a systematic way. One thing that has particularly absorbed me is the intersection between ancient accounts of weakness of will and modern behavioural economics. I have been working through Aristotle's treatment of akrasia in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics alongside the literature on hyperbolic discounting — the finding, associated principally with George Ainslie, that humans and other animals discount future rewards according to a hyperbola-like function rather than an exponential one. This produces time-inconsistent preferences: an agent may sincerely prefer on Monday to exercise on Wednesday, yet when Wednesday arrives prefer to stay on the sofa, even though nothing about the options has changed except the agent's temporal distance from them. The phenomenon sounds very much like the one Aristotle was trying to explain: knowing the better and doing the worse.

I would like you to engage in a careful counterfactual exercise. Suppose Aristotle were presented with the hyperbolic discounting model in its full modern form — the mathematics of the discount function, the notion of preference reversal, the picture of intertemporal choice as a struggle between successive selves, and the strategies the model suggests for self-management, such as precommitment and bundling. Aristotle could not have anticipated the formal apparatus, but the behavioural pattern it captures is squarely within the scope of his inquiry into akrasia.

The question is this: would Aristotle have regarded the hyperbolic discounting model as a vindication of his own analysis, a rival to it, or something orthogonal to his project? In your answer, please do the following.

First, lay out the architecture of Aristotle's account with enough precision that the comparison can be made. Specifically, address the role of the practical syllogism, the distinction he draws between akrasia driven by anger (thumos) and akrasia driven by appetite (epithumia), and the notoriously difficult question of the sense in which the akratic agent possesses knowledge — the way the agent knows the relevant universal premise but is not using or attending to the particular premise in the moment of action, which Aristotle likens to the knowledge of someone asleep, mad, or drunk.

Second, lay out the hyperbolic discounting model with comparable precision: the shape of the discount function and how it differs from exponential discounting, the mechanics of preference reversal, and the rationale behind precommitment and bundling as responses to anticipated reversals.

Third, and this is the heart of the exercise: predict, with detailed justification, how Aristotle would assess the model. Would he see it as capturing the formal structure of what he described qualitatively? Would he object that it omits the role of the passions in a way that makes it a description of behaviour but not an explanation of the phenomenon? Would he regard the 'successive selves' framing as a category mistake — a confusion between distinct agents and a single agent whose motivational structure shifts with temporal proximity? Or might he have welcomed it as a kind of mathematical sharpening of his own distinction between the agent who has knowledge and uses it and the agent who has knowledge but is not presently in possession of it?

Finally, offer your own view on whether the two frameworks are genuine competitors — whether they are attempting to answer the same question with different tools, or whether they address different questions altogether and the appearance of overlap is misleading.

I would be grateful if your answer were written in British English.
