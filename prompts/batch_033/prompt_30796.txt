I'm a retired chemist — thirty-odd years running a spectroscopy lab — and I've recently gotten into philosophy through a reading group at the university here. Last week two of the regulars, Dana and Viktor, got into an argument about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and I left more confused than when I arrived. I'm used to disputes that can be settled with a good instrument, so I'd appreciate a careful referee. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Dana said she can't take illusionism seriously because it's self-refuting. If phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, then someone has to be undergoing the illusion. And undergoing an illusion is itself a conscious experience — it feels like something. So the view denies the very thing it needs to get off the ground.

(2) Viktor said that's the standard objection and he doesn't think it lands. Illusionists don't deny that we have experiences or that we can be fooled. They deny that experiences have a special private qualitative glow — the "phenomenal" bit. An illusion is just a misrepresentation, and misrepresentation doesn't require a felt quality. A smoke detector can misread its input without there being anything it's like to be a smoke detector.

(3) Dana replied that a smoke detector doesn't feel misled. The whole reason the hard problem is hard is that we aren't just processing information — there's something it's like. If you take that away, what's left to be fooled? Illusionism just relocates the mystery: now you have to explain how a purely physical system becomes convinced, from the inside, that it has qualia. That conviction is exactly the kind of thing that needs a subject.

(4) Viktor granted the relocation but said relocation is progress if the new problem is tractable. The new problem is about introspective representations — why our cognitive systems produce simplified, "phenomenal" glosses of there own processing. That's a hard problem in cognitive science, but it isn't the hard problem of how physical stuff produces private feels. The mystery of a judgment is a mystery about representation, which we already need to solve anyway.

(5) Dana said that's too quick. If the gloss has no phenomenal character, then it's just a physical event — a bit of neural machinery emitting a report. But then why would anyone be so overwhelmingly convinced that there's something it's like? You've turned the one thing we know most directly into an unexplained side effect of some representational system, and the conviction itself is still conscious. You can't deflate the thing by calling it a judgment unless you explain what it is for a judgment to feel like something — and that's the original problem again.

(6) Viktor said she keeps saying "feel like" as if that settles it. The whole point of illusionism is that the feeling is itself part of the misrepresented gloss. The judgment that there's something it's like is a representational state, and representational states can be systematically wrong about there own nature. That isn't circular; it's a hypothesis about introspection.

They went back and forth in this vein for half an hour and never converged. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about which point, where each of them overreaches or talks past the other, and who has the better case overall? And is there anything important both of them are missing?

Oh, and could you write your answer in British English?
