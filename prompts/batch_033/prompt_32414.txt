I'm taking a philosophy of language course this semester, and we've been working through speech act theory. I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about when performative utterances succeed or fail, going by my gut reactions to cases rather than by any theory I've read. Here are my verdicts so far:

1. A judge, in full courtroom procedure during a sentencing hearing, says "I sentence you to ten years." The defendant has been properly convicted and is present. Verdict: the sentencing succeeds.

2. The same judge, at a dinner party, raises a glass and says jokingly to a friend, "I sentence you to doing the dishes." Everyone laughs. Verdict: no sentencing occurs.

3. A friend says "I promise to pay you back next week" and fully intends to do so. Verdict: the promise succeeds.

4. A friend says "I promise to pay you back next week" with no intention of ever doing so, but I believe them and lend them the money. Verdict: the promise still succeeds — they've genuinely promised, even though they're insincere. They're on the hook.

5. A ship's captain, at an official launching ceremony with the champagne bottle and the assembled guests, says "I name this ship the Aurora" and smashes the bottle. Verdict: the ship is named.

6. A random spectator at the same ceremony, just before the captain speaks, shouts "I name this ship the Garbage Barge!" Verdict: the ship is not named the Garbage Barge — nothing happens.

7. Two close friends are bantering. One says "I bet you five dollars it rains tomorrow." Both are laughing, and neither expects money to change hands. Verdict: no bet has been made.

8. Two colleagues are discussing the weather seriously. One says "I bet you five dollars it rains tomorrow." The other says "You're on." Verdict: a bet has been made.

9. A licensed officiant says "I now pronounce you married" to a couple at a wedding ceremony with witnesses, and the couple has a valid marriage license. Verdict: they are married.

10. The same officiant says "I now pronounce you married" to the same couple in the same setting, but unbeknownst to the officiant, the marriage license is forged. Verdict: I'm genuinely torn here. Legally they aren't married, but it feels like the speech act went through in some sense — the officiant did everything right from their end.

11. An employee, in a heated argument with their boss, says "I quit" and walks out. The boss takes it seriously. An hour later the employee regrets it and claims they didn't really mean it. Verdict: they quit.

12. The same employee, during a relaxed Friday afternoon, sighs and says "ugh, I quit" while laughing about a difficult project. The boss laughs too. Verdict: they didn't quit.

I can feel that there's something about the combination of speaker authority, appropriate circumstances, and maybe uptake by the hearer, but I can't get these verdicts to cohere into a single picture. In particular, cases 4 and 11 seem to commit me to the view that a performative can succeed even when the speaker is insincere, as long as the external conditions are met — but case 7 seems to pull the other way, where the joking context defeats the act even though the words were said. And case 10 keeps bothering me: if I say the marriage succeeds as a speech act despite the forged license, I seem to be saying that the speaker's reasonable beliefs about the circumstances matter more than the actual circumstances, which would force me to rethink case 2 (what if the dinner-party judge somehow believed they were in court?).

Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures most or all of these verdicts? If some of them have to go to make the picture consistent, which ones should I give up and why? And what does the resulting account say about a case I haven't listed: a person who says "I apologize" in a flat, clearly insincere tone to satisfy a social requirement, where everyone present knows they don't mean it?
