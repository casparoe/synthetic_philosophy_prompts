Two friends of mine got into it over dinner last week and I still can't sort out who was right. The topic was whether individuals have any obligation to reduce their carbon footprint given that one person's emissions are a drop in the bucket.

Here's roughly how it went.

Maya kicked it off by saying she'd stopped feeling guilty about flying because her individual choice makes no detectable difference to climate outcomes. "The plane's going to fly whether I'm on it or not," she said. "My seat might even get filled by someone else."

Sam pushed back: "If everyone reasoned like that, nobody would reduce their emissions and we'd be cooked. You can't just ask what happens if *you* act — you have to ask what happens if everyone adopts your policy."

Maya said that's exactly the wrong way to think about it. "The question is whether *my* action causes any harm. It doesn't. The plane flies either way. So generalizing to everyone is changing the subject — you're asking a different question about what rule I'd want universally followed, not whether my actual act makes a difference."

Sam came back with: "Fine, but there's still a tiny chance your flight is the one that pushes the airline over some threshold — like, if enough people fly, they add a route, and maybe you're the marginal passenger. The expected harm might be small but it's not zero, and multiplied by the stakes it's still significant."

Maya replied that this threshold argument cuts both ways. "The probability that I'm the threshold passenger is so tiny that the expected value calculation is basically a rounding error. And anyway, for every threshold I might trigger in the bad direction, there's some symmetric threshold I might trigger in the good direction — like, if I fly less, maybe the airline cuts a route and someone else drives instead, which is worse. The causal chain is just too long and noisy for expected value reasoning to give a determinate answer."

At this point Leah jumped in and said they were both missing the point. "The obligation isn't causal at all. It's about integrity. If you believe climate change is a moral catastrophe, you can't coherently believe that and then act as if it doesn't matter. Your actions have to express your values, otherwise you're a hypocrite. So Maya does have a reason not to fly, but it's not the reason Sam was giving."

Sam liked this but Maya didn't. "That's just moving the goalposts. Now we're not talking about harm at all — we're talking about self-expression. And if the obligation is just to express my values, then I can offset my flight and call it a day. The original question was wether my individual act makes a difference to the outcome, and the answer is still no."

Leah said: "But the original question is badly framed. You're assuming the only kind of reason that counts is a causal one. That's question-begging."

And that's about where they left it. I'd like you to go through this exchange move by move and adjudicate: who's right about what, which arguments hold up and which don't, and whether anyone is making a category mistake that the others are rightly catching. Don't just declare a winner — tell me what each person gets right and wrong, and whether there's a way to reframe the whole thing that makes more sense than any of their individual positions.
