I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on concepts, categories, and creativity — fourteen students, seventy-five minutes per session — and I want to devote one meeting to the problem the Letter Spirit project raises: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? I mean the puzzle that a letter can appear in radically different hands, weights, and typefaces — a blackletter 'a', a geometric sans-serif 'a', a child's careful ball-and-stick 'a', a rushed italic 'a' — and still be read unhesitatingly as an 'a', while an 'o' or a 'd' that shares more local features with one of those forms is not. I want the session to make students feel, before it is named, that a letter category is neither a fixed geometric template nor a checklist of strokes, but something closer to a system of roles and pressures: parts, junctions, proportions, and a 'style' that distributes variation consistently across the whole alphabet. Design a classroom activity or game in which they produce, sort, deform, or communicate letterforms under constraints, and in doing so discover for themselves where the boundary of 'a'-ness can be pushed and what they have to trade away to preserve it. Ideally the rules should force the tension between categorical identity and stylistic consistency — for instance, by requiring them to keep a letter recognizable while bending it to match a shared style, or to build a whole alphabet in one style and then judge where individual letters break down. But I am open to a different mechanism if you see one that works better. There should be no lecture up front, and the phrases 'Letter Spirit' and 'essence of a letterform' should not be uttered until the debrief.

The deliverable should include a full seventy-five-minute timeline; a list of materials (paper, pens, cards, projectable grids, or whatever the activity needs); precise rules and, if there are rounds or scoring, a payoff structure that does not require real money; and a debrief that maps concrete moments from the activity onto the philosophical distinctions at stake — the letter level versus the style level, the roles played by a letterform's parts, and the idea that what is essential to 'a' is not a shape but a set of constraints satisfiable in many different ways. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students converging on a single prototypical 'a' and never pushing the boundary, or the exercise collapsing into free drawing, or the scoring overwhelming the looking — and how to head those failures off or turn them into material for the debrief. Finally, be honest about which feature of the Letter Spirit idea the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, the computational claim that such a space of letterforms could be generated by a program — and say how much of that I will need to supply myself after the session.
