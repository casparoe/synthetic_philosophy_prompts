I retired a few years ago from a career in experimental biology, and I have lately been reading moral philosophy, mostly with a small discussion group. We recently spent several weeks on antinatalism, and one member of the group offered the following argument, which he claimed shows that the asymmetry at the center of Benatar's position has consequences far beyond anything its defenders accept. I was trained to distrust arguments that outrun their evidence, and my instinct is that this one does; but each step looks plausible to me, and every objection I try to formulate seems to beg the question against one of the premises.

Here is the argument as he gave it.

(1) There is a moral asymmetry between pain and pleasure when comparing a life that is lived with a life that is never begun: the presence of pain in the lived life is bad; the presence of pleasure in the lived life is good; the absence of pain in the never-begun life is good even though no one is there to enjoy it; and the absence of pleasure in the never-begun life is not bad, because no one is there to be deprived of it.

(2) From this it follows that, in the comparison between bringing a person into existence and never bringing that person into existence, the pain side always favors never bringing them into existence, while the pleasure side can at best leave the two options tied. Coming into existence is therefore never an advantage over never existing, and it is a positive disadvantage whenever the life would contain any pain.

(3) But the same asymmetry can be applied to the comparison between a person's continuing to exist for some further stretch of time and that person's ceasing to exist before that stretch begins. If the person continues, any pain in that stretch is bad; if the person ceases, the absence of that pain is good. If the person continues, any pleasure in that stretch is good; if the person ceases, the absence of that pleasure is not bad, since once the person is gone there is no subject to be deprived of it.

(4) By the same reasoning as in (2), then, continuing to exist is never an advantage over ceasing to exist, and it is a positive disadvantage whenever the future would contain any pain.

(5) The asymmetry therefore collapses the very distinction it was introduced to preserve — the distinction between not starting a life and ending one. It implies that, for any person whose future would contain some pain, a painless ceasing to exist is better than going on.

(6) But that conclusion is one almost no one accepts, including most antinatalists.

So at least one step must go, or the asymmetry must be restricted in a way that blocks the transfer from (2) to (4). The restrictions I have tried seem either ad hoc or damaging to the original antinatalist argument. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to hold on to (1) and resist (5), but I cannot see how to do both without rejecting (3) or (4), and I cannot tell which rejection is principled rather than ad hoc.
