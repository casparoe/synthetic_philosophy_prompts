For a long essay I am writing on moral partiality and self-alienation, I have been interviewing a philosopher whose work is organized around a well-known worry in Bernard Williams: that a person can save a loved one and yet, by adding a covering justification, have what is often called one thought too many. Over several conversations, I have collected his verdicts on a series of short scenarios. In the familiar case of the husband who rescues his wife from drowning, he says the husband whose fully spelled-out reason is simply that it was his wife has the right shape of motive, while the husband whose fully spelled-out reason is that it was his wife and that a permission applies to situations of this kind has had one thought too many. His judgments on other cases track this. A woman who lies to protect her brother from a minor legal penalty is fine when her thought is that he is her brother, but not when her thought is that he is her brother and that family relationships ground special permissions. A firefighter who carries an injured friend to safety is fine when the thought is that he is his friend; a parent who donates a kidney to her child is fine when the thought is that she is her daughter. So far, he has only tested cases in which the extra thought is an impartial or generally formulated moral permission.

The case I want to put to him next is designed to press on that gap. An emergency physician is first to reach a crash site. Several casualties need immediate stabilization; one is her husband. She treats him first. Asked afterward for her fully spelled-out reason, she says: he is my husband—and as a physician, I would not be criticized for treating a family member first in these circumstances. The appended thought is not an impartial moral principle, but a local professional-role norm attached to an identity she has voluntarily taken on. Please reconstruct the leading rules consistent with his prior verdicts, explain what each predicts for the physician case, offer your best prediction with the strongest reason for preferring one reading over the others, and name a further vignette that would most efficiently separate the remaining candidates. End with \answer{one thought too many} or \answer{not one thought too many}.
