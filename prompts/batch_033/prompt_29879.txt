ok so here's something that's been bugging me. the prosaic alignment crowd — and by now basically every major lab — treats RLHF as the main thing you do to make a language model "aligned". the practice is: you have human raters compare pairs of model outputs and pick which one they prefer, you train a reward model on those comparisons, and then you fine-tune the LLM to maximise that reward. and everyone talks about this as "aligning the model with human values" or "human preferences."

but i want to know what theory of value this whole setup actually presupposes. not what the researchers say they believe — what the practice itself commits you to. for instance: does it presuppose that values are revealed by pairwise choices (like in revealed preference theory in econ)? does it presuppose that human values are consistent enough that a single reward model can capture them? does it presuppose that "human values" is a thing you can get at by averaging over individual raters, or does that move already smuggle in a kind of utilitarian aggregation? and if you were a constructivist who thinks values emerge from deliberation rather than being pre-existing preferences you can elicit, would the whole RLHF pipeline be philosophically incoherant, or just suboptimal?

i guess what i'm asking is: reconstruct the implicit theory of value that RLHF-as-alignment presupposes. distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with or what the practioners happen to believe. and then tell me what parts of the pipeline would have to change if you replaced that theory with a substantively different one — say, a view where values are intersubjectively constituted, or where they're facts about welfare that preference elicitation can only ever approximate.
