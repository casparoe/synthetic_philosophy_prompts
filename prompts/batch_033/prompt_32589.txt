When the Treasury evaluates a proposal—say, a flood defence scheme that will prevent damage for decades, or a programme to phase out a carcinogenic industrial chemical—it applies a discount rate to future costs and benefits, so that a life saved thirty years from now counts for much less in the appraisal than a life saved this year. The same mathematics that makes a pound today worth more than a pound next year is applied to human wellbeing, to ecosystem collapse, to the extinction of a species. The practice is defended by appeal to the opportunity cost of capital, to the fact that future people will probably be richer than we are, and to the empirical claim that people themselves discount their own future consumption. But wellbeing is not consumption, and the people whose lives are discounted are not the same people as those doing the discounting. I have recently been asked to contribute to a review of our department's appraisal methodology, and I find I cannot get clear on what moral picture this practice actually commits us to. Is it strictly a matter of efficiency—a technical constraint on public spending that says nothing about the relative value of lives across time—or does the practice presuppose a substantive view about the moral weight of future people's interests? If it does presuppose a view, what is it: that distance in time diminishes a person's claim on us, or that we have weaker obligations to people who do not yet exist, or simply that the living are entitled to prioritise themselves? And if we replaced the Treasury's discount rate with a near-zero rate for wellbeing, as some philosophers argue we should, what else in the appraisal framework would have to change—would the entire practice of cost-benefit analysis become unworkable, or would it merely produce different recommendations? Please answer in British English.
