I've spent a while thinking about a moral view I first met under the label Scanlon's contractualism, and I've come to find it compelling: roughly, an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by a principle that no one could reasonably reject, where reasonable rejection is judged from the standpoint of each person rather than from the standpoint of the group taken as a whole. The thing that keeps bothering me is the aggregation problem. If you can save either one person or five people, the view seems unable to explain why you should save the five. Each of the five has a claim to be saved, and the one has a claim to be saved; if you refuse to add the five claims together into a larger claim, the single person's claim looks just as strong as the claim of any one of the five, and the view seems stuck between saying numbers don't matter and quietly helping itself to the very aggregation it claims to reject.

I want you to treat this contractualist position as a live hypothesis of mine and tell me what would count against it. Start by reconstructing the aggregation objection in its strongest form, then lay out the position's exposure: which objections, cases, or commitments should move me most; which are merely awkward; which would defenders themselves concede count against the view; and which would they dismiss as question-begging or as resting on a rival picture. I would also like an honest judgment about whether the position has been refined over time in ways that insulate it from counterexamples, and whether that insulation is a sign of maturity or a problem. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.

Please answer as a professional moral philosopher who works on contractualism and aggregation. Think carefully: before committing to a verdict, brainstorm the strongest versions of both the objection and the replies, and make clear where you are stating a point of general agreement and where you are taking a side in a live debate.
