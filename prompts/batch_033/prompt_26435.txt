"The constructivist challenge to realism, as Korsgaard develops it, is that the realist pictures normative reasons as free-floating rationales — normative facts that simply exist independently of us and that we are supposed to somehow apprehend and be moved by. The core worry is that if reasons are free-floating in this way, it becomes mysterious how we could ever come to know them, since they wouldn't be constituted by our own practical reasoning. It also becomes mysterious how they could motivate us: if a reason is just a fact out there in the world, recognizing it shouldn't necessarily move us to act, and the realist can't explain the connection between normative judgment and motivation without appealing to something like a special faculty of intuition.

Parfit is vulnerable to this challenge because he defends a form of non-naturalist realism on which there are irreducibly normative truths about what we have reason to do. Since he holds that these truths are independent of our desires and attitudes, he is committed to the existence of free-floating rationales. Parfit tries to avoid this by saying that normative truths are not metaphysical in any problematic sense — they are non-ontological, merely truths about what matters — but this is a distinction without a difference. If a truth is independent of all human attitudes and concerns, it is free-floating by definition, regardless of whether we call it 'metaphysical' or not.

This shows that anyone who accepts agent-neutral reasons is committed to free-floating rationales. Agent-neutral reasons are reasons that apply to all agents regardless of their particular desires, and if a reason applies to you regardless of what you care about, then it doesn't depend on your practical reasoning — it's free-floating with respect to you. So the distinction between agent-neutral reasons and free-floating rationales that the question asks about turns out to be no real distinction at all. Nagel's view in *The View from Nowhere*, for instance, is that there are agent-neutral reasons that we discover by taking up an impartial standpoint, and this is just another way of saying that there are free-floating rationales accessible from a detached perspective. The realist should simply bite the bullet and accept that there are free-floating rationales, rather than trying to pretend this isn't what their view amounts to."
