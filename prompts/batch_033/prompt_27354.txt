I am giving a workshop talk next month in which I defend a version of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. The thesis I will defend is this. There is an intensity threshold on suffering such that the prevention or relief of any pain above that threshold lexically dominates every other moral consideration: no finite amount of happiness, and no finite amount of avoided below-threshold pain, can outweigh it. Below the threshold, pleasure and pain remain commensurable on an ordinary cardinal scale, and familiar trade-offs between them are legitimate. The threshold is meant to mark the point at which pain becomes totalizing: it dominates the subject's experiential field, blocks the pursuit of all other goods, and gives the subject's experience a character that pleasure does not have in reverse. My argument, compressed, is as follows. First, the phenomenology of intense pain is structurally unlike the phenomenology of intense pleasure, not merely its negative image on a single valence scale. Second, because of that, the good of relieving such pain and the good of promoting happiness are not quantities of one kind, and no amount of the latter can compensate for the former. Third, a threshold is required because it would be implausible to give every pain, however mild, this lexical status. Fourth, the resulting view captures the widely shared conviction that, past a certain point, suffering is not the sort of thing happiness can make up for.

The audience is a mixed workshop of value theorists, welfare economists, and two animal welfare scientists. The largest contingent are classical utilitarians who take well-being to be cardinal and fully commensurable and who treat any pain-pleasure asymmetry as a bias to be corrected rather than a structural feature of value. There is also a group of prioritarian-leaning philosophers who accept that worse-off individuals count for more but are suspicious of discontinuities and lexical priorities as ad hoc. The welfare economists think in terms of disability weights, quality-adjusted life years, and willingness-to-pay measures, and they will resist any claim that some goods cannot be represented on a common scale. No one in the room is already committed to negative utilitarianism.
