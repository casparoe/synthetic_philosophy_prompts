I am preparing a seminar on decision theories that are tested against predictors able to condition on an agent's decision procedure, and I want the specification of the problem before I read the candidate theories. Write out the desiderata that any adequate theory of rational choice in these scenarios must satisfy: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the demands it cannot simply inherit from one camp as if they were neutral.

The motivating cases must include at least the following three. Newcomb's problem. Counterfactual mugging: a perfect predictor flips a fair coin; if it comes up tails, it asks you for $100, and if it had come up heads, it would have paid you $10,000 if it predicted that you would have paid in the tails case. XOR blackmail: the predictor sends you a message just in case either your house is infested with termites or you will pay it $1,000, but not both, where the termite damage would cost you far more than the payment.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one of the major camps (causal, evidential, updateless, functional). I especially want a considered answer to these questions. First, can the requirements that push toward paying in counterfactual mugging and toward refusing to pay in XOR blackmail be jointly satisfied, or does the apparent conflict dissolve once the requirements are stated precisely? Second, which single requirement, if any, does the most to exclude familiar candidate theories? Third, are the requirement to evaluate policies rather than acts and the requirement to be updateless one desideratum or two, and which of them does the actual work in these cases? Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases; if you score evidential, causal, updateless, and functional decision theory against the list, do so briefly at the end. Please answer in American English.
