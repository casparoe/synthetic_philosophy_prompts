I've noticed a recurring pattern in committee decisions that I don't have a word for, and I'm hoping you can tell me whether it has an established name and how people think about resolving it.

Suppose a three-judge panel is deciding a contract case. The defendant is liable only if two conditions both hold: there was a valid contract, and there was a breach. The judges vote as follows:

Judge A: contract yes, breach no → not liable
Judge B: contract no, breach yes → not liable
Judge C: contract yes, breach yes → liable

Each judge is perfectly consistent individually. But if the panel votes issue-by-issue, they get: contract yes (2–1), breach yes (2–1), yet liable no (2–1). The group's collective position is that there was a contract and there was a breach, but no liability — which contradicts the legal rule they all accept.

I have seen the same structure in hiring committees (a candidate must satisfy three criteria; each criterion gets majority support but the candidate is rejected), in board decisions, and in faculty votes on curriculum changes that have multiple necessary conditions. It seems like a deep problem: you cannot just tell people to vote differently, because each vote was individually reasonable. And it is not simply a matter of poor procedure — it seems like any issue-by-issue majority vote can produce this, even when everyone is perfectly rational.

Is there an established name for this phenomenon? What are the main positions on how a group should handle it — should they privilege the conclusion vote and revise the premise votes, or the reverse, or something else? And does the answer depend on what kind of group it is (a court versus a legislature versus a hiring committee)?
