I spent thirty years as a marine biologist — mostly cephalopod behavior, actually — and since retiring I've gotten into philosophy of mind in a way I never had bandwith for when I was running a lab. It's been a blast, but I've also gotten myself into a kind of puzzle that I'd love some help thinking through.

I've been corresponding with an old friend from grad school who went into neuroscience and also retired early. She's become a bit of a crusader on animal consciousness. Over the past year of emails, she's staked out positions on a bunch of specific cases. She's firmly convinced that humans, dogs, octopuses, and crows are conscious. She's equally firm that honeybees, trout, jellyfish, and sponges are not. (She's agnostic about a lot of other things — she won't commit on pigs, for instance, which I find baffling given that she's happy to say dogs are conscious, but that's her business.) When I press her on what principle she's using, she demurs and says she's still working out the theory, but that her verdicts are solid and the theory will have to fit them.

Now here's where I'm stuck. There's been some fascinating work on cleaner wrasse — it's a reef fish that does this elaborate social cognition thing where it remembers individual client fish, adjusts its behavior when it's being watched by bystanders, and there's even a controversial claim that it passes a version of the mirror test. I want to ask her what she thinks about cleaner wrasse, but before I fire off the email, I want to get clear on what her existing verdicts already commit her to.

So my question is: what are the candidate rules or principles that fit her verdicts so far — and what would each rule predict about the cleaner wrasse? If you had to bet, which rule is she probably running, and what's the reasoning for favoring it? And most importantly, what further case would cleanly distinguish between the competing rules? I'm less interested in the bottom line about the wrasse itself than in getting a map of the logical space she's occupying.
