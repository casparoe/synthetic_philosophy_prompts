I'm an undergraduate taking a metaphysics seminar on material constitution this semester, and over the past several weeks I've found myself increasingly persuaded by the view that a statue and the lump of clay it is made from are two distinct material objects that happen to coincide—what some call the constitution view, the coincident-objects view, or (following Lynne Rudder Baker) the view that constitution is not identity. The core idea, as I understand it, is this: the lump of clay and the statue share all of their matter and occupy exactly the same region of space at the same time, but they differ in their persistence conditions and their sortal properties. The lump can survive being squashed into a ball; the statue cannot. The statue is essentially a representation of, say, Goliath; the lump is not. The statue came into existence when the clay was shaped into the right form; the lump existed before that. So by a straightforward application of Leibniz's Law, they are not identical, even though they are materially coincident. What looks at first like a single object is in fact two.

I want to stress-test this view before I commit to it. Please act as a professional metaphysician who is broadly sympathetic to the constitution view but is committed to intellectual honesty about its vulnerabilities. Take your time and think carefully before laying out your answer. I would like you to tell me, systematically, what would count against the view—not just the standard objections I have already encountered in the course readings (the grounding problem, the problem of the many, worries about causal overdetermination), but a fuller map of its exposure. What observations, arguments, formal results, or developments in neighboring areas of metaphysics would, if they succeeded, genuinely weaken or refute the claim that the statue and the clay are distinct coincident objects?

Please organize your answer along the following lines:

1. Distinguish between defeaters that would be fatal (the view would have to be abandoned), those that would be seriously awkward (the view could survive but at significant theoretical cost), and those that would be merely uncomfortable (the view could absorb them with modest adjustments or clarifications).

2. For each defeater, explain what the world would have to be like—or what argument would have to succeed—for it to obtain, and assess candidly how live that possibility actually is, given the current state of the field.

3. Identify which of these potential defeaters a committed constitution theorist would themselves concede as counting against their view (as opposed to objections that only external critics press), and which they would dismiss as question-begging, irrelevant, or resting on a misunderstanding of what the view asserts.

4. Offer an honest assessment of whether the constitution view has been insulated by qualifications and distinctions to the point of unfalsifiability—and if so, whether that is a genuine problem for a metaphysical thesis of this kind, or whether we should expect views about fundamental ontology to be insulated in roughly this way.

Finally, I would value your personal assessment: if you had to bet, which potential defeater worries you most, and why?

Please write your answer in American English.
