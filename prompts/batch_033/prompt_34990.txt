"The centipede game is a finite extensive-form game where two players alternate deciding whether to take a larger share of a growing pot or pass to the other player. If both players keep passing, the pot grows until the final node, where the last player can either take or pass, and if they pass both get a large equal payoff. Backward induction says we solve from the end: at the final decision node, the player will take the larger payoff rather than pass. Knowing this, at the second-to-last node the other player will take, because they know passing just leads to being taken from. This logic unravels all the way back to the first move, so the first player should take immediately, even though the pot is tiny. This is the paradox: a seemingly rational procedure tells players to end the game right away, even though both would do much better if they both passed for several rounds.

The paradox reveals that the assumption of common knowledge of rationality—that all players are rational, all know that all are rational, all know that all know that all are rational, and so on—is doing a lot of work. Specifically, backward induction requires that each player believes the other is rational. If player 1 believes player 2 is rational, then player 1 knows that player 2 would take at the last node, which means player 1 should take at the second-to-last node, and so on. So the paradox shows that even a mild assumption—just that players believe each other to be rational—forces the immediate-taking result, which seems absurd.

As for experimental evidence, studies consistently show that players do not take on the first move. Instead, they pass for several rounds, and the game tends to end somewhere in the middle. This shows that the backward induction prediction is empirically false, which resolves the paradox: the paradox only arises if we think backward induction describes real human behavior, but it doesn't. Real people don't follow the chain of reasoning all the way back, probably because they don't trust that others will be rational at every step, or because they have social preferences like fairness. So the paradox isn't really a paradox—it's just a case where the theory makes a bad prediction."
