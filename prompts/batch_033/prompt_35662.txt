So I've been going through this thing lately where I keep signing up for stuff that stresses me out — new hobbies, travel plans, even just watching movies I know are gonna mess with my head. And my partner was like, "why do you do this to yourself, you could just relax." Fair point. So I started reading about why people seek out novelty, and I fell down this rabbit hole about predictive processing and the free energy principle.

From what I understand (and I'm not a neuroscientist, just a guy with too much time on Wikipedia), the free energy principle says that any self-organizing system — including the brain — basically works to minimize surprisal, or prediction error. Like, the brain is constantly generating predictions about what sensory input it's gonna get, and when those predictions are wrong, it updates its model. And the overarching imperative is to keep prediction error as low as possible. So far so good.

But here's the thing that's been bugging me. If the brain's whole deal is minimizing prediction error, shouldn't the optimal strategy be to just... sit in a quiet room with the curtains drawn? Like, literally the most predictable environment possible. No surprises, no new information, minimal prediction error. That would be the rational thing to do under the FEP, right? And yet people (and animals) go out of their way to expose themselves to novelty and uncertainty — we travel to places we've never been, we watch films where we don't know the ending, we start conversations with strangers. Hell, babies spend all their time staring at things that violate their expectations. It seems like the exact opposite of what the theory predicts. Is the free energy principle just wrong about what organisms actually optimize for, or am I misunderstanding something fundemental about how it works?
