I'm on my university's academic integrity board and we've got a case that's splitting us down the middle. A PhD student in philosophy submitted a paper to a journal that takes a famous argument from the epistemology literature—an argument about when evidence justifies belief revision—and "ports" it wholesale to aesthetics, arguing that the same structure explains how we revise aesthetic judgments. The paper is completely upfront about what it's doing. But when you lay the two texts side by side, the mapping is exhaustive: every theoretical term in the original has a one-to-one replacement in the new paper, every inferential step has a direct analogue, and even the examples are transformed by a systematic substitution rule (scientific experiments become art exhibitions, lab observations become gallery viewings, etc.). The student's advisor says this is legitimate philosophical methodology—you test a conceptual structure by seeing whether it survives translation to a new domain, and the fact that the mapping is so tight is exactly what makes it interesting. The complaining faculty member says it's plagiarism with a find-and-replace function, and that the student contributed nothing of their own beyond the initial hunch to swap domains. Our board needs to decide whether this rises to plagiarism. Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before our meeting, with a clear recommendation and reasoning that genuinely engages the strongest version of the losing side's argument.
