I'm putting together a reading group on Bernard Williams for some advanced undergrads and one of them sent me this summary they found on a philosophy blog, asking whether it's a fair account of Williams's 'one thought too many' objection. I have my own views but I'd love a second opinion before I respond. Here's the summary:

"Williams imagines a man whose wife and a stranger are both drowning. The man rescues his wife — of course. But Williams says: suppose the man had paused and thought, 'She's my wife, and in emergencies it's morally permissible to prioritize your spouse, so rescuing her is justified.' That thought — the appeal to a general permission — is 'one thought too many.' Williams's point is that genuinely ethical conduct in personal relationships should be spontaneous and uncalculated; if you have to deliberate about whether to save your wife, you've already failed as a husband. This, Williams argues, shows that utilitarianism is false, because utilitarianism demands that we justify every action from an impartial standpoint, which is incompatible with the kind of partiality that authentic relationships require. In fact, Williams thought this problem generalizes to all moral theory — any system that asks you to step back and evaluate your commitments from a neutral perspective is alienating. So Williams became a kind of anti-theory skeptic who believed we'd be better off without moral philosophy."

I can spot a couple of things that seem off — I don't think Williams literally thought we should ditch moral philosophy entirely — but I'm not confident I'm catching everything, and I want to make sure I don't misrepresent him to my students. Could you go through this summary and separate what's accurate from what's distorted or oversimplified? What would a Williams scholar accept, what would they push back on, and is the summary's overall verdict fair?
