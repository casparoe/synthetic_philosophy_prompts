I've been reading around in moral psychology and metaethics and keep noticing a pattern I don't have a clean label for. The pattern goes like this. Someone encounters a moral intuition they find repugnant — say, the intuition that familial partiality is morally permissible, or that retribution has intrinsic value, or that certain sexual practices are wrong. They reach for a debunking explanation: the intuition is a product of evolutionary pressures that selected for kin favoritism, or a cultural vestige, or a cognitive heuristic misfiring in a modern environment. Having explained the intuition's causal origins, they treat it as discredited — a bias, not a genuine moral insight. But when the same person encounters a moral intuition they endorse — say, the intuition that suffering is bad, or that fairness matters — they do not reach for a debunking explanation, even though equally plausible evolutionary or cultural stories are available. The asymmetry is striking: the causal-origins move is deployed selectively against disfavored intuitions, while favored intuitions are left untouched, as if their causal pedigree were simply irrelevant. I am not asking about the general project of evolutionary debunking in metaethics, nor about the global skeptical challenge (if all intuitions are debunked, we are left with nothing). I am asking about this specific, local pattern: the selective application of debunking-style reasoning to intuitions one antecedently dislikes, while sparing the ones one antecedently endorses, without any independent criterion for when a causal origin disqualifies an intuition and when it does not.

Does this pattern have an established name in the literature? I am aware of the bias blind spot in psychology, but that concerns the detection of biases in oneself versus others, which is adjacent but not the same thing. I am also aware that Sharon Street's evolutionary debunking argument and the responses to it circle around a version of this asymmetry worry — why think evolutionary forces pushed our evaluative attitudes away from the truth rather than toward it? — but that is a different, more abstract issue. What I am after is the specific dialectical move of selectively debunking disfavored intuitions while giving favored ones a free pass, and whether that move itself has been named and analyzed. If it has no established name, I would appreciate a coinage that distinguishes it from related concepts, along with a clear account of its boundaries: when, if ever, is selective debunking legitimate, and what would a principled criterion for distinguishing biases from genuine moral preferences even look like?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
