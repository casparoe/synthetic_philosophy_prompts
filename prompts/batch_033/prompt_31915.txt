I write literary fiction, and my whole method for inner monologue relies on a picture I absorbed without really thinking about it: that introspection is basically perception turned inward. You shine the flashlight of attention on your own mental states and you see them — the anger, the flicker of jealousy, the half-buried memory — just like you'd see objects on a table. It's how I decide what my characters can plausibly notice about themselves, and it's how I judge whether a passage of interiority rings true.

I've been reading some cognitive psych lately (Nisbett & Wilson, the confabulation stuff, some Kahneman) and now I'm rattled. If people are demonstrably wrong about why they did things, or about what they're feeling in the moment, then the whole "look inward and report" model starts looking like a folk theory that's doing real work in my fiction without my having signed off on it.

Walk me through the analogy properly — give me a correspondence table: what plays the role of the eye, the object seen, the act of looking, the lighting conditions, the blind spots. Then tell me where it snaps, which of those breaks are fatal for what I'm trying to do as a novelist, and which are just philosophers' quibbles that don't touch the craft. Final question: does writing inner life *as if* it's perceptually available survive the audit, or do I need a different underlying model? Keep it to 350 words.
