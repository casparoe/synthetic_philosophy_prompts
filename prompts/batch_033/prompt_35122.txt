I've been trying to wrap my head around Lewis's Principal Principle and I keep running into the same wall. The PP says: if you know the objective chance of P is x, and you have no inadmissible evidence, your credence in P should be x. Fair enough. But here's what bugs me. Objective chances are supposed to be real features of the world — like the half-life of uranium isn't about what anyone believes, it's just a physical fact. So the PP is saying that a physical fact (the chance) directly dictates a normative fact (what I should believe). That's weird on its face — it's like saying the mass of an object dictates what I should desire. But fine, maybe there's a special connection between chance and belief. The deeper problem is: if chances have this built-in normative authority over rational belief, then what makes them "objective" in the first place? They start to look like they're defined partly by their role in guiding credence, which makes them sound a lot like subjective probabilities with better PR. So is the Principal Principle actually smuggling in a subjectivist account of chance while pretending to be neutral? Or am I missing something about how the normativity is supposed to work?
