I have been reading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments and cannot let go of his account of the impartial spectator — the imagined perspective, "the man within the breast," from which we judge our own conduct as a stranger would. The idea has become personally urgent because I have spent weeks trying to determine whether an action of mine was justified, and I keep noticing that the voice that condemns me and the voice that excuses me are both, in some sense, my own.

Please write a sonnet about this. The octave should give the partial, self-justifying agent making the case for his own conduct in the first person. The volta should be the entry of the impartial spectator's gaze: the moment the speaker begins to see the action from outside, as someone with no stake in it would see it. The sestet should not reconcile the two perspectives but hold them in tension, and the closing couplet should land on the vertigo of the fact that the impartial standpoint is itself something the self has to author — that the judge who condemns the self is as much a construction of the self as the story being condemned.

The poem should be exact enough that a reader who knows Smith would recognize the mechanism you are drawing on, but it should work as a poem, not as versified exposition.
