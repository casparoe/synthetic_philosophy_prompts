I've been managing a small investment club for about a year now, and I keep noticing a pattern in how we make decisions that I don't have a proper name for. It's not simply that we're risk-averse — we all understand that risk aversion, in the standard sense, just means preferring a sure thing to a gamble with the same expected value. What we're doing feels structurally different.

Here's an example. Last month we were comparing two funds. Fund A had a 90% chance of returning 10% and a 10% chance of losing 5%. Fund B had a 50% chance of returning 30%, a 40% chance of returning 5%, and a 10% chance of losing 50%. The expected return of Fund B was higher, but several members argued against it. When I pressed them to explain why, they didn't point to the variance or the standard deviation. They said something more specific: "the worst ten percent of outcomes for Fund B are catastrophic, and that matters more than the fact that they're only ten percent likely." They weren't saying "ignore everything except the worst case" — they still cared about the other ninety percent of outcomes. They just wanted the bad outcomes to count for more than their raw probability, and they wanted this extra weight to track the rank of the outcome in the distribution, not its absolute magnitude. I've noticed the same pattern in other contexts — evaluating job offers, choosing medical treatments, even planning vacations. People seem to apply an invisible magnifying glass to the bottom of the outcome distribution, and sometimes to the top as well, but asymmetrically, with the bottom getting more magnification.

When I try to formalize what they're doing, it looks like a coherent decision rule: rank the possible outcomes from worst to best, apply a nonlinear weighting function to the cumulative probabilities, and then compute the weighted average. My question is whether this decision rule has a name, and whether there is a serious normative theory that endorses it. I'm not asking about prospect theory or other descriptive accounts of how people actually behave with all their biases — I'm asking whether any philosopher or decision theorist has argued that this is how you should reason. And if so, how does the theory handle the objection that this kind of probability transformation can lead to violations of the independence axiom? I'd also like to understand how it differs from simple expected utility with a curved utility function, since I know that's the standard way of modeling risk attitudes. If the view has no single established name, I'd appreciate a clear delineation of the nearest candidates and how they differ. Please end with a line giving the best label for the phenomenon, in the form \answer{...}.
