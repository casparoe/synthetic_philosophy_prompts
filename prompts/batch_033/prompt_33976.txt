I'm a high school student, and I've been going down something of a rabbit hole with the demarcation problem — the question of what distinguishes science from non-science or pseudoscience. I first encountered Popper's falsificationism in my biology class, where it was presented as the clean answer to why creationism isn't science: it doesn't make falsifiable predictions. That seemed powerful. But as I read further, I kept finding objections that seemed just as powerful. Popper himself acknowledged that falsification is never logically decisive, since you can always blame a failed prediction on an auxiliary assumption rather than the core theory. Kuhn argued that scientists in a paradigm don't even try to falsify — they solve puzzles and ignore anomalies until a crisis forces a revolution. Lakatos tried to salvage something like falsificationism by shifting the unit of appraisal from individual theories to research programs, but then the line between a degenerating program and a progressive one turned out to be murky. Feyerabend concluded that there is no principled distinction at all — that methodologically, the only rule that survives historical scrutiny is that there are no rules. And yet, decades later, the debate hasn't really moved: falsificationism still gets invoked in public arguments about what counts as science, philosophers still publish on the demarcation problem, and none of the major positions seems to have been definitively refuted or vindicated.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse comes from the fact that "demarcation" is really several different projects bundled together. One project is descriptive: what methods do successful sciences actually share? Another is normative: what methods should scientists follow? A third is political or legal: what gets taught in schools, what gets funded, what counts as expert testimony in court? And perhaps a fourth is conceptual: what does the word "science" even mean? If these are different questions, maybe they have different answers, and the debate stalls because people are talking past each other — each camp has a criterion that works for one project but gets criticized by the standards of another.

I'd like a diagnosis of the state of this debate. Is my hypothesis on the right track, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement here that I'm missing? What kind of disagreement is this, really — is it verbal, about different normative projects, or is there a hidden empirical or formal question at the bottom? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Please separate what's settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
