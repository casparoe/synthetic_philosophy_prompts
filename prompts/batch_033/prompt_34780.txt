The Hart–Fuller debate has been running for decades, but the two sides—legal positivism and natural law theory—continue to talk past each other on several points. Draft the joint statement that a conscientious legal positivist and a conscientious natural law theorist could both sign after a year-long adversarial collaboration.

The statement should include:

(1) A numbered list of claims both sides accept. These should not be trivial; they should capture the substantive ground on which the debate does not turn—including claims about the adjudication of particular hard cases, about what the Nazi legal system does and does not show, and about the conceptual role that the idea of "law" plays in practical reasoning.

(2) A disagreements table. Each row should state one point of residual disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position. Avoid formulations that one side would dismiss as question-begging.

(3) For each disagreement, identify the underlying crux—the sub-question whose answer would, if settled, move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete test, argument, or case that both sides agree bears on it, and state in advance what each side predicts about that test.

Where the collaboration discovered that a standing disagreement was partly or wholly verbal, note the clarification and say what both now accept once the verbal issue is removed.
