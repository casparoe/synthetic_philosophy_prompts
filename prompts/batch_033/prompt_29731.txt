After my foundation's most recent portfolio review, I had to sit with an uncomfortable fact: the proposals I had argued hardest for were disproportionately the ones whose narratives were most detailed and causally specific, and their success rate was no better than the field average for their category, while proposals I found narratively less satisfying did no worse. I have concluded that I have been systematically overweighting what is usually called the inside view — the particulars of the proposed project, its team, its mechanism, and its plan — and underweighting the outside view, the distribution of outcomes for relevantly similar projects in the past. I want to correct this without swinging to the opposite error of ignoring case-specific information altogether.

I am a program officer at a private foundation that makes roughly forty grants a year in early-stage biomedical and public health research. For each proposal I write a recommendation — advance, decline, or advance with conditions — for a committee that mostly defers to my recommendation. I typically have about ninety minutes of focused attention per proposal, I have no formal statistical training beyond the basics, and the proposals vary widely enough that no single reference class is obvious in most cases. The grants are multi-year and the cost of my being wrong, in either direction, is substantial.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the inside view/outside view distinction and a sensible form of epistemic modesty, that I could actually run in that time. In particular, it should tell me: how to construct an outside view for a proposal without gerrymandering the reference class to get the answer my narrative already suggests; how to decide what information from the proposal counts as legitimate evidence that this case is an exception to the base rate rather than merely vivid detail; how to combine the outside view and the inside view into a recommendation; where the procedure must hand the decision back to judgment; and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as mechanical rules.

Then apply the procedure to three representative proposals, including at least one where it tells me to decline a project I would otherwise find genuinely compelling, and one where it tells me to advance a project whose proposal is not especially persuasive on its face. I would also like an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do — for example, whether it can correct for my ability to select a self-serving reference class, and whether it can distinguish warranted modesty about my own judgment from simple deference to a base rate.
