I'm taking this grad seminar on moral conflict and I'm supposed to write a paper arguing that genuine moral dilemmas are possible — not just apparent ones that dissolve under closer analysis, but real situations where an agent faces two or more courses of action each of which is genuinely wrong, and there's no way to escape doing something wrong. I know the main objection is the "ought implies can" thing: if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, but you can't do both, then you can't be obligated to do both, so at least one of the oughts must give way. And then there's the move where you say the obligation is disjunctive — you ought to do A or B — which dissolves the conflict.

I want to push back on that. My rough idea is to argue that the disjunctive-obligation move misses something about the phenomenology of hard choices — specifically, the rational residue of guilt or regret that persists even after you've made what was clearly the best choice available. If all you had was a disjunctive obligation, there'd be nothing to regret. But in cases like Sophie's Choice, or even less dramatic cases (like breaking a promise to one friend because you have to help another in an emergency), the regret seems rational, not just a psychological quirk. I'm also thinking of drawing on Bernard Williams's stuff about remainders and maybe Ruth Barcan Marcus's argument that moral dilemmas are real and that the principles generating them can be consistent even if they can't all be satisfied in every possible world.
