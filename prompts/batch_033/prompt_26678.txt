I'm a novelist working on a book that weaves together memory, the physics of time, and the felt experience of temporal passage. While researching, I sat in on a conversation between two acquaintances — one a statistical physicist, the other a philosopher of physics — that grew into a disagreement I still can't adjudicate. I need to know who's right about what so my novel doesn't embarrass me. Here's the exchange as I recorded it:

(1) Physicist: "The direction of time is settled, at least in outline. All temporal asymmetry — memory, causation, the sense that the past is fixed and the future open — reduces to the thermodynamic arrow plus the low-entropy boundary condition of the early universe. Entropy was lower yesterday than it will be tomorrow; everything else follows."

(2) Philosopher: "That conflates correlation with explanation. I grant that all the arrows co-vary. But saying the thermodynamic arrow is fundamental while the others are derivative is a modeling choice, not a deduction. I could just as well take the causal arrow as primitive and derive thermodynamic behavior from it. After all, if causes didn't precede effects, you couldn't even set up the experiments that support thermodynamics in the first place."

(3) Physicist: "You couldn't take the causal arrow as primitive, because causation itself has to bottom out in something non-causal. The fundamental microphysical laws are time-symmetric. Causation only emerges statistically from those symmetric laws plus the past hypothesis. So it can't be the ground floor — you'd be starting at the wrong level."

(4) Philosopher: "Fair. But then you face a different problem. The thermodynamic arrow gives you an asymmetry of gradient, not an asymmetry that singles out one direction as 'the future.' In a universe where entropy increases globally, a being at any point will observe lower entropy in one temporal direction and higher in the other. That's a relational asymmetry between two directions — but why identify the low-entropy direction as 'past' rather than merely 'that way'? The arrow tells you the directions differ; it doesn't tell you which one is which."

(5) Physicist: "Because memory formation and all cognitive processing are entropy-increasing processes. We literally cannot accumulate records of the high-entropy direction, so we call the low-entropy direction the past. The semantics follow the physics."

(6) Philosopher: "That's exactly my point. You've just described a fact about what we can have records of, not a fact about time itself. The semantics follow the physics, as you say — but 'past' is a semantic notion. You've explained why we talk a certain way, not why time is directed. Isn't that the whole problem in a nutshell: the thermodynamic arrow explains asymmetric cognition, but that's not the same thing as explaining temporal asymmetry?"
