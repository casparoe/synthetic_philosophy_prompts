One thing that puzzles me: in a lot of RPGs there's a morality/karma system that ding's you for picking pockets or killing town guards, but the same game happily lets you kill hundreds of "enemy" NPCs with no moral cost and gives you XP for it. Some games even make essential NPCs literally unkillable, while generic civilians can be murdered for a small karma hit. Then there are games with pacifist routes or a no-kill achievement, as if the killing did matter morally after all. What theory of moral standing (or of what the meter is even tracking) does this design pattern presuppose? Possible readings: only humanlike characters count, enemies are legitimate because they're aggressors, the meter tracks the player-character's reputation or self-image rather than any real wrong, or fictional characters can't be wronged and the whole thing is just game mechanics. Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice is consistent across those cases, and tell me what a game built on the view that sentience is what matters (not species or faction or role in the plot) would have to change. And if fictional characters can't be wronged, what exactly is the no-kill achievement rewarding?
