Two colleagues of mine, both philosophers, got into an argument at a dinner party about whether death is bad for the person who dies. I took notes afterward because the exchange was good enough that I wanted a third party to go through it. Here is roughly what they said, with the moves numbered as I remember them:

(1) A: The Epicurean argument is basically right. Death cannot be bad for you because you cease to exist — there is no subject left to experience the harm. Something can only be bad for you if you are around to suffer it.

(2) B: That is a substantive claim about the nature of harm, not a logical deduction. It is perfectly coherent to say that death is bad because it deprives you of a life you would have had. The deprivation is bad for you even if you never experience it as such.

(3) A: Deprivation requires a subject who is deprived. When you are dead, there is no subject. When you are alive, death has not happened yet. So there is no time at which you are both alive and dead, and therefore no time at which you are deprived of anything by death. The timing problem is real.

(4) B: You are insisting that harm must be experienced at a time, but that is exactly what is in question. If someone embezzles your life savings and covers it up so perfectly you never find out, that is still bad for you — your life goes worse than it would have, even though you never experience the loss. Death is like that: it makes your life as a whole go worse than it would have.

(5) A: The embezzlement case is different. You still exist, and the theft affects the actual course of your life — you have less money, fewer options, even if you do not know it. The comparison is between your actual life and a better counterfactual one. But with death, there is no actual life post-death to compare to a counterfactual one. You are comparing existence to non-existence, and non-existence is not a state of the person.

(6) B: That is exactly what is at issue. You are assuming that comparative harm requires an actual state of the person to compare. But the deprivation view says that a life can be worse because it is shorter — that is a property of the life as a whole, not a state of the person at any time. Saying "her life was worse because she died at 30 rather than 80" is a claim about the shape of the life, not about any experience she had.

(7) A: But "worse for whom?" If the life is over, there is no one for whom it is worse. You keep talking about the life as a whole, but a life is not a thing that can be harmed — only the person living it can be harmed, and that person ceases to exist at death.

(8) B: This is circular. You are defining "harm" in a way that requires a continuing subject, and then concluding that death cannot be a harm because there is no continuing subject. I could just as well define "harm" as any factor that makes a life go worse than it could have, and then death obviously qualifies. The question is which definition we should adopt, and you have not given a reason to prefer yours.

(9) A: Fair enough — here is my reason. The point of the concept of harm is to guide action and ground attitudes like regret and compensation. If someone is dead, there is no one to compensate, no one whose future we can improve, and no one who can regret their own death. The concept of harm does no practical work when applied to the dead. So the deprivation view gives us a notion of harm that is practically inert. That is a reason to reject it.

(10) B: That is a better argument, but it does not work. The fact that someone will die can and should guide our actions now — we can extend lives, or at least try to. And we can regret that someone died young even if they cannot regret it themselves. The harm of death is precisely what makes it worth preventing. If death were not a harm, why would we bother with medicine at all?

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence — say who is more right overall and what the core of the disagreement really is. Then go through the moves one by one, rating each as clearly right, mostly right, a tie, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, with a sentence or two of justification. No preamble, no restating of the question.
