I've just started teaching a historiography module at my college and I've ended up in a months-long row with a collegue about what a historical explanation actually is. He's a covering-law person: he thinks an explanation in history only earns its keep if it's implicitly leaning on some general regularity about how people, economies or institutions behave, and that without one you've just got narrative description. I'm on the narrative side: I think a story can explain an event by showing how it hangs together with the events around it, and that no general law has to be hiding in the background. We've both read around it — Hempel on one side, Dray and the narrative theorists on the other — and the argument hasnt moved in months. Then I realised the academic version of this debate seems to have been stuck for decades as well: all the standard counterexamples are on the table, everyone has a prepared reply, and nobody changes their mind. I'm not asking you to settle who's right. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. My hunch is that we might be using the word 'explanation' for two different jobs — one about showing why something was to be expected, the other about making a sequence of events intelligible. But it could equally be a real dispute about what understanding requires, or about which examples get to count as data in the first place. So: give me a diagnosis of the stalemate, and say what would actually count as progress — for me and my colleague, not just for the profession.
