I am trying to reconstruct what Émilie du Châtelet would have said about a particular case, drawing on the verdicts she gives across her *Discourse on Happiness*. I will list the cases she addresses, with her verdict on whether the person in question can be happy — or what kind of happiness is open to them — and then ask about a new case that does not appear in her text.

Here are the cases she rules on:

1. A person animated by strong passions — love, ambition, the desire for glory — who pursues them actively and successfully. Verdict: this person has the materials for happiness, but it is a dangerous kind. Passion exposes one to dependence on others and to devastating loss. Happiness is possible but precarious, and the higher it rises the harder it falls.

2. A person who has lost the capacity for strong passions, whether through age, illness, or disillusionment. Verdict: this person should turn to study. Study yields a happiness that is calm, independent, and within one's own control. It is less intense than the happiness of passion but far more secure, and it is available to nearly everyone who cultivates a taste for it.

3. A woman of talent and ambition who desires glory but finds the standard avenues — public office, military command, scientific academies — closed to her sex. Verdict: she can still be happy, but she must redirect her ambition. The love of study, pursued for its own sake, is her surest path, supplemented by the private esteem of a small circle of worthy judges. What looks like a consolation is in fact a superior kind of happiness, because it does not depend on the opinion of the crowd.

4. A person who has no taste for anything — no passion, no intellectual curiosity, no project that engages them. Verdict: this person cannot be happy. Happiness requires being attached to something; without a taste for something, one merely vegetates. The absence of suffering is not happiness.

5. A person who is in love, and the love is reciprocated, but the beloved is unfaithful or the attachment is otherwise unstable. Verdict: this person experiences the greatest happiness life can offer — the happiness of passionate love — but it rests on an illusion, because one believes oneself loved uniquely when one is not. The illusion is necessary for the happiness, yet the happiness is fragile and will almost certainly end in suffering. She does not say the person should therefore renounce love; she says the person should understand what they are doing.

6. A person who knowingly cultivates a pleasant illusion — for example, persuading themselves that a modest achievement is more significant than it is, or that a hoped-for outcome is more likely than the evidence warrants. Verdict: this is not only permissible but wise. Illusions are necessary for happiness, and we should not try to dispel them in ourselves or in others, provided they are not harmful. The person who sees everything exactly as it is will be miserable.

Now the new case. A person has both a genuine intellectual passion — a deep engagement with natural philosophy, say — and a strong romantic attachment, but the two pull in different directions. The intellectual work demands solitude and sustained, uninterrupted concentration. The romantic attachment demands time, attention, and a kind of emotional availability that disrupts that concentration. The person feels torn between them and senses that each pursuit is diminished by the presence of the other. The conflict is not a temporary scheduling problem; it is a genuine incompatibility between two ways of life, each of which du Châtelet has separately endorsed as a path to happiness.

What would du Châtelet say about this person's prospects for happiness? Does she have a rule that settles the case, or do her earlier verdicts underdetermine it?
