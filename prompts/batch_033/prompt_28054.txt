My study group has an argument that seems to show rational people can never disagree, and none of us can find the exit. It runs like this.

(1) A rational agent updates by conditionalizing on their evidence.
(2) Suppose two rational agents share a prior over some question, then each receives different private evidence. If they take turns announcing their resulting probabilities, those announcements become common knowledge between them.
(3) A standard agreement theorem in game theory says: if two agents share a prior, and their posterior probabilities for a given claim are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal.
(4) So two rational agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree.
(5) But we see well-informed, honest, smart people persistently disagreeing — two economists giving different recession odds for months on end.
(6) By (4), the only ways to make sense of (5) are that at least one of them isn't rational, or their priors differ.
(7) Yet "their priors differ" doesn't explain the disagreement, it just relabels it: we asked how two rational people can end up with different beliefs, and now we're told they started with different beliefs. So the theorem seems to prove too much — either rational disagreement is impossible, or the common-prior assumption is doing all the work while pretending to be innocent.

I'd like to keep (1) and (5); I'm suspicious of (2) but I cant say exactly why. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
