A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about Schopenhauer's account of music. We kept sliding between two positions — roughly, a Schopenhauerian one and a formalist one — and by the end neither of us could state the other's view in a form the other would accept. I would like to see the two positions written out properly.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent characters, one defending each position. The Schopenhauerian holds that music is unique among the arts: unlike the other arts, which present the Ideas — the Will's objectifications at various grades — music is a direct expression of the Will itself, and this is supposed to explain both its universality and its unusual emotional power. The formalist holds that music has no content beyond its own tonal structures, and that its apparent metaphysical depth and emotional effects are better explained by form, expectation, and association.

The characters should respond to each other's actual claims rather than talk past each other, and the dialogue should get into the weeds. Among the issues it should work through are these: why Schopenhauer treated music as independent of the phenomenal world in a way the other arts are not, and whether that claim is intelligible; whether his mapping of the bass, the middle voices, and the melody onto the ascending grades of the Will's objectification is a genuine observation about music or a metaphysical projection onto it; whether saying that music expresses the Will explains its felt significance or merely re-describes it; and whether the formalist can account for the way music seems to present feelings in the abstract, detached from any particular occasion. Do not have the characters settle the question by fiat; if the two positions turn out to be partly compatible, let the characters discover that in the course of arguing.
