I've been going in circles on reasons for attitudes and could use a diagnosis. Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it:

(1) If a demon threatens to punish you unless you admire him, then the fact that admiring him lets you avoid the punishment counts in favor of admiring him.
(2) If a consideration counts in favor of an attitude, it is a reason for that attitude.
(3) So you have a reason to admire the demon.
(4) But that can't be right. Admiration is answerable to whether its object is admirable, and the threat says nothing about whether the demon is admirable.
(5) So something like this must be true: a consideration is a reason for an attitude only if it bears on the object in the way proper to that attitude — truth for belief, admirability for admiration, and so on.
(6) Now take a belief case. A doctor tells you that you'll recover only if you believe you'll recover, and the belief really does causally contribute to recovery. The fact that believing you'll recover helps you recover seems to count in favor of believing it.
(7) By (5), that fact is not a reason for belief, since it doesn't bear on whether you'll recover — it bears on the effects of the belief, not its truth.
(8) But that seems absurd. Surely there's a reason here, the kind people call pragmatic.
(9) So the restriction in (5) proves too much. But if I drop (5), I'm back to having a reason to admire the demon, which (4) said can't be right.

I want to keep both (4) and (8) if I can. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
