I'm putting together a workshop session on epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment, and I'd like to use a dialogue as the centerpiece for discussion. The two views often get lumped together because they respond to the same data — the bank cases, the airport cases, the general sense that whether someone "knows" seems to depend on what's at stake. But the explanations diverge in ways that matter. The contextualist treats "knows" as an indexical-like term whose semantic standards shift with the attributor's conversational context, so that the very same subject with the very same evidence can make a knowledge ascription true in one context and false in another. The pragmatic encroachment theorist, by contrast, treats knowledge itself as sensitive to the subject's practical interests: high stakes don't just raise the bar for calling something knowledge, they can actually destroy knowledge by making the subject's epistemic position insufficient for the practical reasoning she needs to undertake. On this picture, the truth conditions of knowledge ascriptions are invariant — it's knowledge that bends, not the word.

Write a dialogue between a contextualist and a pragmatic encroachment theorist in which they try to determine whether their disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Both characters should be equally capable and well-informed; neither should serve as a foil for the other. The dialogue should include at least one moment where they test their views against a case in which the attributor's stakes and the subject's stakes come apart — for instance, a third-person knowledge ascription where the speaker is in a low-stakes situation but the person being talked about faces high stakes. I'd also like the dialogue to press on a deeper question: even if the two views make the same predictions about the truth of ordinary knowledge ascriptions, does the choice between them matter for anything else — for how we should conduct inquiry, for when we should act on our beliefs, or for what we owe each other as epistemic agents? The dialogue shouldn't resolve neatly; it should leave the participants (and the reader) with a sharper sense of what would actually settle the dispute and what hangs on settling it.
