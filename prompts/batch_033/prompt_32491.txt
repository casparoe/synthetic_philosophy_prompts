Take the experience machine thought experiment: a device that can give you any experiences you choose, indistinguishable from the real thing, but you are not actually doing what you experience — you are floating in a tank, and the life you feel yourself living is a simulation. Now consider the proposition:

*The widespread intuitive aversion to plugging into the experience machine for a full life is tracking a genuine feature of prudential value — that is, the judgment that a simulated life is worse for the person living it is, on balance, correctly identifying something that matters for well-being, rather than being largely driven by status quo bias, imaginative failure, or evolved heuristics that favor real-world engagement for reasons unrelated to well-being.*

Please give me your credence in that proposition, decomposed into at least the following terms:

(a) The probability that hedonism about well-being is false.
(b) Conditional on hedonism being false, the probability that the correct theory of well-being implies that a full life inside the experience machine is prudentially worse — by a meaningful margin — than a comparable life lived in reality.
(c) The probability that the intuitive rejection of the experience machine is a reliable judgment (formed by a process that generally tracks the relevant normative facts) rather than a systematically biased one.
(d) Any further terms you think the decomposition requires.

For each term, give a number and a brief line of justification. Then show how they combine to yield your overall credence, and identify which single term your final number is most sensitive to — that is, where a small change in the sub-credence would produce the largest shift in the overall figure. Finally, tell me what specific kind of evidence — an empirical finding, a philosophical argument, or a modification of the thought experiment — would most reliably move that term, and in which direction.
