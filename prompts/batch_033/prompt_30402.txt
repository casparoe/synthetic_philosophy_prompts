A friend laid this out and I can't find the exit. (1) If it's rational for you to believe each proposition in a set, then it's rational for you to believe their conjunction. (2) In a fair million-ticket lottery with exactly one winner, the probability that any given ticket loses is 0.999999, so for each ticket it's rational to believe that that ticket loses. (3) By (1), it's rational to believe the conjunction: ticket 1 loses and ticket 2 loses and ... and ticket 1,000,000 loses. (4) But it's also rational to believe that some ticket wins, since the setup guarantees it. (5) So a fully rational person could rationally believe something equivalent to a contradiction. (6) But no rational person believes a contradiction. (7) Therefore either (1) or (2) is false. He then says the preface paradox shows (6) is the step to drop, since an author can rationally believe each sentence in her book while also rationally believing the preface sentence that at least one sentence in the book is false. But dropping (6) feels like it just relocates the problem: if rational belief sets can be inconsistent, what is left of the idea that rational belief aims at truth? Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (2), but I worry that forces me to reject (1), and I do not know how to live without (1) either.
