(1) Newcomb's problem: one-box. The evidential reasoning is right — one-boxers walk away richer, and the fact that the prediction is already in the past doesn't matter because your choice is evidence about which world you're in.

(2) Counterfactual mugging (standard version where Omega tells you the coin came up tails): don't pay. The coin already landed tails, Omega already decided not to give me the $10,000, and my $100 now doesn't change any of that. The counterfactual heads branch is gone; I'm not going to pay real money for a purely counterfactual payout.

(3) XOR blackmail: pay. I know I got the letter, so I know I'm in the branch where I was predicted to pay. But I still want to be the type of agent who pays when blackmailed, because being that type is what makes the prediction go well across the board. If I defect now, I'm the sucker who gets blackmailed and then has the evidence come out anyway.

(4) Decision theory should be unified: the right answer to a decision problem shouldn't depend on when you happen to deliberate about it. The structure of the problem is what it is.

(5) There's a principled difference between the counterfactual mugging and the other two cases that explains why my verdicts differ. In the mugging, once I know the coin is tails, my action gives me no evidence about anything I care about — the payout is already settled. In Newcomb's and XOR blackmail, my action still gives me evidence about something I care about (what's in the box / whether the evidence gets revealed).

I have a feeling (2) and (3) are doing the same kind of reasoning and getting opposite answers, and that (4) might conflict with the distinction I'm drawing in (5). But I can't tell which of these tensions are real and which are just me confusing myself. Walk me through it.
