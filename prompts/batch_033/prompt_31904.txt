A friend of mine has been working through the literature on standing to blame—the question of when someone is in a position to blame another, as distinct from whether the target deserves blame. She keeps a running list of her verdicts on cases, and I'd like help predicting her next one.

Her verdicts so far:

- Alex, who regularly speeds on residential streets, blames a neighbor for speeding in a school zone. Verdict: no standing.
- Blair, who has never committed a traffic offense but was convicted of embezzling from her employer, blames the same neighbor for the same school-zone speeding. Verdict: has standing.
- Carmen, who used to speed like the neighbor but stopped years ago and now campaigns for stricter traffic enforcement, blames the neighbor. Verdict: has standing.
- Dev, a dispatcher who designed the delivery schedule that pressures drivers to speed, blames the drivers for speeding while defending the schedule. Verdict: no standing.

The new case: Erin, a junior dispatcher, helped design the very schedule that pressured those drivers to speed. She has since left the job, now publicly condemns the practice and explicitly includes her own role in the condemnation, and is campaigning to change it. She blames the drivers for the speeding the schedule produced.

What should I expect my friend to say about Erin?

Please structure your answer as:

- Candidate rules (about 250 words): state the two or three most plausible rules that fit her four verdicts, and briefly show how each handles the four earlier cases.
- What each rule predicts for Erin (about 150 words).
- Best guess (about 150 words): which verdict my friend is most likely to give, which rule she is implicitly running, and why.
- The decisive further case (about 100 words): one further case I could ask her about that would best distinguish the remaining candidate rules, with your predicted verdict under each.
