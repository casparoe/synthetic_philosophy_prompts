I'm writing a novel about a revolutionary cell that starts out trying to take down a corrupt regime and, over the course of the book, turns into something uncomfortably like it. Someone pressed Pascal's Pensées on me, and there's this fragment about justice and force that I can't get out of my head.

Pascal's claim is roughly this: it's proper that what's just should be obeyed, but it's necessary that what's strongest should be obeyed. Justice without force is helpless, since there will always be wicked people who won't respect mere justice; force without justice is tyrannical, and everyone condemns it. So the two have to be joined — and his words are that "what is just must always be made strong, or what is strong just." Then, as I read him, he pretty much concedes that you can't make what's just strong, so the only real option is to make what's strong just.

Which lands me in a bad place. Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it:

(1) For a social order to actually hold together, the rules it enforces have to be seen as legitimate by the people living under them.
(2) You can't make a rule powerful simply because it's just; power builds up according to it's own logic, not according to merit.
(3) So whenever a rule is both enforced and widely accepted, that acceptance came from some pre-existing power dressing its rules up as just — not from justice attracting power to itself.
(4) Therefore "justice" in any functioning society just is whatever the currently strong have managed to get accepted under that name.

If this is right, my revolutionaries were never idealists. They were the losers of the old arrangement trying to become the winners of a new one, and the only difference between them and the regime they hate is that they haven't won yet. That's a horrible thing to believe about your own characters, and I don't want it to be true. But I can't locate the hole.

So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is false and what I'd have to give up to deny it — I'd honestly rather keep (1) and (2) than swallow (4), but I don't know if that's an option. And if it's not valid, show me where the slide happens, because every step felt air-tight as I wrote it down.
