After a long argument with a friend about what makes an utterance a promise or a warning, I wrote down her verdicts on five cases:

- "I promise to water your plants," said sincerely to a friend who understands English: a promise.
- The same sentence spoken by an actor on stage during a play: not a promise.
- "I promise to water your plants," said with no intention of following through, to a friend who understands and takes it as a genuine commitment: a promise, though an insincere and later broken one.
- "I'll water your plants this weekend," said with no intention of committing, to a friend who takes it as a harmless prediction: not a promise.
- "I warn you, the bridge is out," said to a driver who hears and understands: a warning.

Tomorrow I will ask her about this case: "I promise to water your plants," said with complete sincerity to someone the speaker believes understands English but who in fact speaks no English and understands nothing. What should I expect her to say, and why? More importantly, which rule about promising and warning best explains her verdicts so far, and which other rules fit those verdicts just as well? I am considering a speaker-intention rule, a hearer-uptake rule, and a conjunction of the two, but tell me if something else fits better. Then say which further case would cleanly separate the remaining candidates, and how confident I should be in the prediction.
