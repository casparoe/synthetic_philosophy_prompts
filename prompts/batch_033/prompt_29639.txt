I run a small grantmaking outfit — we fund weird, high-variance projects, the kind of thing that sometimes attracts people who've read a lot of decision theory. Twice now we've gotten proposals that are basically acausal trades dressed up as funding requests. The first was a counterfactual mugging: a group claimed that if we don't fund them, we're revealing ourselves as the type of agent that doesn't honor counterfactual commitments, which makes us predictably worse off in any universe where such commitments matter, and they walked us through the whole Omega logic. The second was XOR blackmail: they said their internal funding model had already made a prediction about whether we'd fund them, and on the basis of that prediction they'd either shared or withheld some research from us, and now if we don't fund them we're confirming that their model was right to classify us as defectors, which means they won't share future work either.

I need a decision procedure my team can actually run on these. Not a philosophy paper — a checklist. The questions we ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to move to the next step, where the procedure says "pay," where it says "ignore and don't think about it again," and where it says "this can't be resolved by a procedure and a person has to decide." It has to be usable by people who haven't read the sequences and it has to take under an hour.

Then apply it to three cases: the counterfactual mugging above, the XOR blackmail above, and a third one — someone who says "we ran a simulation of your foundation and the sim said no, but if you fund us now you'll prove the simulation was wrong and we'll update all our future simulations to treat you as a cooperator." I want to see where the procedure spits out an answer I won't like, and I want you to flag what the procedure deliberately leaves out or can't handle.
