Draft the joint statement that a retributivist and a consequentialist about legal punishment could both sign after a year-long adversarial collaboration. Both are sophisticated theorists, not caricatures: the retributivist holds that desert is the primary justifying aim of punishment but acknowledges that consequentialist considerations properly constrain severity, form, and the allocation of scarce penal resources; the consequentialist holds that punishment is justified by its deterrent, incapacitative, and rehabilitative effects but acknowledges that desert places principled side-constraints on who may be punished and in what proportion. Each has spent the year engaging the other's arguments and internal evidence in good faith.

I want the document in three sections.

First, a numbered list of claims both can affirm without reservation. These should include: what punishment is and what distinguishes it from mere penalty or civil remedy; what any adequate theory of punishment must explain or accommodate; cases where their two frameworks converge on the same verdict (and why); empirical facts whose truth both would treat as relevant to the design of penal institutions, even if they weight them differently; and points on which each has revised their view over the course of the collaboration.

Second, a disagreements table. For each point of genuine disagreement, provide three columns: (a) the retributivist's formulation of their own position, phrased so the retributivist would unhesitatingly sign it; (b) the consequentialist's formulation of their own position, phrased so the consequentialist would unhesitatingly sign it; and (c) a neutral statement of the question at issue that both would accept as capturing what they disagree about. Avoid formulations that make either side sound foolish or strawmanned.

Third, a list of cruxes — the sub-questions such that a settled answer would cause at least one side to substantially revise their view — and for each crux, propose a concrete hypothetical case or an empirical finding both would agree bears on it, along with each side's advance prediction about what that case or finding would show.

Pay particular attention to the following. Is the permissibility of punishing an innocent person a genuine crux, or is it a talking point each side uses to misdescribe the other — and if the latter, what is the real disagreement underneath it? Do the two views come apart on the question of disproportionately severe punishment for a minor offense when doing so would produce net deterrence gains, and if so, does the dispute turn on a difference in moral epistemology, in empirical expectations, or in something else? And when desert is held constant — say, two offenders are equally culpable for equally grave acts — does retributivism have any internal resources for choosing between prison, fines, and restorative justice, or must it borrow from consequentialism to fill that gap? If you conclude that any part of their dispute is merely verbal, identify it and propose a reformulation that eliminates it.
