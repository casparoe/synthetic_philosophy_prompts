I'm teaching a philosophy of science seminar for advanced undergrads — about 15 students, 90-minute block. The topic is reduction and emergence, and I want an in-class activity that makes them *feel* why some philosophers think strong reductionism is false. Not just understand the argument, but viscerally get that knowing all the micro-level facts and laws doesn't necessarily let you predict the macro-level patterns without doing the work at the macro level.

Here's what I'm picturing: some kind of game or simulation where they're given complete information about the low-level rules and the initial state, and they have to make predictions (maybe with small stakes — extra credit points or candy) about what will happen at the higher level. The rules should be simple enough that anyone can apply them in a few minutes, but the outcomes should be surprising — the kind of thing where you can't just "see" what's going to happen from the rules alone. Then in the debrief we map their experience onto the philosophical positions: reduction, supervenience, weak vs. strong emergence, and maybe the difference between epistemological and ontological emergence.

I don't want to use Conway's Game of Life — half of them will already know it and it'll ruin the surprise. I need something less familiar but still simple to explain in five minutes.

Please give me: the full rules and materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, the prediction mechanic (how they bet, what they win/lose), and a detailed debrief script that connects specific moments in the activity to specific philosophical claims. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the emergence debate the activity *can't* capture — I'm guessing it only really gets at epistemological emergence and can't touch the ontological stuff, but maybe you disagree.
