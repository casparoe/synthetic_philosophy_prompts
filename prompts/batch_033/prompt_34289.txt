I'm currently taking a seminar on Sartre's *Being and Nothingness*, and there is a conceptual issue I cannot get to sit right in my head. I'm hoping you can walk me through it carefully.

Sartre holds that consciousness is translucent to itself: every conscious state involves a non-positional, pre-reflective awareness of itself. He explicitly rejects the Freudian unconscious and any picture on which the mind is partitioned into compartments that can hide things from one another. And yet the central phenomenon he wants to diagnose — bad faith — is a kind of self-deception. The person in bad faith somehow keeps the truth from themselves. This looks like a problem: if consciousness is unitary and transparent, where is the hiding supposed to happen? How can I be both the deceiver and the deceived without already knowing, at some level, what I am supposedly concealing, which would seem to undo the deception?

I am not looking for a paraphrase of the familiar examples — the café waiter, the woman on the first date. I have read those. What I need is an explanation of the *conceptual machinery* that Sartre thinks makes the phenomenon possible on his own terms. In particular, please explain how the distinction between facticity and transcendence, together with Sartre's account of belief as a project rather than a static state, is supposed to resolve the apparent paradox. If you think the machinery does not actually resolve it — if there is a residual tension Sartre does not adequately address — I would be interested in that assessment as well, but only after you have laid out how the account is supposed to work from the inside.
