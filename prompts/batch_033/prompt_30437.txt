I want you to construct a thought experiment that isolates a specific tension within Frank Ramsey's view of the meaning of life as expressed in the epilogue to his 1925 collection *The Foundations of Mathematics and Other Logical Essays*. In that epilogue, Ramsey rejects the idea that the vastness of the cosmos should induce humility or diminish the value of human life. He writes that he does not feel humble before the heavens because the stars, though large, cannot think or love; his picture of the world is drawn in perspective, with human beings occupying the foreground and the stars shrunk to the size of threepenny bits. The eventual cooling of the universe is discounted to near-nothing by its remoteness in time. The world, he says, is not in itself good or bad—it simply thrills him, and he takes this to be a reasonable stance because being thrilled is pleasanter and better for one's activities than being depressed. The view, in short, combines a kind of pragmatism about the good with a stubborn refusal to let cosmological scale dictate what matters.

Now here is the tension I want the thought experiment to bring out. Ramsey's position seems to rest on two commitments that could come apart: (i) the *perspectival claim*—that we are entitled to draw the world in perspective, giving foreground to what we care about and treating scale or duration as irrelevant to value; and (ii) the *pragmatic claim*—that a feeling or attitude is reasonable if it is pleasanter and more conducive to flourishing than its alternatives. In the epilogue these two claims pull in the same direction, but a well-designed case might reveal circumstances in which they point different ways.

Construct a realistic, one-paragraph scenario in which an ordinary person—call him David—must decide how to respond to a piece of information that, by his own lights, is cosmically deflating. The scenario should be engineered so that:

(1) David's most natural *perspectival* response—the attitude that would keep human concerns in the foreground and refuse to let cosmological scale dictate what matters—is one that, in this particular case, makes him *less* happy, less effective in his activities, and overall worse-off by pragmatic criteria.

(2) Conversely, the response that would be pragmatically better—the one that would leave him more cheerful and more capable in his daily life—is one that requires him to *abandon* the perspectival stance and instead take the cosmic fact seriously as a reason to revise what he cares about.

The cosmic fact should enter the story naturally (something he reads, a remark from a colleague, an ordinary experience), and the scenario must be mundane—set it in a workplace, a family, a friendship, or a similar everyday context. No trolleys, no demons, no science-fiction elements, and no perfect predictors.

After presenting the scenario, do the following:

(a) State clearly what David's two possible responses are, and explain which response the perspectival claim favors and which the pragmatic claim favors. Show your reasoning: what, specifically, about each response aligns it with one claim and puts it in tension with the other?

(b) Identify the structural feature of the scenario that causes the two claims to diverge. Is it that David's flourishing depends on a belief he cannot sustain while maintaining the perspectival stance? Is it that the perspectival stance itself introduces a kind of emotional cost in this particular context? Be precise. Then explain whether removing that feature would collapse the case back into one where Ramsey's two commitments again pull together—or whether it would simply shift the tension elsewhere.

(c) Finally, explain how a defender of Ramsey might try to resolve the tension without abandoning either the perspectival or the pragmatic claim—for instance, by refining one of them, or by arguing that the scenario misapplies one of them. Then explain why a critic would find this response unsatisfying, drawing on the specific details of the scenario you have built.

The goal is not to refute Ramsey's view but to produce a case sharp enough to force a Ramsey-style position to clarify the relationship between its two core commitments—a case that could anchor a serious seminar discussion about whether the epilogue's cheerful confidence is stable under pressure.
