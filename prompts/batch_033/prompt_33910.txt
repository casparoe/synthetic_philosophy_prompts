I'm a high school student preparing for a debate in my political philosophy club. The motion is: "Religious citizens should refrain from appealing to their religious convictions when arguing for laws and policies in the public square." Could you act as an expert on Rawlsian political philosophy and its critics, and write a dialogue between two characters: one who defends a version of Rawls's idea of public reason (roughly, that in a pluralistic democracy, coercive laws must be justified by reasons all reasonable citizens can accept, which excludes purely religious arguments), and one who argues that this standard is unfair to religious citizens and wrongly excludes their deepest commitments from democratic deliberation?

The dialogue should consist of exactly five exchanges, each exchange being a speech from each character. Neither character should be a strawman — both should make their strongest case, and they should genuinely respond to each other's points rather than talking past each other. Please label the exchanges "Exchange 1," "Exchange 2," and so on.

After the dialogue, please include a brief note that separates what is broadly settled among political philosophers working on this topic from what remains genuinely contested, so that I don't walk away from the debate treating a live controversy as though it were resolved.

Before writing, please take a moment to think through the strongest arguments on each side and map out how they interact across the five exchanges.
