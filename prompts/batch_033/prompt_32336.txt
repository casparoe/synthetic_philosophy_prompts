I'm enrolled in a seminar on the philosophical problem of the meaning of life, and we recently spent two weeks on Moritz Schlick's view that a meaningful life is one lived in the spirit of play — that is, activity pursued for its own sake, whose value is intrinsic to the doing rather than derived from any external goal it serves. I'd like to put together a diagnostic tool for my classmates: a "Which account of the meaning of life do you actually hold?" quiz.

The quiz should sort respondents by their pattern of answers to concrete cases, not by which label they find appealing. I want eight to ten questions, each presenting a brief scenario and offering two or three response options that force a choice between competing criteria of meaningfulness. The cases should include, at a minimum:

- A musician who spends decades practicing alone, never performing for an audience, purely for the joy of the activity.
- A scientist who makes a major discovery but dies before anyone else learns of it.
- A parent who sets aside a promising career to raise children.
- A child absorbed in building a sandcastle that will be destroyed by the tide within the hour.
- A political activist who devotes years to a cause that fails.
- A person on their deathbed who reflects that their life was full of suffering and failure, yet they loved deeply and made something beautiful.
- A person who feels that their life has meaning only if God exists.
- Someone who works a job they dislike solely to fund a future dream.

The scoring key should map combinations of answers onto at least the following positions, each of which should appear as a leaf:

- Schlick's play account (meaning is intrinsic to activity done for its own sake).
- A purpose or achievement account (meaning comes from accomplishing goals or contributing to something beyond the self).
- An objective-list account (meaning consists in engaging with certain objectively valuable things: love, knowledge, beauty, and so on).
- A narrative account (a life is meaningful insofar as it tells a coherent, valuable story).
- A supernatural or divine-purpose account.
- A hedonist account (meaning reduces to the quality of subjective experience — pleasure, happiness, or the balance of enjoyment over suffering).
- An existentialist or constructivist account (meaning is not discovered but created by the individual through authentic choice).
- A nihilist or deflationary account (the question of life's meaning has no positive answer; the concept is confused or empty).

The quiz should be built from the logical space first: identify the key distinctions that separate the positions (intrinsic versus instrumental value of activity; whether meaning can be subjective or must be objective; whether suffering is compatible with meaningfulness; whether meaning requires contribution beyond the self; whether a divine framework is necessary; whether meaning is discovered or created; whether meaning is a property of moments or of whole lives), and then design questions that test those distinctions through cases. After designing the questions and the scoring key, please note any combinations of answers that do not correspond to a named position, and briefly diagnose each: is the combination unoccupied because the view it would represent is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored in the literature?

Finally, please flag any pair of positions that the quiz cannot reliably separate, and explain what case or question might be added to distinguish them.

The quiz is meant for philosophy students who have some familiarity with the landscape of theories but who may not have reflected on which position they actually occupy. They should not need to know the labels to answer the questions; the questions should pull out their commitments through their reactions to the cases.
