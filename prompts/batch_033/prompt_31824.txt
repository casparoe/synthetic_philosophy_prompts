I'm a software engineer who works on data compression, and I've been drawn into a recurring argument at work that I'd like to get to the bottom of. Several colleagues, citing the Hutter Prize and some papers on algorithmic information theory, keep asserting that compression and prediction are formally the same thing. The story they tell goes like this: Solomonoff induction shows that if you have a sequence of observations, the shortest program that outputs that sequence — its Kolmogorov complexity — is also the best predictor of the sequence's continuation. The intuition is that a program achieves compression only by finding genuine regularities in the data, and those same regularities determine what comes next. A program that overfits by memorizing noise will be longer than one that captures the true structure, so minimizing description length automatically guards against overfitting. From this, they conclude that Occam's razor is not just a heuristic but a theorem: under any computable prior, the simplest theory converges to the truth. And they apply this to justify using Minimum Description Length for model selection, to argue that the model that best compresses the training data is the one that will generalize best, and sometimes to claim that progress in compression is progress toward general intelligence.

I find the analogy compelling but I also know enough to be uneasy. The Solomonoff predictor is uncomputable — we can never actually find the shortest program, only approximate it. The choice of universal Turing machine introduces an additive constant into all Kolmogorov complexity measurements, and for any finite dataset that constant could be arbitrarily large relative to the data, so two UTMs can disagree on which of two programs is simpler. And the actual Solomonoff predictor is a weighted mixture over all programs, not the single shortest one — that seems like more than a technical detail, since the mixture might give different predictions from the MAP program. I want someone to audit the "compression equals prediction" analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table: what maps onto what in the picture, and what role each element is supposed to play. Then walk through where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that are fatal to the intended use of the analogy from the ones that are merely caveats. And tell me whether the conclusion — that the best compressor is the best predictor, and that this formally justifies Occam's razor — survives once everything is on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying more weight than it can bear.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
