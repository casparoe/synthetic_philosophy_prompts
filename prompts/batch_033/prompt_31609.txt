Alan Turing argued that the realistic path to a thinking machine was not to program adult-level intelligence directly, but to build a comparatively simple and partly unprogrammed device and then educate it over time, much as one educates a child—through a graded curriculum and something like reward and punishment. He died in 1954, before any of the relevant technology existed in its present form. Today the field has developed in a direction that partly resembles his proposal and partly departs from it: large language models are trained by next-token prediction on vast corpora of human text and then further shaped by reinforcement learning from human feedback, and these same systems are now being used as tutors, graders, and writing aids in schools and universities. Reconstruct as carefully as you can what Turing would have thought of these developments, making the reasoning from his recorded views to your predictions explicit. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The five points should address: (1) which features of contemporary machine learning he would most likely regard as vindicating his views about learning machines; (2) which features he would most likely regard as departures from, or betrayals of, the kind of machine education he had in mind; (3) whether he would see reinforcement learning from human feedback as a genuine implementation of his reward-and-punishment idea or as something importantly different; (4) what he would likely say about the use of these systems as educators of human students; and (5) the advice or prediction he would most likely offer about the next stage of machine learning.
