I am a retired condensed-matter physicist — forty years in the laboratory — who has turned to philosophy of science in my retirement. One thing that has long troubled me, and which I now have the leisure to think through properly, is the phenomenon of research fads: entire subfields converging on a technique or a theoretical framework with enormous confidence, only for it to unravel a decade later. I have been reading the literature on information cascades and rational herding — Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch (1992), Banerjee (1992), and more recent philosophical work on the epistemology of scientific consensus — and I want to operationalise what I have learned.

Specifically, I want a decision procedure I can use to assess whether a given scientific consensus or research trend is genuinely evidence-driven or is being sustained by an information cascade. This is for my own use as I read new papers and listen to colleagues at conferences. I want something I can run mentally, without a spreadsheet, in perhaps fifteen minutes of reflection.

The procedure should take the form of a sequence of diagnostic questions, ordered so that the cheapest or most decisive questions come first. Each question should be something I can answer from publicly available features of the research field — citation patterns, the replication record, the structure of the argument in the key papers, the sociology of who entered the field when and why, the presence or absence of a clear stopping rule, and the fragility of the consensus to the release of new public information. The procedure should distinguish between at least the following:

- A genuine consensus, where independent agents have each updated on shared evidence and converged;
- An information cascade, where later entrants rationally discount their private signals because they observe the choices of earlier entrants;
- A reputational cascade, where people conform not because they believe but because they fear the professional costs of dissent;
- And any other distinct type the literature identifies that you think deserves its own leaf.

I want the procedure to be honest about what it cannot do. In particular, flag any steps where the distinction between an information cascade and a genuine consensus is in principle unobservable from the outside, and say what sort of evidence would be needed to break the tie. Also flag any steps where the procedure requires a judgement call rather than a factual determination, and indicate how much room for reasonable disagreement that judgement call admits.

Please also run the procedure on two cases from the history of science that you know well: one where the procedure would have flagged a genuine cascade, and one where it would have vindicated a consensus that was in fact reliable. I am especially interested in a case where the procedure gives an answer that would have been unpopular at the time — that is, it would have told someone to doubt a consensus that everyone around them accepted. For each case, walk through the questions in order and show where the procedure stops and what it concludes.

Before you write the final procedure, please spend some time thinking about which diagnostic questions genuinely cut between the different types, and which are merely correlates. The questions should be diagnostic, not just suggestive. I would also like you to note any combination of answers that the procedure places in a particular category but that you think is philosophically unstable or incoherent — that is, a region of logical space that is unoccupied for good reason.

Please adopt the role of an expert in social epistemology and the philosophy of science, with a thorough command of the information cascade literature and its philosophical implications. Think through the problem carefully, and take the time to get the diagnostic questions right. I am not in a hurry.
