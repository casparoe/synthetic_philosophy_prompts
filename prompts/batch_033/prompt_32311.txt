I work as an electrician, and for the past few years I've kept a notebook in which I write down predictions about my own life—how long a job will take, whether a friendship will survive a move, how I'll feel about a decision six months out—and then check them later. The habit has forced me to notice something uncomfortable: my future self is often more confident than I am now, but not always more reliable. After a long shift, or after I've spent an evening with a very persuasive friend, I find myself almost certain of things that look shaky to me now. That has left me stuck on a question. Should my present opinions defer to the opinions I expect to have later? I've since learned that one version of this idea is called Van Fraassen's Reflection principle: roughly, my confidence in a claim, conditional on my future self being, say, 80% confident of it, should itself be 80%. The principle has an obvious appeal—my future self will have thought about it longer, or will have more information. But it also seems to have obvious counterexamples: if I know I'll be sleep-deprived, drunk, grieving, or being manipulated, why should the confidence I'll have under those conditions carry any authority over what I believe now? And yet, if I reserve the right to dismiss my future self whenever I don't like where my beliefs seem to be heading, haven't I just found a way to never update on anything inconvenient?

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two people who disagree about this. One defends something like the Reflection principle: the view that your present beliefs should line up with the beliefs you expect to have later, at least in the relevant cases. The other rejects it, pressing cases of anticipated irrationality, impairment, and manipulation, and arguing that the principle either admits fatal exceptions or collapses into something too weak to be interesting. Please make both characters equally competent; neither should be a strawman. The defender should have real answers to the counterexamples—for instance, about how to draw the line between a future self worth deferring to and one that isn't, or about what it even means to treat your future self as an expert—and the critic should have real answers to the charge that rejecting the principle licenses epistemic stubbornness. Above all, the dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth in which each character responds to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them, and by the end the reader should see precisely what makes the question hard.
