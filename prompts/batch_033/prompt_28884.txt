In most ordinary moral discourse, we treat a piece of moral criticism as dismissible — or at least as carrying less weight — when it comes from someone who engages in the very conduct they are condemning. The cheating spouse who lectures others about fidelity, the polluting corporation that runs ads about environmental responsibility, the politician who evades taxes while denouncing public fecklessness: in each case the common reaction is not that the criticism is false, but that the speaker lacks the standing to make it. We say "you're one to talk," "clean up your own house first," "who are you to judge?" And this reaction isn't merely an emotional one; it is treated, in practice, as a reason to set the criticism aside — in workplace complaints, in public debate, in friendship, and sometimes even in legal reasoning (for instance, in the clean-hands doctrine in equity, or in the admissibility of certain character evidence).

But this practice is puzzling in several ways. First, it seems to treat moral criticism as something other than the assertion of a true proposition: if the criticism is true, why should the identity of the speaker matter? We don't dismiss a mathematician's proof because the mathematician is a hypocrite. Second, the practice is selective. A judge who has committed crimes can still sentence a criminal; a philosopher who doesn't live by their own ethical views can still be correct; a doctor who smokes can still tell a patient to quit. So the practice of discounting hypocritical blame doesn't apply to all normative speech — it seems to track something specific about blame in particular. Third, the practice seems to presuppose that blame does something beyond describing a wrong: that it involves a kind of standing, authority, or entitlement that can be forfeited. But what exactly is that thing, and how is it forfeited? Does any inconsistency between one's behavior and one's criticism destroy standing, or only certain kinds? If I condemn dishonesty but once told a white lie, have I lost standing to blame a fraudster? If not, where is the line?

I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of blame — of what blame is and what it requires of the blamer — that this widespread practice presupposes. In particular:

- What must blame be, such that hypocrisy undermines it in a way it doesn't undermine other forms of normative speech (advice, legal judgment, mathematical criticism)?
- What account of standing does the practice commit us to? Is standing a matter of the speaker's own moral record, their commitment to the norm they invoke, the relationship between speaker and target, or something else?
- Is the practice internally consistent? That is, does it treat hypocrisy as undermining blame in some contexts but not others, and if so, can those distinctions be justified by the underlying theory, or do they reveal a contradiction?
- What would have to change in our practices — in how workplaces handle complaints, in the norms of public political criticism, in the law — if we replaced the presupposed theory with a view on which blame is nothing more than the assertion of a moral truth, and standing is irrelevant?

Please take a definite position: don't just lay out the options. Tell me what theory of blame you think the practice actually commits us to, where the practice is underdetermined (so that several theories could license it), and where the practice is philosophically inconsistent and needs revision.
