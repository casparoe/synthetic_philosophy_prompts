Here's a practice I can't figure out what to make of. In most of the US, getting a basic driver's license means passing two tests: a written test on traffic laws, signs, and safe-driving rules, and a behind-the-wheel test where an examiner watches you actually drive. The two are treated as totally non-substitutable. Ace the written test but fail the road test, no license. Drive the road test flawlessly but flub a technical question on the written test about right-of-way at an uncontrolled intersection, also no license. The DMV is acting like knowing-how-to-drive and knowing-that-about-driving are two seperate boxes that both need ticking, and that neither reduces to the other. So: what view of the knowing-how/knowing-that relationship does this practice actually presuppose? Does it really commit the DMV to anti-intellectualism, the view that knowing how to drive isn't just possessing a set of true propositions about driving? Or can an intellectualist shrug and say the written test is just a cheap, legible way of sampling whether you possess the relevant propositions while the road test samples whether you can act on them under pressure? And is the practice even consistent with itself? We make a teenager pass both tests once, then keep renewing their license for decades without ever rechecking whether they can still state or apply any of it; plenty of experienced drivers can't tell you the exact safe following distance or what some sign means, and we don't take their licenses away for that. What does that asymmetry show about what the two tests are actually tracking? Finish by telling me what the licensing system would have to look like if we fully bought either of the two big views: (a) knowing-how just is a kind of knowing-that, or (b) knowing-how is irreducible and the written test is really about liability and accountability rather than epistemic competence.
