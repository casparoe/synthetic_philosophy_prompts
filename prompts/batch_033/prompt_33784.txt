A friend and I went back and forth for hours last night about negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics, and he ended with an argument he said I couldn't escape. I've been turning it over since and I still can't find the exact place it goes of the rails.

(1) Suffering is bad in itself, wherever it happens and whoever it happens to — humans, farmed animals, wild animals, doesn't matter.
(2) Extreme suffering can't be made up for by happiness: no amount of happy lives elsewhere can make a world with even one life of torture acceptable, or better than that same world without the torture.
(3) The actual world contains an enormous amount of extreme suffering, and any realistic future with sentient life in it will too.
(4) A world with no sentient life at all contains none of that suffering.
(5) So, by (2), the lifeless world is better than the actual world — and better than any realistic future with sentient life — whatever else those worlds contain.
(6) If a world is better, and we could painlessly bring it about without anyone being deprived, we should bring it about.
(7) So suffering-focused ethics says: in principle, painlessly end all sentient life if we ever can, and short of that, don't create any new sentient life — no kids, no farmed animals, and ideally fewer wild animals.
(8) But that's obviously a monstrous conclusion, so (1) or (2) has to go, and the whole suffering-focused family collapses.

He says the only honest move is to reject (1) and admit suffering isn't bad in the way I think it is. I really don't want to do that — I'd give up a lot before I'd give up (1) or (2) — but I can't put my finger on where the argument cheats. Is this argument valid? If it is, which premise is least costly to reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If it isn't, which step is doing the sneaky work, and why did it look fine?
