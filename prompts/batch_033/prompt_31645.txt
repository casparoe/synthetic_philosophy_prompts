I am a novelist at work on a book set in Britain during the years around the 2016 referendum on membership in the European Union. My characters disagree about the vote itself, but the question my research keeps turning over is prior to that: who was entitled to a say in the first place. The franchise, as I understand it, was restricted to British, Irish, and qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in the United Kingdom, together with British citizens living abroad who had been on the electoral register within the previous fifteen years. It therefore excluded most citizens of other EU member states who were living and working in the UK, excluded British citizens who had lived abroad for more than fifteen years, and excluded sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds. Several of my central characters are among the excluded — a Polish care worker in the Midlands, a retired couple from Yorkshire who have lived in Spain for twenty years — and I find I cannot write their situation honestly without first understanding the structure of the question itself.

I would like you to analyze this referendum as a case study for the boundary problem in democratic theory: the problem of determining who should constitute the demos for a given decision. I am aware that the literature offers several standard answers — the all-affected-interests principle, the all-subjected principle, a stakeholder view, and the view that the demos is simply the historically given political community — and I want a disciplined mapping rather than a rehearsal of slogans. For each position, tell me where it would have drawn the boundary of the franchise, how it would judge the franchise that was actually used, and which features of the episode it handles well. Then say plainly where the theory runs out: which aspects of the referendum it misdescribes, ignores, or can accommodate only by stretching its own terms. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theory itself — whether it vindicates one of the standard positions, exposes a flaw in the way the question is usually posed, or reveals something the theory never anticipated. I want the analysis to run in both directions: the theory illuminating the case, and the case putting pressure on the theory.
