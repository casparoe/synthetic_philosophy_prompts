I cover science and ideas for a living, and I have been trying to pin down what makes some identity claims informative and others empty. Here are my rough verdicts on cases. That the morning star is the evening star: a genuine astronomical discovery, clearly informative. That the morning star is the morning star: empty, not something anyone could discover. That water is H2O: a real chemical discovery, informative. That water is water: empty. That Samuel Clemens is Mark Twain: informative to someone who knows both names but not that they belong to one man, and it seems to lose its informativeness once you learn it. That Clark Kent is Superman: informative to Lois Lane, trivial to the reader after the reveal, and trivial to an omniscient narrator, which suggests the informativeness lives in the knower rather than in the sentence itself. But here is the case that confuses me: if I stipulate in front of you that two newly introduced terms will both name one and the same planet, the claim that the first is the second seems trivial right away, even though it contains two different terms just as the morning star claim does. I am told philosophers have an old puzzle about exactly this. Is there one principle about meaning, reference, or cognition that captures all of these verdicts, or close to it? What would that principle say about a case I have not listed, such as the claim that the inventor of bifocals is the first Postmaster General of the United States? And which of my verdicts, if any, would it force me to revise?
