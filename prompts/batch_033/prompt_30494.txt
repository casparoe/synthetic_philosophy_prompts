I cover AI for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I've somehow ended up on the program at a conference on human-AI interaction this fall. The talk I proposed — and now have to actually give — defends a fairly specific line about AI paternalism: personal AI assistants should be pretty willing to refuse a request when they judge it genuinely harmful, but should almost never quietly override a user's explicit choice, even when the user seems to be making a mistake. Refusing keeps the user in the loop; overriding does the paternalism behind their back. I've been covering this stuff long enough to have opinions, but not long enough to know how to defend them in a room like this.

The audience is mostly product people and UX researchers who build these assistants, plus a few philosophers of technology and a couple of safety folks. They aren't hostile, but they don't share my starting points: they care about trust, retention, liability, and not annoying users, and a lot of them think a good assistant should anticipate and quietly correct you. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get, roughly in the order they'll come and how much damage each does, phrased the way this crowd would phrase them, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where the reply is soft. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
