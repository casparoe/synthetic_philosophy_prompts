'Explain Russell's paradox and what it shows about the limits of naive set theory. In particular, does the paradox show that self-membership is incoherent, and is banning self-membership enough to solve it?'

The student wrote (condensed a bit):

Russell's paradox comes from the unrestricted comprehension principle, the idea that every property determines a set. Take the property of not belonging to itself, and let R be the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. If R is a member of itself, then by the definition of R it is not a member of itself; but if it is not a member of itself, then it satisfies the defining condition and so is a member of itself. Either way you get a contradiction. So naive set theory, and Frege's system built on Basic Law V, is inconsistent. Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory avoids the paradox by banning self-membership: the axiom of foundation says no set can be a member of itself. So Russell's paradox shows self-membership is incoherent, and any set theory that forbids self-membership is safe. It's basically the set-theoretic version of the liar paradox, and both show that self-reference is the underlying disease.
