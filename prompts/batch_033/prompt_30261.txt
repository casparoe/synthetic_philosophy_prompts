As someone who teaches a graduate seminar on global priorities research and existential risk, I've been thinking about a challenge to longtermist reasoning that my students keep raising. The simulation argument, as Nick Bostrom formulated it, presents a trilemma: either the vast majority of civilizations at our technological stage go extinct before reaching the capacity to run many detailed simulations of conscious beings, or the vast majority of such civilizations have no desire to run such simulations, or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. Suppose one takes the third disjunct seriously — suppose one assigns a nontrivial probability, say above 0.3, to the proposition that we are in a simulation. The question I want to press on is whether this should substantially dampen the kind of fanaticism about the far future that underwrites strong longtermism: the view that the primary determinant of the value of our actions today is their effects on the very long-run future, and that reducing existential risk is therefore the dominant moral priority.

I can see several distinct pathways by which simulation-scenario probabilities might or might not undercut far-future fanaticism. It might matter whether simulated futures are likely to be cut short because the simulators terminate the program. It might matter whether our actions inside the simulation have any causal connection to what happens in base reality after the simulation ends. It might matter whether simulated beings and their experiences have moral weight at all, and if they do, whether a simulated far future counts for as much as a base-reality far future. And it might matter whether the simulation argument itself shifts our credence in the relevant empirical claims about existential risk, technological maturity, and the motivations of advanced civilizations — for instance, if we are in a simulation, perhaps the fact that we have not yet been terminated is evidence that simulators tend to let simulations run, which in turn bears on the expected duration of our own future. I would like you to give me your actual credence — a number between 0 and 1 — on the following proposition: *The simulation argument, if its premises are granted, substantially dampens the case for fanaticism about the far future*, where "substantially dampens" means it reduces the expected value of far-future-oriented interventions by at least an order of magnitude relative to a baseline that ignores simulation considerations. Then decompose that credence: what are the main sub-questions your number depends on, what rough probability do you assign to each, how do they combine, and which sub-question is your final credence most sensitive to — that is, which one would you most want to resolve before acting on far-future considerations?
