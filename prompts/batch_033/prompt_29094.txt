ok so ive been going back and forth on whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I keep seeing people argue past each other because nobody says what a theory of deaths badness actually has to do. before I read another paper I want the full spec. write the desiderata: what any adequate account of the badness of death has to deliver, with the case or thought behind each requirement.

cover at least these threads, and add anything you think ive missed:
- the Epicurean point that you and your death never coexist, so if something is bad for you it seems you have to be around for it to be bad for you. does an adequate theory have to reject that experience requirement, or can it keep the requirement and still get death to come out bad?
- the Lucretius symmetry argument: why is the nonexistence after you die bad for you if the nonexistence before you were born isnt. a theory has to do better than "one is in the future."
- the dying / being dead / losing-more-life distinction, and the timing problem: if death is bad, when is it bad? before it happens, at the moment it happens, or after, when the person no longer exists? can the badness be located at any time at all?
- cases where death is clearly not bad or even good: the terminal patient in agony, someone who rationally wants to die. any theory that makes death bad in every case is already out.
- the variability of how bad a death is: it tracks the counterfactual life lost, and maybe the person's age and projects and the shape of the life. does a death at 90 have to come out less bad than a death at 30 even if both lose the same number of conscious years, or is that a further commitment a theory can decline?
- posthumous harms: being defamed after you die, promises to the dead being broken, your lifes work destroyed. does a theory of deaths badness have to handle these, or are they a seperate question about posthumous harm?
- who is the subject of the badness supposed to be: the dead person, the person before death, the people left behind. can a dead person be the subject of a harm at all?

then tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences (like maybe the experience requirement is a commitment some views carry rather than a desideratum itself). flag any pairs you think cant be jointly satisfied — for instance if you locate the badness of death at a particular time, do you have to give up on it being bad for the dead person. i most want to know whether the list itself forces a choice between attractive requirements. and say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate theories.
