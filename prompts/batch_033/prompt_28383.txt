I've been researching evolutionary biology for a novel I'm writing, and I've ended up deep in the philosophical literature on biological functions. There is a debate there that has been running since at least the 1970s — between etiological accounts and causal-role accounts — and as far as I can tell, it has barely moved in fifty years. On the etiological view, the function of a trait is the effect for which it was selected: the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood explains why hearts were favored by natural selection. On the causal-role view, the function of a trait is the contribution it makes to some larger system capacity of the organism: the heart's function is to circulate blood because that is the role it plays in the circulatory system's capacity to deliver oxygen.

Both sides have their stock examples and standard replies. The etiological theorist points to a heart that, by developmental accident, never pumps blood but only makes a thumping noise, and says the heart is malfunctioning — its function is still to pump blood, and only a selectionist account can capture that normative dimension. The causal-role theorist replies that physiologists assign functions all the time without knowing or caring about selection history, and that their account handles artifact functions and engineered systems without an awkward appeal to a history of design. Each side has counterexamples the other side has been living with for decades. Nobody seems to budge.

What I cannot figure out is what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a substantive dispute about the nature of biological reality — about what properties organisms really have? Is it a merely verbal dispute about how to use the word "function," such that both accounts are compatible descriptions of the same world? Are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading — one asking why a trait is present in a lineage, the other asking how a trait contributes to the organism's present organization? Or is there a hidden empirical question buried in the disagreement that neither side has formulated clearly? I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, grounded in what each side actually argues, together with an honest account of whether anything — an empirical discovery, a conceptual distinction, a formal result — could resolve it, or whether the persistence of the debate is itself a signal that the question was ill-posed from the start.
