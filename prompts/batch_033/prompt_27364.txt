I have been designing a text typeface for the last several months, and the lowercase 'a' has turned into a philosophical problem. I have to decide, for every candidate glyph I draw, whether it is still an 'a' or has lost its a-ness, and my judgments are surprisingly stable even though I cannot state the rule behind them. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts.

Here are the cases. In each one, imagine the glyph set at the same x-height in an otherwise ordinary lowercase Latin alphabet.

1. A conventional two-story 'a': two closed counters stacked one above the other and sharing a vertical stem on the right. Verdict: clearly an 'a'.

2. A one-story 'a': a single closed loop with a straight vertical stem attached to its right side, the form common in geometric sans-serifs and in handwriting. Verdict: an 'a'.

3. A Greek lowercase alpha: a loop with the stroke continuing past the loop into a downward-curving tail on the right, the loop slightly teardrop-shaped and the whole thing nearly identical to (2). Verdict: not an 'a'. If I saw it in a Latin text, I would call it the wrong letter, not an 'a' with a Greek accent.

4. A capital 'A': two diagonals meeting at the top with a crossbar between them and no closed counter at all. Verdict: an 'a' — the same letter as (1) and (2), merely the uppercase form. I would not call it a different letter.

5. An 'o' with a short vertical stem added to its right side, where the stem starts at the top of the loop and runs down only two-thirds of the way to the baseline, ending in a clean terminal. Verdict: an 'a', though a crude one.

6. The same 'o', but with only a nub at the upper-right corner, about a quarter of the x-height long, rather than a real stem. Verdict: not an 'a'; that is an 'o' with a wart.

7. A two-story 'a' whose upper arch is left open at its left end, so that it does not close against the stem and only the lower loop is a fully enclosed counter. Verdict: an 'a'; the top story can be quite open before I stop reading it as an 'a'.

8. A mirror image of (1), reflected left to right: same parts, same measurements, but the stem is on the left and the counters open to the right. Verdict: not an 'a'; in a Latin text it reads as a reversed glyph, not as a letter of my alphabet.

9. A blackletter 'a' in a Textura hand: narrow, angular, built from straight strokes, with no smooth bowls at all and looking, at first glance, more like a boxy 'u' with a short stroke inside. Verdict: an 'a', once I recognize the style.

The rule I keep reaching for is something like this: an 'a' is a closed, roughly round counter — or a pair of such counters — sitting on the baseline at x-height with a vertical stroke attached to its right side. But I can already see that this rule misfires: it would let the alpha in (3) count as an 'a', and it would exclude the capital 'A' in (4) and the blackletter form in (9). So either my rule is wrong, or some of my verdicts are.

What I would like from you: state the principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that best captures these verdicts, as precisely as you can, and say which case motivates each clause. Then run your principle on a few cases I have not settled: (a) a one-story 'a' whose loop is left open at the bottom, as in many rapid handwriting styles; (b) the exact outline from (2) placed in a line of Greek text among alphas, omicrons, and betas; (c) a two-story 'a' whose lower loop is left open at the bottom; and (d) an 'a' reduced to a triangle with a vertical stem on its right side, in a highly geometric display face. Tell me what the principle says about each. Finally, tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would rather overturn one of my verdicts or one of the principle's clauses, and why.

Please write your answer in American English.
