Recently in a reading group on classical Chinese philosophy, someone kept invoking Gongsun Long's line from the White Horse Dialogue — a white horse is not a horse (bái mǎ fēi mǎ) — to settle a broader dispute about whether the kind-terms in our language track real divisions in the world. His move was roughly this: since the line can be made out to be true under some careful interpretation, our ordinary classifications must be imposed by language or convention rather than read off reality. Nobody pressed him on which interpretation he had in mind, and I suspect the conclusion rides entirely on that choice. I also suspect he is running together two readings of the pivotal fēi ("is not") — non-identity of concepts or terms versus exclusion from a class — without noticing that they yield very different claims.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that "a white horse is not a horse" could be used to express — for example, a claim about the identity of concepts or intensions, a claim about the extensions of terms, a claim about what it takes to be counted as a horse in a given context, and the more radical readings on which the line denies that particulars fall under kinds or that kind-terms answer to anything mind-independent. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false; then say which of the readings would actually license his conclusion that our kind-terms are merely conventional, and which leave natural kinds untouched. I am less interested in the purely historical question of what Gongsun Long "really" meant than in the menu of live readings, though please draw on the historical material wherever it clarifies a distinction.

Finally, say how confident you are in your overall treatment and single out the point you are least sure about.
