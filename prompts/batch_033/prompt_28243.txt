I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and we're deadlocked on a policy question I'm supposed to draft the memo for. Here's the situation.

There's solid evidence that administering propranolol (a beta-blocker) within hours of a traumatic event substantially reduces the chance that the memory consolidates into PTSD. It's already on our formulary for cardiac patients. The proposal is to develop a protocol where ER physicians can offer it to patients who come in after sexual assaults, serious car crashes, or similar traumatic events.

The commitee is split right down the middle, and not along the usual lines. One camp says this is straightforwardly therapeutic: PTSD is a devastating psychiatric illness, we have a chance to prevent it, and withholding that chance is like refusing to set a broken bone because the patient "only" has a fracture, not a chronic disability yet. The other camp says no — these patients are not sick, they've undergone a terrible but normal human experience, and what we're proposing is to pharmacologically alter their memory consolidation and emotional encoding. That's enhancement of normal functioning, not treatment of pathology. They point out that we'd be intervening in the very process by which a person makes sense of something terrible, and that there's something morally different about that compared to, say, giving a tetanus shot.

I need to draft a short decision memo — maybe three pages — that actually recommends something: adopt the protocol, reject it, or adopt with conditions. It has to engage the strongest version of the losing side's argument, not just dismiss it as slippery-slope handwringing or as cold-hearted gatekeeping. And we can't just say the therapy/enhancement distinction is fuzzy so we table it; we have to decide.

The thing I'm really wrestling with, and what I'd want the memo to address head-on, is whether there's a principled way to draw the line here that doesn't collapse under pressure — because if we say yes to this, I can already see the next request coming (exam anxiety, normal bereavement, shyness), and if we say no, we're drawing a line between "disease prevention" and "modification of normal experience" that I'm not sure I can defend. What's the best framework for thinking about this, and what does it imply for our vote?
