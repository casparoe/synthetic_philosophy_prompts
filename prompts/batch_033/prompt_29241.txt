I sit on my university's Senate committee on records and memorials, and I have been asked to draft the short opinion for a case the committee must decide next month. Two final-year students were expelled in 1963 under Regulation 14(2), which prohibited what the university then called conduct unbecoming a member of the University and was, in practice, used only against students known or suspected to be gay. The regulation had been lawfully enacted by the University Council, was published in the Calendar, and the disciplinary hearing followed the procedures then in force; it was repealed in 1974, but the expulsion still stands on the two students' academic records. The motion before us asks the committee to declare the expulsions void from the outset and to confer the degrees the students would otherwise have earned. I am a relatively new member of the committee, and I read Hart and Fuller as a law student years ago without expecting to meet them in a records committee.

The committee is split. Some members argue that the expulsions were lawful under the university's own regulations as they then stood, and that we should expunge the records and award the degrees as a present-day act of reparation, without pretending that the 1963 decision was invalid. Others reply that a rule whose sole function was the persecution of a class of students was so defective as an exercise of law-making that it was never university law at all, and that honesty requires us to say so outright. I would like the opinion to take the Hart–Fuller debate seriously. The Hart side would caution us not to blur the question whether this was valid university law with the question whether it was abhorrent, and not to rewrite the past merely to spare ourselves the discomfort of admitting that valid law can be wicked. The Fuller side would answer that law-making is a purposive enterprise subject to an internal morality, and that a provision of this kind is not merely bad law but a failure of legality itself.

The opinion should be something the committee could genuinely act on: a clear recommendation, the reasoning for it, and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Please write the body of the opinion as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction, headings, or sign-off; begin directly with the first numbered item. One of the five items should state what would change your recommendation — which argument or consideration, if it could be established, would lead you to a different outcome, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Use British English throughout.
