Exam question: Explain Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction in Two Dogmas of Empiricism. What exactly does he take to be wrong with the distinction, and what does he propose in its place?

Student answer: Quine's target in Two Dogmas of Empiricism is the empiricist assumption that there is a principled distinction between analytic truths, which hold solely in virtue of meaning, and synthetic truths, which hold in virtue of how the world is. He identifies two dogmas. The first is the analytic-synthetic distinction itself; the second is reductionism, the view that every meaningful sentence can be translated into a construction built from terms referring directly to experience. Quine argues that the first dogma rests on an unexplained notion of cognitive synonymy: explaining that "All bachelors are unmarried" is analytic by saying that "bachelor" means "unmarried man" presupposes the very synonymy it is meant to explain. He reviews attempts to define synonymy by appeal to definitions, to interchangeability salva veritate, and to semantic rules, and argues that each is circular or otherwise inadequate. He then links the two dogmas: the verification theory of meaning would license a notion of analyticity as truth that experience cannot overturn, but confirmation is holistic. Scientific statements meet experience only as a body, so any statement can be preserved in the face of recalcitrant experience if we adjust elsewhere in the web, and no statement is absolutely immune to revision. From this Quine concludes that the analytic-synthetic distinction cannot be drawn and that there are no analytic truths, only statements occupying more or less central positions in the web of belief. He recommends replacing the distinction with a holistic, pragmatic empiricism.
