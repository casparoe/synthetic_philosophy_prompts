I manage the inventory database for a dealership that buys and sells antique scientific instruments—brass microscopes, sextants, barometers, theodolites, and similar pieces. Several times a month we find two entries in the database that match on every field we normally record: maker, model, approximate date, dimensions, materials, engraving, and condition notes. Many of these instruments have no serial number, photographs are often missing or too poor to compare, and provenance is usually just a line like "estate sale, Leeds, 2019." When two entries match, I have to decide whether to merge them into one record or keep them as two. Merging when they are really two instruments means we may sell the same physical item twice or promise a customer something we do not actually have; keeping them separate when they are really one means we undercount stock and list the same instrument twice. A wrong merge is hard to undo because the records' provenance notes get combined, so I want the decision made carefully. I sign off on every merge, but I want the staff to reach a clear recommendation before it gets to me, and they need a procedure they can run in under half an hour without any background in philosophy.

I keep coming back to a thought experiment that I think bears on this. Imagine a universe that contains nothing at all except two spheres, exactly alike in size, material, color, and every other way you could describe them. Are there two things in that universe, or is there just one thing described twice? If two things can be exactly alike in every respect and still be two, then no amount of matching in my database could ever prove that two records point to the same instrument. If, on the other hand, exact likeness forces identity, then matching records ought to settle the question. I do not know which is right, and I suspect my inventory problem depends on it.

Please write me a decision procedure my staff can actually run on a pair of matching records, grounded in whatever is actually known about the difference between being exactly alike and being the very same thing: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to me because the question is one of judgment rather than record-matching. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire or cannot settle the matter. Then run it on these three cases, and do not soften the result when the procedure tells me not to merge records I would have merged: (1) two entries for a mid-nineteenth-century brass microscope that match on every field, but one record includes a repair invoice from a Glasgow workshop dated March 2019, and the other includes an auction-house photograph dated February 2019 showing an identical microscope in a Toronto collection; (2) two entries for a brass theodolite that match on every field, both acquired from the same estate, with no photographs and no serial number; and (3) two entries for a barometer that match on every field, except that one record carries a note reading "re-entered after the 2018 database migration, old number 0142" while the other does not.
