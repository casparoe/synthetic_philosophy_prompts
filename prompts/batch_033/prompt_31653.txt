I'm trying to get clear on what a theory of consciousness actually has to deliver when it comes to the relationship between phenomenal experience and our judgments about that experience. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is my entry point. In its canonical form, the paradox is pressed against epiphenomenalism: if phenomenal properties have no causal powers, then our beliefs about our own phenomenal states cannot be caused by those states themselves — yet we form detailed, confidently held, and apparently accurate beliefs about our experiences all the time, and the natural explanation of why we believe we are in pain right now is that the pain itself prompts the belief. The epiphenomenalist seems forced to say that the neural substrate of the experience causes both the experience and the belief, and that the correlation between the two is a byproduct — which makes the accuracy of the belief look like a coincidence of the worst kind. But the problem generalizes. Any theory that distinguishes between the phenomenal character of a state and its causal or functional role — whether it treats the former as identical to the latter, supervenient on it, or emergent from it — owes an account of why the judgments we make about our own experiences track the phenomenal character rather than merely the functional role. And any theory that denies the distinction owes an account of why there seems to be one.

So before I read another paper that treats the paradox as a local difficulty for one view, I want the full specification of what a theory in this area must get right. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of the experience–judgment relation must explain, which cases it must handle, which distinctions it cannot collapse, and which results from psychology or neuroscience it must accommodate — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Then mark which of the desiderata are non-negotiable and which are parochial commitments of particular camps. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, because the conjunction of causal closure, the reliability of introspective judgment, and the claim that phenomenal character cannot be reduced to functional role turns out to be inconsistent — identify the conflict and say which desideratum has to give way. That is the part I most want to see. Optionally, score epiphenomenalism, interactionist dualism, a higher-order thought theory, and one other view of your choice against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves; the scoring is only there to show that the list does real discriminatory work.
