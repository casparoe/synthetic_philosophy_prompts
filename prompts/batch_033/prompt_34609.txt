At a gallery opening last month, two friends of mine got into a running argument about a painter whose work was being shown posthumously — major canvases, critically admired — but who had been credibly accused of serious sexual violence. The argument went roughly like this:

(1) The first said it was perverse to stand there admiring the brushwork given what the man had done. The second replied that the paintings themselves contain no trace of the crimes, and that formal excellence doesn't evaporate when we learn something ugly about the maker.

(2) The first answered that aesthetic experience is never purely formal — you can't just quarantine biographical knowledge. Knowing the artist was a predator changes what you see in the work, and it should. The second said that's a psychological fact about what we happen to feel, not a normative fact about what the work is worth.

(3) The first pushed back: normative aesthetic judgment is answerable to the full experience of the work, and the full experience now includes revulsion. To insist on judging the work as if you didn't know is to ask for a make-believe evaluation. The second replied that this conflates the work with the encounter — the work's properties are stable, even if our access to them fluctuates.

(4) The first then shifted ground: even granting the distinction, there's a separate question about whether it's morally decent to treat the work as valuable. Calling it good art is a form of commendation, and commending things made by monsters is morally objectionable. The second answered that this turns aesthetic discourse into a policing exercise, and that the history of art is full of awful people — if we purge the canon on character grounds, we lose a lot of what makes the tradition worth having.

They never resolved it. Go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate: who has the better of each step, and what — if anything — are both of them missing? Present your adjudication as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
