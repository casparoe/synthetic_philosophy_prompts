Here is the exam question:

"In Reasons and Persons, Derek Parfit discusses a case in which a hitchhiker might benefit from being able to make threats he would actually carry out, and Gregory Kavka discusses a case in which someone is offered a reward for intending to drink a toxin. Both cases seem to suggest that it can be rational to make oneself irrational, or that there is something puzzling about the relationship between rational action and rational dispositions. Explain what each case is meant to show, identify at least one important difference between them, and discuss what that difference tells us about the limits of instrumental rationality."

And here is the student's answer:

"Both Parfit's hitchhiker and Kavka's toxin puzzle are meant to show that there are situations where instrumental rationality seems to recommend that an agent become irrational. In Parfit's case, a hitchhiker who has been robbed and left stranded in the desert would benefit from being able to credibly threaten a passing driver: 'Give me your money or I'll detonate this bomb and kill us both.' The problem is that carrying out the threat would harm the hitchhiker as much as the driver, so a rational hitchhiker would not actually follow through. But if the driver knows this, the threat is empty. So it would be in the hitchhiker's self-interest to be the kind of person who irrationally follows through on such threats. Kavka's toxin puzzle makes a similar point: you are offered a large sum of money tomorrow if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a non-lethal but very unpleasant toxin tomorrow afternoon. You don't actually have to drink it — you just have to intend to. But you can't simply decide to intend something; intentions respond to reasons. Since you have no reason to drink the toxin (you get the money based on the intention, not the act), you can't form the intention, so you can't get the money. Again, it would be useful to be irrational — to be able to form intentions against your own reasons.

The key difference between the two cases is that Parfit's hitchhiker involves a threat made to another person, while Kavka's toxin puzzle is purely self-regarding. This matters because it shows that the problem of rational irrationality isn't limited to strategic interaction; it can arise even in a single-agent setting. This in turn suggests that the puzzle isn't just about game theory or credible commitment, but about something deeper in the structure of instrumental rationality itself: the way it ties reasons for action to expected outcomes can systematically disadvantage agents who are fully responsive to those reasons. Parfit ultimately concludes that this shows the Self-interest Theory is false, because it is not always in one's self-interest to be disposed to act in one's self-interest."
