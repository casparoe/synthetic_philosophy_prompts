A friend and I argued late into the evening about whether a system that performs well on average is safe enough, or whether safety properly requires a guarantee about the worst case. He kept insisting that average-case performance is all anyone can realistically demand; I kept coming back to cases where a good average hides a rare but catastrophic failure. We could not settle it, and it has left me unsure how to do my job.

I sit on the review panel at a company that deploys machine-learning systems which make consequential decisions: screening loan applicants, allocating shifts in a hospital, and flagging equipment for maintenance. We see roughly one deployment proposal a fortnight. A mistake can mean real financial or clinical harm; we have an hour or two per proposal; most panel members have no formal background in this material; and the compliance director signs off only when the written rationale is defensible.

I would like a reusable decision procedure for deciding, for a given proposal, whether the evidence that a system behaves well on average is enough to trust that it will keep behaving as intended, or whether we should require worst-case guarantees — and, if the latter, which ones (for example, robustness to unusual or adversarial inputs, bounds on the worst plausible failure, or a proof that certain bad outcomes cannot occur). The procedure should be a checklist or set of tests to run in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run it on two or three hypothetical proposals, including at least one where the procedure gives an answer the panel will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Please write the answer in British English.
