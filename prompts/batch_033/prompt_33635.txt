Write a sorting quiz for an upper-level undergraduate epistemology reading group, under the working title Which Epistemology of Knowledge Do You Actually Hold? The quiz should place respondents into positions on the relationship between knowledge, the closure principle, and the tracking-style conditions associated with Nozick, but it must do so entirely through their verdicts on concrete cases, never by asking them which slogan or label they accept.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions. Each question presents a short, self-contained case and asks the respondent to judge, in the first person or about a named subject, whether the subject knows a given proposition, or whether the respondent would retract or insist on a knowledge claim in the situation. Draw on the standard stock of cases — fake barn country, the lottery, the zebra at the zoo, the stopped clock, the brain in a vat, the high-stakes bank — but re-describe each with enough detail that no one needs to have encountered it before. Give each question two or three answer options. No question may ask directly whether the respondent accepts closure under known entailment, the sensitivity condition, or the safety condition.

The positions the scoring key must distinguish are:

1. a Nozick-style tracking view, on which knowledge requires sensitivity — if the proposition were false, the subject would not believe it — and closure therefore fails;
2. a Dretske-style relevant-alternatives view, which also denies closure, but because only relevant alternatives need be ruled out, not because the belief must be sensitive;
3. a safety view, on which knowledge requires that the belief could not easily have been held while false, and which tends to preserve closure;
4. a contextualist view, on which the truth of a knowledge attribution depends on conversational standards, so ordinary knowledge claims and skeptical denials can both be correct in their own contexts;
5. a skeptical invariantist view, on which skeptical hypotheses cannot be ruled out and ordinary knowledge claims are therefore almost always false;
6. a Moorean, closure-preserving anti-skeptical view, on which one knows ordinary propositions and can use closure to rule out skeptical hypotheses.

If subject-sensitive invariantism — the view that knowledge is invariant but sensitive to the subject's practical stakes — can be made a separate leaf, add it; otherwise say why the quiz's questions cannot pull it apart from contextualism.

For each question, state which positions the answer options are meant to separate, and note any question where two positions are expected to give the same answer. Then give a scoring key: assign each answer option a vector of scores across the positions, and specify how a respondent's pattern maps onto the leaves. Be explicit about which answer patterns leave a respondent unclassified, and for each empty region say whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unreachable by these questions. Finally, flag any two positions the quiz cannot separate cleanly, and describe one additional case or question that would separate them.

Deliver the quiz as a numbered list of questions followed by the scoring key and the diagnostic notes, not as a prose essay. The aim is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by the labels they like; the diagnostic work should live in the cases and the answer options.
