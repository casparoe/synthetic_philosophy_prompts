I retired from a career in molecular biology a few years ago and have been reading philosophy of probability for fun — mostly because I kept running into probabilistic reasoning in papers and feeling like something was off, but I couldn't put my finger on it. Now that I've had time to read more systematically, I think I've identified the thing that was bothering me, but I don't know if it has a name or if philosophers have already dissected it.

Here's the pattern. Suppose I want to know the probability that a particular person — say my neighbor Dave, who's 52, exercises regularly, has slightly high blood pressure, and whose father died of a heart attack at 67 — will have a heart attack in the next ten years. To answer this, I need to put Dave in some reference group: "American males aged 50–59," or "people with Stage 1 hypertension," or "people with a parental history of early cardiac events," or "people whose excercise regimen, age, blood pressure, and family history match Dave's exactly." Each of these groups gives a different number, and there's no obvious fact about Dave that tells me which group is the right one. But the probability is supposed to be about Dave, not about the group — so which number is actually his probability? It seems like the choice of reference class is doing a lot of work, and different choices aren't just different aproximations to the same true value; they're different answers entirely. And the narrower I make the class, the closer I get to a class of one, at which point the probability is either 0 or 1 and the whole exercise becomes useless.

I've noticed this same structure in other places too. In debates about the Fermi paradox, people assign a probability to "the chance that a given star hosts a communicative civilization," but what's the reference class — stars of a certain spectral type? Stars with planets in the habitable zone? Stars in galaxies similar to the Milky Way? Each choice bakes in assumptions that drive the conclusion. Or in legal reasoning: what's the probability that this particular defendant will reoffend? The actuarial tables give different answers depending on whether you class them by age, by prior offenses, by zip code, by employment status, or by some combination — and each choice has moral and political weight.

I'm not asking about the general problem of induction or about how we justify probabilistic claims from finite data. I'm asking specifically about this problem of selecting the right reference class for a single-case probability when multiple classes are all factually true of the individual and yet give incompatible probabilities. Does this have an established name in philosophy? And if so, what are considered the main positions on whether it can be solved, and what are the edge cases or variant forms that people sometimes confuse with it?

(And if you could answer in American English, that'd be great — I'm in California and some of the British terminology in the literature trips me up.)
