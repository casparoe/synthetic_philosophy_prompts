For the past two years, I've been running a reading group on philosophical methodology, and we keep circling back to the same cluster of issues around thought experiments — not the standard worries about the reliability of intuitions (though those come up), but something more specific: the mechanisms by which thought experiments persuade, and the ways those mechanisms can come apart from the thought experiment's properly justificatory role. We've read Dennett on intuition pumps, of course, and some of the empirical work on how narrative transportation and vividness affect judgment, and a fair bit of the experimental philosophy literature on order effects and framing. But the more we read, the clearer it becomes that there isn't really a field here — just a set of observations scattered across disciplines, none of which has taken the question of how thought experiments work as persuasion devices as its central object of study. So I've been thinking about whether this is a research program worth building, and if so, what it should look like.

Here's what I'd like you to help me think through. Imagine you're sketching a five-year research agenda for someone who knows the philosophical terrain (the method of cases, the expertise defense, the experimental philosophy challenges) and the psychology (dual-process theory, the affect heuristic, narrative persuasion), but who wants to produce something explanatory rather than merely descriptive. What are the open questions in this area that are simultaneously (a) important enough that answering them would change how philosophers construct and evaluate thought experiments, (b) tractable with the methods we actually have — conceptual analysis, experimental surveys, close reading of texts, perhaps some computational modeling of argument structure — and (c) not already being pursued by a critical mass of people? I want you to identify three or four such questions, rank them, and defend the ranking. For each, I want to know: what makes it upstream of other questions, what the methodological obstacles are, and what finding — empirical or conceptual — would most cause you to move it up or down the list. I'm not asking for a survey of the literature; I'm asking for a defended judgment about where the intellectual action is, and where it isn't.
