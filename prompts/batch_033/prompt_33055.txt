My friend who took a metaethics seminar last term explained the 'wrong kind of reasons' problem to me, and I'm not sure I trust her summary. Here's what she said, roughly:

"When you have a reason to adopt some attitude — like admiration, desire, or belief — the reason can be either object-given or state-given. An object-given reason points to a feature of the thing the attitude is about: the cake is delicious, so you have a reason to want it. A state-given reason points to some benefit of having the attitude itself: an evil demon will reward you if you admire him, so you have a reaon to admire him. The problem is that state-given reasons aren't real reasons. They're just pragmatic payoffs that have nothing to do with whether the attitude is fitting. And this is a big problem for buck-passing accounts of value, because those accounts say that for something to be valuable just is for there to be reasons to have certain attitudes toward it — so they can't distinguish the right kind of reasons from the wrong kind. Once you see the problem you realize buck-passing is hopeless."

I'm inclined to think she's overstating things, but I don't know the literature well enough to say where. Can you go through her summary and tell me which parts a careful philosopher working on this would accept, which parts are subtle misrepresentations, and whether her conclusion about buck-passing actually follows from the accurate parts?
