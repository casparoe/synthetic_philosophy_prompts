You are convinced the paradox is not a mere curiosity but exposes something deep about the grammar of psychological verbs — in particular, that "I believe p" does not function as a description of a private mental state but as a qualified assertion of p, which is why "p, but I don't believe p" strikes us as absurd even though it is not a formal contradiction. Here is my opening.

You say that "I believe it is raining" is not a report about myself but a tentative assertion about the weather — it is more like "It is raining" than like "He believes it is raining." But imagine this. I am wired to a device that scans my brain and displays my doxastic states on a screen in real time. The screen reads: "Currently believes: it is raining." I glance out the window, see clear skies, and say: "Apparently I believe it is raining — but it is not raining." That seems perfectly coherent. It does not sound like the nonsense of "It is raining but I don't believe it." Yet on your view, "I believe it is raining" is never a self-description; it is always an expression of belief. Here, however, I am plainly reporting on a belief I take myself to have while denying its content. Does this not show that "I believe p" can serve as a genuine self-report after all, and that Moore's paradox arises only when we mistake the expressive use for the only use?
