You are speaking to a machine learning engineer who builds and combines reward models for RLHF and preference-based fine-tuning. They are fluent in Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce preference models, logits and softmax readouts, the fact that a reward model is identified only up to a positive affine transformation, per-rater calibration and scale anchoring, ensembling reward models across raters and objectives, and Pareto frontiers in multi-objective RLHF. They have no formal background in welfare economics or moral philosophy, and they have never encountered the problem of interpersonal comparisons of utility under that description.

Restate the debate about interpersonal utility comparisons — whether, and on what basis, we can say that one person's gain is greater than another person's loss, and what must be true for adding utilities across persons to be meaningful — in this engineer's vocabulary, as faithfully and systematically as you can. Your translation should cover at least the following moves: the claim that a utility function representing a person's preferences is unique only up to a positive affine transformation; the claim that, because the rescaling may be done separately for each person, any aggregate that sums utilities across persons is underdetermined unless a common unit and a common zero are fixed; the Pareto criterion as the part of welfare judgements that survives without any such fixing; and the main philosophical responses — roughly, the ordinalist line (Robbins) that interpersonal comparisons are not factual and should be avoided, the line (Sen) that comparisons are value judgements but can still be made, perhaps only partially, and the line (Harsanyi) that an impartial or sympathetic spectator can ground them by treating each person's preferences as their own.

Then give the residue report, which is the point of the exercise. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly into things the engineer already does or knows? (I expect the affine-ambiguity point, the Pareto frontier, and the need to anchor or normalise scales before averaging to map closely.) Which parts did you have to distort to fit? Which parts have no counterpart in the target vocabulary at all — for example, the difference between measuring a common scale and choosing one, the idea that pooling raters can be a normative stance rather than a modelling assumption, or the question whether there is a ground-truth common reward to converge on at all? Where the engineer's framework would quietly assume a common scale as a modelling convenience, say explicitly that it is assuming rather than deriving it. For each item of residue, say whether it shows that the engineer's vocabulary is missing a distinction, or that the original philosophical claim was less precise than it looked. End with an honest verdict on the engineer's likely reaction — 'this is just a calibration problem' — and state what that reaction would leave genuinely undecided.
