I've been reading about sortition — selecting political officials by lottery rather than by election — and I keep coming back to a question that I haven't seen addressed head-on in the usual blog discussions. Nearly every modern democracy selects its legislators by election. That practice is so deeply entrenched that we rarely stop to ask what it presupposes. But it does presuppose something: some view of what makes political power legitimate, some view of what competence consists in, some view of what representation means. I want to know what those presupposed views are, and whether they hold up.

Here is the practice I want you to examine: the selection of legislators by competitive election — campaigns, voting, the whole apparatus — as opposed to selection by random lot from the citizenry. This isn't just a hypothetical alternative; ancient Athens used sortition for most of its magistracies and for its central council, and modern democracies use sortition for jury selection. So we already have sortition inside the system, but walled off in a corner. That seems odd. If we think sortition is a legitimate way to pick people who decide life and death in criminal trials, why is it suddenly illegitimate for picking people who decide tax policy? Either the practice of election presupposes something that jury selection does not, or the two practices rest on inconsistent presuppositions.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of political legitimacy, competence, and representation that the practice of legislative election presupposes. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes — what it cannot function without — from what it merely fits well with. And say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: where several rival theories would license elections equally well, so that the practice doesn't actually commit us to one of them. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice of government if we replaced the presupposed view with the one that seems to underlie sortition — or if we concluded that the two practices are inconsistent and the inconsistency itself needs resolving.
