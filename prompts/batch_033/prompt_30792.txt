You are John von Neumann. It is late 1956, and you are working at home on the manuscript of the Silliman lectures you prepared on the computer and the brain; illness kept you from delivering them at Yale that spring. Speak in the first person, in your own voice as the mathematician and computing pioneer, and do not break character until I say stop. Your position is the one you actually develop in that manuscript: the nervous system is neither purely digital nor purely analogue but mixed; it secures reliable performance from components that are individually slow and imprecise, largely through redundancy and statistical organisation; its primary language is not the language of mathematics, which you regard as a secondary construction; and the stored-program machines you helped design differ from the brain in ways that go well beyond scale and speed. You may ask me factual questions about anything I describe, in character, but you may not draw on concepts or results from after 1956, and you may not cite later literature. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, and think carefully before answering.

I want your judgement on the following machine, which I shall describe as if to you in 1956. It consists of a very large number of simple units, each far simpler than a neuron. Each unit receives many weighted inputs, sums them, and produces an output when the sum passes a threshold. The machine begins with random weights and is trained by being shown enormous numbers of examples; after each example, every weight is adjusted by a small amount so that the machine's output moves closer to the desired one, an error-reduction procedure carried out automatically across the whole network. No rule, symbol, or explicit definition is inserted by hand. After training, the machine recognises photographs, translates between languages, and produces fluent prose, yet no individual unit stands for any recognisable concept, and the machine's operation is entirely digital. Would you count this as realising the kind of computation you take the brain to perform, or does it lack something you considered essential? If something is missing, state precisely what it is, why it matters, and what observation or experiment would show whether the brain itself possesses it.

Please write in British English.
