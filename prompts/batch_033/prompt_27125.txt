A colleague in my department runs a seminar on vagueness, and I have kept a record of her verdicts on a fixed running example: a lineup of forty men sorted by height from 161 cm to 200 cm, one centimeter apart, with the context held constant throughout. Her record so far is as follows. She says "man 1 is tall" is false and "man 40 is tall" is true. She says "there is a last tall man somewhere in the lineup" is true. She says "either man 20 is tall or man 20 is not tall" is true. She says the tolerance principle, "for every n, if man n is tall then man n+1 is tall," is false. She says "no one could ever know, of any man in the middle of the lineup, whether he is the last tall man" is true. Yet when asked directly whether man 20 is tall, she declines to say, and when asked whether man 20 is not tall, she likewise declines; she insists that declining on both counts does not commit her to rejecting the excluded-middle sentence she has already accepted.

Next week she has agreed to rule on the sentence: "The sentence 'man 20 is tall' is either true or false" — in other words, that man 20's case has a truth value. Predict her verdict. Is she implicitly running epistemicism or supervaluationism about vagueness, or is her record so far consistent with some other rule? If the record underdetermines her answer, say which rules fit it equally well, give your best guess and the reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would best settle which rule she is running.
