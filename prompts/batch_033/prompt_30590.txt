You are a professional philosopher who works on AI alignment and conceptual engineering, comfortable with the technical literature but careful about distinctions. Think before you write; it may help to sketch in a few lines the strongest version of each position so that neither character is a strawman.

Write a short dialogue between two equally competent alignment researchers, Ada and Bo, who disagree about how the alignment problem should be defined.

Ada defends the narrow definition, intent alignment: the central problem is to make advanced systems robustly do what the human operator actually intends in the context of a given task, rather than pursuing a proxy, gaming the specification, or misgeneralizing. She doubts that any thicker target—"human values" or "what we would want upon reflection"—is well defined enough to serve as an engineering goal, and she suspects it smuggles in a different problem.

Bo defends the broader definition, value alignment: intent alignment is at best a way station, because an operator's actual intentions can be mistaken, inconsistent, malicious, or simply absent. The goal should be systems that act on what is genuinely valuable, or on human values in a suitably idealized sense, not just whatever someone happens to want in the moment.

Make the dialogue a real back-and-forth in which each character responds to the other's strongest points rather than talking past them. They should work through at least these fault lines: whether "intent" remains a coherent and accessible target when the system is more capable than its user; whether a perfectly intent-aligned system is still a failure when the user's intent is harmful or rests on false beliefs; whether the value-alignment target can be specified well enough to be an engineering target or collapses into unresolved normative disagreement; and whether the two views actually diverge in practice on corrigibility, sycophancy, and refusals of harmful requests.

Close the dialogue with a short, honest assessment of where their disagreement is substantive and where it may be partly verbal. Then, separately and briefly, flag which positions in the dialogue are settled among knowledgeable people and which are genuinely contested, being careful not to present any contested view as the consensus.
