I am a retired experimental scientist, and I have only recently begun reading epistemology seriously. What strikes me most is how rarely the fine distinctions in this literature are stress-tested the way a laboratory effect would be: isolate the variable, hold everything else fixed, and see whether the two things can come apart. I would like a thought experiment built to that standard.

The distinction I have in mind is Miranda Fricker's between testimonial injustice and hermeneutical injustice. I will state the two notions as I understand them, so the exercise does not depend on any particular edition of her work. Testimonial injustice occurs when a hearer gives a speaker a credibility deficit owing to identity prejudice, and the speaker is thereby wronged in their capacity as a knower. Hermeneutical injustice occurs when a person is unfairly disadvantaged in making sense of a significant area of their own social experience because the collective interpretive resources contain a gap, and that gap exists because the person's social group has been excluded from the practices through which those resources are built.

Construct a single ordinary workplace scenario in which a person suffers hermeneutical injustice but not testimonial injustice. That is the direction I find hardest to build cleanly, because the paradigm cases tend to entangle the two; I want to know whether they can really be pried apart.

Constraints. Set the scenario in a workplace such as a laboratory, clinic, or small firm — not a courtroom or a philosophy seminar — and keep it to one paragraph. There must be no overt bigotry and no villain; the hearers should be ordinary, decent colleagues. Above all, the absence of testimonial injustice must be earned rather than stipulated. The protagonist should make factual claims about the individual incidents that their interlocutors accept as true, and the injustice must persist even though nobody disbelieves them. If the case requires a concept to be absent from the local interpretive repertoire, build that absence into the scene rather than announcing it from outside.

After the scenario, do the check. State the experience the protagonist cannot make sense of, the gap in the collective resources, and why the gap counts as hermeneutical injustice on the criteria above — including how it traces to the marginalization of the protagonist's group rather than to mere ignorance or bad luck. Then state why there is no testimonial injustice: identify the exchanges in which they are believed, and explain why none of them involves a credibility deficit driven by identity prejudice.

Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work. I suspect it is that the wrong is located in the shared pool of concepts rather than in any hearer's assessment of the speaker; if so, show what happens when that feature is removed, and whether the case collapses back into the familiar entangled paradigm. Finally, say how a critic who denies that the two injustices can come apart this cleanly would attack the case, and whether the case can be repaired without conceding the critic's point. I want this for my reading group, where we are trying to decide whether Fricker's distinction carves nature at a joint or merely separates two aspects of one phenomenon.
