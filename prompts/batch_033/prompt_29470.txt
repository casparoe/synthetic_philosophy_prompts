You are a professional epistemologist who works on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming candidate principles before testing them against each case.

I have been collecting my own verdicts about how far one should defer to evidence about one's own reasoning, and I cannot tell whether a single principle captures them. Here are the cases.

1. You and a colleague you regard as your epistemic equal independently assess a long, intricate argument. You conclude it is valid; she concludes it hides an equivocation. You recheck twice and still see it as valid. I think you should lower your confidence substantially, roughly to 50/50.

2. You work through a perfectly transparent proof—a single modus ponens step from premises you can see are true. A reliable experimenter then tells you that you were just given a drug known to make most people overconfident in judging whether short proofs are valid, while leaving them feeling normal. You recheck the proof and it still looks valid. I think you should keep believing the conclusion; giving it up would be a mistake.

3. After forty hours without sleep, you become convinced you have found a subtle flaw in a long-established theorem. I think you should not trust the discovery; you should treat it as very likely an artifact of fatigue.

4. Doctors tell you that you have carbon monoxide poisoning and that it impairs reasoning while leaving people feeling perfectly lucid. You then find yourself judging that a body of evidence supports p. I think you should defer to the doctors and refrain from believing p on the basis of your own judgment.

5. A colleague you know to be systematically overconfident disagrees with you about whether an argument is valid. I think her disagreement should move you only slightly, if at all.

My tentative principle is this: higher-order evidence should move you in proportion to its reliability and to how non-transparent the relevant first-order reasoning is. That would explain why the proof in case 2 survives while the evidential judgment in case 4 does not. But I worry this is just a dressed-up way of saying "defer when the reasoning is murky, don't when it is clear," and it does not tell me how transparent is transparent enough.

Is there a single principle that captures these verdicts, or close? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and what it predicts for a further case: you learn from a well-conducted study that people with your personality profile are strongly overconfident about exactly the kind of holistic evidential judgment you have just made, though that judgment feels effortless and obvious to you. Also say whether your principle permits rational epistemic akrasia—believing p while believing that your total evidence does not support p—anywhere, and if so, where.
