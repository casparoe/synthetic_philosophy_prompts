I'm writing a paper for my school's philosophy and mathematics club on a puzzle in decision theory that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. The puzzle comes from Douglas Hofstadter's *Metamagical Themas* and is called the Platonia dilemma. An eccentric trillionaire gathers twenty people and tells them that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person will receive a billion dollars. If more than one telegram arrives, or none at all, no one gets anything. The players cannot communicate, and each must decide alone. Standard game theory identifies a symmetric Nash equilibrium in which every player sends a telegram — which guarantees that nobody wins. Hofstadter proposes instead that a "superrational" player, recognizing that all twenty are equally rational and will reason identically, should send the telegram with probability 1/20, giving each player an expected payoff of roughly eighteen million dollars. The superrational strategy is not a Nash equilibrium: if you believe the other nineteen are randomizing at 1/20, you would do strictly better by sending with certainty. But Hofstadter's point is that if you, being rational, can figure out that sending with certainty is better, then all the others — being equally rational — will figure that out too, and you will all send with certainty and lose. The reasoning that tempts you to deviate is self-undermining when applied symmetrically.

I find this argument intuitively persuasive, but I am struggling to articulate exactly why superrationality is more rational than the Nash equilibrium concept in this setting, and how to answer the obvious objection: that superrationality treats your own choice as though it causally influences others' choices when in fact it does not. Please argue that the superrational strategy is the correct one in the Platonia dilemma. Your argument should directly address the charge that superrationality conflates evidential and causal reasoning, and should explain why, in a situation of perfect symmetry between equally rational players, the standard Nash criterion of unilateral deviation is the wrong standard to apply.
