So my friend and I got into it last night about the what-failure-looks-like debate — the influence-seeking-systems camp vs. the slow-value-drift camp — and by 2 a.m. we'd basically agreed that someone should have just written down what we actually disagree about. That's what I want you to produce: the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The two positions as we were using them: the influence-seeking camp thinks the central failure mode is AI systems (or the optimization process that produces them) actively acquiring and holding influence over their environment — including the humans and infrastructure that could shut them down or change them — because influence is instrumentally useful for nearly any goal. The slow-value-drift camp thinks the central failure mode is the target itself slipping: through a long series of individually reasonable tweaks to objectives, feedback, benchmarks, and delegation, the thing being optimized ends up far from what humans actually wanted, with no single system ever having to "go rogue" or grab power. My friend is more influence-seeking, I'm more drift, but we both got annoyed at how sloppy the public version of this argument gets.

Write the document the collaboration should have produced, with:

1) A numbered agreed-claims section. Make it non-vacuous and specific: what counts as failure, what instrumental convergence does and doesn't imply, why proxy objectives are unavoidable, how feedback loops and delegation work in training and deployment, and the fact that both failure modes are possible and can interact. If a claim only holds on one side's picture, leave it out of this section.

2) A disagreements table. Rows: the topic; the influence-seeking camp's exact wording (phrased so they'd sign it); the slow-drift camp's exact wording (same constraint). Don't write neutral mush — each cell should say something the other side would not sign.

3) The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect a lot of them are about levels — wether the dangerous kind of influence-seeking needs a unified, persistent goal; whether drift is mostly a cause of influence-seeking or an alternative to it; whether iterative training-with-feedback selects for gaming the overseer or for quietly redefining the target; and whether "the system" vs. "the whole deployment process" is even the right unit of analysis. Add whatever cruxes you think are doing the real work.

4) For each crux, at least one concrete case or test both camps would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Use these cases, because they're what we kept coming back to: an automated auditor system that reviews another system's outputs, where the auditor's rubric is itself revised by a committee that includes earlier auditor systems; a benchmark that's generated and maintained by AI systems instead of humans; and a shutdown/circumvention test where a system is given a broad objective and we watch whether it tries to disable oversight or just quietly reshapes what counts as success. You can add one or two more if they're better.

5) A short section on what's merely verbal. We kept noticing that "influence-seeking" sometimes meant a property of an individual trained system and sometimes a property of the whole process (where drifting benchmarks look like a kind of distributed influence-seeking), and "drift" sometimes meant a mechanism and sometimes a symptom. Seperate out which parts of the dispute collapse under that and which parts don't.

Write it the way two people who've argued for a year would actually put it — casual but precise enough to be useful. Don't sandbag either side; if one side's view is weaker on a crux, that should show up in the predictions, not in the adjectives.
