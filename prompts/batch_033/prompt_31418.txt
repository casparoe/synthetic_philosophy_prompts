I'm trying to work out what I actually think a species is, and I'd like help turning my reactions to cases into a principle—or learning that no single principle can have all of them.

The definition I was taught is that a species is a group of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations. My verdicts don't line up with it.

1. A ring of populations around a mountain range. Population A interbreeds with B, B with C, C with D, D with E, and E with F, but A and F, whose ranges meet on the far side, do not interbreed. I want to say A and F are different species. But I also want the relation same species to be transitive: if A is the same species as B, and B as C, then A is the same species as C. And there is no adjacent pair in the chain where I'm willing to say the species changes. I can't see how to keep all three of these.

2. A group of organisms that reproduces only asexually—say, a clonal lineage of rotifers—has diverged into several clusters that are genetically and ecologically distinct but never exchange genes at all. I still want to call the clusters different species. So interbreeding can't be necessary for specieshood.

3. Two populations meet in a broad hybrid zone and produce fertile hybrids there, but they remain sharply distinct in appearance, habitat, and genetics across most of their ranges. I want to call them two species despite the gene flow. So interbreeding can't be sufficient for lumping them, either.

4. A single lineage in the fossil record changes gradually over several million years. The earliest forms and the latest forms look very different, and paleontologists divide the lineage into an earlier and a later species at some convenient point in the sequence. I want to say the division is conventional rather than the discovery of a real boundary, and I'm uncomfortable treating the earliest and latest forms as different species in the same sense as the two populations in case 3.

5. Two populations separated by a mountain range look and behave nearly identically, and I'm confident they would interbreed freely if the barrier were removed, but for now they have no contact. I want to call them the same species.

6. Domestic dogs and wolves. They can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, and yet I find it very hard to accept that a chihuahua and a wolf are the same species.

The principle I keep reaching for is roughly this: a species is a lineage evolving separately from other lineages—a population or metapopulation that maintains its own cohesion and its distinctness from the rest. But I don't know how to make separately evolving precise enough to handle the asexual case and the hybridizing case; it seems to say nothing about how much change makes a new species in the fossil record; and it may not respect my transitivity instinct in the ring case.

What I'd like from you: test this principle against the cases, repair it or replace it with something better, and state the result precisely. For each case, say what the principle delivers and why. Then tell me which of my verdicts, if any, I have to give up—and why those rather than the others. Finally, apply the principle to a case I haven't listed: Neanderthals and modern humans, who separated, evolved apart, met again, and interbred enough to leave a small percentage of Neanderthal DNA in many modern human populations. What does your account say about whether they are one species or two, and does that follow from the same principle that handles cases 1 through 6?
