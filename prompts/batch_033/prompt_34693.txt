I'm helping organize a workshop on democratic theory, and we've got two participants who have been going back and forth for months about sortition (random selection of citizens for political office) versus elections. One is basically a lottocrat — thinks we should replace most elected legislatures with randomly selected citizen assemblies. The other thinks elections are genuinely important for democratic legitimacy and that replacing them with lotteries would be a disaster. They've agreed to try an adversarial collaboration: produce a document they can both sign, laying out what they agree on, where they exactly disagree, and what would actually move either of them.

I've been tasked with drafting the first version of this document for them to react to. I need: a numbered list of claims both camps can accept (about the failings of actually existing electoral systems, about what sortition does well, about what elections do well, about the historical record, about the epistemic properties of random selection vs. elections, etc.); a disagreements table where for each point of disagreement, the precise statement each side would endorse is written in a way that side would accept as fair; the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would cause at least one side to significantly revise their view; and for each crux, a concrete test or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about what that test would show.

I'm especially worried about them talking past each other on "accountability." The lottocrat says sortition is more accountable because citizens can't be captured by donors; the electoralist says it's less accountable because you can't vote the bastards out. I suspect this is partly a verbal dispute but I'm not sure how to split it. If you think any part of their disagreement is merely verbal, flag it and say why. Also, please include a note at the end about what argument or piece of evidence would most likely change your mind about how you've framed the cruxes — I want to know where your own reasoning about the structure of this debate is shakiest.
