I cover science and ideas for a general-audience publication, and I am drafting a long-form essay about the phrase "the genetic code" and whether DNA is, in any substantive sense, a code. The essay grew out of a conversation with a developmental biologist who objected when I wrote, in an earlier piece, that a particular gene "codes for" a behavioral tendency; her point was that I had quietly imported a picture of DNA as an instruction manual. My working thesis is that DNA is a code only in a deflationary sense: there is a real, empirically established mapping between nucleotide triplets and amino acids, and sequence features help regulate expression, but the richer idea of DNA as a blueprint, a program, or an instruction manual is a metaphor that systematically misleads. In the essay I plan to distinguish causal or correlational information, in roughly the Shannon sense, from semantic information, and to argue that DNA carries the former only relative to the rest of the cell, while the latter is not something the molecule itself carries. I also plan to use a version of the parity argument — the idea that many non-genetic factors in development are just as causally specific and necessary as DNA sequence, so treating DNA as the privileged locus of information requires independent justification — and then to close by spelling out what this deflationary view implies for how journalists report on heritability, polygenic scores, and gene editing.

These are still notes rather than a full draft, and I would like your comments on the essay's conceptual structure before I commit to it. In particular: Is the distinction between causal and semantic information drawn correctly, and does it carry the argument as far as I need it to? Where is a thoughtful defender of the view that DNA carries semantic information most likely to push back, and how should I revise the argument to avoid a straw man? Does the parity argument overstate matters, given that DNA does have a distinctive role in templating protein sequence? And am I overreaching when I move from the philosophy to practical prescriptions for science journalism — if so, which implication should I soften or drop? I am less interested in copy-editing than in being told where the argument is wrong, unfair, or likely to embarrass me with a philosophically literate reader.
