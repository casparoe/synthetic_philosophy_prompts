I am a novelist working on a book in which one of the central characters was raised in a culture with a relational conception of personhood — something close to Ubuntu, where a person becomes a person through other persons. I find that I cannot write this character honestly without knowing where I myself stand, and the novel keeps forcing me into corners where my Western, individualist intuitions about personhood clash with the relational view my character would hold. I have been trying to settle the matter by testing my gut reactions against a range of cases. Here are the verdicts I find myself with:

1. A newborn infant, hours old, who has barely begun to form relationships. I have no hesitation: this is a person.
2. A child raised in total isolation from birth — a genuine feral child, never spoken to, never held. I do not want to say this child is not a person, but something feels missing, and I cannot say what.
3. An adult with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes anyone, no longer speaks, and seems to have lost every social connection she once had. I still feel she is a person, and I would be angry at anyone who said otherwise.
4. A man who commits a terrible crime and is permanently exiled from his community, cut off from all human contact. I do not think his personhood is diminished by the exile, even though his relationships are gone.
5. A family dog who has lived with the same household for twelve years, is deeply woven into its emotional life, and is grieved as a member of the family. I would not call the dog a person, but I am not sure my reasons are any good.
6. An artificial intelligence that can sustain rich, reciprocal, emotionally textured relationships with multiple people over many years. If it really can do this — a big if — I find myself pulled toward saying it is a person, or something near enough that the difference does not matter.
7. A recently deceased grandparent whose presence is still felt, consulted, and spoken to by the family. I do not believe the dead are persons, but I can feel the gravitational pull of the Ubuntu view that ancestors remain persons in the relevant sense.
8. A human fetus late in pregnancy. I have no settled view, but my gut ranks it above the dog and below the newborn.
9. A multinational corporation. It participates in relationships — contracts, partnerships, legal obligations — but calling it a person strikes me as a category mistake, and I want to say my resistance is not merely political.

I am trying to discover whether there is a single principle that captures all or most of these verdicts, and what that principle would look like. Does it end up closer to the view that personhood is an intrinsic property of the individual, or to the relational Ubuntu view? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up or revise? And what does the principle say about a case I have not listed but that matters for the novel: an adult human who, by settled choice and without pathology, lives as a hermit with no social ties for decades — is this person more or less a person than someone embedded in a dense web of relationships?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
