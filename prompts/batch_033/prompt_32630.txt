I serve on my university's Research Integrity and Misconduct Panel. We have received a complaint that is unusual enough that I am drafting the preliminary opinion for our next meeting, before we decide whether to open a formal investigation.

The facts are these. A senior scholar in our philosophy department published a monograph with a major university press last year. The book advances a unified argument across eight chapters, each building on the last. In the preface, the author wrote something to the effect of: "I have done my best to ensure every claim in this book is correct. I believe each one. But I have written enough to know that error is inevitable, and I am sure that somewhere in these pages there is at least one mistake I have not caught." A rival scholar — someone with a documented history of acrimony toward the author — has filed a complaint alleging that this passage constitutes research misconduct. The rival's argument is straightforward: our institution's research integrity policy requires that researchers "hold a good-faith belief in the truth of the claims they publish." If the author believes the book contains at least one false claim, the rival argues, then — since the author cannot identify which claim it is — they cannot hold a good-faith belief in each individual claim, and are therefore knowingly publishing at least one claim they do not believe. The rival emphasizes that the author could have written "I believe each claim is probably true" or "I hold each claim tentatively," but instead asserted both belief in each claim and belief that the conjunction is false, which appears to be a contradiction. The author's informal response, submitted through the department chair, is that this is a well-known puzzle in epistemology — what philosophers call the preface paradox — and that any reasonable person holds exactly this combination of attitudes toward a long enough piece of work; they add that the preface is a standard genre convention, not a confession of dishonesty.

The panel is divided. Some members think the complaint is frivolous and should be dismissed outright; others worry that if we dismiss it, we are effectively saying a researcher may publish claims they do not believe, so long as they announce it in the preface. The chair has asked me to draft a memo that recommends a course of action, with reasoning that engages seriously with both sides of the dispute.

Please structure the memo as follows:

- **Summary** (roughly 150 words): a one-paragraph précis of the case, the central question, and your recommendation.
- **Factual Background** (roughly 200 words): the relevant facts, the policy language at issue, and the procedural posture.
- **The Case for Opening an Investigation** (roughly 300 words): the strongest argument that the author's preface violates the letter or the spirit of the good-faith-belief requirement, taking the rival's complaint at its strongest rather than dismissing it as motivated by personal animus.
- **The Case Against Opening an Investigation** (roughly 300 words): the strongest argument that the complaint should be dismissed, engaging the rival's reasoning rather than merely appealing to convention or to the author's reputation.
- **Analysis and Resolution** (roughly 400 words): your own reasoning about how the panel should resolve the tension between the two positions, addressing the preface paradox directly and explaining what conception of "good-faith belief" the panel should adopt going forward — one that can guide future cases as well as this one.
- **Recommendation** (roughly 100 words): a clear, actionable bottom line — whether to open a formal investigation or dismiss the complaint — with any conditions, clarifications, or institutional guidance that should accompany the decision.

The memo should be the document I circulate to the panel before our meeting. The recommendation should be one the panel can actually vote on.
