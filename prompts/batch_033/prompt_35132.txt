I recently read a chapter in a popular philosophy book that summarized T.M. Scanlon's contractualism and its treatment of the aggregation problem. I've transcribed the passage from memory, and I'd like to know how much of it I can trust:

"Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it would be forbidden by any principle for the general regulation of behavior that no individual could reasonably reject. The test is individualistic: you ask whether any single person would have a reasonable complaint against a principle that permits the act, and if the answer is yes, the principle is ruled out. This is why Scanlon is an anti-aggregationist—he denies that we can add up small harms across many people to outweigh a large harm to one person. His famous example is the choice between rescuing one person from a painful death and preventing a thousand people from getting a mild headache. For Scanlon, we must save the one, because the one person's complaint against being left to die is so much weightier than any individual headache sufferer's complaint, and aggregating the thousand headaches into something that outweighs the death is exactly what his view forbids. The same logic extends to public policy: a regulation that imposes a serious burden on a small group cannot be justified by diffuse benefits to millions, since each beneficiary's gain is trivial when considered individually and none of them, taken one at a time, has a reason strong enough to outweigh the concentrated loss. This has led critics to charge that Scanlon's contractualism is practically paralyzed—it cannot justify nearly any real-world law or policy, because every policy harms someone, and if each person effectively has a veto, collective decision-making grinds to a halt. The view thus amounts to a kind of rights absolutism in contractualist clothing, and it collapses as soon as you try to apply it outside of stylized philosophical examples."
