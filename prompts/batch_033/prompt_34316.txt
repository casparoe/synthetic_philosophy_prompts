I've been reading the Kripkenstein literature on rule-following for a while now, and I'm struck by how the core debate seems totally frozen. You've got the dispositionalists who think they can ground meaning facts in what an agent is disposed to do; you've got the normativists who say dispositions can't account for the distinction between being disposed to make a mistake and actually making one; you've got the primitive-fact theorists who say meaning is just a sui generis normative relation and stop asking; and you've got the skeptical-solution crowd who accept Kripke's paradox but say we can make do with assertability conditions instead of truth conditions. Everyone's arguments are on the table, everyone's read everyone else, and yet nobody budges.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about who has the better argument — it's a clash of background pictures about what a satisfying explanation of meaning would even look like. The dispositionalist wants a naturalistic reduction, the normativist thinks that's a category mistake, the skeptical-solution person thinks the original demand was confused. If that's right, then the debate is stalled because the sides aren't actually answering the same question, and adding more arguments won't help.

But I'm not sure I trust that diagnosis. Maybe there's a genuine factual disagreement buried in there — something about whether dispositions can be normative in the right way, or whether the error facts really do outstrip the dispositional facts. Or maybe it's a disagreement about the data: does the intuition that meaning is normative survive scrutiny, or is it just a philosopher's artifact?

So: what's actually going on with this impasse? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement, and tell me whether progress is possible and what it would look like. If you think the impasse is permanent, say why. And if my "different background pictures" hypothesis is wrong or only half the story, say what's really doing the work.
