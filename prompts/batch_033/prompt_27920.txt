I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on social choice and democratic theory — about 18 students, one 75-minute session. We have just introduced Arrow's theorem, and I want them to encounter the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem not as a proof on the board but as something they feel in their own strategic choices. Design a classroom activity that makes the impossibility of a strategy-proof, non-dictatorial voting rule (with three or more candidates) visceral.

The activity should work as follows. Before anyone says the word "strategy-proof" or "manipulation," students participate in several rounds of voting under different rules, each time with private, assigned preference rankings over three candidates. The rules, the preference distributions, and the sequence of rounds should be arranged so that at least some students discover — without prompting — that they can get a better outcome by voting against their true preferences, and that this incentive persists across every rule tried. By the end they should suspect that something structural is going on, not just a quirk of one voting method.

Please specify:

1. **Setup and materials.** How many candidates, what they stand for (keep it concrete but not politically charged — e.g., where to hold a department party, or what to name a new campus building), and how you distribute preference orders to students. Give me the exact preference profiles you would use and say why that distribution is chosen.

2. **Rules for each round.** Which voting mechanisms you run, in what order, and why that order. For each mechanism, give the precise ballot instructions students receive. Say whether students vote simultaneously or sequentially, whether ballots are public or secret, and whether you reveal intermediate tallies.

3. **The pivot moment.** At what point in the activity do you expect the first student to realize they can misrepresent their preferences profitably? How is the activity structured to make that realization likely without the facilitator nudging it? What do you do if no one discovers it on their own?

4. **Debrief.** After the final round, how do you walk the class from their in-game experience to the formal statement of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem? Map specific moments in the activity onto the theorem's conditions: non-dictatorship, unrestricted domain, at least three outcomes, and the failure of strategy-proofness. Identify which condition each round's manipulation exploits.

5. **What will go wrong.** Anticipate at least two ways the activity can misfire the first time it is run, and explain how the facilitator should handle each in the moment. Also be honest: which feature of the theorem does the activity unavoidably fail to capture, and how should the instructor fill that gap afterwards?

The activity should require no special equipment beyond paper ballots or index cards. Assume the room has a whiteboard or chalkboard for tallying. The tone should be playful — students should feel like they are outsmarting a system, not being tricked by the instructor.
