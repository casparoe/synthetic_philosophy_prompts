I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been trying to teach the BHK interpretation in my intermediate logic course, and I've got myself tangled up in an argument that seems to show the whole intuitionist picture is incoherent. I need someone to tell me where exactly it goes wrong and what the intuitionist has to pay to fix it.

Here's the argument:

(1) The intuitionist identifies truth with constructive provability: to say that P is true is to say that there exists a mental construction—a proof in the informal sense—that establishes P. (Call this the Truth-as-Provability thesis, or TP.)

(2) Under TP, the intuitionistic negation ¬P is explained as: a proof of ¬P is a construction that transforms any hypothetical proof of P into a proof of an absurdity (⊥). So ¬P is true just in case there is a construction that rules P out.

(3) Now, the intuitionist's central complaint against classical logic is that it accepts excluded middle (P ∨ ¬P) for all propositions, even those for which neither P nor ¬P has been proved. The intuitionist points to propositions like Goldbach's conjecture (call it G) and says: we have no proof of G, and no proof of ¬G, so we may not assert G ∨ ¬G.

(4) But what does it mean to say "we have no proof of G and no proof of ¬G"? On the face of it, it means: ¬(∃x Proof(x, G)) ∧ ¬(∃x Proof(x, ¬G)). That is, there does not exist a proof of G, and there does not exist a proof of ¬G.

(5) But TP tells us that "G is true" just is "there exists a proof of G." So "there is no proof of G" is equivalent to "G is not true," i.e., ¬True(G). And by TP again, ¬True(G) is equivalent to ¬G (since truth just is provability, so ¬True(G) is ¬Provable(G), which is exactly what ¬G means under TP).

(6) Similarly, "there is no proof of ¬G" is equivalent to "¬G is not true," i.e., ¬True(¬G). And by TP, ¬True(¬G) is equivalent to ¬¬G.

(7) So the innocent claim "G is undecided" becomes, in the object language: ¬G ∧ ¬¬G.

(8) But ¬G ∧ ¬¬G is a contradiction even in intuitionistic logic. (¬G ∧ ¬¬G entails ⊥, since ¬¬G is (G → ⊥) → ⊥, and from ¬G we get G → ⊥, and putting them together yields ⊥.)

(9) So the intuitionist cannot even state that any proposition is undecided, on pain of contradiction.

(10) But the claim that there are undecided propositions is not just a casual remark for the intuitionist—it's the whole motivation for rejecting excluded middle. If the intuitionist can't say it, the position collapses.

I can feel that something sneaky happens around step (5), where the meta-level claim "there is no proof of G" gets pushed into the object language as ¬G.
