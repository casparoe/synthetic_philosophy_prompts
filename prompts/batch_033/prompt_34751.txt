ok so i'm writing a paper on moral particularism for a grad seminar and i want to check whether my position actually hangs together before i commit to it in print. here's roughly where i've landed:

(1) i'm a particularist in dancy's sense: there are no exceptionless moral principles, and the holism of reasons is true — a feature that counts in favour of an action in one context can count against it in another, or be irrelevant.

(2) but i also think some features are pretty much always morally relevant in the same direction. like, causing suffering is almost always a reason against. this is just a contingent fact about the world, not a conceptual truth, but it's a very robust one.

(3) i think moral knowledge works through something like moral perception — we can just see, in a particular situation, what matters and what we ought to do, without subsuming the case under a principle.

(4) i also think reflective equilibrium is the right method in moral epistemology. you start with considered judgements about cases, then try to bring them into coherence with principles, background theories, etc., revising at any point.

(5) i think that in practice we need codified rules — in medical ethics, in law, in institutional life — and these rules don't just summarise the underlying reasons; they have genuine normative force of their own.

(6) i'm worried that particularism makes moral disagreement really hard to resolve. if there are no shared principles to appeal to, what do we do when two people with different moral sensibilities look at the same situation and see different things? this seems like a serious problem and i'm not sure the standard particularist replies work.

so that's the set. what i want to know: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how bad are the tensions? rank them by severity. and if there's a single revision that would clean up the most, tell me what it is and what it costs.
