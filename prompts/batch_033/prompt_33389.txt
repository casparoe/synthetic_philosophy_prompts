I've been working on reasons-first approaches in metaethics and have become persuaded by the view that there are free-floating normative reasons — reasons that are not constitutively tied to any particular agent's desires, projects, or standpoint. The thought is that certain considerations (say, that an action would cause gratuitous suffering) count in favor of or against certain responses simpliciter, not merely relative to a set of concerns someone happens to have. This is stronger than just saying reasons are objective rather than subjective; it's the claim that the reason-relation itself does not require an agent-relativizing term. I find the position attractive because it seems to capture the phenomenology of moral and epistemic deliberation: when I encounter what I take to be a reason, I experience it as making a claim on me from outside, not as a deliverance of my own motivational set.

I want to know what would count against this view. I'm not asking for the standard Humean objections I already know — that all reasons must be explained by desires, or that normative judgements alone can't motivate. I'm asking something more specific: suppose someone holds that there are free-floating reasons of the kind I've described. What observations, arguments, conceptual problems, or results in neighboring areas should make them reduce their confidence? I'd like the defeaters ranked from those that a defender of the view should find most troubling to those that are merely awkward. For each, I'd like to know whether defenders have good replies, and whether the reply involves qualifying the view in a way that risks making it unfalsifiable. I'd also like to know whether there are things the view predicts that its rivals don't — and whether anything has arguably already happened in the literature or in our understanding of agency that counts against it.
