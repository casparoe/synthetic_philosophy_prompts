Question: In De Interpretatione 9, Aristotle considers the claim that if it was already true yesterday that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the sea battle's occurrence seems to have been fixed all along. Explain (a) the reasoning that leads from the prior truth of such a future-tensed assertion to the conclusion that the event is necessary, and (b) Aristotle's proposed way out, making clear which principles about truth and which laws of logic he keeps and which he gives up.

Student answer: Aristotle's worry is that if every future-tensed assertion already has a truth value before the event occurs, then the future is settled in advance. Suppose it was already true yesterday that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. Then the assertion already corresponded to a fact about tomorrow, and since what was true cannot later turn out false, it would now be impossible for the battle not to occur. Aristotle's solution is to deny bivalence for future contingent singular statements: the assertion that there will be a sea battle tomorrow is neither true nor false until the matter is settled one way or the other. He does not give up the law of excluded middle, because the disjunction that either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not be is still necessarily true even though neither disjunct is true. What he gives up is only the principle that every declarative sentence is either true or false. One consequence this seems to have is that the law of non-contradiction also lapses for future contingents: since the affirmation and the negation are both not-true, there is nothing left to rule out holding both of them. Aristotle's move is therefore equivalent to introducing a third truth value for future contingents, of the sort later made precise in three-valued logic. This leaves the future genuinely open for the relevant class of statements, without making either the affirmation or the negation true in advance.
