Last week a friend of mine got very excited about something called Good's theorem after reading a blog post on it, and now he brining it up every time anyone says they'd rather not know something. His summary, as best I can reconstruct it, is this: "Good proved mathematically that a rational agent can never be made worse off by acquiring new information. So any rational person should always want more evidence, even about painful things, and should never prefer ignorance. Refusing to find out is therefore always either irrational or a sign that you're secretly admitting the information would change your mind in a way you can't defend." He's used this on everything from "I don't want to know the date of my death" to "I don't want to read the reviews of my own book." I gather there is a real theorem by I. J. Good and that the actual claim is something narrower, about the expected value of cost-free information being non-negative for an expected-utility maximizer. But I don't know enough decision theory to see exactly where my friend's version slides from that into his much stronger practical conclusion. Could you go through his summary claim by claim and tell me what the theorem actually licenses, where he's overstating or conflating, and whether "you should always want to know" follows from anything true in the theorem? I'm especially suspicious of the jump from "not worse off in expectation beforehand" to "never worse off, and irrational to decline," but I'd like to know whether those are the right places to push.
