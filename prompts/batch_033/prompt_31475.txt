I have been following the debate about the paradox of deontology for years, and I have noticed something that bothers me: the debate has all the structural features of a stalemate that is not going to resolve itself. On one side, consequentialists keep pointing out that deontological constraints require agents to choose a state of affairs that is worse by the agent's own lights — for example, refusing to kill one innocent person even when that refusal predictably results in five innocent people being killed by someone else. The deontologist, they say, is committed to preferring a world with more violations of the very constraint they claim to uphold. On the other side, deontologists keep replying that this objection only looks like a paradox if you already accept that morality is fundamentally about maximizing the good — that the whole force of the objection presupposes the consequentialist framework it is meant to challenge. The two sides do not just disagree about the answer; they seem to disagree about what would even count as a reason to change one's mind.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. My working hypothesis is that the debate is not primarily about first-order moral intuitions about cases — both sides largely agree about what happens in the cases — but about the metaphysics of moral reasons: whether all reasons are ultimately state-given (reasons to bring about certain states of affairs) or whether some reasons are object-given (reasons to perform or refrain from certain actions, where the reason is not reducible to the value of the resulting state of affairs). If that is right, the debate is substantive but not ethical in the way it appears: it is a debate in metaethics or the theory of reasons, and the two sides have been talking past each other by treating it as a debate within first-order normative ethics. But I am not confident in this hypothesis, and I can imagine other diagnoses: that it is a bedrock clash of intuitions for which no further argument is possible, that it is a verbal dispute about the word "reason," or that the two sides are simply doing different things — one doing normative ethics, the other doing something closer to decision theory — and mistaking their different projects for a single disagreement.

Give me your diagnosis. Be specific about what each side is actually committed to, what kind of evidence or argument could in principle move the debate, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis. If you conclude that no progress is possible, explain why and tell me what follows from that — whether we should abandon the debate, reframe the question, or accept a permanent pluralism.
