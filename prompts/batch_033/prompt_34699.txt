In the bounded rationality literature, one frequently encounters the analogy that satisficing stands to optimizing roughly as heuristic search stands to exhaustive search: just as a chess engine cannot enumerate every branch of the game tree and must instead prune, evaluate heuristically, and stop when a move's evaluation crosses a threshold, a human agent facing a real decision problem cannot enumerate all possible actions and outcomes and must instead search sequentially until an option meets an aspiration level. The analogy is then deployed to support a normative claim — that satisficing is not merely a concession to cognitive limitations but the rationally appropriate procedure for any agent with finite resources, and that optimizing, once search costs are properly accounted for, is itself irrational.

I want you to audit this analogy. Provide an explicit mapping: what in the heuristic-search domain corresponds to what in the choice-under-bounded-rationality domain? Then identify the disanalogies — the places where the computational domain supplies features that the domain of human choice lacks. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the normative conclusion from those that are merely incidental. In particular, address whether the analogy quietly imports a well-defined objective function, an exogenous aspiration level, or the availability of performance guarantees that do not carry over to human decision-making. Finally, tell me whether the normative conclusion — that satisficing is the rationally required procedure — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying more weight than it can bear.
