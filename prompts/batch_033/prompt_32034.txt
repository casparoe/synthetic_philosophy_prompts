I'd like you to read Shakespeare's The Tempest through the lens of Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" and the idea of cultural relativism it is often taken to inaugurate. Montaigne describes the Tupinambá people of Brazil, their ritual cannibalism of dead enemies, and their social order, and he uses this description to unsettle the European distinction between civilized and barbarous, arguing that calling them savages reflects our own parochialism more than any truth about them. The essay is a direct source for Gonzalo's speech in Act II, Scene 1 about his ideal commonwealth, where he imagines a society without sovereignty, commerce, or labor—a passage that lifts almost verbatim from Florio's translation of Montaigne.

A reading that simply notes the borrowing is easy. What I want is a reading that takes the philosophical tension seriously. The play gives us Caliban, the dispossessed islander whom Prospero teaches language and then enslaves, and who is described by other characters as monstrous, fish-like, brutish, and lustful. Caliban is in some ways the play's Tupinambá: the native whose humanity is at issue. But where Montaigne's essay works to make the reader see the cannibal as fully human and his society as coherent, Shakespeare seems to stack the deck. Caliban is not given a functioning society—he is alone, his mother is dead, and he is ruled by a foreign magician. He attempts to rape Miranda and conspires to murder Prospero. The play offers no sustained depiction of an alternative social order on the island that might genuinely challenge European assumptions; instead, Caliban's world is already broken before we see it, and the closest thing to a native political structure is his comic allegiance to Stephano.

I want a reading that asks whether the play dramatizes something Montaigne's essay misses, or whether it simply backs away from the radical implications of the relativist view. Does the play's treatment of Caliban show that Montaigne's relativism is naive—that real encounters across cultures are never the symmetrical, respectful affair the essay imagines, because they are always entangled with domination, fear, and the projection of monstrosity? Or does the play, by making Caliban sympathetic in certain moments (the "isle is full of noises" speech, his grief at being dispossessed), vindicate a Montaignian refusal to write off the other, even as it shows how hard that refusal is to sustain in practice? Where does the play complicate the idea that we can simply set aside our own categories and see another culture clearly? And where, if anywhere, does the play suggest that the very project of judging another culture—whether to condemn or to romanticize—is the problem Montaigne diagnosed, even when the play itself cannot fully escape it?

The reading should be faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the play to a philosophical allegory, and it should not distort Montaigne's essay into a straw man. It should end by saying what the play, as a work of drama with characters and consequences, adds to the philosophical debate about relativism that argument alone cannot deliver.
