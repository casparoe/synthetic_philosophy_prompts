I'm on my university's IACUC and we're deadlocked on a protocol that's turned into a philosophy seminar. A neuroscientist wants to run a pain-study on pigs — electrode implants in the brain, nociceptive stimulation, the whole deal. She's already running a near-identical protocol on mice (approved last year, no friction). The pig version would use fewer animals overall, and she argues the data will translate better to humans because pig neuroanatomy is closer to ours. So on the Three Rs logic we normally apply, it looks decent: fewer animals, better science.

Here's the split. Three of us think pigs are obviously a harder case than mice because pigs are more cognitively sophisticated — better memory, more behavioral flexibility, maybe something closer to suffering-as-we-know-it. Two others say this is just speciesism with a neuroscience gloss: we're drawing the line at "animals whose minds seem more like ours" and calling it an ethical distinction, same way people draw it at dogs vs. pigs or mammals vs. fish. They point out that we didn't ask any of these questions when the mouse protocol came through — we applied our standard checklist and approved it. Now suddenly for pigs we're inventing extra hurdles. They want us to evaluate the pig protocol on the exact same criteria we used for mice and stop treating pigs like they're in a different moral category just because we find them more relatable.

The chair wants each of us to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's vote. I need to recommend: approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and if conditions, they need to be concrete enough the researcher could actually respond to them. The opinion should honestly engage the strongest argument on the side I'm not taking, and it should give the committee something we can act on, not a tour of the animal-ethics literature.

Also: tell me how confident you are in the recommendation, and single out the point you're least sure of. If there's a place where your reasoning goes soft I want to know where.
