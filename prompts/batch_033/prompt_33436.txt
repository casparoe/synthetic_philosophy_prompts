I've been building a personal productivity tool that lets users set up commitment devices — scheduled website blockers that can't be turned off during work hours, financial penalties that auto-donate to a cause you dislike if you miss a deadline, that sort of thing. The philosophical justification I've settled on, after reading around a bit, is what I understand to be the Ulysses contract idea: that binding your future self is not a failure of rationality but an expression of it. You know, at time t1, that your preferences at t2 will be distorted by akrasia, temptation, or some other predictable bias, and you take steps at t1 to constrain what t2-you can do. This seems to me not just practically useful but philosophically defensible as a form of autonomy — you're authoring your life according to your considered values rather than surrendering to your momentary impulses.

I've extended this intuition to the political domain too: constitutional precommitments — supermajority requirements, bills of rights, independent central banks — seem to be the same move at the collective level. A polity knows it will panic during a crisis, or that a temporary majority will trample minority rights, so it ties its own hands in advance. I find this broadly persuasive, and I'm in the camp of people who defend precommitment as rational and autonomy-preserving rather than as a paradoxical or self-defeating maneuver.

But I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I know that when I don't stress-test a system I've built, I'm probably missing something. So I want to know: what would actually count against this position? Not just the obvious counterarguments I've already heard ("you can't bind a future sovereign majority," "future selves are different people"), but the full map of what would have to be true for the precommitment-as-autonomy view to be wrong, or at least seriously limited. I'm looking for the empirical findings, the edge cases, the conceptual distinctions, the formal results, the shifts in how we think about agency or identity that would undermine it. I want to know which of these a committed defender of the view would concede as genuine costs or vulnerabilities, and which they'd dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding. And I want an honest answer about whether the view has been insulated against falsification to the point where it's not really saying anything risky — and if so, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.
