I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on contemporary moral philosophy—about 18 students, one 75-minute session. We have just finished reading Bernard Williams's critique of utilitarianism in *Utilitarianism: For and Against*, and I want them to feel the force of his "one thought too many" objection rather than merely reciting it as a bullet point. The core idea, as I understand it, is that an agent who saves a loved one and then reaches for an impartial moral justification—something like "it is permissible to prioritise one's spouse because the institution of marriage produces greater overall utility"—has already gone wrong. The very act of justifying one's partiality in impartial terms introduces a kind of alienation from one's own commitments: the agent has substituted a thought about the permissibility of the action for the direct, unmediated response that the relationship itself calls for. I want to design a structured activity in which the participants run into this alienation themselves, before we name it, so that the idea shows up in what they do and feel rather than in what I tell them afterwards.

Here is what I am imagining. The activity has two phases. In Phase 1, each student receives a slip of paper with a short, vivid scenario in which they must choose whom to pull from a burning building: a person they care about deeply (described in concrete, personal terms—a sibling, a best friend, a mentor) or two strangers they have never met. They have thirty seconds to mark their choice in private. In Phase 2, we reconfigure the room: half the students become "rescuers" and the other half become an "impartial review board." Each rescuer must stand before the board and explain their decision. The board's job is to press for a justification that any reasonable person could accept—they are instructed to ask questions like "What makes your person more worth saving than two others?" and "Would you accept that reasoning if someone else used it to save their person instead of you?" The rescuers must stay in the hot seat for at least two minutes. I suspect something will shift in the room during Phase 2: the rescuers will start to feel that something is wrong with the very demand for justification, and the board members may feel it too.

I need your help fleshing this out: the exact wording of the scenario slips, the instructions for the board, the physical setup (how to arrange the room to make the board feel formal and mildly adversarial), and most importantly, a detailed debrief plan. The debrief should map what happened in the activity onto Williams's argument—the moment of private choice (the unmediated, partial response), the demand for justification (the impartial requirement), and the discomfort that arises in the gap between them. It should also surface what the activity cannot capture: the fact that in the real case the rescuer is not justifying himself to a board but to himself, and that the "one thought too many" is an internal thought, not an external demand. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—students who gamify the exercise, students who defend their choice with ease and feel no unease, or students who miss the point and think the lesson is simply that impartial morality is overbearing—and how to handle each of those.
