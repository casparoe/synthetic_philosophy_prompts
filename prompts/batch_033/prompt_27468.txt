A couple friends of mine in my department's philosophy reading group got into a long argument about Davidson's radical interpretation and I'm still not sure who had the better of it. I'll reconstruct it as best I remember.

Maya started by saying she'd been thinking about radical interpretation and found it kind of vertiginous: if all we have to go on is the speaker's behavior and the principle of charity, then meaning is ultimately just whatever a charitable interpreter would attribute, and since different interpreters bring different beliefs to the table, there's no fact of the matter about what a speaker really means. She said this felt like a pretty radical conclusion Davidson's own view forces on you, even if he wouldn't have put it that way.

Evan pushed back hard. He said that's exactly what the principle of charity is designed to block. The whole point is that interpretation isn't unconstrained — you have to maximize agreement between the speaker and yourself, and that requirement is strong enough to rule out the kind of indeterminacy Maya was describing. If you and I both interpret the same speaker charitably, we should converge on roughly the same interpretation, because we're both optimizing for agreement with the same behavioral evidence.

Maya replied that "maximize agreement" is doing all the work, and it's not at all clear that it forces convergence. She said: suppose I believe the world is full of middle-sized dry goods and you're a process metaphysician who thinks reality is fundamentally events. When we each charitably interpret a speaker saying "there's a table in the corner," I'll attribute a belief about an enduring object and you'll attribute a belief about a stable pattern of events. We're both maximizing agreement — we're each making the speaker agree with us as much as possible — but we end up attributing different meanings. So charity doesn't force convergence; it just makes each interpretation parasitic on the interpreter's own ontology.

Evan came back with: that's not a problem for Davidson, it's a problem for the process metaphysician. Davidson's view is that charity requires us to take the speaker to be mostly right about the world, and if process metaphysics is false, then an interpretation that attributes process-metaphysical beliefs is attributing mostly false beliefs, which violates charity. So there's a fact of the matter: the correct interpretation is the one that attributes mostly true beliefs, and that depends on what's actually true, not on what the interpreter happens to believe.

Maya said this just pushes the bump under the rug. How do we know what's actually true, independent of our own beliefs? The interpreter has no God's-eye view — all they have is their own beliefs about what's true. So in practice, and maybe in principle, the interpretation still bottoms out in the interpreter's beliefs. Evan said that conflates epistemology with metaphysics: the fact of the matter is one thing, our access to it is another. Maya said that in Davidson's own framework, where truth is immanent and there's no standpoint outside our own beliefs, the distinction collapses.

They went back and forth like this for a while and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. I'd like someone to go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what. Don't just pick a winner — I want to know at each step whether the point being made holds up, and where exactly the real disagreement is located. Take a definite position and defend it; I'm not looking for a survey of possible readings.
