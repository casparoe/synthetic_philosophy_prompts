I've been thinking about the film Arrival (the Denis Villeneuve one, based on Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life") and how it functions as a kind of intuition pump about free will and foreknowledge. The central idea is that learning the heptapod language lets you perceive time non-linearly — you see your whole life at once, past and future. And the gut punch is that Louise knows her daughter will die young, but she chooses to have her anyway. The film is basically pumping the intuition that foreknowledge doesnt undermine choice or meaning — that you can know the future and still act freely within it.

I want to read this as an intuition pump in Dennett's sense — a thought experiment dressed up in narrative to make a philosophical move feel inevitable. Where does the film's persuasive power come from, narratively? The non-linear editing, the way it withholds information, the emotional weight of the daughter relationship — these aren't arguments, but they do a ton of work. And here's what I'm really wondering: does the film mislead? Specifically, the heptapod language gives Louise knowledge of her *actual* future, not just possible futures. But that's a way stronger premise than most free-will debates assume — it's not just that she can predict, it's that the future is fixed and she sees it. Is the film pumping intuitions about a scenario that's so far from ordinary agency that the intuitions don't transfer? And does the film itself know it's doing this, or does it present the emotional resolution as if it's settled the philosophical question? I'm writing an essay on this and want to be fair to both the film and the philosophy — I don't want to just trash the film for being philosophically sloppy, but I also don't want to let it off the hook for moves that wouldnt survive scrutiny.
