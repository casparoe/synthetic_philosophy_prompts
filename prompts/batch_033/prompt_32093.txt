At my church there's a practice I've started to find philosophically strange, and I can't tell whether I'm overthinking it. Every week people write down the names of friends or family members who aren't believers, and then the congregation prays for them — but in a very particular way. The prayers aren't just "save Sarah"; they ask God to put certain people in Sarah's life, to bring about a crisis that will make her receptive, to arrange her circumstances so that she'll finally be open to him. At the same time, the same people will insist, if you push them, that God never overrides anyone's free will and that whether Sarah comes to faith is ultimately her own choice. So here's my puzzle. If God already knows what Sarah would freely choose in every possible set of circumstances, then he doesn't need us to suggest that he arrange the helpful ones — and if he wants her saved, why didn't he create a world where those circumstances already held? If instead God doesn't know what Sarah would freely do in various circumstances (because she's free and it hasn't happened yet), then what exactly is the prayer asking God to do — run an experiment? What view of God's knowledge and human freedom does this practice presuppose? I've heard the phrase "middle knowledge" thrown around, but I don't know if the practice actually requires it, or wether it makes just as much sense on simple foreknowledge, open theism, or even a Calvinist view. I'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory behind this style of prayer, what it strictly commits people to versus what it merely fits well with, and what would have to change about how they pray if middle knowledge turned out to be false.
