I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I've been tangled up in the following argument for weeks. It concerns the Sleeping Beauty problem, but the issue it raises is broader — it seems to show that self-locating evidence behaves in ways that violate fundamental Bayesian principles, and if the argument goes through, it would destabilize far more than just this one puzzle.

Here is the standard Sleeping Beauty setup. On Sunday, Beauty is told the protocol. A fair coin is flipped. If Heads, she is awakened on Monday, interviewed, and the experiment ends. If Tails, she is awakened on Monday, interviewed, given an amnesia drug that erases her memory of the Monday awakening, awakened again on Tuesday, interviewed, and the experiment ends. When awakened, she knows the protocol but does not know what day it is.

Now consider the following argument for the halfer position — that upon awakening, Beauty's credence in Heads should remain 1/2:

(1) On Sunday, Beauty's credence in Heads is 1/2, matching the known objective chance.
(2) Upon awakening, the only new proposition Beauty learns is that she is awake now — that is, that the present moment is an awakening under the protocol.
(3) But on Sunday, Beauty was already certain that she would be awake at some point under the protocol. She assigned probability 1 to the proposition that there exists at least one awakening.
(4) If you are certain of a proposition at an earlier time, and at a later time you learn only a proposition entailed by what you were already certain of, your credences should not change. To do otherwise would be to revise your beliefs without genuine new evidence.
(5) Therefore, Beauty's credence in Heads upon awakening should remain 1/2.

Now here is where the argument seems to prove too much. Consider a variant: everything is the same, except that if the coin lands Tails, Beauty is awakened not twice but one million times — on a million consecutive days, each time with amnesia. If Heads, she is awakened exactly once. Apply the same argument:

(6) On Sunday, Beauty's credence in Heads is 1/2.
(7) Upon awakening, she learns only that she is awake now.
(8) On Sunday, she was already certain she would be awake at some point.
(9) So her credence in Heads should remain 1/2.

But now consider Beauty's epistemic situation upon awakening. She knows that if the coin landed Heads, this awakening is the unique one. If Tails, this awakening is one of a million subjectively indistinguishable awakenings. The thirder would say her credence in Heads should be roughly 1/1,000,001. The argument in (1)–(5) is entirely insensitive to the number of Tails awakenings; it yields a credence of 1/2 whether that number is two, a million, or, in the limit, arbitrarily large. If we accept the argument, we must also accept that even if Tails involved a billion awakenings, Beauty should still be evenly divided between Heads and Tails upon waking. That consequence seems to me obviously wrong.

So something has gone wrong. But what? The premises (1)–(4) each seem individually plausible. I find myself most reluctant to give up (4), since it looks like a straightforward consequence of the Bayesian injunction against belief revision without evidence, and I also want to hold onto (1), since abandoning the link between credence and known chance would be a heavy price. But I cannot see how to keep both and avoid the absurd conclusion in the million-awakening variant.

Which premise must be rejected, and why does it look innocent? In particular:

- If the problem lies with (4), what exactly is the new evidence Beauty acquires upon awakening — evidence she was not already certain of on Sunday? Spell out its content precisely, and explain why it is not captured by the proposition that she would be awake at some point.
- If the problem lies with (2) or (3), how should we redescribe what Beauty learns upon awakening, and why does this not collapse into the claim that she already knew she would be awake?
- If the argument is in fact sound and we should accept the verdict in the million-awakening case, explain why the intuitive resistance to it is misguided and what revision this demands of our ordinary reasoning about self-locating evidence.

Finally, whatever diagnosis you give, I want to know what it costs for Bayesian epistemology more generally. If the correct response requires distinguishing centered from uncentered propositions and restricting (4) accordingly, what else does that distinction force us to revise? If it requires saying that Beauty's Sunday credence was not really 1/2 in the relevant sense, what becomes of the relationship between chance and credence? The puzzle, as I see it, is that a natural, minimal set of epistemic principles generates an intolerable result when extended to cases of self-location — and the fix, whatever it is, will have to be paid for somewhere else.
