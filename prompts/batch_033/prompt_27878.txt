I'm a retired physicist — 40 years in condensed matter, now spending my time on philosophy of science and formal epistemology. I've been reading about judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma lately (List & Pettit, Kornhauser & Sager, that crowd), and it's got me rethinking something from my own experience.

I sit on the grants comittee of a small private foundation — just three of us. We review proposals on three criteria: feasibility, significance, and novelty. Each of us rates each criterion pass/fail, and then we each cast an overall vote: fund or don't fund. The foundation's stated rule is that a proposal should be funded only if it passes all three criteria. We've done four rounds so far. Here's the record:

Round 1 (Proposal A): All three of us passed all three criteria. Overall vote: 3-0, fund.

Round 2 (Proposal B): Two of us passed all three criteria; the third failed all three. Overall vote: 2-1, fund.

Round 3 (Proposal C): One of us passed all three; the other two failed all three. Overall vote: 1-2, don't fund.

Round 4 (Proposal D): All three of us failed all three criteria. Overall vote: 0-3, don't fund.

So far so good — no surprises. But now we've got Proposal E, and the individual judgments are messier:

- Reviewer 1 (me): feasibility pass, significance pass, novelty fail → I vote don't fund
- Reviewer 2: feasibility pass, significance fail, novelty pass → votes don't fund
- Reviewer 3: feasibility fail, significance pass, novelty pass → votes don't fund

So here's the puzzle. Each criterion gets two passes and one fail — that's a majority pass on all three. If you go premise-by-premise, the proposal passes every criterion and ought to be funded. But every single one of us voted against funding overall. If you go conclusion-by-conclusion, it's 0-3 against.

Given our track record on A through D, what should we decide on E if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Do the past cases already commit us to one procedure or the other, or do they genuinely underdetermine it? And if they underdetermine it, what principle would we be adopting with each choice — what does each way of resolving E say about what the committee is actually doing when it decides?

I'd like your answer to lead with the bottom line — what we should decide and why — in the very first sentence, and then walk through the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
