In our course on collective decision-making we've been working through the unilateralist's curse: when any one member of a group can unilaterally take a high-stakes, hard-to-reverse action on behalf of everyone, the action goes through if even the single most favorable member judges it worthwhile, even if the rest of the group would oppose it. Before I read further, I want the specification of what an adequate account of decision-making under this curse has to deliver — that is, the desiderata for a theory of when a group should permit unilateral initiative and when it should require consensus or some higher threshold. For each requirement, give the case or failure mode that motivates it: for instance, the asymmetry between acting and refraining, why each person's relying on private judgment can fail even when everyone is individually rational, the difference between reversible and irreversible initiatives, and the cases where unilateral action is clearly right (a single bystander calling an ambulance). Say which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are contested or parochial, and tell me whether any subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable — that tension is the part I most want to see. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on cataloguing the proposed fixes; a brief scorecard of the main responses is fine at the end.
