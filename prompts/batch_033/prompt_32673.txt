I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of logic, with fourteen students and one seventy-five-minute session. Design a structured role-play or tabletop game, built around Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which a Tortoise accepts the premises of a simple inference but refuses to accept the conclusion until the inference itself is written down as an additional premise, and then demands a further premise to license the use of that one, and so on without end. The activity should make participants feel, through their own repeated attempts to close the gap, the difference between a premise and a rule of inference, and the apparent impossibility of converting a rule into a premise without generating an infinite regress. They should not be told the point in advance and should not hear the name of the paradox until the debrief. Give me the full runnable design: roles, exact rules, materials, a timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between premises and rules of inference, the structure of the regress, and the main possible responses, such as accepting that some rule is simply followed rather than stated as a premise, or arguing that the regress is not vicious. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be explicit about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
