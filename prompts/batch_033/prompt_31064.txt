So I was at this philosophy department happy hour last week and two of the grad students got into it about Buridan's ass — you know, the donkey that starves because it's exactly halfway between two identical bales of hay and has no reason to prefer one over the other. I ended up sitting there for like forty minutes listening to them go back and forth and I still don't know who's right. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Elena said the whole point of the example is that rationality underdetermines action. If you're a purely rational agent with symmetric reasons, you're stuck. So you need something outside rationality — a randomizer, a brute act of will, a habit — to get moving. She said this is a problem for anyone who thinks reasons alone can guide action.

(2) Marcus said no, the paradox is fake. Rationality doesn't require that you have a reason to pick the left bale over the right bale. It only requires that you have reason to pick a bale over starving. Since both bales satisfy that, picking either is fully rational. The donkey isn't paralyzed by rationality; it's paralyzed by a bad theory of rationality.

(3) Elena came back: okay, but if picking left is rational and picking right is rational, and there's nothing that favors one over the other, then the actual selection is arbitrary. And if rationality is compatible with arbitrariness at the moment of choice, then what work is rationality doing? You could say the same thing about any decision — just declare the options "tied" and flip a coin.

(4) Marcus said that's exactly right for genuine ties, and the work rationality does is narrowing the option set. It eliminates dominated options and options you have reason against. Once you're down to a set of permissible options, how you pick among them isn't rationality's business. He said this is basically the difference between the "rational" and the "reasonable" in some traditions.

(5) Elena said that move just relocates the problem. If you can always redescribe a choice situation so that the permissible set has multiple members, then rationality never tells you what to do — it just tells you what not to do. She said a theory of practical reason that can't ever recommend a specific action is incomplete.

(6) Marcus said that's only a problem if you expect rationality to output a unique action for every situation, which is an unreasonable demand. He said even in easy cases — like choosing between two identical cans of soup at the grocery store — we don't think there's a rational mistake to be made either way.

At that point someone spilled a drink and the conversation moved on, but I've been turning it over since. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I'm especially interested in whether Elena's point (5) — about rationality never telling you what to do — is a genuine problem or just a misunderstanding of what rationality is supposed to deliver.
