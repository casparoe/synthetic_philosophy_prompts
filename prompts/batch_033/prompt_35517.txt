Two people in my political philosophy reading group got into a long argument last week and I walked away more confused than when they started. The topic was whether people with very different value systems can both come out ahead by trading moral concessions the way people trade goods. One of them — call her Rosa — used a coalition example. Suppose a green party wants a carbon price and a labor party wants industrial subsidies, and each thinks the other's flagship policy is, at worst, a minor wrong by it's own lights. Rosa said: if the greens care way more about the carbon price than they dislike the subsidy, and labor cares way more about the subsidy than it dislikes the carbon price, then a deal where each votes for the other's policy leaves both parties better off by their own standards. She called it gains from trade in moral currency.

The other one — call him Marcus — said that's a category mistake. His point was that if you believe the subsidy is a waste or an injustice, voting for it isn't like spending money you'd rather spend elsewhere; it's doing something you believe is wrong, and you can't cancel that by getting a carbon price in return. Principles constrain what you may do, he said; they aren't items in a preference basket.

Rosa pushed back: you're not cancelling a wrong, you're comparing two outcomes. By your own lights the package is better than the no-deal status quo, since the carbon price matters more to you than the subsidy does. Refusing the deal leaves you with the outcome you judge worse, so how is refusing "faithful to your values"?

Marcus answered that this only works if your values are just a ranking of outcomes. His values, he said, also tell him not to be the one who enacts a policy he thinks is wrong; he's responsible for the subsidy if he votes for it, but not responsible for the carbon price failing if he doesn't. So the whole gains-from-trade story quietly assumes consequentialism and begs the question against agent-relative constraints.

Rosa came back: fine, then the trade just can't include something a party treats as a side-constraint. That still leaves plenty of compromises where each side concedes only what it finds suboptimal but permissible — and there the gains-from-trade point stands, and each side would be irrational by its own standards to refuse. Marcus replied that the suboptimal-but-permissible case is just ordinary negotiation, and nobody needed a theory of moral trade for that. The interesting claim only kicks in when a concession is actually wrong by someone's lights, and exactly there the analogy to trade breaks down.

I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches or misses something, and who's more right overall?
