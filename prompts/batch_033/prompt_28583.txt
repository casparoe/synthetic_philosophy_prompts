I keep running into a move in philosophy of perception that I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know whether it has been identified and discussed as a distinct argumentative step. The move goes like this. Someone points out that a hallucination — say, Macbeth's dagger — can be subjectively just like seeing a real dagger. From this they conclude that the mental state in the veridical case must be of the same fundamental kind as the mental state in the hallucinatory case, with the only difference being that in the veridical case the state is appropriately caused by an external object. The inference from "subjectively indistinguishable" to "same underlying kind of mental state" is treated as though it hardly needs defending. But indistinguishability is an epistemic notion — it tells you what a subject can tell apart — and it doesn't obviously settle the metaphysical question of whether the states are the same kind of thing. (Two things can be indistinguishable along some dimension without being the same kind of thing.) I'm aware that disjunctivists reject this move, but I'm after the name and anatomy of the move itself, not the rejection. Is there an established term for it? And if the move comes in importantly different versions — say, one that treats it as a direct inference and another that treats it as an inference to the best explanation — I'd want to know how those differ and whether they have separate labels.
