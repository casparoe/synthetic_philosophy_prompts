I recently had to decide whether to undergo a prophylactic mastectomy. I carry a gene variant that gives me a roughly forty per cent lifetime risk of breast cancer, and the surgery would bring that down to something under two per cent. But the procedure carries its own risks, including a small but non-negligible chance of serious complications — the surgeon quoted a one-in-two-hundred chance of a complication requiring further surgery, and a roughly one-in-a-thousand risk of death from anaesthesia or post-operative infection. I have been leaning against having the surgery, and two friends of mine — neither of them medical professionals, but both quantitatively literate — ended up arguing about my decision at dinner last week. I was present but said very little, and I have been turning their exchange over in my head since. I would like you to adjudicate it, move by move.

Here is roughly what was said:

(1) Michael told me I was making a mistake. He laid it out in expected-value terms: suppose the cancer, if it occurs, has a thirty per cent mortality rate. Then my lifetime risk of dying of breast cancer without the surgery is 0.40 × 0.30 = twelve per cent. With the surgery, it drops to 0.02 × 0.30 = 0.6 per cent. The surgery itself carries a 0.1 per cent mortality risk. So the net reduction in mortality risk is 12 − 0.6 − 0.1 = 11.3 percentage points. The one-in-two-hundred complication risk is a cost, but it is not a mortality cost, and in any case its expected disutility is dwarfed by the mortality arithmetic. Refusing the surgery, he said, is equivalent to placing an absurdly high premium on avoiding a very small immediate risk at the expense of a much larger future risk. It is irrational.

(2) Eleanor pushed back. She said Michael's calculation treats all probability-weighted mortality as fungible, and that is precisely what is at issue. A one-in-a-thousand chance of dying on the operating table next Tuesday is not the same kind of thing as a twelve per cent chance of dying of cancer sometime in the next thirty years — not because the probabilities differ in magnitude, but because the structure of the risk is different. The surgical risk is concentrated, involuntary in the moment, and mediated by a decision I actively make. The cancer risk is diffuse, distributed across decades, and arises from inaction. She said that the expected-value framework simply assumes that these differences do not matter, and that this assumption is doing all the work in Michael's argument.

(3) Michael replied that Eleanor was dressing up familiar cognitive biases — loss aversion, scope insensitivity, the omission bias — in philosophical language. He said that if the probabilities are well-calibrated and the outcomes are measured in the same units, the arithmetic is sound, and refusing to accept it is no different from refusing to believe that a fair coin is fair after it lands heads five times.

(4) Eleanor answered that the coin analogy concedes her point. A coin's probability is a feature of the coin; my cancer risk is a model's estimate, built from population data, and the model itself has uncertainty. More importantly, she said, even if the probabilities were known with certainty, Michael's approach assumes that a 0.1 per cent chance of death is always worth exactly one-tenth of a one per cent chance of death, no matter how it is embedded in a life. She said that is not a truth of rationality; it is a substantive claim about how risks aggregate, and many people reasonably reject it.

(5) At this point Michael said that if Eleanor is right, then there is no principled way to make any medical decision under uncertainty — you can always point to some structural feature of the risk and refuse the arithmetic. Eleanor replied that this is not an argument against her position but a description of the actual difficulty of medical decision-making, which the expected-value framework disguises rather than solves.

Please adjudicate this exchange. I want you to go through each of the five moves and assess who is right about what, distinguishing between points that are clearly correct, points that are clearly mistaken, and points that are genuinely open. I would also like you to tell me whether the structure of their disagreement maps onto any deeper debate in decision theory — I have the sense that it does, but I cannot name it — and whether resolving that deeper debate would actually settle the question of what I should do, or whether the gap between theory and this particular decision is larger than either of them acknowledged.
