Compose a sestina on the intransitivity of "better than," using a spectrum argument as its subject. The argument to render is this: a chain of possible lives runs from a brief spell of excruciating pain at one end to a vastly longer spell of barely noticeable discomfort at the other; between the endpoints, each adjacent pair of lives differs only slightly in the intensity of the pain while the duration increases substantially, and in each adjacent comparison the later life is judged worse than its predecessor. Transitivity would then force the final life to be worse than the first, but a direct comparison of the two endpoints reverses that verdict. Let the six end-words of the sestina name the six stages of this chain, ordered from the most intense pain-term to the mildest, so that the first stanza holds the endpoints apart at its first and last lines, the fixed rotation of end-words across the middle stanzas enacts the step-by-step trading of intensity for duration, and the envoi brings the first and last end-words together in a single line, forcing the direct comparison in which the direction of the chain reverses. The poem should be precise enough that a value theorist would recognize a genuine spectrum argument rather than a loose moral about small differences adding up; it should leave open whether the failure lies in transitivity itself or in one of the adjacent judgments, and it should not resolve the tension by declaring some step the bad one.
