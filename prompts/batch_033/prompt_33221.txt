Here's the setup — the centipede game. Two players take turns. On your turn, there's a pot of money. You can Take (you get the larger share, the other player gets the smaller share, game ends) or Pass (the pot grows and it's the other player's turn). The game has a known finite number of rounds — say 100. Each time someone Passes, the total pot gets bigger, so if both players Pass for a while, they both end up with way more than if someone Takes early.

The backward induction argument:

(1) At the last round (round 100), the player whose turn it is should Take. No future rounds to care about, and Taking gets you more than Passing. Straightforward.

(2) At round 99, the player knows that if they Pass, the round-100 player will Take (by (1)). So Passing at 99 just lands you the smaller payoff from round 100. You should Take at 99.

(3) By the exact same logic, at round 98, the player knows the round-99 player will Take, so they should Take at 98.

(4) This unravels all the way back: the rational move is to Take at round 1. Game ends immediately with both players getting a tiny payout.

(5) But this is nuts. If both players just Passed for a bunch of rounds, they'd walk away with much more. A theory of rational choice that tells you to grab the small pile on move one, when you could both cooperate and get rich, seems broken.

I'm not saying (5) is a formal premise — it's more like the reductio that makes me doubt the whole thing. But (1)–(4) looks airtight. So either one of those steps is wrong, or there's a hidden assumption somewhere, or rationality doesn't work the way the argument assumes.

I've heard people say the problem is "common knowledge of rationality" — that the argument assumes both players know both are rational, and know that they know, and so on, and maybe that's the weak link. But I'm not sure why that's a bad assumption for a simple game where everyone's incentives are totally transparent.
