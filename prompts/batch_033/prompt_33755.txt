I've been reading around in just war theory and I keep coming back to the moral equality of combatants debate, and honestly it feels like it's been stuck for decades. The orthodox view (Walzer and the tradition behind him) says soldiers on both sides of a war have the same moral permissions: an unjust combatant who kills a just combatant in a fair fight hasn't done anything wrong qua combatant, because soldiers arent the ones who decide whether the war is just, and once the fighting starts the two of them are in a morally symmetrical position. The revisionists (McMahan and the people who've followed him) say this is basically incoherent: killing someone requires them to be liable, and a person can't be liable to be killed by someone who is fighting to keep them enslaved or invaded, so the unjust combatant's kills are nearly always wrong, even if the revisionists still want the law of war to treat both sides the same for pragmatic reasons.

What's weird to me is that after thirty-plus years both sides seem to have made all the moves and nobody has moved. The revisionists have the first-principles arguments — liability, necessity, and proportionality applied to the individual act — and the orthodox side has the institutional and epistemic arguments: ordinary soldiers can't know whether their war is just, the law of war needs symmetry to do its job, and the rules soldiers actually live under shouldn't ask them to settle questions their leaders and publics can't settle. Each side even concedes the other's strongest point in a way. Revisionists say yes, the law should stay symmetric, but that's a separate question from the morality of the individual act. Orthodox people say yes, maybe in some idealized deep morality the unjust combatant is in the wrong, but that's not the morality war is actually governed by. And yet the argument keeps going.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps arent really answering the same question: the revisionists are asking what makes an individual act of killing permissible full stop, while the orthodox side is asking what morality should demand of combatants given the institutional reality of war and the limits of what soldiers can know. But I'm not sure that's right, because the revisionists explicitly present themselves as correcting the morality of war, not doing some separate exercise, and the orthodox side denies that their view is only about law or convention.

So: is this a substantive moral disagreement, a verbal dispute about what the morality of war means, or a disagreement about method (first principles versus considered judgments about institutions)? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new distinction, an empirical claim about what ordinary soldiers can know, a formal result, or is the question just ill-posed and in need of being split up? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the debate, not a survey of the positions.
