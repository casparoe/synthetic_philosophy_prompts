I've been reading a lot of popular science lately, and I keep tripping over the same pattern. Someone observes a human behavior — say, the fact that stepchildren are statistically more likely to be abused than biological children — and then they say: "Well, in our ancestral environment, investing resources in offspring that don't carry your genes would have been selected against, so we evolved a tendency to favor our own." And it sounds so reasonable. But here's what bothers me: if the statistics had come out the other way — if stepchildren were treated better — someone would have said "well, in the ancestral environment, forming alliances through caring for a new mate's existing offspring would have been adaptive." The story always fits whatever the data happens to be, after the fact. And nobody seems to notice that the same method could have "explained" the opposite result. Is there a name for this specific thing — not just for evolutionary storytelling in general, but for the move where you retrofit an adaptive explanation to whatever finding you already have, in a way that the explanation can't really be checked? I know Gould and Lewontin wrote about something related, but I'm trying to pin down whether this exact epistemic pattern has its own label, and if not, what distinguishes it from a normal scientific hypothesis about the past.
