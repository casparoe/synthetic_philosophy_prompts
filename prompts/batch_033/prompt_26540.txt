I'm trying to get clear on what we should actually demand of a principle connecting rational credence to objective chance. Lewis's Principal Principle says, roughly, that your credence in a proposition, given that its objective chance is x and you have no inadmissible information, should equal x. But the literature has spent decades adding caveats — undermining futures, the admissibility clause, the question of whether the principle defines chance or constrains credence, and whether it survives under Humean supervenience. Before I read further, I want the desiderata. What must any adequate account of the credence–chance relationship get right? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial to a particular metaphysics of chance (e.g., Humean versus non-Humean). I especially want to know whether any pair of requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — that is, whether the literature's messiness reflects a genuine tension in what we want from such a principle. A brief scorecard for Lewis's original formulation, the New Principle, and one non-Humean alternative would help, but the criteria are the main thing.
