I have come to hold that the distinction between the right kind and the wrong kind of reasons for an attitude is not a principled one. On my view, so-called wrong-kind reasons — for example, a credible threat that gives me a reason to admire someone, even though nothing about that person counts in their favor — are simply genuine reasons for having the attitude, and the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction marks at most a difference in whether a reason bears on the object of the attitude or on the attitude itself, not a difference in its normative force. Tell me what would count against this. I want the arguments, cases, and theoretical costs that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, and I want you to separate the considerations that a defender of my view would themselves concede as counting against it from those they would dismiss. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the view can be qualified so as to absorb the standard objections, and whether that qualification amounts to insulating the view from criticism in a way that should itself worry me.
