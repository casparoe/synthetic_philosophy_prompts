I got into a stupid argument last weekend with my roommate about whether you can actually know a piece of art is good without experiencing it yourself. She's an art history major and insists that reading about paintings or hearing other people's descriptions doesn't give you aesthetic knowledge — you have to stand in front of the thing. I pushed back with some cases and now I'm not sure my own reactions are even consistent. So I'm trying to figure out if there's a principle that captures what I actually think.

I went through a bunch of cases and here's where I landed. (1) My friend Elena has basically the exact same taste in movies as me — like, uncannily so. She tells me a new film is brilliant. I haven't seen it. I feel like I now know it's good, at least good enough to recommend it to someone else, but I also feel wierd saying "it's a brilliant film" in a conversation as if I'd seen it. (2) Similarly: I read a long, detailed description of the Pantheon's interior — the proportions, the oculus, the way the light moves — and felt like I had a pretty good grasp on it. When I finally went to Rome and walked in, it was exactly as described and yet I was still floored. I don't think I knew its beauty beforehand in the way I now do, even though I had true beliefs about all the features that produce that beauty. (3) At a wine bar, the sommelier tells me a red's got notes of "leather and dried cherry." Before she said it, I just tasted "red wine." After, I could taste those things clearly. I want to say I gained knowledge about the wine, not just that my experience changed — the notes were there to be tasted. (4) But then: someone at a gallery says loudly that a painting I'm about to look at is "derivative and shallow." Now I can't see it fresh. My experience has been poisoned. If I end up agreeing, did I arrive at a genuine aesthetic judgment, or did their testimony just hijack my perception? I want to say the latter — which seems in tension with the wine case. (5) Before I heard Shostakovich's Eighth Quartet, a friend told me it was devastating. I listened to it knowing that, and it was devastating. I don't think I knew it was devastating before listening, though — I knew my friend thought so and I formed an expectation, but the knowledge of its devastatingness came only through hearing it. That feels different from the Elena movie case, and I feel like it should be, but I can't articulate why.

Is there a principle — or a small set of principles — that gets all five of these verdicts? And if nothing clean captures all of them, which verdict would you say I should give up and why? Also: what would the principle (or your preferred one) say about a case I haven't settled yet — reading the score of a piece of music you've never heard performed, and forming a judgment that it's beautiful based purely on reading the notation?
