The thesis I'm defending is roughly this: the very features that make a thought experiment a good intuition pump — vividness, emotional pull, stripped-down simplicity, the feeling that you can just see the answer — are also features that cognitive science shows push people's judgments around independently of the merits of the case. So the fact that a case feels immediately and vividly compelling should be treated as a reason to distrust the intuition it delivers, not as evidence for the conclusion.

The audience is a mainstream analytic philosophy department: mostly moral philosophers and philosophers of mind who assign the trolley problem, Mary the color scientist, the Chinese room, and Twin Earth every single year, plus two cognitive psychologists who work on moral judgment and one experimental philosopher who already thinks armchair intuitions are worthless.
