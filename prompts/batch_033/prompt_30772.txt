Couple of mates got properly stuck into it at the pub last night about whether thought experiments in philosophy are just dressed-up emotional manipulation. I stayed quiet and eventually they turned to me and asked me to settle it, and I had nothing. Hoping you can go through their exchange step by step and say who's right about what.

It started when Dan brought up Searle's Chinese Room — you know, the one where a guy who doesn't speak Chinese is in a room with a big rulebook, and people slide Chinese characters under the door, and he follows the rules to produce the right output characters, and from the outside it looks like the room understands Chinese, but obviously the guy doesn't. Dan said this is basically cheating: the whole thing works because you imagine a lone bloke shuffling papers, and of course that doesn't feel like understanding, but that feeling isn't an argument, it's just an intuition pump dressed up as philosophy.

Priya pushed back. She said that's not fair — Searle is making a specific claim about computation. The idea is: if the computational theory of mind says running the right program on any hardware necessarily produces understanding, then Searle has described a system running exactly that program, and we can see it doesn't produce understanding. That's not just a vibe, it's a structured argument, and calling it an intuition pump is a way of refusing to engage with it.

Dan came back: the 'structured argument' is a Trojan horse for the intuition. The reason anyone buys it is that the homunculus — the guy in the room — is a person we identify with. If Searle had described the same system at the level of transistors or synapses, nobody would have the 'aha, no understanding here' reaction. The intuition pump works precisely because we project ourselves into the little man and feel his cluelessness.

Priya said that's a dangerous line to draw, because by that standard every thought experiment is an intuition pump. Galileo dropping connected weights from the tower, Einstein chasing a light beam — they all work by getting you to 'see' the point. If you're going to dismiss the Chinese Room as an intuition pump, you need a principle that sorts the good ones from the bad ones, and Dan hasn't offered one.

Dan said he has got one: a thought experiment is legitimate when the intuition it generates survives being cashed out in actual empirical terms. Galileo's passes — if you really dropped two connected masses, you'd get the result his reasoning predicts; the thought experiment is a stand-in for a real experiment. The Chinese Room doesn't pass, because 'does the system understand Chinese?' isn't settled by any possible observation — it's settled entirely by whether you personally feel like calling it understanding. The whole thing runs on the moth-eaten intuition that understanding requires a homunculus who feels like he's understanding.

They looked at me and I just ordered another round. Can you adjudicate this — go through each move and say who's carrying the point, and then give me a view on where the disagreement actually bottoms out? Please write the answer in British English.
