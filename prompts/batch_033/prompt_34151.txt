I'm taking a graduate seminar on cross-cultural epistemology, and we've spent the last three weeks on Nyāya. One of the things that's struck me is how case-driven the tradition is — the pramāṇas aren't just abstract categories, they're tested against a huge range of examples, many of them quite subtle. Our professor has been giving us verdicts on a series of knowledge-attribution cases, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the principle she's running. I have her rulings on eight cases, and next week she's going to give us a ninth. I want to predict what she'll say and, more importantly, articulate the candidate rules that fit her verdicts so far.

Here are her verdicts:

1. A person sees a clay pot on the ground in full daylight, at arm's reach, with unimpaired vision. The pot is there. Verdict: knowledge, via perception (pratyakṣa).

2. A person sees smoke rising from a distant hill and infers that there is fire on that hill. There is indeed a fire. Verdict: knowledge, via inference (anumāna).

3. A person has been told by a reliable forester that a gavaya is like a cow. Later, in the forest, the person sees an animal and recognizes it as a gavaya on that basis. Verdict: knowledge, via comparison (upamāna).

4. A person hears from a trustworthy companion that there are mangoes on the far side of the river. There are indeed mangoes there. Verdict: knowledge, via testimony (śabda).

5. A person sees a rope coiled on the path in fading twilight and forms the belief that it is a snake. It is not a snake. Verdict: not knowledge.

6. A person hears a traveling merchant claim that a certain root cures fever. The merchant is known to exaggerate and has no expertise in medicine. The root does, by coincidence, cure fever. Verdict: not knowledge.

7. A person correctly infers that a distant figure is a human being from the fact that it appears to have hands. The inference is formally sound, the figure is indeed a human being, but what the person took to be hands were actually two thin branches swaying in the wind that, by chance, looked exactly like hands from that angle. Verdict: not knowledge.

8. A person sees a clear crystal resting on a red cloth and takes the crystal itself to be red. The crystal is colorless; the redness is contributed entirely by the cloth. Verdict: not knowledge.

The new case she's going to give us: A person sees what is unmistakably a mango hanging from a tree — good light, close range, unimpaired vision. The object is indeed a mango. However, unbeknownst to the person, they are looking through a freshly cleaned windowpane. The glass is perfectly transparent and introduces no distortion whatsoever. But the glass has a peculiar property: if it were even slightly smudged, it would make a plastic mango look exactly like a real one. The glass happens to be clean, and the mango happens to be real. Does this person have knowledge on the Nyāya view?

I'm less interested in the bare yes or no than in (a) what the candidate principles are that fit verdicts 1 through 8, (b) which of those principles predict what for case 9 and why, and (c) which further case would most cleanly discriminate between the candidate principles. If you think the eight verdicts already determine the answer to case 9 uniquely, make the case for why the apparent ambiguity is illusory rather than genuine.
