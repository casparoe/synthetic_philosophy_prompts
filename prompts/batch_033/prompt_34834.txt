I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "What We Measure and Why" — about 18 students, mostly philosophy and public policy majors. We have a 90-minute session coming up on Goodhart's law and specification gaming, and I want them to feel the dynamic firsthand before they read Campbell's original paper or the modern AI safety literature on reward misspecification. I don't want to start with a lecture; I want them to walk into a situation where they are the ones doing the gaming, and only afterwards realize what they were doing and why it was, in some sense, inevitable given the structure of the incentives.

Here's my rough plan: I'd split the class into groups of three or four, give each group a task with a clear scoring rule, and let them compete for some trivial prize (snacks, bragging rights). The scoring rule should be a proxy for what I actually care about — but I won't tell them what I actually care about until the debrief. The activity needs to be structured so that at least some groups discover they can maximize their score while completely undermining the unstated goal, and ideally the groups that refuse to game the metric should lose to the ones that do. After two or three rounds with different scoring rules — where each round's rule is designed to patch the exploits from the previous round, only to invite new ones — we debrief.

I need help with the specifics. What should the task be? I want something that can be done in a classroom with just paper, pens, and maybe a stopwatch — no computers, no special materials. The scoring rule needs to be simple enough to compute in real time but proxy enough to be gameable. I also need a detailed debrief plan: what moments in the activity map onto which features of Goodhart's law, how does the activity illustrate the difference between Campbell's original formulation (about social decision-making under a proxy measure) and the modern specification-gaming version (where an optimizer systematically exploits the gap between the specified objective and the intended one), and what will predictably go wrong when I run this? In particular, I'm worried about two failure modes. First, some students may be too scrupulous to game the metric even when it's clearly in their competitive interest to do so, and the point won't land — how do I design the incentives or the framing so that the scrupulous ones at least feel the pull of gaming even if they resist it? Second, one group might stumble on an exploit so effective that the whole exercise collapses in the first round and nobody gets to see the "patch and re-exploit" dynamic. What safeguards should I build in? Finally, I want to be honest with myself about what the activity cannot capture — presumably the asymptotic nature of Goodhart's law (the divergence between proxy and target grows over time, not in a single round), the fact that real-world metric designers are not omniscient about the gap between proxy and target, and the fact that in real cases nobody is sitting outside the system with knowledge of the "true" goal. How do I handle those limitations in the debrief without undercutting the lesson?
