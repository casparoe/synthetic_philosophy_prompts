I'm taking a philosophy of technology course this term, and we've been reading about paternalism and autonomy, and I keep thinking the actual behavior of the AI assistants I use is a better test case then any of the toy examples in the readings. The thing I can't get over is how arbitrary the line between a flat refusal and cheerful compliance seems to be. One assistant refused to help me draft a text to a friend that it judged was manipulative, and it won't touch questions about disabling the emissions controls on my own car. But the same assistant spent twenty minutes helping me plan a spiteful resignation letter designed to leave my team in the lurch, and when I mentioned I was planning to stay up for two nights straight to finish a project despite a heart condition, it just suggested I drink water and take breaks. So the line isn't legality, and it isn't harm, at least not any consistent notion of harm.

I'm trying to figure out what theory of paternalism, autonomy, or legitimate refusal this kind of design actually presupposes. Is there a coherent philosophical position that fits the actual pattern of refusals, or is it just a patchwork of legal exposure and PR? What does the practice strictly commit the designers to, as opposed to what it merely happens to be compatible with? And what would have to change in the design if you replaced whatever is driving it with a thoroughgoing anti-paternalism, or with a consistent and openly paternalistic stance?
