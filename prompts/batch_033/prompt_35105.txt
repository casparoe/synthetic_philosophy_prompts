I'm a science journalist — I write about ideas for a living, mostly for magazines and the odd radio piece — and I've agreed to do a talk at a small ideas festival in Bristol next month. The slot is forty minutes plus Q&A, Saturday morning, people with coffee and croissants, bright but not specialists. Some will have read a bit of philosophy or cognitive science, but I can't bank on it, and honestly the ones who've read a little are sometimes the hardest because they come with half-remembered undergrad framings that get in the way.

The title I pitched is "Where Does Meaning Live?" and I want to build the whole thing around Hofstadter's three-way distinction in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* — frame message, outer message, and inner message. Roughly: the inner message is the content you get after decoding, the outer message is the implicit information about *how* to decode it (what language it's in, what conventions apply), and the frame message is the meta-signal that says "there is a message here at all, this thing is meant to be interpreted." I've always thought this is one of those genuinely deep ideas that, once it clicks, changes how you look at a sentence, a painting, a DNA sequence, a gesture — but getting there is tricky because the layers nest and people's intuitions about "where the meaning is" pull hard in one direction or another.

What I need from you isn't the talk itself — it's a teaching plan. I want to know: what absolutely has to come first in the explanation and why (what's the dependency structure here?), what one central example should carry the whole thing and what properties it needs to have to work, which misconceptions I should expect the audience to form and at exactly what point in the sequence I should head each one off, and one question I can pose near the end that will tell me whether the distinction actually landed rather than just producing nodding. Also, tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — what bit of the idea I should flag as "this goes deeper but we're stopping here." I have a bad habit of overpacking talks, so I'd rather do less and have it stick than cover everything and lose them. And please write the whole thing in British English.
