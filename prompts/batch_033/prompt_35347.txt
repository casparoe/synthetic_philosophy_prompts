I've been reading about dispositions — properties like fragility, solubility, or lethality — and I keep running into two puzzling patterns that I suspect someone must have named and analyzed, but I don't know the terms, and I'd like to know whether they are treated as distinct problems or as variants of the same one.

The first pattern goes like this. An object seems to have a disposition, but if you were to trigger it, the very process of triggering would destroy the disposition before the manifestation could occur. Imagine a wine glass wired to a sensor: the moment anything strikes it with sufficient force, the sensor triggers a mechanism that instantly heats the glass so it becomes soft and yields rather than shattering. Is the glass fragile? If we say yes, we seem committed to saying that a glass can be fragile even though it would not break if struck — which sounds wrong. If we say no, we seem to be saying that fragility depends on what would happen if you struck it, which sounds right — but then the disposition depends on facts about the very event that is supposed to test it, and the glass's intrinsic structure (which is that of ordinary glass) ends up being irrelevant. And if we try to fix this by saying the glass is fragile *until* struck, we seem to be introducing time-dependence into a property that doesn't intuitively seem time-dependent in that way.

The second pattern is different. Here the disposition is genuinely present, the stimulus occurs, and the disposition is not destroyed — but something else intervenes to block the manifestation. A dose of cyanide is lethal — it has the disposition to cause death — but if the victim has taken an antidote beforehand, the cyanide is ingested and yet the victim survives. The cyanide still seems lethal; we would not say the antidote made the cyanide non-lethal. But then lethality does not guarantee death even when the stimulus (ingestion) occurs, which seems to break the simple conditional analysis according to which "x is lethal" just means "if x is ingested, death results." And unlike the first pattern, the disposition itself is not being altered by the stimulus — the cyanide's chemical properties are unchanged; the blocking happens downstream.

I am trying to figure out: do these two patterns have established names in the literature on dispositions? Are they treated as the same kind of counterexample to the conditional analysis, or are they importantly different in ways that require different responses? And if they do have names, what exactly marks the boundary between them — could there be cases that are ambiguous between the two, and how have philosophers classified those? I am less interested in being told the bare labels than in understanding what each phenomenon is, what it is not, and where the edges are.
