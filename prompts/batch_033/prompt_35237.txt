I recently spent several months on a hospital ethics committee during a period in which a scarce treatment had to be allocated among more patients than could receive it, and I keep turning over a pattern of reasoning I encountered there, for which I have never found a satisfactory name.

The pattern concerns forced choices between saving some number of people and saving a larger number. Almost everyone on the committee agreed, in the abstract, that if one had to choose between saving one patient and saving five equally needy patients, one should save the five. But a substantial minority of my colleagues denied this when the cases were made concrete. They did not claim that the single patient mattered more, or that we owed him special loyalty, or that the comparison was impossible. Their claim was that each of the six patients has a separate claim to be saved, and that these claims do not combine into a larger claim on one side of the ledger. The presence of five other patients, they argued, does not weaken or cancel the one patient's claim. From this they concluded that the bare fact that more people stand on one side does not settle what we should do. Some of them thought the only defensible procedure was a lottery giving each patient an equal chance; others thought any choice was permissible as long as we did not pretend that five equal claims added up to five times the weight of one.

I later recognized the same pattern in a dispute about clearing a road after a storm, where some residents argued that the fact that one road contained more stranded people did not decide which road to clear first, because each stranded person's claim stood separately.

I do not think this is the ordinary utilitarian view, and I do not think it is simply the familiar tendency to be moved more by an identified victim than by a statistical one, since the people who argued this way applied it to complete strangers and offered it as a principle. I also do not think it is the claim that lives are incommensurable, since they were willing to compare and choose. But I have no name for it, and I am not sure whether it is one position or a family of positions. What I would like to know is whether this stance — the refusal to let the number of separate claims settle a forced choice about who is saved — is a recognized position, and if so what it is called. I am equally interested in how it should be distinguished from nearby views, and where its boundaries lie: the same people who refused to count heads in the rescue case were often willing to approve public-health measures expected to save a larger number of statistical lives, and I cannot tell whether that combination is consistent or whether it marks the edge of the concept.
