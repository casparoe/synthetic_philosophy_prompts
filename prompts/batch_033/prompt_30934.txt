I am a doctoral student working on the metaphysics of normativity, and I have been unable to locate the error in an argument that a colleague pressed on me, though I am convinced its conclusion is too strong. The argument generalizes the Euthyphro dilemma, and I want to know which step to reject and what rejecting it would cost.

(1) Consider any view according to which a normative fact — that an action is right, or that there is reason to perform it — is explained by its relation to some source: a divine command, an ideal observer's approval, the outcome of a procedure of practical reasoning, the terms of an agreement, a legislative act, or a promise. (2) For any such view, the Euthyphro question arises: does the source select φ because φ is antecedently right, or is φ right because the source selects it? (3) If the source selects φ because φ is antecedently right, then the source merely tracks a rightness that is already there, and the view does not explain what rightness consists in. (4) If φ is right because the source selects it, then the selection is not answerable to any independent standard, so any selection whatsoever would have been equally right; had the source selected cruelty, or the breaking of every promise, those things would have been right. (5) An account of normativity on which the selection of cruelty or promise-breaking would be equally right is unacceptable. (6) Therefore no source-based account of normativity can be correct. (7) But this conclusion rules out, at a stroke, contractualism, Kantian constructivism, ideal-observer theories, legal obligation, and promissory obligation. (8) Some of these are serious candidates, and promising and legislation are practices we ordinarily take to create obligations; a principle that refutes all of them proves too much, so at least one step of the argument must fail.

I would most like to keep the claim that some constructivist or contractualist account of normativity is correct, and the claim that promising can create obligations; I am prepared to give up whichever other step is least costly. But I need to know precisely which premise or inference is at fault, whether the fault is an equivocation or a false dichotomy or a substantive false premise, and whether the generalized dilemma fails for the same reason the original dilemma fails or for a different one. A bare answer that only names the step to reject without saying what picture of source-based normativity remains available would not be enough; I want to know what rejecting that step commits me to, and which of the views listed in (7) survive the repair.
