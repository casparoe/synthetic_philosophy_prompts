I have become convinced that the backward induction argument is correct as a matter of normative game theory: in the centipede game, and in finite extensive-form games with a similar structure, rational play requires defecting at the first move, and the game unravels. The fact that this is not how people behave — that even game theorists often continue for several rounds in experimental settings — does not refute the argument; it shows that real players lack common knowledge of rationality, or that they are playing something closer to a repeated game, or that their preferences are not faithfully captured by the stated payoffs.

What I want is a map of the view's theoretical exposure. Put aside the behavioral evidence and the intuitive repugnance of the conclusion. Which premises of the backward induction argument are the load-bearing ones, and what formal results or conceptual arguments would have to be produced to undermine each of them? Which of these escape routes are live theoretical possibilities rather than mere logical ones — for instance, has anyone shown that a modest weakening of common knowledge of rationality changes the result in a principled way, or that backward induction rests on a conception of counterfactual reasoning that can be challenged without abandoning the rest of the edifice? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want an honest assessment of whether the view, as I have stated it, has been insulated against the most serious objections in a way that is or is not itself a problem. Take a definite position — do not lay out the options and leave me to decide. I want your best judgment about what would actually move a reasonable defender of backward induction, and what would not.
