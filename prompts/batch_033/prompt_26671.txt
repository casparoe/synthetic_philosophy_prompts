So this happened at a party last weekend and I can't stop thinking about it. Two friends of mine — let's call them Dan and Mira — got into it about preferences, of all things. We were talking about weekend plans and Dan said, completely casually, that he'd rather have a quiet night in than go to a concert, would rather go to the concert than do a big group dinner, but would rather do the group dinner than have a quiet night in. Like A over B, B over C, but C over A. He said it like it was the most normal thing in the world.

Mira, who's doing a PhD in something economics-adjacent, immediately pounced. She said if those are really his preferences, someone could exploit him: start him with a quiet night, offer to swap to the concert for a dollar, he'd say yes; then offer to swap the concert for the dinner for another dollar, he'd say yes; then offer to swap the dinner for a quiet night for a third dollar, he'd say yes — and now he's back where he started, three dollars poorer. She said this proves his preference pattern is irrational.

Dan said that's a parlor trick, not an argument. The trades don't happen all at once in some abstract marketplace — each choice is made on a different day, in a different mood, with different information. His preferences genuinely shift with context, and that's not a bug, it's how humans work.

Mira said that's exactly the problem: if your preferences shift that easily, you're not a stable agent, you're a puppet of whatever context you happen to be in, and you'll get taken advantage of by anyone who notices the pattern — even if no one literally offers you the three trades in sequence.

Dan shot back that Mira was confusing a model with reality. The model says preferences should be orderly and consistent, but real people have messy, context-dependent preferences, and it's the model that's wrong for demanding consistency, not the person for lacking it.

Mira said the whole point of calling a preference pattern irrational is that it leads you to act against your own interests — ending up back where you started minus money is a perfect demonstration of that, even if the trades are hypothetical.

Dan said that only works if you assume his preferences shouldn't change between trades. But they do change, and that's just being a person with a psychology rather than a utility function.

They asked me to break the tie and I had absolutley no idea what to say. Can you go through their arguments move by move and say who's right about what? I'm especially curious whether Mira's trade-cycle scenario actually proves anything if Dan is right that his preferences genuinely shift with context — or whether that just turns it into a different kind of problem entirely.
