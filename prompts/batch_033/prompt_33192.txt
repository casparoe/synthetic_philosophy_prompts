In a philosophy of physics seminar last term, a classmate presented a version of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, and I have been unable to shake it. The argument, as she laid it out, runs roughly as follows:

(1) According to special relativity, there is no absolute, frame-independent simultaneity relation: whether two spatially separated events occur "at the same time" depends on the observer's inertial frame. (2) For any event e in what I would ordinarily call my future — say, a supernova five hundred light-years away that will be visible from Earth next year — there exists some inertial frame in which e is simultaneous with an event occurring right now in my own vicinity (for instance, my typing this sentence). (3) If two events are simultaneous in a given frame, then they are equally real — they co-exist, they are both present — for an observer in that frame. (4) But being real, or being present, is not a frame-relative affair: if something is real, it is real simpliciter; the notion of "real for observer A but not for observer B" is unintelligible or at least deeply suspect. (5) From (2), (3), and (4), it follows that the distant supernova is equally real right now, and more generally that all events — past, present, and future — are equally real. (6) Therefore we live in a block universe: time does not pass, the future is as fixed and determinate as the past, and our experience of an unfolding present is an illusion.

I find the conclusion troubling, but I cannot locate the step that deserves the blame. The relativity of simultaneity is well confirmed; (1) and (2) seem to be straightforward consequences of the physics. Step (3) has an intuitive pull — if you and I agree that two events happen at the same time, it is natural to say they both occur, they both exist, neither is "more past" or "more future" than the other. Step (4) captures the conviction that reality is not the sort of thing that can be perspectival the way simultaneity is: what exists should not depend on how fast you are moving. And the inference from (2)–(4) to (5) and (6) looks formally tight. Yet the conclusion seems to prove too much — it erases the distinction between past, present, and future that underwrites our practices of deliberation, regret, and anticipation, and it treats the passage of time as a kind of systematic error.

I want to know which premise or step you would reject, and what rejecting it costs. If you think the argument is valid, then tell me which premise you consider the least costly to surrender, and explain what the rest of your metaphysics of time looks like once you give it up. If you think there is a hidden equivocation or a scope error, identify it precisely: where does the argument slip from one sense of "real" or "present" to another? Please lay out the options — including the standard responses (e.g., the appeal to a metaphysically privileged foliation, the rejection of the inference from frame-simultaneity to ontological parity, or the bullet-biting acceptance of the block universe) — and price each of them: what else must you believe, or stop believing, to make that move?
