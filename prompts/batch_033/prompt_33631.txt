My younger sister is getting married next month, and she has asked me to give the toast at the reception. I am a philosopher working on L. A. Paul's account of transformative experience — the idea that some choices (marriage, parenthood, career changes, religious conversion) are epistemically distinctive: they change you in ways that make it impossible to know, from your current perspective, what it will be like to have made them, and so standard decision-theoretic reasoning cannot guide you through them. My sister knows my work and has asked me not to avoid the tension this creates. She wants me to acknowledge that she and her fiancé are stepping into something they cannot fully anticipate, but she wants the toast to treat that fact not as a reason for hesitation but as part of what makes the decision worth making. The room will be roughly half philosophers — my advisor, several colleagues, and graduate students from my department are attending — and half family and friends who have never heard of transformative experience. Write a five-minute toast that navigates this. It must be warm, genuinely celebratory, and land for both halves of the room. The philosophers should recognize the conceptual moves without needing them named; the family should feel they are at a wedding, not a seminar. Do not explain the technical apparatus; let the idea do its work structurally, in what the toast can and cannot say.
