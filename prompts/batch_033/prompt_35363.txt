I am presenting a paper next month at a microeconomic theory seminar in a top-twenty economics department. The paper defends the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution as normatively superior to the Nash solution for a specific but important class of bargaining problems: those in which the disagreement point is best understood as a formal baseline rather than a credible threat, and in which each party's maximal feasible payoff — their aspiration level — carries independent normative weight. My core claim is that replacing independence of irrelevant alternatives with individual monotonicity yields a solution that tracks a fairness intuition Nash obscures: roughly, that when the feasible set expands in a way that improves one party's best attainable outcome without improving the other's, the other party should not end up worse off. I argue that Nash's IIA, whatever its merits for individual decision theory, conflates genuinely irrelevant alternatives with alternatives that shift what counts as a reasonable claim, and that the absence of a noncooperative foundation for KS is not fatal because the question I am asking is normative, not predictive.

The audience is not going to be friendly. The seminar is dominated by game theorists, most of whom have published on Rubinstein-style alternating-offers models or related noncooperative bargaining work; for them, a bargaining solution without a strategic foundation is at best incomplete and at worst empty. There are also two or three behavioral economists who run bargaining experiments and are skeptical of axiomatic solutions altogether, and one philosopher of economics who works on the normative status of axiomatic characterizations and is sympathetic to the idea that "characterization" is not the same as "justification." The Nash solution is the default in this room; KS is treated as a historical footnote that was interesting in 1975 but never caught on, and most people in the room think they know why.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from this audience — not the objections I would get from, say, social choice theorists or fair-division people, who are more sympathetic to monotonicity — ordered by how likely and how damaging each one is. Phrase each objection the way a Rubinstein-trained game theorist or an experimentally-minded behavioral economist would actually put it. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it would be so damaging.
