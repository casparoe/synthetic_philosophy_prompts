At a workshop on macroeconomic policy, two graduate students, Mara and Jonah, argued about a paper they had both read. The paper used a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium model with a representative household, rational expectations, and no borrowing constraints to argue that tightening a particular consumer-credit regulation would reduce welfare. The argument ran roughly as follows.

(1) Mara said the model's assumptions are straightforwardly false: households are not a single representative agent, expectations are only imperfectly rational, and borrowing constraints are the very object the regulation touches. She concluded that the model cannot support the paper's policy conclusion.

(2) Jonah replied that this is the familiar complaint about unrealistic assumptions, and it misses the point. What matters in evaluating a model is not whether its assumptions are descriptively accurate but whether the model predicts well — the position sometimes called Friedman's F-twist. The model's aggregate predictions track the data, so attacking its assumptions is attacking the wrong target.

(3) Mara answered that the paper is not using the model merely to predict. It is using the model to explain and to recommend an intervention. Once a model is used to change policy, the mechanism matters: if the mechanism in the model does not operate in the world, the intervention can fail even when the model fits current data well. Changing the policy moves the economy into a regime in which the false assumptions may no longer be harmless.

(4) Jonah said this was still a point about prediction, not about realism for its own sake. The real question is whether the model remains accurate under the new policy, and that is an empirical matter of out-of-sample performance and structural stability. If a friction mattered for the prediction at issue, he added, a model that omitted it would fit worse.

(5) Mara replied that this testability is only in principle. The counterfactual policy experiment cannot be run before the policy is adopted; the model is what we use precisely because the data from the new regime do not yet exist. An appeal to future predictive success therefore cannot settle the dispute now.

(6) Jonah conceded that some assumptions can be load-bearing, but he said that just means they make a difference to predictions. Unless Mara could point to an observable difference that including borrowing constraints would make, her objection was, in his words, an aesthetic preference for realistic pictures rather than a scientific criticism.

I was in the room and could not referee them. Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it: for each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating; say who is right about which specific point; and say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
