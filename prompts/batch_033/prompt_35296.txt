I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've developed a side interest in consciousness and the mind. Recently I fell down a rabbit hole reading about split-brain patients — people who've had their corpus callosum severed to treat intractable epilepsy. The experiments are remarkable. In a typical setup, a word like 'bell' is flashed to the patient's left visual field (which projects to the right hemisphere), and another word like 'key' is flashed to the right visual field (left hemisphere). Asked what they saw, the patient says 'key' — that's the speaking left hemisphere reporting. But when asked to retrieve the object with their left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere), they pick up the bell. Then, crucially, when you ask why they picked up the bell, the left hemisphere — which didn't see 'bell' and doesn't know about the right hemisphere's action — confabulates: 'Oh, I thought I heard a ringing sound earlier,' or something similarly ad hoc. It's as though the verbal system is a press secretary inventing post-hoc justifications for decisions it had no part in.

I was describing all this to a friend over drinks, and he cut me off with 'Well, the self is an illusion anyway — split-brain cases just make it obvious.' He said it as though this settled the matter, and as though 'the self is an illusion' named a single, clear thesis that the experiments straightforwardly confirm. But when I pressed him on what exactly he meant, he got vague. The more I've thought about it since, the more I realise that phrase could mean a dozen different things. It might mean there is no unified subject of experience — that consciousness is fundamentally fragmented. It might mean the sense of being an 'I' that persists through time is a narrative construction, not a metaphysical fact. It might mean free will is an illusion because the conscious self is not the originator of actions. It might mean what Buddhists mean by anatta — that there is no permanent, unchanging self underlying experience. It might mean something much more modest: that our introspective access to our own cognitive processes is unreliable and systematically confabulatory. Or it might mean something much more radical: that there is literally no one home — that consciousness itself, not just the self, is an illusion.

I want to get clear on this before I bring it up with him again. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that 'the self is an illusion' could be making, ordered roughly from least to most radical? For each reading, I'd like to know: (a) whether the split-brain data actually supports it, (b) whether it's independently plausible on other grounds, and (c) whether it's the kind of thing someone who shrugs and says 'the self is an illusion' in a casual conversation probably has in mind. And please write the answer in British English.
