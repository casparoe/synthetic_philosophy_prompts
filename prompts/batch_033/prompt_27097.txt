At a philosophy department colloquium last month, I found myself in the common room afterwards listening to two fellow PhD students — one working on the metaphysics of causation, the other on philosophy of law — who got into a protracted argument about causation by omission. Neither would yield, and I have been turning it over ever since. I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

The dispute began when the metaphysics student, Callum, said he found the whole notion of causation by omission philosophically suspect. His opening move was roughly this: if we say the gardener's failure to water the plants caused them to die, we must also say that the Queen's failure to water them caused their death, and the Prime Minister's, and so on for every person who did not water them. A notion of causation that proliferates this wildly, he argued, cannot be tracking anything real.

The law student, Helena, pushed back. She said proliferation is a genuine worry, but it is a problem for causal selection, not for the metaphysics of causation itself. We routinely select among the vast set of causal factors when we give explanations — we cite the match, not the oxygen, when explaining a fire — but that does not mean oxygen is not causally relevant. Similarly, she argued, the fact that we do not cite the Queen's omission does not entail that it was not a cause; it just means our explanatory practices are selective.

Callum replied that this analogy fails. Oxygen is a genuine difference-maker in the fire: remove it and the fire goes out. But the Queen's watering the plants was never on the table — there is no possible world in which she waters my garden. So her omission is not even a candidate cause. Genuine causation, he said, requires at minimum that the putative cause be a difference-maker in some relevant sense, and the Queen's omission makes no difference.

Helena conceded the difference-making point but argued he was drawing the wrong conclusion. She said: look at legal cases where omissions ground liability. A lifeguard who watches a swimmer drown is held to have caused the death by omission, precisely because the lifeguard had a duty of care. The duty creates the relevant counterfactual structure: had the lifeguard acted as they ought, the death would not have occurred. So the difference-making test is met; it is just that the relevant baseline is normative, not merely statistical.

At this point Callum said she had made his case for him. If whether an omission counts as a cause depends on what someone ought to have done, then causation is being made to depend on norms. But causation, he insisted, is a worldly relation — it is part of the furniture of the universe, not a projection of our evaluative practices. If the metaphysics of causation by omission requires normative baselines, then omission "causation" is not really causation at all; it is a normative judgement dressed in causal language.

Helena's reply was that this was a false dichotomy. She pointed out that even in physical causation, our concept of cause involves a contrast with a default or normal state. A spark causes a fire in normal atmospheric conditions; in a vacuum it does not. The "normal" here is not normative in the moral sense, but it is still a baseline that is not purely physical. She suggested that the baseline for omissions is just a special case of a more general feature of causal reasoning.

Callum was unmoved. He said the atmospheric example involves physical conditions that are actual, not merely stipulated. The gardener's actual failure is a physical absence, and physical absences cannot be causes — causation requires a relatum, something that exists, not something that does not.

They went back and forth a while longer, but I think these were the main moves. I would like someone to go through the exchange step by step and say who has the better of each argument, and whether there is a way to resolve the impasse that neither of them saw.
