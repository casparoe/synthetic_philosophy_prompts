I spent my career in experimental physics, and I have recently been reading more philosophy of physics in retirement. I have been working through the classic arguments against substantivalism about spacetime, and I am struck by what seems like a structural parallel between Leibniz's static shift argument against absolute space and the hole argument against spacetime substantivalism. Both arguments, as I understand them, deploy a symmetry transformation to generate observationally indistinguishable but putatively distinct worlds, and then argue that this proliferation of empirically equivalent worlds is metaphysically problematic. But I am having trouble articulating the precise differences between the two arguments. In particular, I have noticed that the static shift can be defused relatively straightforwardly by arguing that shifted worlds are merely different descriptions of the same world—a move that leaves substantivalism more or less intact. By contrast, the analogous move in the hole argument—identifying diffeomorphic models as representations of the same physical situation—is widely taken to require abandoning straightforward substantivalism for something more revisionary, such as sophisticated substantivalism or structural realism. I would like to understand why the hole argument is so much harder to defuse. What is it about the local nature of the diffeomorphism, or about the structure of the models in general relativity, that makes the hole argument bite in a way that Leibniz's global shift does not?
