So I've been chewing on this argument about epistemic justification and I think it proves that the whole regress debate is incoherent, which cant be right. Here's the argument:

(1) Take any belief p that you're justified in holding. You're justified in holding p only if you have a reason q that supports p.
(2) That reason q is itself either a belief or not a belief.
(3) If q is not a belief — say it's a perceptual experience or an intuition — then q is the kind of thing that can cause beliefs but not justify them. Causes aren't reasons; the fact that a loud noise caused me to believe there's a burglar doesn't justify my belief unless I have a further belief connecting loud noises to burglars.
(4) So reasons must be beliefs. Therefore q is a belief.
(5) But q is your reason for p, so q is doing justificatory work. That means q must itself be justified — an unjustified belief can't transmit justification.
(6) So q needs a reason r, which is a belief and needs justification, and so on.
(7) This regress can't be infinite because you're a finite creature with finitely many beliefs.
(8) It can't be circular because a circle of beliefs — p justifies q justifies r justifies p — is just mutual reinforcement among beliefs none of which have independent support.
(9) It can't stop at an unjustified belief because that's just giving up on justification.
(10) So the regress can't be infinite, can't be circular, and can't stop. That's impossible.
(11) Therefore no belief is justified.
(12) But I'm justified in believing I have hands. So something in (1)-(11) is wrong.

I think the problem is somewhere in (3) or (4) — something about whether experiences can justify without being beliefs. But every time I try to pin down what it means for an experience to justify a belief, I end up either making the experience into a belief (which puts me back in the regress) or making it a mere cause (which doesn't justify). So I'm stuck. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
