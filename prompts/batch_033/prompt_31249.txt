I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been trying to get clear about a pattern in my own reactions to other people's aesthetic judgments. When a friend whose taste I know well tells me a film is excellent, I'll decide to see it and I'm comfortable telling someone else it's excellent before I've seen it myself; I'd say I know it's worth my time, though I'd hesitate to say I know it's excellent. When the same friend tells me a novel is beautifully written, I won't say I know it's beautiful—only that I've heard it's beautiful—and I'd feel odd asserting it in a discussion. When a colleague tells me a codebase is elegant, I treat the claim as a hypothesis and go read the code before I count myself as knowing it, yet I notice I'd be comfortable saying 'the code is elegant' in a design meeting before I've looked, which is exactly the kind of assertion I refuse to make about the novel. When a sommelier tells me a wine has notes of blackcurrant and leather, I'll happily buy it and describe it that way to guests, but I don't think I've come to know anything about how it tastes; I'm merely deferring for practical purposes. When a friend tells me a joke is hilarious, I won't claim to know it's funny, but I'll retell it as if I do and feel they've misled me if it flops.

My tentative rule is that testimony can transfer practical verdicts—worth seeing, worth buying—but never the aesthetic quality itself. But the codebase case and the joke case don't sit well with that rule, and I can't tell which side is the exception. Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts and the distinctions between them? What would it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a close friend tells me a piece of music is sublime, and I both want to accept it and feel I can't until I've heard it myself? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to abandon?
