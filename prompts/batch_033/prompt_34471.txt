The question was:

"Explain the difference between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimonial justification, and give one major objection to each view."

Here's the student's answer:

"Reductionism about testimony is the view, associated most famously with Hume, that testimonial justification is not a basic source of knowledge. Instead, when we justifiably believe something on the basis of testimony, that justification ultimately reduces to other epistemic sources — specifically, perception, memory, and inductive inference. On Hume's picture, whenever we accept someone's testimony, we are consciously performing an inductive inference: we recall past instances where their reports (or reports of that general kind) turned out to be true, and on that basis we infer that this particular report is likely true as well. So testimonial justification is just inductive justification applied to speech acts.

Anti-reductionism, by contrast, holds that testimony is an epistemically basic source of justification, on a par with perception and memory. The key figure here is Thomas Reid, who argued that we have a natural disposition to believe what others tell us — he called this the principle of credulity — and that this disposition is generally reliable because speakers have a corresponding principle of veracity. The anti-reductionist's core claim is that we are entitled to believe testimony regardless of any counterevidence we might have; the justification comes from the act of testifying itself, not from background checking.

The main objection to reductionism is that it can't account for how children acquire knowledge. A five-year-old believes vast amounts of what her parents tell her, but she hasn't gathered anything like the inductive evidence base that Hume's account would require — she hasn't independently verified enough reports to run a reliable induction. So reductionism seems to entail that children lack testimonial justification, which is absurd.

The main objection to anti-reductionism is the gullibility problem. If we're entitled to believe testimony by default, then we'd be justified in believing the claims of liars, cranks, and the misinformed, which seems to license massive irrationality. Anti-reductionists have tried to respond by saying that default entitlement can be defeated by positive reasons for doubt, but critics argue that this just collapses the view back into reductionism, since now you need reasons after all."
