I'm in a seminar on free will and punishment, and last week we discussed the Dennett–Caruso debate. After class, two classmates got into an argument that I've been turning over in my head ever since. I'll recount it as faithfully as I can.

The background: Dennett is a compatibilist who thinks we can justify punishment in terms of just deserts even without libertarian free will; Caruso is a free-will skeptic who thinks we should scrap desert-based punishment entirely and replace it with a public-health quarantine model focused on incapacitation and rehabilitation.

(1) Anna started by saying she found Caruso's position unstable. If you quarantine someone who poses a danger to society, you're still depriving them of liberty — and the length and severity of that deprivation will track the seriousness of what they did. So it looks like punishment by another name. She said Caruso owes us an account of why his model isn't just desert-based punishment with better PR.

(2) Ben replied that this misses the point entirely. The justification is different, and justification is what matters. In Caruso's model, you detain someone because they're dangerous, the way you isolate a carrier of a contagious disease — not because they deserve to suffer. The fact that both systems involve confinement doesn't make them the same thing any more than quarantine and kidnapping are the same thing.

(3) Anna pushed back: the disease analogy breaks down because we don't calibrate quarantine length to how bad the disease is in any moral sense — we calibrate it to incubation periods and transmission rates, which are empirical facts. But Caruso's model would give longer detention to murderers than to shoplifters, and that calibration looks suspiciously like it's tracking moral gravity, not just empirical risk. If the only consideration is dangerousness, why does a calculating fraudster get years while a bar fighter gets months, when the fraudster might actually be more likely to reoffend?

(4) Ben said that's a fair point, but it cuts both ways: Dennett's compatibilism has its own problem explaining why someone who acted with full reason-responsiveness at the time of the crime, but has since undergone a radical character change, still deserves punishment. If desert is tied to capacities at the time of the act, then the person in the dock may no longer be the person who deserves punishment in any meaningful sense. So neither view handles these edge cases perfectly.

(5) Anna responded that Ben was conflating two different questions: whether someone deserves punishment and whether we should actually administer it. Dennett can say the reformed person still deserves punishment but we have good consequentialist reasons to suspend it — mercy, rehabilitation, or the fact that punishment would now be pointless cruelty. Caruso can't even say the first part; he has to say nobody ever deserves punishment, which she finds wildly counterintuitive. If your sister is murdered, the claim that the murderer doesn't deserve to suffer at all seems like a failure to take the victim seriously.

(6) Ben said that intuition is exactly what Caruso would diagnose as the retributive impulse we need to overcome — it feels true, but so did the intuition that the sun goes around the earth. The whole project is to show that we can have a functioning system of criminal justice without that intuition.
