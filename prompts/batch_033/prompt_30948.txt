I teach the decision-theory grad seminar, and one of my colleagues keeps giving confident verdicts without saying what principle she's running. I've got her down on three, and I want to predict her on a fourth. The three:

- Newcomb, predictor right 99% of the time: she one-boxes.
- Death in Damascus: Death has already predicted where she'll go tomorrow, with no errors, and she can pay €1,000 to flee to Aleppo or stay for free. She stays. Her line is that fleeing would just be evidence Death predicted she'd flee, so she'd pay to die in Aleppo.
- XOR blackmail: a reliable predictor sends a letter iff exactly one of these is true -- she will pay $1,000 on receiving it, or her house has termites. Termite damage would be $10,000. She receives the letter and pays, because paying means the termite disjunct has to be false.

The fourth: standard counterfactual mugging. Omega has already flipped a coin; she's on the tails branch and knows it. On tails Omega asks for $100. On heads, which didn't happen, Omega would have given her $10,000 iff it had predicted she would have paid on tails. Does she pay the $100 or not?

What I really want is the set of candidate rules that fit the three verdicts, what each predicts on counterfactual mugging, and which further case would cleanly separate the remaining candidates. If part of the answer is that 'reliable predictor' needs to be made precise, say which precisification matters for the prediction.
