Just got back from a three-hour argument with a friend about the dogmatism paradox and I can't tell anymore which step is doing the work. We ended up with this on the whiteboard:

(1) If I know p, then p is true.
(2) If p is true, then any evidence against p is misleading.
(3) So if I know p, then any evidence against p is misleading, and I'm in a position to know that just from knowing p.
(4) If I know a piece of evidence is misleading, I can rationally set it aside.
(5) So if I know p, I can rationally set aside any evidence against p.
(6) But that's absurd: a scientist who knows her theory could rationally ignore every disconfirming result forever, and nobody could ever rationally change their mind about anything they already know.
(7) So one of (1)-(4) has to go, but each looked unobjectionable.

My friend keeps saying the culprit is (4), because 'misleading' just means the evidence points away from the truth, not that it gives me no reason to look again. I'd rather keep (1) and (4) and lose something else, but I can't find a clean place to break the chain. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And is there a reading of the argument where it's valid and the only real question is which premise is least bad to give up? After the prose diagnosis, give me a table with columns: step, candidate diagnosis, cost of rejecting it, verdict (reject / keep / reinterpret).
