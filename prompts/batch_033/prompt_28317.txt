I've been re-reading the old adaptationism dispute — the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique on one side and the adaptationist program on the other — and I keep noticing that I do my actual thinking about it in the vocabulary of machine learning rather than evolutionary biology. Like a lot of people in my circles, I've absorbed enough rationality-adjacent writing about optimization and selection to have strong intuitions here, but I don't trust my own mapping. I'd like you to act as someone fluent in both the philosophy-of-biology literature and modern optimization/ML, and to work through the translation carefully before drawing conclusions.

Start by laying out the central claims of each side and restating them in the vocabulary I reason in natively: objective functions, fitness landscapes, selection pressure, architectural constraints, inductive biases, regularization, training dynamics, local optima, emergent features, and artifacts of a trained system. On one side: the claim that current utility is not automatically evidence of selection for that utility; that many features are architectural byproducts; that organisms are integrated wholes subject to constraints; and that adaptationist explanations are often Panglossian just-so stories. On the other side: the reply that natural selection is the only known generator of complex functional design, and that reverse engineering from present function to selective history is a legitimate heuristic rather than a fallacy. For each side, flag every place where the translation has to fudge.

Then give me the residue report: which claims map cleanly onto something in ML, which map only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I especially want a verdict on two questions. First, is a spandrel best rendered as a byproduct or artifact of optimizing something else, as a constraint-induced feature, as a neutral feature later co-opted for a new use, or as something my vocabulary cannot say? Second, is the adaptationist's inference from present utility to selective history genuinely analogous to inferring a training objective from a model's weights and behavior, or does that analogy quietly assume the very thing it is meant to establish? My standing suspicion is that the optimization vocabulary handles the constraint-and-byproduct half of the critique well but silently drops the historical and comparative half — lineages, homologies, what could have been selected for — and I want you to tell me whether that dropped residue is a gap in my vocabulary or a sign that part of the original argument was doing less work than its rhetoric suggested. Be explicit about which residues you attribute to each side, and be willing to conclude that some of the residue is on the biologists' side rather than mine.
