I work in product at a company that builds LLM-based tools, and over the last year I've watched the same argument happen at every launch decision, so I'm trying to figure out whether the pattern itself has a name. The situation is this. Any safety measure we could add—red-teaming, extra refusal training, a slower rollout, turning down some high-margin customers—costs time and money and makes the product a bit slower or less impressive in a demo. Our competitors, who skimp on those steps, ship faster and cheaper. Buyers genuinely can't tell the difference up front, because a model that will occasionally fabricate a source or help with something awful looks identical in a demo to one that won't, and the failures are rare enough that most customers never see them—or when they do, the harm often lands on a third party, not on the buyer who chose the product. So the careful vendors either lose the deals or quietly drop the expensive safety work, and the ones who keep it get called nieve by their own boards. It's not that anyone is evil; each person is making the rational move given what the market rewards. I know this is adjacent to a race to the bottom, a market for lemons, and a tragedy of the commons, but none of those seems to be exactly it. The thing I'm looking at is specifically the extra cost of making a system that reliably does what its operator intends and refuses the bad stuff, and the fact that competitive pressure makes that cost unpayable even when every firm would prefer a world where everyone paid it. Does this configuration have a name of its own, and if not, what should it be called? I'm not after a slogan—I want the carving: what exactly distinguishes this from a plain race to the bottom on price or quality, which of the nearby concepts fits best, and where the edges of the phenomenon are. When you answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—the name or nearest concept—and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating my question.
