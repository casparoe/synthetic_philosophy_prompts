I am a novelist, and I have been asked to speak at a cross-disciplinary workshop on narrative and the emotions, where I will be the only practising writer on a panel that includes philosophers of mind. In preparation, I have tried to set down my working theory of what emotions are — not merely how to write them, but what I take them to consist in. Before I present these commitments, I want an audit of whether they hang together. I am too close to them to see the tensions clearly myself.

Here is the set:

(1) Emotions are essentially embodied. To be afraid is, in part, for your body to be in a particular state — heart rate elevated, breathing shallow, muscles tensed, attention narrowed toward threat. Strip away the bodily component and you have not got an emotion left; you have a bloodless appraisal. I take this to be broadly Jamesian: the bodily response is not a symptom of the emotion, it is constitutive of it.

(2) Emotions have internal conditions of fittingness. Fear is fitting when its object is genuinely dangerous; grief is fitting when its object is a real loss; indignation is fitting when one has been wronged. These norms belong to the emotion types themselves — they are not reducible to social conventions about when an emotion is useful or prudent to feel. A person who feels searing grief at a minor disappointment is not merely inconveniently sad; their grief is unfitting, and that unfittingness is a defect in the emotion qua grief.

(3) Some emotions are cognitively demanding in a way that simple affects are not. To feel indignation you need the concept of injustice; to feel nostalgia you need a conception of the past as irrecoverable; to feel romantic jealousy you need to grasp the idea of an exclusive commitment. These are not the same underlying bodily states with different labels applied after the fact. The cognitive content enters into the emotion itself — it shapes what the emotion is.

(4) In my fiction, I have found that the most reliable technique for making a reader feel a character's emotion is never to name it. Instead, I render the character's bodily condition — the heat spreading across the chest, the stillness of the hands, the texture of the breathing — and I embed that description in a narrative context that makes the evaluative stakes plain. If I have done my job, the reader registers the emotion without my ever supplying its name. I take the success of this technique as evidence that emotions are, at bottom, embodied responses to evaluatively-laden situations.

(5) I reject the view that emotions are reducible to judgments or beliefs. A character can judge, with full sincerity, that the spider is harmless while her body screams danger. A character can judge that her colleague's promotion is deserved while feeling envy burn in her chest. Judgment and emotion can come apart in both directions, so emotion cannot simply be a species of judgment.

I need to know whether these five claims form a consistent package. I suspect there are strains, but I cannot tell which are real and which are merely apparent. In particular, I worry that (1) and (2) pull in opposite directions: if emotions are bodily states, in virtue of what do they admit of fittingness or unfittingness? My body does not get evaluated for correctness when I have a stomachache. And I worry that (3) and (4) together might be in tension with (5): if some emotions require concepts, and if my writing technique works by embedding bodily description in an evaluative context, does that not suggest that emotions have a cognitive-evaluative core of the very sort that (5) seems to rule out?

Please go through these five claims and identify every pair that is in genuine tension, every pair that only appears to be in tension, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, specify which claim or claims would have to be revised or abandoned to resolve it, and what the least costly revision would be. I am not signalling which commitment I would be willing to give up; I want the audit to be indifferent to that.
