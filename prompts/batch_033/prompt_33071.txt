I teach an upper-level seminar on public reason and religion in liberal democracy: sixteen students, one 75-minute session. Design a structured deliberation that makes the students feel the force of the asymmetry objection to the demand that citizens justify coercive laws with reasons that others can reasonably accept, without my lecturing on the objection first and without naming it until the debrief. Choose a contested policy—ideally one that is not so immediately personal that students cannot adopt assigned positions—on which both a religious argument and a secular comprehensive argument (for instance, an autonomy-based liberal argument or a utilitarian one) are naturally in play. The rules, roles, or constraints should be arranged so that students trying to comply with a public-reasons-only rule run into the asymmetry themselves: religious reasons are filtered out as nonpublic while secular reasons that are equally sectarian in their foundations pass without comment, and students who must translate their reasons into generally accessible terms end up doing work that others are not asked to do. Provide roles or rules, a timeline, any brief reason-cards or handouts the facilitator needs, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto the distinction between public and nonpublic reasons, the wide view of public political culture, and the translation proviso. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including how to handle students who spot the asymmetry early, and which feature of the real debate the exercise cannot reproduce and I will need to supply myself.
