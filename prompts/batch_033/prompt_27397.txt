so i picked up this pop-sci book at an airport — one of those "your brain on tech" things — and it had a chapter on the extended mind thesis by Clark and Chalmers. the author summarized it roughly like this:

"Clark and Chalmers argue that your mind is not confined to your brain. When you use a tool like a notebook or a smartphone consistently and trust it the way you trust your own memory, that tool becomes literally part of your mind. The classic example is Otto, a man with Alzheimers who writes everything in a notebook. When Otto checks his notebook to find the museum address, the notebook is doing exactly what your biological memory does, so it counts as a mental state just like a belief. The upshot is that the boundary between mind and world is a myth — wherever your cognitive tools are, that's where your mind is. This has huge implications: if you outsource your memory to Google, your mind extends into the cloud, and if you lose your phone, you've literally lost part of your mind."

i'm a software engineer so this idea is kind of seductive — i definitely feel like my thinking changes when i've got my IDE open, and i basically treat my notes app as external storage for my brain. but the "literally part of your mind" language feels like it might be running ahead of what the actual argument supports. like, if my phone is part of my mind, does my mind change every time i switch note-taking apps? and the cloud thing seems especially dubious — my wifi goes down and suddenly my mind shrinks?

i'm trying to figure out how much of this summary a philosopher who defends the extended mind thesis would actually sign off on. what's accurate, what's a subtle stretch, and does the "literally part of your mind" conclusion follow from the accurate bits or is the book overclaiming? i don't need a whole bibliography, just want to know which parts to trust when i bring this up with people.
