I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about when it's rational to cut your losses versus when you should see a commitment through, going by my gut on cases. I'd like you to help me extract the principle — or small set of principles — that best captures my verdicts, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and apply it to a case I haven't resolved.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. **The classic dollar auction.** Two bidders compete for a dollar bill under the rule that both the winner and the runner-up must pay their final bids, but only the winner gets the dollar. Bidding has reached $0.90 (Alice) and $0.95 (Bob), and it is Alice's turn. My verdict: Alice should not bid $1.00, and the whole situation is a trap both should have avoided entering. But I also think that if I were Alice and had already bid $0.90, I would feel a strong pull to bid $1.00, and I am not confident I would resist it — and I do not think that pull is simply a cognitive error, though it might be.

2. **The failing corporate project.** A company has spent $50 million developing a product. A competitor has just released a superior version. An internal analysis says that spending another $10 million gives a 15% chance of catching up and recouping $30 million in revenue, and an 85% chance of recouping nothing additional. My verdict: the company should kill the project. The $50 million is gone either way.

3. **The sixth-year PhD student.** A doctoral student is in year six of a humanities PhD, has finished all coursework and exams, and has two dissertation chapters written out of a planned five. They hate the work, their advisor is neglectful, and the academic job market is terrible. They estimate two more years to finish. They have no debt but also no savings. My verdict: they should finish. Walking away at this stage would be a mistake, even though the expected career payoff is modest and the misery is real.

4. **The long-unhappy relationship.** Two people have been together for eight years, married for five. The relationship has been genuinely bad — not abusive, but characterized by frequent conflict, little warmth, and no shared projects — for at least three of those years. They have tried counseling twice with minimal improvement. No children. My verdict: they should separate. The years already spent are not a reason to stay.

5. **The stalemated war.** A country has been fighting a war for four years. The original objective — regime change in a neighboring state — now looks unattainable at any acceptable cost. Approximately 3,000 of its soldiers have died. Withdrawal would mean admitting defeat and likely emboldening rivals, but continuing would mean more deaths for an objective that cannot be achieved. My verdict: withdrawal is the right call, but I feel the weight of the counterargument about emboldening rivals more than I do in the corporate-project case. I am not sure whether that is a rational consideration or just my own version of the sunk-cost fallacy dressed up in strategic language.

6. **The poker player down for the night.** A skilled recreational poker player has lost $800 in a cash game over six hours. They are playing well but running badly. They tell themselves they will quit once they are even or at least down only $400. The game is good — several weaker players are still at the table. My verdict: this is irrational. The $800 is gone. The only question is whether the remaining session has positive expected value, and if it does, that is the reason to stay, not the desire to get even.

7. **The over-budget public infrastructure project.** A city has spent $2 billion and twelve years on a subway extension that was originally budgeted at $800 million and five years. The project is now 80% complete. Finishing will cost another $600 million and three years. Abandoning it would leave a useless tunnel segment and require spending $200 million on remediation. The completed extension is projected to generate $1.5 billion in economic benefits, net present value. My verdict: they should finish it. The original overruns are a scandal, but the forward-looking case for completion is solid.

8. **The abandoned novel.** A writer has spent two years and 70,000 words on a novel. They have come to believe the premise is fundamentally flawed — the central conceit does not work, and revision would require essentially starting over. They have another novel idea they are excited about. My verdict: abandon it. The two years taught them something, and that is the only return they will get.

What I notice is that I am not a simple "ignore sunk costs always" person. In the corporate project, the poker game, the relationship, and the novel, I say walk away. In the PhD, the subway, and — with unease — the war, I say stay the course, or at least I feel the pull of staying. The dollar auction I regard as a trap but also as genuinely difficult in a way that is not just about cognitive bias.

Is there a coherent principle, or a small set of principles, that captures this pattern? What feature distinguishes the cases where I favor persistence from those where I favor cutting losses? If no simple principle fits all my verdicts, which ones should I revise, and why? And what does the principle you extract say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a founder who has spent five years and raised $3 million for a startup that has modest traction but no clear path to profitability, and who is considering whether to pivot to a new idea or shut down and return to a salaried job?

Please write your answer in American English.
