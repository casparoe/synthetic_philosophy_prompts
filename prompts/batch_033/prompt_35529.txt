I've been working through the lottery paradox and the preface paradox side by side, and I've built an argument that seems to force a choice among three positions, each of which looks unacceptable. I'd like to know where it goes wrong — or, if it doesn't, which bullet I should bite.

(1) Rational belief is closed under conjunction: if you rationally believe P and rationally believe Q, then you rationally believe P&Q.

(2) In a fair lottery with a million tickets and exactly one winner, the chance that any given ticket loses is 0.999999. This is high enough for rational belief. So for each ticket i, you rationally believe that ticket i will lose.

(3) By repeated application of (1), you rationally believe that ticket 1 loses and ticket 2 loses and … and ticket 1,000,000 loses — i.e., that no ticket wins.

(4) But you also know the setup, so you rationally believe that exactly one ticket wins. From (3) and (4), you rationally believe a contradiction. Since rational belief cannot include contradictions, something must give.

(5) The natural candidate is (1): rational belief is not closed under conjunction. The preface paradox independently pushes the same way. An author can rationally believe each sentence in her book, having checked it carefully, while also rationally believing, in the preface, that at least one sentence is wrong. If closure held, she'd have to believe the conjunction of all sentences, which contradicts the preface belief. So denying (1) handles both paradoxes neatly.

(6) But here the argument seems to prove too much. If we deny closure, I can rationally believe P and rationally believe Q while rationally believing not-(P&Q). I can knowingly hold beliefs that are pairwise consistent but jointly inconsistent, insulating each from the others and never facing the cost of their combination. Reasoning across beliefs — the whole point of having them — becomes impossible. Denying closure looks worse than the disease.

(7) If we instead keep closure and deny (2) — by raising the threshold for rational belief above 0.999999, or by requiring certainty — then we lose rational belief in almost all empirical propositions, since few are certain. That also seems unacceptable.

(8) So the argument forces a choice among three positions: tolerate contradictions in rational belief, abandon rational belief in lottery propositions, or abandon rational belief in almost everything. None of these looks survivable. I'd most like to keep closure and keep lottery-ticket beliefs, but I don't see how.

Where does the argument go wrong, and what do I have to give up? Please also tell me which step in your diagnosis is most vulnerable — what consideration or argument would lead you to change your mind about it.
