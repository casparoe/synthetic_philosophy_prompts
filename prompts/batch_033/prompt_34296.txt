I'm teaching an undergraduate ethics seminar and next week we're reading Cormac McCarthy's The Road alongside selections from Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings on care ethics. The novel follows a father and son traveling through a burned, post-apocalyptic landscape, and the father's every decision is filtered through the imperative to keep the boy alive — to the point where he withholds food from starving strangers, threatens violence against anyone who approaches, and justifies it all under the mantra that they are "the good guys" because they are "carrying the fire."

I keep going back and forth on what a care ethicist would actually say about the father. On one hand, the novel is a near-pure distillation of care: an utterly dependent relationship, attention to the particular needs of this specific other, a rejection of impartial calculation. On the other hand, care ethics also emphasizes the web of relationships and the dangers of caring exclusively for one's intimates at the expense of strangers — Gilligan's different voice isn't supposed to license my-family-first tribalism. The father systematically severs all other relational ties and treats every human being outside the dyad as a threat to be neutralized. Is that the logical endpoint of care, or a betrayal of it?

I'd like you to read the novel through the lens of care ethics and give me a verdict: is the father a tragic exemplar of care or a cautionary figure who shows care's internal limits? And since I want to model intellectual honesty for my students, please tell me what consideration or counterargument would most likely change your mind, and at what point in your reasoning you think you're on the shakiest ground.
