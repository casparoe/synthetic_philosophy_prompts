I've been reading Weil's argument that political parties should just be abolished, and it's got me wondering what the institution actually presupposes, rather than whether she's right. Here's the practice: a party demands its members vote the platform even when they privately think it's wrong, it whips votes, it treats the other side's proposal as worth opposing mainly because it's the other side's, it disciplines internal dissent as disloyalty, and it's overriding aim is to grow and win, not to get the answer right. Meanwhile the official story is that parties are vehicles for democratic representation and deliberation. What theory of collective judgment — or of truth in politics — does that practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that truth is subordinate to the party's interest, or that individual judgment has to be suppressed for the sake of collective power? Is the implicit view coherent, or is the practice underdetermined by several theories? And what would a politics built on Weil's alternative — ad hoc, issue-based associations that dissolve once the issue is settled — have to change about how disagreement is handled?
