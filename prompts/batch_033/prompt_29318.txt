At a metaphysics reading group I attend, two members got into a sharp disagreement about the unity of the proposition, and neither would concede. I'm going to reconstruct their exchange as accurately as I can. They were discussing how a simple subject-predicate proposition — say, that Socrates is wise — manages to be a unified whole rather than a mere list of its constituents (Socrates, wisdom).

(1) The first, Callum, defended what he called a relational account: the proposition is unified by a relation of predication or instantiation that holds between Socrates and wisdom. Without such a relation, he said, you just have two items sitting side by side, which is not yet a proposition — something has to do the combining.

(2) The second, Naomi, replied that this invites Bradley's regress. If instantiation is itself a relation, then to unify Socrates and wisdom you need instantiation; but now you have three items — Socrates, wisdom, and instantiation — and they are just as much a mere list as the original two were. To unify these three you would need a further relation, call it instantiation₂, and so on ad infinitum. Since the regress never bottoms out, the original unity is never achieved.

(3) Callum answered that the regress is infinite but not vicious. A relation, he said, is the kind of thing that relates its relata; it is internal to the nature of a relation that it holds between the things it relates. There is no need for a further external unifier because the relating is already built into the relation itself. The regress only looks vicious if you assume that every connection must be supplied from outside, which is exactly the assumption he was rejecting.

(4) Naomi pushed back: saying that relating is internal to the nature of the relation just attributes a property — being-self-relating, or holding-between-its-relata — to the instantiation relation. But now you face the same problem one level up: what unifies the instantiation relation with that property? If you say it's internal again, you've started a parallel regress about the relation and its nature. You haven't stopped the regress; you've just moved it.

(5) Callum said this mischaracterizes his view. To say a relation is internally relating is not to posit a further relational property that needs gluing; it is to say that the relation and its relating are not two things in the first place. The relation just is the relating. The demand for a further unifier only arises if you reify "being internally relating" as a separate constituent, which he was not doing.

(6) Naomi responded that if the relation just is the relating, then the original problem was never solved — because the original problem was to explain what relating consists in, such that it turns a plurality into a unity. Saying "the relation just is the relating" renames the explanandum rather than providing an explanans. Callum's account, she said, ends up treating the unity of the proposition as a primitive, which is exactly what the relational account was supposed to improve upon.

They looked to me to break the tie, but I found myself going in circles. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether the speaker is making a sound point, a weak point, or a mistake, and explain why. Then give an overall assessment: which of the two has the stronger position by the end, and what — if anything — are both of them missing?
