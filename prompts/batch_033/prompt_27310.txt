I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Self-Reference in Formal and Natural Systems" — about 18 students, one 90-minute session. We have just finished the material on Gödel numbering and the diagonal lemma, and next week we turn to Hofstadter's Typogenetics (from Gödel, Escher, Bach) and the claimed structural isomorphism between DNA self-replication and Gödelian self-reference. Before they read the chapter, I want them to discover the core idea by doing it.

Design a classroom game in which students manually execute a toy formal system and stumble onto self-replication without being told what to look for. The system should have: an alphabet of four letters (A, C, G, T); a fixed, public translation table that maps pairs of letters to simple string-manipulation instructions (copy the next n letters, insert a specified letter, delete the next letter, skip to the next occurrence of a specified letter, repeat from the start, halt, and the like); and a set of starting strands — strings of roughly 12 to 20 letters — that each student or pair receives on a strip of paper. Students execute their strand by reading its letters in consecutive pairs, following the corresponding instruction from the table, and building an output string on a blank strip. They should not be told that the goal is to find self-replicators — only to "execute your strand and write down what it makes." The moment of discovery should come from them.

The strand set must be designed so that: (i) some strands produce a copy of themselves when executed (self-replicators); (ii) at least one strand produces a copy of a different strand (a parasite or a hypercycle); (iii) at least one strand produces a copy of itself with a single-letter change (a mutation); and (iv) most strands produce either garbage or nothing intelligible. The self-replicating strands should not be trivially short — they should involve at least a handful of instructions so that the fact of self-replication feels non-obvious and earned.

Materials: printed strips, a poster or handout of the translation table, blank strips for output, and optionally a worksheet for recording results. No computers.

Debrief (20–25 minutes): Map the activity onto three ideas. First, the formal structure of Gödel numbering — the translation table is a code that lets strings talk about strings, including themselves. Second, the diagonal lemma — the self-replicating strand is a fixed point, constructed by a recipe that, given a property expressible in the system, produces a string that has that property of itself. Third, the biological parallel — a DNA sequence encodes enzymes that operate on DNA sequences, including the sequence that encoded them, and the activity's "execution" of a strand by a student mimics the ribosome's traversal of a genetic tape. Then draw the isomorphism explicitly: the code table plays the role of Gödel numbering; the self-replicating strand plays the role of a sentence that says something about its own Gödel number; the fact that the system does not "know" which strands self-replicate until you run them mirrors the fact that provability is a syntactic property of the system, not a semantic one.

Finally, name what the activity cannot reproduce. Gödel's result requires negation — the ability to construct a sentence that says "I am not provable." Pure self-replication has no analogue of negation, so the activity captures self-reference and fixed-point construction but not the limitative force of the incompleteness theorems. The facilitator must supply this point in the debrief; the game cannot deliver it.

Also tell me: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students miscounting letter pairs, strands that accidentally produce themselves in trivial or unintended ways, pairs finishing at wildly different times, students who grasp it blurting it out before others have discovered it), and how to handle each. And provide the exact translation table and the full set of designed strands — I need to be able to print them and run the activity.
