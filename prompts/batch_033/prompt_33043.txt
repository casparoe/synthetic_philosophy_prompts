I hold the view that statements about future contingent events are neither true nor false at the time they are uttered. This is the position Aristotle works through in De Interpretatione 9 with the sea battle: the sentence "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" is not now true and not now false, because if it were already true, it would seem to follow that the battle is now inevitable, and if it were already false, that its absence is now inevitable — but neither outcome is yet settled. The view is that bivalence fails for future contingents, though it holds for statements about the past and present and for statements about what is necessary or impossible. By a future contingent I mean an event whose occurrence is not yet determined by the present state of the world together with the laws of nature, and whose non-occurrence is likewise not yet determined — so the view applies only where the future is genuinely open.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for objections that merely beg the question by assuming bivalence as a background logical given. I want the considerations that should move a person who finds the view independently attractive: the arguments, the formal results, the intuitive counterexamples, the developments in neighbouring areas that put pressure on it. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward. I would like you to distinguish between what external critics say and what a defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it — for instance, a defender might concede that certain consequences of the view are genuinely uncomfortable even if they ultimately accept them. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated from falsification by qualifications — for example, by refining the notion of "determined" in a way that makes the claim about truth-value gaps vacuous, or by retreating to a purely semantic position that makes no contact with metaphysics — and whether that kind of insulation is a problem for a view of this sort. I am particularly interested in whether the view can survive close contact with the metaphysics of time: if the block universe or eternalism is correct, does the view collapse? And if presentism is correct, does that help or hurt? I would also like to know whether there is a version of the view that bites the bullet on the logical revisionism — gives up excluded middle, say — and whether that counts as a cost or just as a clear statement of what the view was committed to all along.
