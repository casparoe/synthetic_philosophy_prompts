I've recently started working for a foundation that makes grants across very different cause areas — global health, pandemic prevention, AI safety — and I've realized that my recommendations implicitly depend on population-ethical judgments I've never made explicit. So I sat down and worked through my gut reactions to a series of cases. I'd like you, as a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics, to help me extract the principle (or small set of principles) that best captures these verdicts, tell me which ones I may need to abandon, and apply the resulting view to the case I keep going back and forth on.

Please think carefully — start by brainstorming which axiological theories could accommodate different subsets of my verdicts, then work toward the most coherent overall position.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. World A: 10 billion people with extraordinarily good lives. World Z: an astronomically large number of people with lives that are just barely worth living — a bit more happiness than misery, but only just. I judge World A better than World Z. The sheer number of barely-positive lives in Z doesn't compensate for how meager each one is.

2. Start with a population of 10 billion people living good lives. Now add 10 billion additional people whose lives are also positive, though noticeably less good than the original group's. The original group is unaffected — their lives go exactly as before. I judge the resulting world no worse than the original. Adding happy people doesn't make the world worse.

3. World S: a thousand people enduring excruciating torture. World T: an astronomically large number of people each experiencing a mild headache. I judge World T worse. Aggregating tiny sufferings across enough people eventually outweighs intense suffering concentrated in a few.

4. A future with no sentient life at all — an empty universe after humanity goes extinct. Versus a future in which trillions of beings live flourishing, happy lives over millions of years. I judge the populated future vastly better. An empty universe would be a tragedy.

5. We have a fixed budget. Option One: substantially improve the welfare of a million currently existing people. Option Two: instead of helping them, use the money to bring a million new people into existence whose lives would be even happier than the improved lives under Option One. I judge Option One better. We owe more to actual people than to merely possible ones.

6. A thousand people with deeply excellent lives — rich in achievement, love, and beauty — versus a hundred thousand people whose lives are merely fine: pleasant enough, but shallow. I judge the thousand excellent lives better, even though total welfare might be higher in the larger population.

7. (The case I go back and forth on.) My partner and I are considering having a child. We have good reason to think the child would have a genuinely good life — not perfect, but clearly worth living. On some days I think: bringing a new person with a good life into the world makes the world better, impersonally speaking. On other days I think: there's no one who is harmed by our not having the child, so how could not having them be worse? I can't settle this.

Is there an axiological principle — total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, a critical-level view, a person-affecting view, something else — that captures verdicts 1 through 6? Which verdicts would I have to revise to make things cohere? And what does the resulting view say about case 7?
