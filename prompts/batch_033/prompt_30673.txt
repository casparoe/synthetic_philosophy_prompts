I've been running into G. E. Moore's proof of an external world in the compressed, sloganized form "here is one hand," and I've begun to suspect that the same four words are being used to assert several quite different things. In a reading group on radical skepticism, someone ended a long exchange about brains in vats by holding up his hand and saying "here is one hand," as if the gesture itself settled the question whether we know there's an external world. A week later, an epistemologist cited Moore's 1939 essay "Proof of an External World" approvingly, but described the argument as a valid proof that is dialectically useless against a determined skeptic. A third person then insisted it was never meant as a proof in that sense at all, but as a claim that the premise expressed by the gesture is more certain than any premise in a skeptical argument, so that modus ponens should be preferred to the skeptic's modus tollens.

I want to know what, exactly, the slogan could be claiming, and which of those three uses each candidate reading would actually license. Lay the readings out as a menu, the way one would disambiguate ought implies can or the map is not the territory: for each reading, say precisely what it asserts — about what exists, about what is known, about what follows, about how warrant is transmitted, about who bears the burden of proof, about what a proof must do to count as a proof — whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and which of my three encounters it supports. I am especially interested in where the line falls between "the argument is valid," "the argument is sound," and "the argument gives someone a reason to reject skepticism," and in whether any reading can do the work the first person was asking it to do without already assuming the conclusion it is used to establish.
