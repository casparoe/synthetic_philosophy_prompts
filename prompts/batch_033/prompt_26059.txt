Two colleagues in my department—both have read Du Bois's 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art"—got into an argument about what Du Bois actually meant when he wrote that "all Art is propaganda and ever must be, despite the wailing of the purists." I was present for the whole exchange and took notes. Here is what they said.

Miriam: Du Bois is making a descriptive claim about the social function of art. He's saying that all art, regardless of the artist's intentions, advances some set of interests or reinforces some view of the world. That's why he says "ever must be"—it's not a command, it's a statement of inevitability. Art cannot help but be propaganda because it cannot help but take a stance, even if only by what it chooses to depict as beautiful or worth attending to.

David: That reading flattens the essay completely. Du Bois is giving a speech to the NAACP about what Negro art should be. He's not doing sociology of art; he's issuing a normative demand. When he says "ever must be," he means "ought to be." The whole essay is an argument that Black artists have an obligation to make their work serve the struggle for racial justice. He says outright that whatever art he himself has produced has been used for propaganda on behalf of Black freedom. That's a personal credo, not a neutral observation.

Miriam: But that sentence cuts both ways. He's describing what he has in fact done with his own writing—not what others ought to do. And look at the sentence right before the propaganda line: he's been talking about Beauty with a capital B, the Cathedral at Cologne, the Venus de Milo. He clearly thinks great art can be both beautiful and propagandistic. He's not saying "sacrifice beauty to politics." He's saying the supposed opposition is false.

David: He's not saying the opposition is false; he's saying that in the context of American racism, the pursuit of pure beauty—art for art's sake—is a luxury Black artists cannot afford. The white "purists" he mocks can afford to wail about propaganda because their interests are already served by the status quo. Du Bois is making a contextual argument: given the conditions Black Americans face, Black art must be propaganda. That's not a universal claim about all art everywhere; it's a strategic claim about art under conditions of oppression.

Miriam: Then why does he say "all art is propaganda"? Not "all Negro art," not "all art under capitalism," not "all art in a racist society." All art. Period. He's a Hegelian—he thinks in universals. The essay opens by saying that the fight for rights and the fight for beauty are the same fight, and he frames the whole thing as a vision of what America could be, not just what Black artists should do. You're reading the essay as a narrower political intervention than it actually is.

David: And you're reading it as a broader philosophical treatise than it actually is. The speech was delivered at an NAACP award ceremony. The audience was Black. The question on the table was what Negro art should be—that's the title of the essay. You can't just bracket the rhetorical situation and treat it as a disinterested analytic claim about the metaphysics of art.

I couldn't tell who was getting the better of the argument, and I'd like a careful adjudication. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about what at each step. Where one of them is clearly on stronger ground, say so; where both have a point, say what each gets right and what each misses. In particular, I want you to separate what is settled in the scholarly understanding of Du Bois's essay from what remains genuinely contested among people who know the text well, and be careful not to present a contested reading as if it were the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in American English.
