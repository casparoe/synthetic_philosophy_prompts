I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, currently completing a twelve-month policy fellowship embedded in the modeling and risk-assessment division of a national public health agency. The division produces probabilistic forecasts across several streams: infectious-disease outbreak trajectories, heat-mortality projections under climate scenarios, and post-market vaccine effectiveness estimates. The forecasts arrive from distinct modeling teams who operate within different statistical frameworks, often without making their interpretive commitments explicit. Some outputs are frequentist confidence bounds derived from surveillance time series. Some are Bayesian posterior probabilities incorporating structured expert elicitation. Some are weighted ensemble outputs from multi-model simulation pipelines in which the meaning of the output probability is itself ambiguous — the number behaves like a credence in some respects, a long-run frequency in others, and neither cleanly. Meanwhile, the communications office must translate these numbers into plain-language briefings for ministry officials and press releases for the public, routinely collapsing distinct kinds of probability into a single "percent chance" statement. On at least two occasions in the past year, a policy decision turned on whether to treat a probability as a stable feature of the world or as a summary of current ignorance, and the disagreement was never surfaced because no one had a shared vocabulary for it.

I have been asked to produce an internal guidance document: a decision procedure that any staff member — an epidemiologist, a risk communicator, a policy officer — can apply to a probability claim to determine (1) which interpretation of probability (frequentist, subjective/Bayesian, propensity, or hybrid) is operative, (2) whether that interpretation matters for the decision or the communication at hand, and (3) where it does matter, how to translate or reframe the claim for the target audience without misrepresenting what is known.

The procedure should take the form of a step-by-step questionnaire or a flowchart in words, something a user with basic statistical literacy but no formal philosophy background can complete in under fifteen minutes for a single claim. It must handle the following hard cases:

- Probabilities that mix interpretations (e.g., a Bayesian update on a prior derived from observed frequencies; a propensity model fed into a subjective decision threshold).
- Situations where two defensible interpretations yield the same numerical value but support different actions or different levels of confidence in the number.
- Cases where no single interpretation fits cleanly, and the user must decide which aspects of the claim to prioritize.
- Cases where the communication consequence of switching interpretations is politically or ethically loaded (e.g., a frequentist framing suggests we lack enough data to act, while a Bayesian framing licenses action on the same evidence).

The procedure must flag the points at which judgment cannot be eliminated and the question must be escalated to a senior analyst. It should be honest about which steps involve value judgments dressed as technical choices.

Please illustrate the procedure by working it through on two realistic cases drawn from the agency's actual work:

(a) A forecast stating that there is a 23% probability of a measles outbreak exceeding 500 cases in a specified region over the next quarter, generated by fitting a stochastic compartmental transmission model to the previous five years of weekly case counts, with no expert priors incorporated. The communications office proposes reporting this as "about a one-in-four chance."

(b) A claim that a newly introduced vaccine against a mosquito-borne virus has a 92% probability of being at least 80% efficacious, derived from a Bayesian analysis of a single-season trial, where the prior on vaccine efficacy was explicitly elicited from a panel of twelve experts using a structured protocol. The communications office proposes reporting this as "the vaccine is 92% likely to be highly effective."

For each case, show what the procedure outputs at each step, whether it flags an escalation point, and what communication guidance it ultimately recommends — including whether it endorses or revises the proposed language. Ideally, the two cases should illustrate places where the procedure recommends different communication treatments despite superficial similarities in the numbers.

Finally, identify what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of probability-involving decisions or disputes it will not resolve, the audiences for whom it will be inadequate, and the conditions under which it is likely to misfire.
