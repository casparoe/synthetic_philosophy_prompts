I cover consciousness research and philosophy of mind for a general-audience magazine, and I recently read a popular book on the science of consciousness that included the following summary of the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment as an objection to epiphenomenalism. I want to know whether this summary is fair before I rely on it in my own writing. Here is the passage:

"Epiphenomenalism is the view that consciousness is a real phenomenon but has no causal power — it is an effect of brain activity that itself causes nothing, like the steam from a locomotive whistle. The standard objection, known as the paradox of phenomenal judgment, runs like this: if consciousness has no causal power, then your belief that you are in pain cannot be caused by your pain. Your brain produces both the experience and the judgment, but the experience itself plays no role in generating the judgment. This means your brain would produce the judgment 'I am in pain' whether or not you were actually in pain, because the pain is not doing any causal work. But then you could never know that you are in pain — your judgments about your own conscious states would be reliable only by an enormous coincidence. Since we clearly do know our own conscious states, epiphenomenalism is self-refuting. Most philosophers of mind therefore regard epiphenomenalism as a non-starter."

I have enough background to suspect that a paragraph this compressed is probably cutting some corners, but I do not know the literature well enough to identify which claims a careful epiphenomenalist would accept, which they would push back on, and which are outright misrepresentations. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what holds up, what needs qualification, and what a defender of epiphenomenalism would say in response — and whether the conclusion that epiphenomenalism is self-refuting follows from the accurate parts once the inaccurate ones are corrected. I am especially interested in whether the step about coincidence is as damaging as the author makes it sound, and whether the "most philosophers regard it as a non-starter" line reflects the actual state of the debate or is rhetorical overreach.
