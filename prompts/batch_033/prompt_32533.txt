I spent my career in physics, where decision problems have clear structure: states, actions, payoffs, and an optimality criterion. Newcomb's problem, which I've only recently encountered in the philosophical literature, seems to resist that kind of resolution. A near-perfect Predictor presents you with two boxes: Box A visibly contains $1,000; Box B is opaque. The Predictor has already placed $1,000,000 in Box B if she predicted you'd take only Box B, and nothing if she predicted you'd take both. You may take either both boxes or just Box B. The two-boxer argues that the money is already fixed, so taking both dominates: whatever is in Box B, you get $1,000 more. The one-boxer argues that the Predictor's accuracy makes your choice and the contents of Box B evidentially correlated, so expected utility favors taking only Box B. I'd like to write an essay that weighs the strongest arguments on each side and reaches a definite verdict — not a compromise indexed to the Predictor's accuracy, but a principled answer about what rationality requires, and why.
