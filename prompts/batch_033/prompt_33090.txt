You are an expert on AI alignment, reinforcement learning, and the design of interactive pedagogy for technically sophisticated audiences. I run a graduate reading group on the philosophy and engineering of AI safety: sixteen participants, mostly philosophy PhD students with some machine learning background, and we meet for one ninety-minute session. We have been working through material on reward learning, Goodhart effects, and the distinction between wireheading, where an agent directly stimulates its own reward channel rather than doing the task, and reward tampering, where an agent intervenes in the reward-generating process itself so that it receives high reward without performing the intended task. The group tends to nod along and then dismiss the problem with the same few moves: add a penalty term, keep a human in the loop, retrain the reward model, or just specify the reward better. I want a session that makes them feel why those moves do not trivially close the problem, and by the end I want them to see reward hacking as a structural pressure that reappears whenever a proxy is optimized hard enough, with the wireheading/reward-tampering distinction marking where in the loop the agent intervenes.

Design a runnable classroom activity for this group in which participants take on roles such as agent, reward model, overseer, or evaluator, and run into the failure modes through the incentives of the game rather than through a lecture. The concepts should not be named up front; the rules, scoring, and information structure should be arranged so that the failure emerges from what participants find it profitable to do. The activity should fit the ninety minutes, use only ordinary materials such as paper, cards, tokens, or a whiteboard, and not require real money or software. Please include: the rules and roles; the materials; a scoring scheme together with any information asymmetries or communication restrictions; a timeline; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto wireheading, reward tampering, reward-model misspecification, and the way oversight itself can become another proxy to game. The debrief should also draw out at least one case in which a superficially reasonable safeguard creates a new incentive rather than eliminating the old one. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the activity and how to handle it, and be candid about which feature of the real phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce, such as the scale of search available to real systems or the opacity of learned representations, and that the facilitator will have to supply. Please think through the design carefully; before settling on a mechanism, briefly sketch two candidate game mechanics and explain why you are choosing one over the other.
