I am a retired natural scientist—most of my career was spent in the laboratory rather than the seminar room—and I have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously. I am organizing a small discussion group around Bertolt Brecht's play Life of Galileo, and I would like to use the play to examine something I find more interesting than the familiar story of science versus the Church: Galileo's own position on scripture. In the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615), the historical Galileo maintains that scripture and nature cannot ultimately contradict one another because both issue from the same divine source; that biblical language is often accommodated to the capacities of ordinary readers; and that where a literal reading of a passage conflicts with a demonstrated physical result, it is the literal reading, not the physical conclusion, that must give way. Brecht's Galileo, on the face of it, is a man who defies ecclesiastical authority, recants under threat of torture, and then resumes his work in confinement; the play appears to treat the quarrel as one between free inquiry and institutional power, with comparatively little direct attention to hermeneutics. I am aware that the play exists in several versions and that Brecht revised it after Hiroshima; for our purposes, any version that includes the recantation and the closing scenes with the Discorsi will do, since those are the scenes I most want examined. I want a reading of the play through the lens of the Letter. Where does Brecht's Galileo actually enact or give voice to the accommodationist view, and where does the play flatten the dispute into a political collision and lose the epistemological question of how scripture is to be read? Do the recantation scene and the closing scenes with the Discorsi illuminate or complicate the Letter's claim that the same divine Word underwrites both the natural order and the text of scripture? I am not asking who was right about Copernicanism. I want to know whether a twentieth-century drama can faithfully represent a seventeenth-century account of the relation between natural knowledge and revelation, and where it cannot. I would appreciate a reading that is faithful to both the play and the Letter, that points to particular scenes, and that is willing to say plainly where Brecht's Galileo is not Galileo.
