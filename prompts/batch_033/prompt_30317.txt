I've spent the past month unable to stop thinking about superrationality, after a falling-out with a longtime collaborator over a one-shot cooperation problem that I thought standard game theory described correctly but advised about wrongly. I've now got a seminar slot to defend a version of superrationality, and I want to walk in prepared for the question period rather than hoping the room is friendly.

The thesis I'm defending is this: in a symmetric one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two agents who know the game is symmetric, who know that each is rational in the same sense, and who know that each is reasoning from the same premises through the same sound procedure, each ought to choose the strategy that is best conditional on both choosing alike — namely, cooperate. I am deliberately not restricting the claim to literal copies, identical algorithms, or agents with a common cause. I want to defend the stronger claim that the symmetry of the reasoning situation alone makes "if I defect, then she will too" the relevant conditional, even though the two players are causally independent.

The audience is a mixed department seminar: mostly game theorists and microeconomists who treat Nash equilibrium and strict dominance as the default standards, plus three or four philosophers of decision who are sympathetic to causal decision theory and wary of anything that smells like evidential decision theory. The economists will press on incentive compatibility and implementation — what stops a player deviating, and whether a non-equilibrium recommendation is a recommendation at all. The philosophers will press on whether my view can be stated without collapsing into evidential decision theory or an arbitrary pact.

You are an experienced decision theorist who has run Q&As for both audiences and knows their vocabularies. Think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming the full space of possible objections before you select the most important ones.

Then give me a prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get from online rationalists or committed Hofstadterians — ordered by a mix of how likely and how damaging each is, and phrased the way these economists and philosophers would actually put them. For each, give a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft and what they could press next. Include the question I should be hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is dangerous. Finish with the one concession I should make up front to keep the room with me.

My argument, in compressed form, is this. (1) Since the two players reason from identical premises through the same sound procedure, they reach identical conclusions; so the only outcomes consistent with their shared rationality are the diagonal outcomes, both cooperate or both defect. (2) Between those two, both cooperate is strictly better for each than both defect. (3) Therefore each should cooperate; the apparent advantage of defecting rests on an incoherent counterfactual in which one player defects while the other, reasoning identically from the same premises, does not. (4) This is a claim about what each should choose, not a claim about causal influence, enforcement, or a mechanism that makes defection impossible. Target the objections at that argument.
