I'm taking a seminar that keeps circling around standing to blame, and I've realized I don't actually know what I should be asking of a theory of it. So before I read another paper, I want the desiderata: what any adequate account of who has the standing to blame whom has to deliver. I mean ordinary interpersonal moral blame, not legal punishment or institutional censure. Give me the cases it has to get right — the unrepentant hypocrite who blames someone for exactly what he does himself, the person who did the same thing decades ago and now blames sincerely, the uninvolved third party with no stake in the wrong, the person complicit in a different but related way, the friend of the victim versus a total stranger — and the distinctions it can't collapse, especially standing versus whether the blame is true or deserved versus whether expressing it would be good or useful. Also include the ordinary-practice data it can't just explain away: that we do pull people up for being in no position to blame, but we usually don't treat that as showing their criticism was false.

For each requirement, say what case or consideration forces it, and mark wich are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of a particular camp (for example, that standing must be grounded in a relationship, or that hypocrisy always destroys standing no matter how sincere the blamer now is). Then the part I most want: tell me whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — I half-suspect there is no one thing called standing that can meet all of them, and if so the list itself should show that. Finally, very briefly, run the main moves against the list: the hypocrisy-based view, the relationship-based view, and the eliminativist view that standing isn't a real normative category at all. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria are the deliverable.
