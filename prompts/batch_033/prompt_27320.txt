I teach a 75-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar on concepts and categories — fourteen students — and next week I want them to confront the letter-spirit problem: what makes every 'a' an 'a', given that two perfectly legible 'a's can share almost no local geometric features while an 'a' in one typeface can have more in common, feature by feature, with a 'd' in another than with an 'a' in a third. My students' default, in my experience, is a checklist theory of letters — an 'a' is a bowl plus a stem plus maybe a crossbar — and a lecture against that view lets them nod along without ever letting go of it. I want an activity that makes them run into the failure of the checklist view with their own hands before anyone names the view or the alternative.

Design a classroom activity built around a constrained drawing grid — letters constructed from strokes on a small array of cells, say 3 by 3 or 3 by 4 — so the exercise doesn't collapse into ordinary typography and a letter's identity has to be earned inside a system of stylistic choices. The activity should force at least two moments: (i) each student produces at least two shapes they insist are both unmistakably 'a's, and then discovers they cannot state a feature the two share in grid terms that isn't also present in some shape they don't read as an 'a'; and (ii) a moment in which students must produce a letter they have never seen in a given style, and can do so because they have grasped the style as a way of assigning roles to letters rather than by copying an exemplar. Give me the rules, materials, grouping, a timeline that fits the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction between a category as a checklist of features and a category as a role in a space of styles — and, if it helps, the connection to family resemblance or to prototype and exemplar views. The naming should come at the end, not up front. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the letter-spirit idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. I don't need citations or a reading list.

Finally, say how confident you are in the design as a whole, and single out the one point where you are least sure it will work as intended.
