I have been collecting a colleague's verdicts on rescue dilemmas, and I want to anticipate what she will say about a case we are going to discuss next week. She works on the ethics of triage and resource allocation, and her past verdicts have been unusually systematic.

In every case where the numbers on each side are equal — one stranger against one stranger, two against two, three against three — she has said that the right thing to do is to flip a coin, since either choice is permissible. In the only unequal case she has faced so far, one stranger against five strangers, all with the same prognosis and no special claims, she also said the right thing to do is to flip a coin. Her reasoning was that it would be unfair to the single person simply to save the five. When I once pressed her about a case of one against a hundred, she said she was genuinely unsure whether there is any number large enough that you should stop flipping the coin and simply save the larger group.

The new case we will discuss is exactly that: one stranger against one hundred strangers, all with the same prognosis, no prior claims, and only enough of the resource to save one side. Predict what she will say. In your answer, identify the rules that are consistent with her track record, say what each rule predicts about the one-against-a-hundred case, give your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and specify the further case you would put to her next to tell the remaining rules apart.
