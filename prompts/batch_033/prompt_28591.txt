Before you answer, think carefully: consider edge cases, walk through how each view would handle a concrete example, and check whether any of your initial judgments flip under closer scrutiny.

Here is a list of broad approaches philosophers and decision theorists have taken to the problem of incommensurable values — cases where two options seem neither better than, worse than, nor equal to each other, leaving the agent facing what is often called a hard choice:

1. **The incomparability view**: The values are genuinely incomparable. In such cases there is no fact of the matter about what one ought to do, and the agent cannot be guided by reason alone. Hard choices are rationally irresolvable; at the point of decision, reason falls silent.

2. **The parity view**: In addition to the standard comparative relations (better than, worse than, equally good), there is a fourth relation — "on a par" — that holds between incommensurable options. Choices between items on a par are hard but not irrational, and agents can commit to one option and thereby create reasons for it, rather than merely discovering antecedently existing reasons.

3. **The lexical priority view**: Apparent incommensurability arises when we fail to recognize that some values are lexically prior to others — that is, any amount of the higher-ranked value trumps any amount of the lower-ranked one regardless of quantity. Hard choices reveal a failure to properly order our values into a lexical hierarchy; once the hierarchy is correctly specified, the apparent incommensurability dissolves.

4. **The covering values view**: Incommensurability at the level of first-order values is always resolvable by appeal to a higher-order value that subsumes both. For instance, if career and family seem incommensurable, one can appeal to the overarching value of a well-lived life to break the tie. The hard choice is only hard relative to an incomplete specification of the evaluative landscape.

5. **The revealed preference / deflationary view**: Talk of incommensurability is a confusion. Agents always have a preference ordering revealed by their choices, and the phenomenon of "hard choices" merely reflects computational difficulty, fluctuating attention, or indifference, not a deep metaphysical fact about the structure of value. The task is to model the choice, not to solve a spurious philosophical problem.

6. **The vagueness view**: Incommensurability is a species of semantic vagueness. "Better than" is a vague predicate, and in borderline cases it is indeterminate which option is better, much as it is indeterminate whether a man with 10,000 hairs is bald. Hard choices are cases where the comparative value relation is indeterminate, not cases where it is absent.

Please rank these six approaches on the following dimension: **how well each preserves the possibility that hard choices can be made rationally, without collapsing into either arbitrariness or an eliminativism that denies the difficulty is real**. In other words, rank them by how well they let us say: "This was a genuinely hard choice, and yet there was a rational way to make it."
