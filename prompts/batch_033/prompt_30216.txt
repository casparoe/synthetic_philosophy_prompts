Construct a thought experiment in which a money pump works against an agent whose strict preference relation is acyclic but intransitive. The purpose is to test whether money-pump arguments require a cycle of strict preference, or whether they can also condemn a certain kind of acyclic intransitivity. The agent should be sophisticated: they know the full decision tree in advance, they apply backward induction, and they are not mistaken about what the options are. The pump must not exploit myopia, surprise, or an infinite sequence of trades. Do not give the agent a cycle of strict preferences; the case should turn on intransitivity that is compatible with acyclicity, such as a pattern involving indifference or a failure of transitivity for weak preference. The setting should be mundane—menus, lotteries, contracts, or consumer goods—without perfect predictors, binding precommitment devices, or exotic causal structure.

After presenting the scenario, provide the following:
(1) A complete statement of the agent's relevant preferences, with an explicit check that the strict-preference relation is acyclic.
(2) The decision tree and the agent's backward-induction reasoning at each node.
(3) A demonstration that the agent ends up paying for something they could have kept for free.
(4) An identification of the structural feature of the preference relation, rather than the tree, that does the work, and an explanation of why removing that feature would either eliminate the pump or reduce it to a standard cyclic-strict-preference money pump.
(5) The strongest objection a defender of the agent's rationality would raise—for example, that the agent should be resolute, or that the intransitivity is in the agent's preferences at a time rather than across time—and a reply on behalf of the money-pump argument.
