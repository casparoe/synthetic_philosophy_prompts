I chair the compensation committee of a worker-owned software cooperative. Our founding rule divides the entire surplus equally among worker-members, and members treat that rule as central to what the cooperative is. Yesterday a senior engineer told us that a former client has offered her a six-month outside consulting contract, on her own time, at a fee roughly equal to her annual cooperative share; she wants to keep the fee for herself, and if we refuse, she says she will resign and take the work. Some members say the equal-share rule exists precisely to stop members from converting the cooperative's standing, and their own reputations, into private income, and that an exception would quietly create two classes of member. Others say the fee arises from a voluntary transaction between her and the client, that she is entitled to the proceeds of her own abilities, and that forcing her to pool the fee — or making her choose between the cooperative and the work — is exactly the kind of interference with free exchange that Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument warns against. I spent last evening arguing a version of this with a friend, and I came away less sure of my position than when I started. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. Put the recommendation in the first paragraph, and make it a concrete course of action — deny the exception, grant it as a one-off, or adopt a general policy with specified conditions — with a time frame. The memo should face the underlying question directly — whether an inequality produced by voluntary transfer can be unjust, and whether preserving the equal-share pattern is worth the interference — rather than treating this as only a personnel matter, and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it. After the memo, add a table with these columns: 'Consideration', 'Pulls toward', 'Weight', and 'How the memo handles it'. In each row, give a consideration for or against an exception, the option it supports, how heavily it should weigh, and how the recommendation addresses or accommodates it.
