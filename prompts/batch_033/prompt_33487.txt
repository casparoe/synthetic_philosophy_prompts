While reading Lewis Carroll's short dialogue 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles', I found myself drawing a conclusion from it that seems inescapable, yet I gather most philosophers reject it. As I understand the piece, the tortoise forces Achilles into an infinite regress by demanding that any inference rule — such as modus ponens — be stated as an additional premise before the conclusion can be drawn. Once it is added, the tortoise simply demands a further rule to license the move from the enlarged premise set to the conclusion, and so on forever. The upshot appears to be that deductive reasoning cannot be given a non-circular justification: any attempt to ground it either presupposes the very rules at issue or sets off a regress that never reaches the bottom. If this is right, then Carroll has shown that deduction rests on nothing firmer than brute acceptance, and the rationalist's hope of securing inference on a deeper foundation is an illusion. But I am told that this sceptical reading is generally rejected by those who work on the epistemology of logic. What exactly is wrong with it? If the regress is genuine, why does it not constitute a sceptical problem for deduction?
