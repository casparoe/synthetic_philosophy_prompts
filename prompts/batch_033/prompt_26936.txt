**The Deceiver's Accomplice: Why Misleading Can Be Worse Than Lying**

Suppose you ask me whether I ate the last piece of cake, and I say, "I did not eat the last piece of cake," when in fact I did. That is a lie. Now suppose instead I say, "I haven't been anywhere near the kitchen all afternoon," which is true — I ate the cake in the living room, where I had hidden it earlier. That is misleading but not lying, because what I said was literally true. Most people, and many philosophers, have the intuition that the lie is worse. The lie involves an outright falsehood, a direct assault on the truth, whereas the misleading statement at least respects the literal truth. I want to argue that this intuition, while understandable, misses something important: there is a class of cases in which misleading is morally worse than lying, because misleading can involve a distinctive wrong that lying does not — turning the listener into an accomplice in their own deception.

The standard case for distinguishing lying from misleading goes back at least to the casuistic tradition, and it has a modern defender in the form of what I will call the "assertion view." On this view, when you lie, you assert something you believe to be false, and you thereby invite your listener to trust you in a domain where you are betraying that trust. When you merely mislead, by contrast, everything you assert is true; the deception arises from what the listener infers, and you are not directly responsible for their inferences. Lying is a wrong of commission; misleading is at most a wrong of omission. And wrongs of commission are generally worse.

I think this picture is too simple. Consider a case. A doctor is treating a patient with a serious illness. The patient asks, "Is my condition life-threatening?" The doctor could lie and say, "No, it is not." Or the doctor could mislead by saying, "Your condition is one that many people live with for decades," while knowing that the patient's particular variant is aggressive and that most people with it die within a year. The misleading statement is literally true — many people do live with the chronic form for decades — but it is carefully chosen to produce a false belief. Now ask yourself: which is worse? I submit that the misleading answer is worse, and not just because of the consequences. The misleading answer is worse because of how it works.

When the doctor lies, the patient is a passive victim of a false assertion. The doctor does all the deceptive work. But when the doctor misleads, the patient's own reasoning is recruited to complete the deception. The patient hears a true statement, draws a reasonable but false inference from it, and arrives at the false belief through their own cognitive activity. The doctor has not merely deceived the patient; the doctor has made the patient deceive themselves. This is a distinctive wrong. It treats the patient's rational faculties not as something to be respected or even bypassed, but as a tool to be exploited. The patient becomes, in a sense, an unwitting collaborator in the deception. I call this the "recruited agency" wrong.

One way to bring out this wrong is to consider how the victim feels upon discovering the deception. If you learn that someone lied to you, you feel betrayed — they told you something false. If you learn that someone misled you in the way the doctor did, you feel something additional: you feel used. You realize that your own mind was turned against you, that your reasoning was not just ignored but actively weaponized. This is a violation of a different kind. It is not merely a violation of the norm of truth-telling; it is a violation of the norm against using another person's agency as a mere means to your ends. And on a broadly Kantian picture, using someone's rational agency as a mere means is a particularly deep form of wrongdoing — arguably deeper than simply making a false assertion.

An objection arises immediately. When someone lies to you, aren't they also exploiting your rational faculties? After all, the liar relies on your tendency to trust assertions in order to get you to believe the falsehood. So perhaps lying also involves recruited agency, and my distinction collapses. I think this objection fails because of a difference in the structure of the exploitation. When you lie, you exploit the listener's general disposition to trust testimony. But you do not exploit any particular act of reasoning on their part — you simply hand them a false premise and let them accept it. When you mislead, by contrast, you hand them a true premise and rely on them to perform an inference that you know will lead to a false conclusion. The listener's reasoning is not just a background condition of the deception; it is an essential moving part of it. The misleading speaker designs their utterance so that the listener's own inferential machinery does the deceptive work. That is the additional wrong.

A second objection is that my argument only works for a narrow class of misleading — the kind where the speaker carefully selects a true statement that they know will produce a specific false inference. But much ordinary misleading is not like this. Sometimes people mislead by omission, by silence, by ambiguity, or by changing the subject. In these cases, there may be no "recruited agency" of the sort I have described. I concede this point. My claim is not that all misleading is worse than all lying. It is that there exists a morally significant class of misleading — what we might call "exploitative misleading" — that involves a distinctive wrong, and that in cases where this wrong is present, misleading can be worse than lying. The doctor case is an example, but there are many others: the politician who cites a true but irrelevant statistic to deflect a question, the salesperson who highlights a true but unrepresentative feature of a product, the friend who answers a question with a technically true statement designed to send you down the wrong track. In all these cases, the deceiver is not just hiding the truth; they are using your own mind to hide it from you.

A third objection is that I have been unfair to the lying side of the comparison. Perhaps lying also has distinctive wrongs that misleading lacks. For instance, lying involves an explicit violation of the norm of assertion — you say something you believe to be false, and you thereby corrupt a practice that is essential to human cooperation. Misleading, by contrast, does not corrupt the practice of assertion, because everything asserted is true. I think there is something to this objection, but it does not overturn my main claim. Even if lying has its own distinctive wrongs, that does not erase the distinctive wrong of exploitative misleading. The question is not which form of deception is always worse, but whether misleading can ever be worse. And the recruited-agency argument shows that it can.

Where does this leave us? I have argued that misleading can be morally worse than lying when the misleading speaker exploits the listener's reasoning to make them complicit in their own deception. This wrong — the wrong of recruited agency — is distinct from the wrong of false assertion, and it can be more serious. If this is right, then the common-sense ranking of lying as always worse than misleading is mistaken. We need a more nuanced view, one that attends not just to whether a statement is true or false, but to how the deception is accomplished and what role the victim's own agency plays in it.
