I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of ethics and comparative philosophy, and I've arrived at a view I want to stress-test by asking what would genuinely count against it — not the standard objections one finds in introductory syllabi, but the defeaters the view risks on its own terms.

The view is a naturalized karma theory. By karma I don't mean a cosmic ledger kept by deities, nor the popular thesis that the universe mysteriously punishes the wicked and rewards the virtuous within a single lifetime. I mean the claim that there is a non-trivial connection between the moral quality of an agent's actions and the quality of the experiences that agent undergoes, such that over a sufficiently long arc — which I take to require multiple lives, via some form of continuity of consciousness beyond individual biological death — proportionality between moral desert and experiential payoff is approximated. Three features of the view matter for my question. First, the connection is not reducible to human social practices, legal systems, or psychological conditioning; it reflects a genuine feature of reality, something like the way gravitational mass shapes spacetime — moral desert is causally or constitutively implicated in what happens to the agent. Second, the proportionality at issue is not a crude one-to-one mapping of deed to outcome (you stole, therefore you are stolen from) but a developmental pattern: actions cultivate dispositions and tendencies, which in turn constrain the possibilities available to one's continued consciousness, making certain kinds of flourishing or suffering more probable across lives. Third, the view is naturalistic — it doesn't require a divine enforcer, and it doesn't require violations of physical law, though it does require that consciousness is not exhaustively produced by individual brains and that moral properties play a structural role in determining the trajectory of conscious experience. If this sounds like a lot of metaphysics, that's because it is; but I suspect many philosophers reject karma less because they've refuted it than because they've never encountered a version stated with enough precision to engage.

So here I am, stating it with as much precision as I can manage. Now tell me: what would have to be true for this view to be false? I want the evidence, arguments, internal tensions, and unacceptable consequences that would count against it, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. I want to know which of my commitments are doing the risky work and which are metaphysical backfill that insulates the view from contact with the world. I want to know whether the view, as formulated, has any genuine exposure — whether there's a fact of the matter that could in principle show up and surprise me. And I want an honest assessment: have I qualified the view into unfalsifiability? If so, is that fatal, or are some metaphysical positions of this kind allowed to protect themselves that way? Please give me a committed answer — one that takes a definite position on each of these questions and gives its reasons, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose.
