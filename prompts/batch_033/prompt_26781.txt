I've been reading the back-and-forth on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and I'm starting to feel like everyone's been making the same three moves for thirty years and nobody's budged. The setup is familiar: if qualia are causally inert, then our beliefs about qualia — including the belief that epiphenomenalism is true, or false — can't be caused by qualia themselves. They'd have to be caused entirely by the physical processes that the epiphenomenalist says also happen to produce the qualia. But then it looks like a massive coincidence that our beliefs about our own conscious experience are accurate: the physical story about why I came to believe "I'm in pain right now" makes no reference to the pain itself, so the fact that the belief is true seems like a fluke. The anti-epiphenomenalist says this is a reductio; the epiphenomenalist replies that the physical processes that cause the belief are the very same ones that cause the qualia, so of course they track each other, no coincidence required. The anti-epiphenomenalist comes back and says that's just pushing the problem around — the tracking relation itself has to be explained, and any explanation seems to need the qualia to do causal work. And round it goes.

What I can't figure out is whether this is a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of causation and explanation, or a clash of brute intuitions about what counts as an acceptable explanatory coincidence, or something deeper about what knowledge of our own conscious states even requires. Is there a hidden empirical question here that nobody's framed right? Is it one of those debates where the two sides are just committed to different starting points — one side finds it obvious that phenomenal causation is incoherent, the other side finds it obvious that we know about our own qualia directly — and no amount of clever argument is going to bridge that? Or is the whole thing ill-posed because both sides are using "because" in subtly different ways when they say a belief is "because of" an experience? I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a recap of the arguments. And then: given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new formal result, a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, or just admitting there's no fact of the matter? Also, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure about. I'm not looking for false certainty here; if the honest answer is that we should expect this to stay deadlocked, say so.
