Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading my way into philosophy, and I recently encountered the so-called “hinge of history” hypothesis in a popular book. The book summarizes the view roughly as follows. The hinge of history hypothesis holds that we may be living through a period in which the long-run future is unusually malleable. The background thought is that the future could contain vastly more lives than have yet existed, and that technologies such as advanced artificial intelligence may soon allow a comparatively small number of decisions to determine which of many possible long-run trajectories humanity enters; as a result, the choices made in the present generation could have much greater leverage over the long term than the choices of almost any other generation. According to the book, if the hypothesis is correct, the natural practical conclusion is that reducing the risk of human extinction should dominate all other priorities, since extinction is the only thing that could foreclose the vast future. The book also notes that the hypothesis is sometimes dismissed as unfalsifiable, and reports that its defenders regard this as a misunderstanding: the hypothesis, they say, is not a prediction about any particular event but a claim about how much leverage we have, and leverage can be estimated.

I find the descriptive core plausible, but I suspect the move from the claim about leverage to the claim about what should dominate our priorities may be too quick, and I am not sure the summary has kept the technical notions straight. I would be grateful if you would go through this characterization and tell me which parts are fair to the view, which parts a careful defender would object to, and whether the practical conclusion about extinction risk actually follows from the accurate parts.
