I keep tripping over the same puzzle in my environmental philosophy course, and I'd like you to work through it with me. Take how endangered species law works in the United States. The Endangered Species Act protects "species," but the definition is stretched so that agencies can also list subspecies and, for vertebrates, distinct population segments of a single species. So the legal unit of protection often isn't what a biologist would call a species at all. Meanwhile, the same agencies spend real money keeping some populations from hybridizing — coyote control around red wolves, that kind of thing — as if a species has natural boundaries that management has to police. But the biologists I read cannot agree on what a species even is: a group of actually or potentially interbreeding populations, the smallest monophyletic group on a tree, a cluster sharing an ecological niche, a bunch of organisms that just look alike. And when taxonomists split one species into two, the legal status of both halfs can flip overnight.

So here's what I want. Reconstruct the theory of species that this conservation practice presupposes. Does it really presuppose that species are real, mind-independent units with sharp boundaries, or could the whole practice be run on a more nominalist or pluralist view of species? Is the practice internally coherent — protecting populations below the species level while also policing hybrids — or does it quietly presuppose two different species concepts in different places? And if we swapped in a thoroughgoing pluralism about species concepts, what would actually have to change in the law and in the management practices? Don't worry about citations; I want the implicit metaphysics dragged into the open.
