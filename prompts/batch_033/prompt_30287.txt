I'm trying to work out what I actually think about our emotional responses to fiction, going by my gut on particular cases. When I watch a horror film, I feel what I want to call genuine fear at the monster: my pulse races and I flinch, even though I don't for a moment believe the monster exists. When a character I have followed for hundreds of pages dies, I feel a sadness that also seems genuine rather than merely pretended. But a jump scare in a video game just startles me; I don't take that to be fear of anything at all. And if I imagine in vivid detail that my partner has died in a car crash, I feel a wave of something very like grief, though I know it hasn't happened. Two further observations pull in opposite directions. The horror-film fear feels real, yet it carries none of the action tendencies of real fear — I don't flee the theater or call for help. And I'm fairly sure I have felt more grief for a fictional character than for a real stranger whose death I read about in the news, which makes me doubt that the two are the same kind of state. Is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts? What does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: feeling relief when the endangered protagonist narrowly escapes, even though I knew all along that the plot would not kill them? And which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to give up?
