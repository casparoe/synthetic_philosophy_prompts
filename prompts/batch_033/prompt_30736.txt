I am a retired research scientist — I spent most of my career in a laboratory — and I have only recently begun to read philosophy in a disciplined way. Lately I have been working through recent debates in population ethics, and the position that has most unsettled me is the procreation asymmetry associated with David Benatar's anti-natalism. As I understand it, the asymmetry says this: the presence of pain in a life is bad, and the presence of pleasure in a life is good; but when we consider a person who never comes into existence, the absence of that pain is good, while the absence of that pleasure is not bad, because there is no one who is deprived of it. The asymmetry is then used to argue that coming into existence is always a harm, or at least that there is strong reason not to bring new people into being.

I have also been rereading Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, and it strikes me that the novel is, among other things, a sustained thought experiment about a being who is deliberately brought into existence, then suffers terribly and curses his own existence. I would like you to read the novel through the lens of the procreation asymmetry. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on population ethics and is also a careful reader of fiction, and take your time: begin by laying out the structure of the asymmetry precisely, then brainstorm the scenes and details that bear on it before committing yourself to an interpretation.

Specifically, I would like you to address the following. First, can the creature's complaint be stated in terms of the asymmetry, or does it rest on a different and weaker claim — that his life contains more pain than pleasure, or that Victor wronged him by abandoning him rather than by creating him? In particular, keep apart two claims that are often run together: that a life is not worth continuing, and that a life was not worth starting. The asymmetry is usually meant to support the second kind of claim even for lives that are, by ordinary standards, worth continuing. Does the creature ever make the second kind of claim, or only the first? Second, does the asymmetry help make sense of Victor's two decisions — to create the first being, and then to destroy the mate he half-builds? Is there a consistent position available to him, or does the novel expose a tension in applying the asymmetry to particular acts of creation? Third, where does the novel resist the anti-natalist reading? The creature's suffering is plausibly caused by Victor's neglect and by human cruelty rather than by existence itself; if so, does the novel end up being a case against a certain kind of creator rather than against creation as such? And finally, what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not — does Shelley dramatize something about the standpoint of the created being that the axiology of possible persons misses?

I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of the asymmetry, and do not distort the asymmetry to fit the novel. If the honest conclusion is that the novel only partially fits the lens, or that it ultimately rejects the anti-natalist reading, say so plainly.
