I'm the arts editor at a monthly magazine — we run about eight reviews per issue across books, film, visual art, theater, music. Small staff, lots of freelancers. Here's the thing: I can't possibly read every book or see every show we review. I have to trust my critics. But I took a philosophy of art course back in grad school and I remember there's this whole debate about aesthetic testimony — whether you can legitimately form aesthetic judgments based on what someone else tells you. The pessimists say no, you basically have to experience it yourself to have a genuine aesthetic belief; the optimists say testimony works fine for aesthetics like it does for anything else.

I need a decision procedure I can actually use at my desk. When I'm editing a review, when can I trust the critic's verdict without having consumed the work myself? When do I need to go see the damn thing before we publish? And when is the right move to get a second critic rather than either trusting or checking?

The procedure has to be something I can run in about fifteen minutes per review. It should flag where the pessimist is clearly right and I'm fooling myself if I think I can judge from the critic's writeup alone. It should also flag where the optimist is right and I'm being precious — where testimony is perfectly adequate for the editorial decision I'm actually making. And be honest about where the philosophy doesn't settle it and I'm just making a call.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a review of a new novel by a big-name author where the critic loved it but I've seen mixed chatter online, (2) a review of an experimental theater piece where the critic hated it but their description makes it sound genuinely interesting to me, and (3) a review of a museum retrospective where the critic is positive but the review is mostly biographical context and barely describes the work itself.
