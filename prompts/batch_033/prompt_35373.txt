I'm in a reading group that has been working through the decision-theoretic literature on evidential cooperation in large worlds — roughly, the idea, associated with Caspar Oesterheld and others, that in a world too complex to enumerate all possible states, your choice of action gives you evidence about what other agents relevantly similar to you will do, and this evidential link can ground a kind of acausal cooperation that causal decision theory misses. I thought I understood the appeal of the view until I tried to work out what it actually recommends in a concrete case, and I have ended up with an argument that seems to show the framework is either empty or arbitrary. I cannot find the flaw, and I would like to know which premise to reject and what it costs.

Here is the argument:

(1) In any decision problem in a large world, the expected utility of an action A according to evidential decision theory depends on the conditional probability of various outcomes given that you choose A.
(2) To compute this conditional probability, you need to specify a reference class: the set of agents or decision-situations that you take yourself to be evidentially linked to — that is, the agents whose choices your choice gives you information about.
(3) For any action A, there are many possible reference classes. A narrow reference class (agents in exactly your situation) makes your choice uninformative about others, so the evidential cooperation term vanishes. A broad reference class (all agents who ever face a structurally similar choice) makes your choice highly informative, so the evidential cooperation term dominates.
(4) The choice of reference class is not fixed by the decision problem itself, nor by EDT, nor by any obvious normative principle. It seems to be a free parameter.
(5) But if the reference class is a free parameter, then for any pair of actions A and B, you can choose a reference class that makes A come out ahead and another that makes B come out ahead. EDT in large worlds therefore gives no determinate guidance.
(6) The proponent of ECL might reply that the correct reference class is the one that an ideal Bayesian reasoner would use — but an ideal Bayesian reasoner is itself defined relative to a prior over possible worlds, and the choice of prior embeds the same arbitrariness at one level up.
(7) So either ECL is empty (it gives no determinate verdicts), or it implicitly relies on causal or metaphysical constraints on reference classes that it cannot state in purely evidential terms — which would mean the view collapses into causal decision theory after all.
(8) Since the whole point of ECL was to show that evidential decision theory captures something CDT misses, and the argument suggests it either says nothing or says what CDT already says, the ECL project seems to undermine itself.

I suspect the problem is in (3) or (4) — perhaps there is a principled way to fix reference classes that I am missing — but every proposal I can think of either imports causal considerations or appeals to intuitions about similarity that just push the problem back a step. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to?
