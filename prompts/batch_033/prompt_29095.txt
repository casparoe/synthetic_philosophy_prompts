I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on Buddhist philosophy alongside my major in psychology. I also work as a trained peer support volunteer at our university's mental health service, where I do 45-minute one-on-one sessions with students who are struggling but don't necessarily meet the threshold for clinical intervention. A pattern I keep seeing: students frame their distress in the language of selfhood. They say things like "I don't know who I am anymore" after a breakup, "I need to find my true self" when they're choosing a major, "I feel like I'm not the same person I was before the depression," or "I've lost myself in this relationship."

In my Buddhist philosophy course, we have been working through the anatta (no-self) doctrine — the view that what we call a "self" is a conventional label for a changing bundle of the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness), that there is no permanent, independent self-entity underlying them, and that clinging to the idea of such a self is a central source of suffering. We read the chariot analogy in the Milindapañha, where the monk Nagasena argues that "chariot" is merely a convenient designation for the assembled parts, not a thing over and above them, and that the same holds for the person. We have also discussed the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth — the idea that it is perfectly fine to say "I went to the store" in everyday life, but that trouble begins when we take that "I" to refer to something substantial whose integrity we must then protect at all costs.

I suspect some of my peers are trapped not just by their circumstances but by the very concept of a "true self" they are trying to locate, protect, or recover — a concept that, on the anatta analysis, does not refer to anything and sets them up to fail. But I am not a therapist, I am not a Buddhist teacher, and I am not trying to convert anyone. I want a practical conversational procedure I can use when a student's distress seems scaffolded by a problematic picture of selfhood, one that stays entirely within my role as a peer supporter.

Please design me a reusable decision procedure — a flowchart in words, with ordered questions and branching paths — that does the following:

1. Helps me decide, early in a session, whether this student's distress is of the kind that might be loosened by gently questioning the self-concept they are invoking, or whether I should steer clear (for example, because the student is in acute crisis, because their self-framing is functional and not the source of the trouble, or because the self-language is merely peripheral to the real problem).

2. If the procedure says "go ahead," it should give me a sequence of conversational moves — not arguments, but Socratic questions and gentle reframings — that help the student notice the contingency and constructedness of the self-concept they are gripping, without ever saying "there is no self" outright and without introducing Buddhist terminology. The moves should operationalize the core insight of the anatta analysis (that the self is a label for a process, not a thing) while being fully intelligible to someone who has never heard of Buddhism.

3. The procedure should include stopping rules: specific signals that mean I should back off, refer the student to a professional therapist, or simply sit with their distress without attempting to dissolve it conceptually.

4. It should flag every step where my own judgment — about the student's emotional state, about what they are ready to hear, about how attached they are to the self-concept in question — is doing irreducible work that no procedure can replace.

Then, run the procedure on two realistic cases:

- Case A: A student who says, "I was the ambitious, high-achieving one in my family, but after failing two courses this semester I don't know who I am anymore. Everything I thought I was has collapsed."

- Case B: A student who says, "I have been dating my partner for two years and I love them, but I feel like I have disappeared into the relationship. I need to find myself again before I can decide whether to stay."

For each case, walk through the decision points, say which path the procedure takes me down, what I actually say at each step, and where the procedure produces an answer I might find uncomfortable or surprising.

Finally, tell me honestly what this procedure cannot do: the kinds of distress it will miss, the students for whom it will backfire, and the ways the translation from anatta doctrine to conversational tool might distort the very insight it is trying to carry, especially when wielded by someone who is not a Buddhist practitioner.
