I'm writing up a position in the philosophy of language and would like a consistency check before I circulate the draft. The view is verificationist-flavored anti-realism. I hold: (1) every truth is knowable — for any true proposition p, it is possible that some inquirer at some time knows p; (2) classical logic, including bivalence and classical reductio, is correct; (3) there are determinate truths no one will ever know, for example the exact number of grains of sand on a particular unrecorded stretch of beach at noon on a particular past day, which no one counted and which left no trace; (4) meaning is epistemically constrained — a statement is meaningful only if there are possible conditions under which an inquirer could recognize its truth-value, and to understand a statement is to know what would count as verifying it. I don't take (1) and (3) to conflict, since knowability is a modal status rather than actual knowledge, and the grain-count was knowable in principle at the time even though it was never known. Still, I have a nagging sense that something in this package is off, and I can't locate it. Please audit the set: which commitments are genuinely in tension with which, how severe is each tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what is the cheapest revision in each case? I'd rather you discriminate than flag every pair as equally problematic.
