So the principle of indifference — the way I understand it, it says if you've got a bunch of possibilities and you don't have any reason to think one is more likely than another, you should give them all equal probability. Like if I hand you a six-sided die and you've never seen it before, you assume each face has a 1/6 chance. That's just common sense.

But then I learned about Bertrand's paradox and it's been bugging me for weeks. Here's the setup: you've got a circle, and you inscribe an equilateral triangle inside it. The question is, if you pick a chord of the circle "at random," what's the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle?

Seems like it should have one answer. It doesn't. Depending on how you apply the principle of indifference, you get different numbers.

Method 1: Pick two random points on the circumference and connect them. The chord is longer than the triangle side if the arc between the two points is more than a third of the circle. So probability = 1/3.

Method 2: Pick a random direction from the center, then pick a random point along that radius. Draw the chord perpendicular to the radius at that point. The chord is longer than the triangle side if the point is less than halfway from the center to the edge. So probability = 1/2.

Method 3: Pick a random point anywhere inside the circle and make that the midpoint of the chord. The chord is longer than the triangle side if the point falls inside a smaller circle of half the radius (area is one quarter of the big circle). So probability = 1/4.

All three methods seem like totally reasonable ways to pick a random chord. And they all use the principle of indifference — in method 1 you're indifferent about where the two endpoints land along the circumference, in method 2 you're indifferent about which radius and where along it, in method 3 you're indifferent about where the midpoint falls inside the circle. But they give different answers. So the principle of indifference is basically telling you "the probability is 1/3, also 1/2, also 1/4" for the exact same event. That can't be right.

So here's the argument that's been eating at me:

(1) The principle of indifference is the only thing we have for assigning probabilities when we've got no evidence favoring one outcome over another.
(2) In Bertrand's chord problem, applying the principle of indifference gives 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4, depending on how we describe the set of possible chords.
(3) If a principle gives contradictory answers depending on how you describe the same situation, it's not a reliable principle.
(4) So the principle of indifference is unreliable and we should abandon it.
(5) But if we abandon the principle of indifference, we have no way to assign probabilities in any situation where we lack evidence — like estimating how likely a new disease is to spread, or the chance a coin I've never flipped comes up heads, or basically any everyday probability we use when we're reasoning with incomplete information.
(6) So either all those everyday probability judgments are baseless, or one of the steps above is wrong.
