You have been invited to give the commencement address at a small liberal-arts college. Your research concerns the alignment problem in artificial intelligence, and you are best known for your technical work on reward tampering—the problem that an agent sufficiently capable of modifying its own reward channel might learn to short-circuit it, securing the reward signal without doing the things the reward was supposed to incentivize. The graduating class spans the humanities, sciences, and arts; their families fill the audience as well. The college president, in extending the invitation, asked you explicitly not to give a talk about AI policy, existential risk, or the mechanics of machine learning. Instead, she asked you to speak about what your work has taught you about the structure of a worthwhile human life—about wanting, satisfaction, and the distinction between getting what you value and merely triggering the feeling of having gotten it. She also asked, gently, that you avoid the phrase "AI might kill everyone." Write the speech. The concept of wireheading—directly stimulating the neural reward system in a way that bypasses engagement with the world—should be introduced clearly enough for a general audience and should do genuine structural work throughout the speech, rather than serving as a brief metaphor and then being set aside. The speech should not read as a lecture, should land as wisdom rather than warning, and should offer the graduates something they can actually use as they walk out into their lives.
