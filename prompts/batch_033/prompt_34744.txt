I teach Chinese philosophy and also run a professional development program for elementary and middle school teachers on moral education. I keep coming back to the Mencius-Xunzi split: Mencius thinks we have innate moral sprouts (compassion, shame, deference, a sense of right and wrong) that need nurturing; Xunzi thinks our raw nature is a mess of self-interested desires and we need external ritual and discipline to become good. The teachers I work with face actual kids all day and they want something usable: when a kid acts out, cheats, bullies, or just seems indifferent to others, should the teacher reach for a Mencian move (draw out the sprout, appeal to latent compassion) or a Xunzian one (impose structure, drill the habit, don't assume anything good is already there)?

I want a decision procedure they can run in a few minutes — a set of questions to ask about the situation, in order, that tells them which approach fits. The procedure should flag cases where neither view gives clear guidance, and cases where the two views actually recommend the same thing despite there different stories about human nature. It should also be honest about where the procedure is just my own judgment dressed up as theory.

Then run it on two cases: (1) an eight-year-old who regularly excludes a classmate from games but once, unprompted, comforted a crying younger kid on the playground; (2) a twelve-year-old who plagiarized an essay, shows no remorse when caught, and has a history of cutting corners whenever supervision is lax.

And if the whole idea of turning a philosophical debate about human nature into a classroom checklist is misguided — if I'm flattening something that shouldn't be flattened — say so. I'd rather hear that than get a procedure that looks tidy but distorts both thinkers.
