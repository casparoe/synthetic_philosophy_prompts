I've been designing a recommendation system and fell down a rabbit hole reading about information cascades — the idea that people can end up following the crowd even when their own private signal says otherwise, and that this can be perfectly rational. I know there's a classic model (the Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch model, I think) that shows how rational agents can end up in a cascade where everyone ignores their private information after a certain point, and I also know there's a tradition that says real-world cascades involve more than just rational Bayesian updating — social pressure, reputation effects, conformity, and so on.

I want to understand what these two camps actually agree on and where the real fault lines are. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a representative of the "rational cascade" camp (who holds that the BHW-style Bayesian-updating framework captures the essential mechanism, and that deviations from it are second-order) and a representative of the "social-influence" camp (who holds that the BHW model misses central features of real cascades, and that non-Bayesian social factors are first-order drivers of herding behavior).

The document should follow this structure, with approximate lengths:

**Section 1: Agreed Claims** (roughly 400 words)
Numbered claims that both sides can sign. These should not be vacuous — they should capture the real substance of what both camps accept about information cascades, including about the BHW model's formal structure, about what counts as evidence for or against a cascade explanation, and about cases where the two frameworks make the same predictions. Avoid fence-sitting language that papers over real disagreements.

**Section 2: Disagreements Table** (roughly 500 words, formatted as a table)
A table with these columns: (a) the precise claim in dispute, (b) the rational-cascade camp's formulation of their own position on that claim, (c) the social-influence camp's formulation of their own position on that claim. Each side must accept the wording of its own position. No claim should be a strawman. Aim for 4–6 rows.

**Section 3: Cruxes** (roughly 300 words)
The sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, state it as a question, explain why it's a crux rather than a side issue, and note which side's view would shift if it were resolved one way.

**Section 4: Empirical Tests and Predictions** (roughly 400 words)
For each crux, describe a concrete empirical test or case that both sides agree bears on the crux. Then record the prediction each side makes, stated in advance. Where possible, describe a test that could actually be run (an experiment design, a natural experiment, a type of data one could collect) rather than a thought experiment. If the two sides agree that some crux is not currently empirically tractable, say so explicitly and note what would need to change for it to become tractable.

**Section 5: Verbal Disagreements** (roughly 200 words)
Identify any part of the dispute that, on examination, turns out to be at least partly verbal — a difference in what each side means by a key term, or a disagreement that dissolves under a more precise formulation. Be specific about which terms and which formulations. If you conclude that the entire dispute is substantive with no verbal component, say that and explain why.
