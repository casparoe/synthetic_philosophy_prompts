Hey — I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on philosophy of computation, and I want a decision tree that sorts people into positions on what computational complexity actually shows about rational agents and mathematical knowledge. The entry question should be something like: does the fact that some inferences are computationally intractable matter for epistemology, or is it just an engineering detail about our machines? Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: lead with the branch questions, and only attach labels to the leaves afterwards.

The angle I care about has two fault lines. First, wether the feasible/infeasible line (polynomial time vs exponential, say) marks a real joint or is just an artifact of worst-case asymptotics and our choice of machine model. Second, whether ideal rationality survives the fact that computing the logical consequences of a set of beliefs is coNP-complete already for propositional logic, and undecidable once you move to first-order logic. I'd also like a third split about proof and surveyability: whether a theorem whose only proofs are astronomically long can be known at all.

At minimum the leaves should include something like: (a) a complexity-sensitive epistemology, where tractability constrains what even an ideal-but-finite agent can know or rationally believe; (b) an idealization-defender view, where logical omniscience is a regulative ideal and doesn't need to be attainable; (c) a surveyability or finitist view, where only feasibly checkable proofs ground knowledge; (d) a deflationary view, where P vs NP is philosophically shallow and the whole debate is an artifact of worst-case analysis; (e) a finding/checking realist view, where the discovery/verification asymmetry is deep but doesn't license strong epistemological conclusions. If a natural leaf has no actual defenders, say so and call it unnamed.

The diagnostic questions are the whole point, so phrase them so someone can answer without knowing the labels. Please include at least these cases as branch nodes: (1) a theorem whose shortest proof in a standard system is longer than any human could check in a lifetime, but which follows from a short metatheoretic argument — does the person know it? (2) a Bayesian agent who assigns probability 1 to some axioms but can't feasibly compute their consequences — is the agent irrational, or just bounded? (3) an O(n^100) polynomial-time algorithm next to an O(2^(n/100)) exponential-time one, where the exponential algorithm is faster for every input size up to about 10^5 (assume the hidden constants are negligible) — does that show polynomial time is the wrong notion of feasibility? (4) a conditional: if someone handed us a polynomial-time algorithm for SAT, would finding a proof be no more impressive than checking one? Each question should split at least two of the leaves.

Also flag any pairs of leaves your tree can't actually seperate, and mark unoccupied combinations in the logical space with a sentence on whether each empty cell is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Aim for maybe seven branch questions and eight or nine leaves, written for people who know what P, NP, and NP-completeness are but haven't read the philosophy. No citations or reading list — I just want the tree and a short justification of each split.
