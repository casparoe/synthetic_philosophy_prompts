**Case A (the original absent-minded driver).** Driver on a highway, two exits. Exit 1 leads to a bad area (payoff 0). Exit 2 is home (payoff 4). Miss both and he ends up at a motel (payoff 1). Before setting out he can adopt a plan: "at whatever exit I find myself, I'll exit with probability p." My verdict: p = 2/3 is the right plan, and once he's on the road he should stick to it. The temptation to think "hang on, if this is exit 1 then exiting gives 0 while continuing might get me to exit 2, and if this is exit 2 then exiting gives 4 while continuing gives 1 — either way continuing looks better" is a reasoning mistake. He should follow the plan.

**Case B (three exits).** Same deal but three exits: Exit 1 gives 0, Exit 2 gives 0, Exit 3 gives 10, missing all gives 1. My verdict: I still think the ex ante plan is binding, but I feel much less confident about what the optimal plan even is, and that uncertainty makes me queasy about the whole "just follow the plan" stance in a way that Case A doesn't.

**Case C (the note).** Same as Case A, but before leaving the driver writes himself a note that he'll see at each exit: "You're at an exit. Stick to the plan: p = 2/3." My verdict: philosophically this shouldn't change anything — the note conveys no new information — but somehow it makes me feel way better about following the plan. That bothers me. **Case D (small exit cost).** Same as Case A, but exiting has a small toll: Exit 1 now gives -0.5, Exit 2 gives 3.5, missing both still gives 1. **Case E (a clue).** Same as Case A, but at each exit there's a sign that says either "Exit 1" or "Exit 2." The sign is 60% reliable (independent at each exit), and the driver knows this. **Case F (forgot the plan).** Same as Case A, but the driver genuinely forgets what plan he adopted. He knows he adopted some plan, just not which one.
