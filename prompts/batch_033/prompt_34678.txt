In the socialist calculation debate, I keep encountering a particular analogy: the economy just is an optimization problem — allocate scarce resources to maximize welfare subject to production constraints — and the market is simply one algorithm for solving it. A central planning board, given sufficient data and computational power, could solve the same problem by functioning as a Walrasian auctioneer: announce provisional prices, observe where surpluses and shortages emerge, adjust prices accordingly, and iterate until supply and demand clear across all goods. On this picture, the Mises–Hayek critique reduces to a claim about computational tractability, one that modern computing has arguably overtaken. I want an audit of how far this analogy holds. Give me exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) what in an actual economy corresponds to what in the optimization-algorithm picture, (2) the most important place the mapping breaks down, (3) which feature of the source domain the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks, (4) whether the conclusion — that Hayek's objection was only about computational feasibility — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and (5) what a better analogy would be, if there is one.
