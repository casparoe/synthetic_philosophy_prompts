Constitutional theorists sometimes describe a constitution as a Ulysses contract: a polity binds itself in advance because it foresees that its future self, under the pressure of panic, war, or short-term electoral temptation, will want to act against its own considered judgment. In the original image, Odysseus has his crew bind him to the mast so that he can hear the Sirens without being able to steer the ship onto the rocks: the constraint is imposed by the same agent it later binds, at a moment of better judgment. Not every legal constraint deserves that name, though. A rule a current majority can revise whenever it likes is a resolution rather than a precommitment, and a rule too rigid to adapt may simply be evaded or swept aside.

Please sort the following ten devices into three tiers — strong, partial, and weak as constitutional precommitments — and give one sentence of reasoning for each placement. Present the result as three labeled tiers, each containing the device numbers. Judge each device by how credibly it binds a future majority against a passing preference: how costly and visible it is to undo, how many alternative routes of evasion remain open, and how much it changes expectations before the temptation arises. If a device is strong on one dimension but weak on another, say so in its sentence. Commit to a placement for each device; do not hedge into neutrality. If the tier list obscures a distinction that matters to the Ulysses analogy — for example, whether the device binds the authors themselves or chiefly binds later generations — note it in a final sentence.
