I've been thinking about the problem of evil for a while now, and I keep coming back to a version of the free will defense that strikes me as genuinely solid — not the one about moral evil requiring libertarian freedom (that's got its own problems), but the one about what kind of world a loving God would create if He also wanted beings capable of real love, which requires the capacity to freely reject Him. And from there, the argument goes, you get a world that has to contain the possibility of all the suffering that comes with that rejection, including natural evil as a logically necessary backdrop for free choices to matter.

I've read some of the standard counters — Plantinga's defense only gets you to "possibly logically necessary," skeptical theism just says we can't see the reasons, the whole thing might collapse if God's middle knowledge is different than we think, etc. But I'm less interested in whether the defense fails to be *conclusive*, and more in what it would take for someone who currently finds it compelling to abandon it. Not rhetorically, but epistemically: what would have to happen, or be shown, or be discovered, for this position to be in serious trouble on its own terms?

I want the defeaters ranked from "this would upend the whole thing" to "this would be annoying but you could patch around it." I'm especially interested in ones that don't just come from the atheist side — like, are there things a theist could come to believe about God's nature that would make the free will defense look like a bad move? And I'd like an honest assessment of whether the position, as it's usually defended, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind, or whether we should expect that from positions in philosophical theology.
