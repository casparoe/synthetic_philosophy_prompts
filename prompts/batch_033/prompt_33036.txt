For years I've kept noticing a particular form of argument in discussions of two-player games — the kind of thing that gets gestured at in blog comments and forum threads more often than it gets stated carefully — and it has started to bother me that I can't say exactly what it is or where it stops applying. The argument runs as follows. To show that the first player in some game has a forced win, you begin by supposing the opposite: that the second player has a forced win. Then you have the first player make an arbitrary opening move, after which the first player adopts the second player's alleged winning strategy, treating the opening move as an inert extra piece that can be played around or ignored. Whenever the copied strategy would call for a move that has already been made, the first player simply makes another arbitrary move. The upshot is that the first player can force a win after all, contradicting the supposition. I've seen this pattern used for Hex and for Chomp, and I gather it does not transfer straightforwardly to chess or go, but I've never seen a clean account of the boundary between the two cases.

My sense is that the argument secretly depends on the opening move's being harmless or beneficial, and on the two players' positions being symmetric in some way I can't quite make precise; I also don't know how it behaves under draws or misère play, or whether it yields an actual strategy in any useful sense or merely an existence proof. Is there an established name for this move, and if so, which condition is doing the real work? I'm less interested in the label itself than in the carving: what the argument proves, what it assumes, and the simplest cases where the same shape of argument would fail.
