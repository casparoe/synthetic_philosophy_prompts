I'm in high school and I mostly think in code: I've written interpreters for toy languages, made a quine once, and I've got a decent handle on the halting problem. Lately I've been reading Gödel, Escher, Bach, and there's this one move Hofstadter makes over and over that I cant tell is genuinely deep or just a vibes-based analogy. As I understand it: Gödel found a way to encode formulas as numbers, which let a formal system sort of talk about itself, and he used that to build a formula that says, roughly, this very statement is not provable. Hofstadter then seems to say that this kind of self-referential loop is basically the same structure as a mind being aware of itself, and that if you stack enough strange loops you end up with self-awareness or even consciousness.

I keep trying to translate the whole thing into programmer vocabulary and it slips. A quine is self-referential but obviously not self-aware. A program that reads its own source is just doing file I/O. The halting problem already tells us there are things a system can't prove about itself, but nobody thinks that gives the program a self. So could you restate the GEB analogy — the numbering trick, the unprovable formula, the jump to self-aware minds — in the vocabulary of code, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart at all? What I actually want to know is whether the part that doesn't survive means code is too thin a vocabulary for what he's claiming, or whether the analogy was doing more work than it lets on.
