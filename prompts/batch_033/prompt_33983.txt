I'm a novelist at work on a book about the emergence of mind — the protagonist passes through radically different ways of learning and adapting to the world — and I'm using Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* as one of my touchstones. I want to understand the creature's developmental arc through the lens of Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds, laid out in *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* and *Kinds of Minds*: Darwinian creatures, whose "learning" happens only across generations by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures, who can learn by operant conditioning and trial-and-error within a single lifetime; Popperian creatures, who can run internal simulations and let their hypotheses die in their stead; and Gregorian creatures, who offload cognitive work onto tools, language, and the accumulated culture of others.

Please adopt the role of a philosopher of mind who takes literary criticism seriously and who knows the novel in detail. Before you write your final answer, I want you to begin by brainstorming — work through the creature's developmental trajectory scene by scene: his first sensory experiences of light and dark, heat and cold; his trial-and-error discoveries about fire, food, and shelter; his covert observation of the De Lacey family; his acquisition of language by overhearing Safie's lessons; his reading of *Paradise Lost*, Plutarch, and *The Sorrows of Werter*; and his mature capacity for long-term planning, moral reasoning, and revenge. Map each phase onto Dennett's categories where the fit is illuminating, and flag the places where the creature's development doesn't sit neatly inside the taxonomy.

Then write a critical reading of about 800 to 1,000 words that addresses the following questions. Does the creature's arc trace a genuine progression through Dennett's kinds of minds, or does the novel complicate the taxonomy in ways that matter? In particular, the creature becomes unmistakably a Gregorian mind — he acquires language, absorbs cultural narratives, and comes to understand himself through the moral categories he inherits — but the novel shows that this makes him more dangerous, not less, and more exquisitely capable of suffering. Does *Frankenstein* suggest that Gregorian minds are uniquely vulnerable to a certain kind of anguish, or capable of a certain kind of evil, that the taxonomy does not capture? And what does the creature's failure to be recognized as a person — despite possessing every Gregorian capacity — say about what the framework leaves out?

Finally, address me as a novelist: what does Shelley's rendering of the creature's inner life get right about the phenomenology of passing through these stages that philosophical argument alone cannot deliver? I want the reading to be honest in both directions — don't reduce the novel to an illustration of the theory, and don't distort the theory to fit the novel. If the novel resists the framework at certain points, say so and explain why the resistance is itself philosophically interesting.
