I spent thirty years in physiology labs before I retired and started reading philosophy — mostly ethics, since I kept bumping into questions my training never equipped me for. Here's one that's been nagging me.

In the United States, you can get paid for donating blood plasma. There's a whole industry built on it — plasma centers in strip malls, compensation on a debit card, the works. You can also get paid for egg donation, and for sperm donation, and surrogate mothers are compensated. But if you have two healthy kidneys and want to sell one to someone who'll die without it, that's a felony under the National Organ Transplant Act. The standard justification is that we shouldn't commodify the human body — but clearly we *do* commodify parts of it, just not *that* part. What's the real distinction?

I'm not asking for the policy arguments you hear in the news (exploitation, slippery slopes, etc.). I want to know what philosophical theory of commodification — or of value, or of bodily integrity — this pattern of permissions and prohibitions presupposes. Because it seems to me the practice only makes sense if there's some morally relevant difference between plasma and a kidney, between eggs and a liver lobe, that justifies treating one as a legitimate article of commerce and the other as something market transactions would corrupt. But I can't get the candidates to line up neatly. Is it about regeneration? Plasma replenishes, eggs are finite but not vital, kidneys don't grow back — but then why is surrogacy (nine months, permanent bodily changes) legal to pay for? Is it about risk? Plasma donation is extremely safe, kidney donation isn't — but we let people get paid for much riskier jobs. Is it about the meaning of the organ? But then what theory of meaning or symbolic value is doing the work, and is it consistent with compensating egg donors tens of thousands of dollars?

More broadly: what does this patchwork of rules presuppose about the nature of commodification? Does it presuppose that some goods have a value that market pricing *degrades*, the way Sandel talks about? Or does it presuppose something more like Elizabeth Anderson's pluralism about modes of valuation? Or is the practice actually underdetermined by theory — could you coherently endorse it from a utilitarian framework, a Kantian one, and a virtue ethics framework, each for different reasons? And if the presupposed theory were made explicit and then rejected, what specific changes to the law would follow?

I'd like the response structured like this:

1. **The Implicit Theory** (~350 words): Reconstruct the theory of commodification, value, or bodily integrity that best makes sense of the pattern — what the practice seems to commit you to.

2. **Strict Presupposition vs. Mere Compatibility** (~250 words): Distinguish what the practice *strictly* presupposes (i.e., what you'd have to reject to find the practice incoherent) from what it's merely compatible with (i.e., theories that can rationalize it but aren't demanded by it).

3. **Coherence Check** (~250 words): Test the practice for internal consistency. Does the plasma/kidney distinction actually track the theory it presupposes, or are there tensions — with surrogacy, with egg markets, with the fact that we also run an altruistic organ donation system in parallel?

4. **What Would Change** (~200 words): If we rejected the presupposed theory in favor of a straightforward consequentialist approach (or some other specific alternative), what would the legal landscape look like? What would we have to change, and what could stay?

Don't worry about policy recommendations or legal details — I'm after the philosophical architecture. And if I've misdescribed anything about the current laws, just run with the version I've given.
