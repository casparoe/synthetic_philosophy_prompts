I'm leading a two-hour seminar session for a mixed group of philosophy graduate students and cognitive-neuroscience PhD students who have all read Dennett's Quining Qualia in advance. The room tends to split before discussion even starts: the philosophers arrive convinced Dennett is trying to deny something obvious, and the neuroscientists arrive convinced the paper is a fuss about terminology that introspection could settle in five minutes. I want the session to make the actual argument available to both camps, and I want it organized around the Chase and Sanborn coffee-tasting case — the two tasters who have both come to dislike the same coffee, one holding that the coffee now tastes different and the other that his standards have changed. Don't explain the argument to me; plan how to teach it.

Please give me the sequence: what has to be in place before the Chase and Sanborn case can do any work, and why that order — in particular, the distinction between a sensory state and the judgment or preference a person forms about it, and whatever else you take to be load-bearing. Then tell me whether Chase and Sanborn is the right carrying example: what an example must have and must lack for this argument, and why something like the inverted spectrum would or would not do as the workhorse instead. For each misconception you expect from the two audiences — I am guessing at least Dennett denies there is anything it is like, introspection is authoritative about one's own qualia, and the example only shows that memory of tastes is fallible — say exactly where in the sequence it should be headed off rather than allowed to harden into an objection at the end. Finish with a single question or short exercise I can use to check whether they have understood the target of the argument rather than just its conclusion, and say which parts of the paper I should openly refuse to cover at this level and how to announce that without making the session feel evasive.
