I run a weekly after-school club for high-school juniors and seniors — about fifteen students, one hour, mostly interested in economics and psychology but with no formal background. We have been talking about how people make decisions when the odds are not fully known, and I want to run a session that lets them discover for themselves a phenomenon I keep coming across in my own reading, usually called the Ellsberg paradox: people are often much more willing to bet when the proportions are exactly specified than when they are merely possible, even when the two situations ought to be worth the same amount. I would rather they feel the pull of it than hear me describe it.

Design a runnable activity for this group. It should use physical props — I am imagining two urns or bags of colored balls, one whose contents are known and one whose contents are unknown — and real choices with small stakes (I can offer a modest prize or points toward a running leaderboard, but not cash payouts for every student). The setup should lead participants to make two choices that each feel sensible in the moment but together reveal something strange: they end up behaving as if the ambiguous bag is simultaneously less likely to contain red and less likely to contain black than the known bag, which cannot be right. I would also like one variant in which the ambiguous bag has a fixed composition that was set by someone else behind a screen, so participants know there is a definite fact about the world they simply cannot see, and one variant in which the bag's contents are determined by a random process they will never observe. Then give me a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction between risk (known proportions) and ambiguity (unknown proportions), explains why the usual idea that a person can always assign a personal probability to each color runs into difficulty here, and says what it would cost to insist that the students' choices were just mistakes rather than evidence of a genuine aversion to ambiguity. The paradox should not be named until the debrief. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which features of the phenomenon a physical classroom game cannot reproduce and I would have to supply in discussion.
