Here's the question I gave them:

"Some philosophers argue that human extinction would be bad in ways that go beyond the deaths it would cause. Explain one such argument — the one sometimes called the 'afterlife conjecture' — and how it differs from the simpler view that extinction is bad just because of all the people who would die."

And here's their answer:

"The afterlife conjecture, as developed by Samuel Scheffler, says that human extinction would be a catastrophe not only because of the deaths involved, but because it would rob our current lives of meaning. Scheffler points out that most of the things we care about — like working to cure diseases, trying to reduce suffering in the world, creating art, or even raising children — only make sense if we assume there will be future generations to benefit from them or carry them forward. If we knew that humanity was going to end right after we die, all these activities would feel pointless. So the real tragedy of extinction isn't just the loss of future lives, but the fact that it would retroactively make our own lives empty. This is different from the standard view, which only counts the deaths themselves as bad. Scheffler's argument is basically a version of the idea that what matters is the total sum of happiness in the world — if there are no future people, all that potential happiness is lost, and that's what makes extinction so terrible beyond the immediate deaths."
