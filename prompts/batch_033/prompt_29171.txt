Last night a friend and I spent an hour reconstructing a dollar auction that got out of hand — the two highest bidders both having to pay, only one getting the prize, and the bids climbing past the prize's value. My friend kept cutting the postmortem short with "at that point you're just throwing good money after bad," as if that settled that the second-highest bidder should have stopped. I doubt it settles anything, because the phrase could be making several quite different claims. Treat "throwing good money after bad" as the slogan to disambiguate. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could express — about sunk costs, about whether past outlays should influence the decision, about whether continuing is worse than stopping, about who should have withdrawn and when, and about whether the original decision to enter the auction was the real mistake. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and identify which reading or readings would actually support my friend's conclusion that a bidder should drop out once the bids have passed the prize's value. Finally, say how confident you are in the analysis and single out the point you are least sure of.
