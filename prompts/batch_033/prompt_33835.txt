I've been reading about the cognitive penetration of perception debate for a while now, and I'm getting frustrated. The question is whether what you believe, expect, or desire can literally change what you see — not just how you interpret it or where you pay attention, but the perceptual experience itself. And the debate seems totally stuck. On one side you've got people pointing to experiments where, say, wearing a heavy backpack makes a hill look steeper, or where racial stereotypes affect how you perceive someone's facial expression. On the other side you've got people like Firestone and Scholl who go through these studies one by one and argue that every single one can be explained by attention, response bias, or post-perceptual judgment rather than genuine changes to perception.

What I cant figure out is what kind of disagreement this actually is. Both sides agree on the empirical data — nobody disputes the experimental results themselves. The fight is about how to interpret them. So is this a substantive empirical disagreement that just needs better experiments, or is it a disagreement about what counts as perception in the first place? Maybe the two sides are using different definitions of 'perception' and talking past each other. Or maybe it's a disagreement about the burden of proof — the skeptics are demanding a standard of evidence that's impossible to meet in practice, and the pro-penetration side are content with a lower bar.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a survey of the experiments. If you think the disagreement is substantive, what experiment or result would actually move it? If it's terminological, what distinction would dissolve it? And if neither side can win because the question is ill-posed, say so and explain why people keep arguing about it anyway.
