I've been chewing on Kuhn for a while and I've come round to thinking that incommensurability is a real, deep feature of paradigm shifts -- not just the mild version where scientists on different sides find it hard to communicate, but the stronger claim that when a paradigm changes, there's no neutral, theory-independent language in which the old and new paradigms can be compared and adjudicated rationally. The standards of evidence, the meanings of the central terms, even what counts as a puzzle versus an anomaly -- all of it shifts. So there's no Archimedean point from which you can weigh them up.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not the stock objections (the "Kuhn was exaggerating" line, or the "scientists clearly do communicate and argue across paradigms" reply), but a proper map of the view's exposure. What would have to be true for the strong incommensurability thesis to be wrong? Which observations, historical cases, or arguments should make me revise it, and which should make me abandon it entirely?

I'm particularly interested in: (a) what a defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it, not just what critics say, (b) whether the view has been so qualified -- say, restricted to certain periods or certain sciences -- that it's become unfalsifiable, and if so whether that's a problem for a view of this kind, and (c) what the view actually predicts that its rivals don't predict about how scientific disputes play out. If it predicts nothing specific, I want to know that too.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. And if there's a piece of evidence or a case that I personally -- someone who finds the idea compelling -- should find especially difficult to dismiss, flag that.
