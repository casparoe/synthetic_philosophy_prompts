I'm presenting a paper at a philosophy colloquium next month. My thesis: the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion is best drawn not by the kind of psychological process the influencer triggers — rational versus emotional, conscious versus non-conscious — but by whether the influence is offered as a consideration the target could, upon full disclosure, recognize as a reason from within their own deliberative standpoint. I argue that this co-deliberative criterion handles hard cases like emotional appeals, openly flagged nudges, and bias-correction better than the standard bypassing-reason or deception accounts.

The audience: a twenty-five-person department, mostly ethicists and political philosophers. Three Kantians who think autonomy requires acting on self-given law and will press me on whether my criterion collapses into a hypothetical-consent theory. Two Aristotelians who think I am neglecting the shared ends that make persuasion a cooperative activity and will ask what my account says about character. One empirically-minded philosopher who works on the ethics of influence and will want to know what my view predicts about specific findings from the persuasion and priming literatures. The rest are agnostics or work in other areas.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order I will get them, phrased the way this audience would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is softest. Include the question I should be praying nobody asks. And if my framing is confused in a way that will get me torn apart, tell me that before you give me the prep sheet — do not politely prep me for a talk that is built on a mistake.
