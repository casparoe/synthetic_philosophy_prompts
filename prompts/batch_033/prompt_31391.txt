I'm taking a moral philosophy seminar this semester — we've been doing a unit on blame, forgiveness, and standing, reading G.A. Cohen, Macalester Bell, that crowd — and I also sit on my university's academic integrity board. The two just collided in a way I wasn't prepared for, and I need to draft a memo for our meeting Thursday.

Here's the case. A sophomore, call her Maya, submitted a formal complaint alleging that her classmate Jordan copied large sections of a final paper from an online source without attribution. The evidence looks solid — the passages are near-verbatim from a blog post, timeline checks out, pretty textbook. But here's the wrinkle: Maya herself was sanctioned by this same board last year for plagiarism on a different assignment. Jordan's defense doesn't deny the copying; instead, Jordan argues the board should dismiss the complaint because Maya lacks standing to bring it — that it's hypocritical for someone with a plagiarism record to file plagiarism complaints against others, and that the board shouldn't entertain complaints from people who've done the very thing they're reporting. Two board members think this is obviously irrelevant (a violation is a violation, our job is to adjudicate the facts), two think it's obviously a problem (you can't have the fox guarding the henhouse, and letting Maya play whistleblower after her own offense sends a weird message), and we're deadlocked.

I need to write the short opinion memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — dismiss the complaint, proceed normally, or some third option — with reasons. It needs to engage seriously with the standing/hypocrisy argument (which, honestly, my seminar has made me take more seriously than I expected going in), and it should give a fair treatment of the losing side's strongest consideration. Also, if you think the whole framing is off — like I'm importing a concept from moral philosophy that doesn't map cleanly onto institutional procedures, or I'm conflating different senses of "standing," or there's a relevant distinction I'm missing between moral blame and filing a procedural complaint — please say so. I'd rather get that pushback now than have someone on the board point it out after I circulate the memo.
