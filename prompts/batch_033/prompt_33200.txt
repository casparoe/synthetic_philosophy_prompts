I've been chewing on causation by omission lately and I can't figure out what principle, if any, is behind my gut reactions. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A parent doesn't feed their infant, infant dies. The parent caused the death. No hesitation.

2. A lifeguard on duty is scrolling instagram and doesn't notice a swimmer drowning. The lifeguard caused the death.

3. Same beach, but it's a random sunbather who sees the drowning and does nothing. The sunbather didn't *cause* the death. Blameworthy maybe, but not causal.

4. My neighbor's house is on fire. I don't call the fire department because I assume someone else already has. I didn't cause the fire damage.

5. Same fire, but I don't call because I want the house to burn down. I still don't think I caused the damage, but it feels closer to causation than case 4, which is weird — why would my mental state affect whether I caused something?

6. Doctor doesn't prescribe a standard antibiotic, patient dies of the infection. Doctor caused the death.

7. My neighbor's gardener doesn't water my plants while I'm on vacation, but we never discussed it, I never asked. Plants die. Gardener didn't cause their deaths. I did, by leaving, or no one did.

8. Same plants, but this time I asked the gardener, they agreed, and then forgot. Now I think they caused the deaths. The promise changed the causation, not just the blame. That seems philosophically suspect but I can't shake it.

9. A government doesn't build flood defenses in a region that's never flooded before, then a freak storm floods it. Government didn't cause the damage. But if they'd built defenses in a flood-prone area and then dismantled them to save money, and a predictable flood comes — now I think they caused it.

10. Here's the one that keeps me up: I'm hiking and see someone about to step on a rattlesnake. I freeze and say nothing. They get bitten. I didn't cause the bite — the snake did. But if I *lured* them onto that trail knowing the snake was there, suddenly I think I did cause it. Structurally both are omissions plus foreknowledge, so why the different verdict?

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on — a programmer who doesn't patch a security vuln they don't know about, versus one who knows about it and decides not to patch?
