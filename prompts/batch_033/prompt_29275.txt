I spent four decades as a research biochemist before retiring and turning, somewhat belatedly, to philosophy. One thing that has struck me, coming from a discipline where hypotheses are tested against data that can genuinely surprise you, is how much weight contemporary Anglophone philosophy places on thought experiments — or what Daniel Dennett calls intuition pumps. Trolleys, Chinese rooms, brains in vats, Twin Earth: these miniature narratives are asked to do an enormous amount of argumentative work.

I have come to suspect that their persuasive force derives less from the logical structure of the scenarios they present than from features that would be considered epistemically irrelevant in the sciences — narrative framing, emotional priming, the vividness of certain details, the order in which information is disclosed. If that is right, then a great deal of philosophical argumentation rests on foundations less secure than the profession tends to assume.

Please argue for the position I have just sketched: that intuition pumps persuade largely through narrative and affective mechanisms rather than through the rational evaluation of a hypothetical case, and that this constitutes a serious — though not necessarily fatal — epistemic problem for thought-experiment-based philosophy. I would be especially interested in an analysis that draws on concrete examples and that engages with the strongest counterarguments (for instance, that thought experiments function like controlled experiments, isolating variables; or that our intuitive responses to them track genuine conceptual truths). Please write your answer in British English.
