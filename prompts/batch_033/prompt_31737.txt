I've been dipping into the philosophy-of-mind literature on the temporal structure of conscious experience — the color phi phenomenon, the flash-lag effect, the cutaneous rabbit, Libet's timing experiments, the specious present, and the whole Orwellian-vs.-Stalinesque debate that Dennett kicked off — and I'm finding that the conversation keeps circling the same drain. People argue for one interpretation of the data over another, but I rarely see anyone spell out what a theory of the timing of experience is actually supposed to deliver before they start defending one. Before I sink more time into evaluating individual accounts, I want to step back and get the specification right.

Here's what I'm after: a list of desiderata that any adequate theory of the temporal structure of conscious experience must satisfy. I mean the phenomena it has to handle — color phi (where subjects report a color change mid-trajectory before the second stimulus is physically present), flash-lag (where a flashed object aligned with a moving one appears to lag behind it), the cutaneous rabbit (where taps at two points create illusory intermediate taps), temporal binding windows, the fact that sensory signals arrive at cortex at different latencies, and the specious present itself. I also mean the distinctions it must respect — the difference between the time at which a neural event occurs and the time at which its content is consciously experienced, the difference between the content of an experience and a subject's later report about it, the fact that "the time of the experience" and "the time represented in the experience" can come apart. And I mean the conceptual constraints — the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction itself (does the brain edit the show before it reaches consciousness, or revise the memory afterward?), the question of whether it even makes sense to ask for the moment something "becomes conscious" given the temporal smearing of neural processing, and the fact that any theory has to say something about why the apparent paradoxes arise in the first place.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and flag which ones you think are genuine non-negotiables and which are parochial commitments of one theoretical camp (for instance, a demand for a sharp "time of entry into consciousness" might be a Cartesian-materialist hangup rather than something a theory must honor). I'd also like to know whether you suspect any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — that is, whether the desiderata list itself tells us something interesting about the limits of what a theory can deliver, the way Bell's theorem tells us that certain desiderata for hidden-variable theories can't be jointly met. If you're willing, a brief scoring of how the main families of views (multiple-drafts or Dennett-style accounts, sensory-delay or "Stalinesque" accounts, and postdictive or "Orwellian" accounts) do against the list would help me calibrate, but the requirements themselves are what I most need.
