I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the relationship between moral judgment and attributions of intentional action, going by my gut on a range of cases. I keep finding that my verdicts don't fit any simple story, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them — or whether I have to abandon some of them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

**Case A (Standard harm).** The CEO of a manufacturing firm is told that adopting a new production programme will increase profits but will also release pollutants that damage a nearby river ecosystem. The CEO says, 'I don't care about the river. Adopt the programme.' The programme is adopted, and the river is damaged. *Verdict:* the CEO intentionally damaged the river.

**Case B (Standard help).** The CEO of a manufacturing firm is told that adopting a new production programme will increase profits and will also, as a side effect, release cleaner water that helps a nearby river ecosystem. The CEO says, 'I don't care about the river. Adopt the programme.' The programme is adopted, and the river is helped. *Verdict:* the CEO did not intentionally help the river.

**Case C (Means).** The CEO is told that adopting a new programme will increase profits, but only by dumping untreated waste into the river — the environmental damage is the very means by which profits are increased. The CEO says, 'I don't care. Do it.' The programme is adopted. *Verdict:* the CEO intentionally damaged the river — and this is even clearer than in Case A.

**Case D (Regret).** Same as Case A, except that the CEO genuinely regrets the environmental damage. He says, 'I truly wish we didn't have to do this, but I see no alternative. Adopt the programme.' *Verdict:* the CEO intentionally damaged the river, but I feel the attribution is weaker than in Case A — I hesitate more before saying it.

**Case E (Skill and difficulty).** An archer attempts an extremely difficult shot at a competition target. She knows that if she succeeds, the sound of the arrow hitting the target will startle and scare away a rare bird nesting in a nearby tree. She doesn't care about the bird. She succeeds. *Verdict:* the archer did not intentionally scare the bird.

**Case F (Known risk).** A surgeon performs a necessary operation on a patient, knowing that the procedure carries a 10 per cent risk of permanent nerve damage. The surgeon takes all standard precautions. The patient suffers nerve damage. *Verdict:* the surgeon did not intentionally cause the nerve damage.

**Case G (Aesthetic harm).** A property developer demolishes a Victorian building, knowing that doing so will destroy a mural inside that is regarded by local residents as part of the community's heritage. The developer doesn't care about the mural. *Verdict:* the developer intentionally destroyed the mural — but I am less confident about this than about Case A, and I am not sure why.

**Case H (Omission).** An executive learns that she could authorise a safety upgrade that would prevent a likely future injury to workers. She decides not to, because the upgrade would reduce quarterly profits. She doesn't care about the workers' safety. An injury later occurs. *Verdict:* I am genuinely torn. Part of me says she intentionally allowed the injury; another part says 'intentionally' doesn't fit omissions in the same way it fits actions, and I should say she was reckless rather than that she intentionally brought about the injury.

**Case I (This one I haven't settled).** A junior employee implements a policy change without checking the environmental impact assessment that was sitting in her inbox. The assessment clearly stated that the change would harm a protected wetland. She didn't know about the harm because she didn't read the assessment, but she could and should have. The wetland is harmed. *Verdict:* I go back and forth. Did she intentionally harm the wetland?

That's eight verdicts and one open case. I can see that the moral valence of the outcome is doing some work — the asymmetry between A and B is the classic Knobe effect, and I'm committed to it. But the rest of the cases complicate the picture. Regret (D) seems to weaken the attribution without changing the moral valence. Skill and difficulty (E) seem to block it. Known risk (F) seems to block it even though the outcome is bad. The aesthetic-harm case (G) gives me a weaker version of the A verdict, and I don't know what feature of the case explains the drop in confidence. And omission (H) leaves me stuck.

What principle or small set of principles best captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to overturn? And what does the principle say about Case I?
