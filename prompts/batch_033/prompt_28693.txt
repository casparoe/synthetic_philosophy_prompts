I spent forty years in evolutionary biology before retiring, and I have recently turned my attention to philosophical aesthetics—specifically the so-called paradox of tragedy. The question of why we willingly subject ourselves to artworks that evoke sadness, fear, and pity has occupied me for the better part of this year, and I have arrived at a set of views I would like to have pressure-tested before I commit them to paper. My training disposes me to look for tensions in a belief set the way I once looked for internal inconsistencies in a model of population dynamics, but I am too close to this material to see where the problems are. I would be grateful for a systematic audit.

Here is where I currently stand:

(1) The paradox of tragedy is a genuine puzzle and not a pseudo-problem. It really is prima facie irrational to seek out experiences that reliably induce negative emotions—sadness, fear, pity—and the fact that we do so, across cultures and throughout recorded history, calls for explanation.

(2) The most promising explanation, in my view, is a transformation account: the negative emotions evoked by tragic art are not the same mental states as their real-world counterparts. The sadness one feels at the end of King Lear is embedded in a larger experience that includes aesthetic appreciation, narrative comprehension, and the awareness of fictionality, and this embedding alters its phenomenal character. It is not simply sadness-plus-something-else; it is a different kind of affective state altogether, one that is not straightforwardly aversive.

(3) This transformation account ought to be grounded in our best understanding of the evolved mind. The cognitive architecture that permits fictional sadness to feel different from real sadness almost certainly has an evolutionary history, and the most plausible story is that engaging with simulated adversity—in narrative, in play, in ritual—conferred fitness benefits on our ancestors by allowing them to rehearse emotional responses, refine social cognition, and explore counterfactual scenarios without paying the cost of real danger or loss.

(4) If (3) is correct, then the pleasure we take in tragedy is, at bottom, instrumental: we are drawn to tragic art because the cognitive systems it engages were shaped by selection to find such simulated-adversity experiences rewarding, in the way that sugar tastes sweet because it was adaptive for our ancestors to seek out calorie-dense foods.

(5) And yet I cannot accept that the value of tragic art is merely instrumental. King Lear is not valuable because it happened to stimulate an adaptive circuit in my Pleistocene ancestors; it is valuable in a way that seems to outrun any evolutionary story. Great tragedies are intrinsically valuable—their value is not exhausted by, and does not reduce to, the adaptive benefits they may have conferred.

(6) I hold, moreover, that this commitment to intrinsic aesthetic value is fully compatible with a naturalistic worldview. One need not posit non-natural properties or Platonic forms to make sense of the idea that some things are valuable for their own sake and not merely as means.

I suspect there are tensions in this set—perhaps between the transformation account and the instrumental account of the pleasure, or between the claim that the paradox is genuine and the claim that fictional emotions are transformed into something non-aversive. But I am not confident I have identified all of them, or correctly assessed their severity. I would like you to go through these six commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally troubling, and for each genuine tension, specify which commitment would have to be revised or abandoned and what the philosophical cost of doing so would be.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
