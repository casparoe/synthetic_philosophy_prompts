Reconstruct his argument, making sure to explain (a) the distinction between the participant stance and the objective stance, and (b) the distinction between excuses and exemptions. here's the student's answer:

"Strawson's strategy is to reframe the free will debate around what he calls the 'reactive attitudes' — emotions like resentment, gratitude, indignation, and forgiveness that we feel in response to the goodwill or ill will others show toward us. These attitudes define what he calls the 'participant stance': the stance we occupy when we engage with others as fellow members of a moral community, holding them responsible for their actions.

The 'objective stance,' by contrast, is what we adopt when we step back and treat a person not as a participant in interpersonal relationships but as an object to be managed, treated, or controlled — the way a therapist might view a patient or a parent might view a young child. When we take the objective stance toward someone, reactive attitudes are suspended: we don't resent them, we just try to deal with them.

Strawson then draws an important distinction between two ways reactive attitudes can be suspended. Excuses — like 'he was pushed' or 'he didn't know the gun was loaded' — show that the particular action didn't manifest ill will, so resentment isn't warranted in that case. But excuses operate within the participant stance: we're still treating the person as a responsible agent. Exemptions are different. They show that the person isn't a proper target of reactive attitudes at all — like someone with severe schizophrenia or a toddler. Exemptions remove the person from the participant stance altogether.

Now, what if determinism is true? Strawson's answer is that this wouldn't force us to abandon the participant stance, for two reasons. First, we simply can't do it. The reactive attitudes are too deeply embedded in human nature; we couldn't sustain the objective stance toward everyone all the time even if we tried. Second, determinism doesn't show that anyone is exempt in the relevant sense. Exemptions work by pointing to specific incapacities in specific individuals — a child can't understand moral norms, a severely mentally ill person can't control their behavior. Determinism is a general thesis about the causal structure of the universe; it doesn't pick out any such incapacity. So it gives us no reason to think anyone is exempt.

I think Strawson's argument largely succeeds. The key insight is that the demand for an external, metaphysical justification of our responsibility practices is misguided. Our reactive attitudes are justified by their own internal standards — whether someone actually showed ill will, whether they had the relevant capacities — and determinism doesn't engage with those standards at all. The worry that determinism reveals the participant stance to be based on a 'systematic error' only makes sense if you think the participant stance is a theory about whether actions are uncaused, which it isn't."
