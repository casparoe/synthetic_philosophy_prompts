I've been arguing about wild animal suffering online for like two years now and the whole thing feels totally stuck. One side says: if suffering is bad, and there's an unfathomable amount of it in nature—animals starving, being eaten alive, dying slowly of parasites—then we have at least some reason to look into interventions, like vaccination programs or gene drives, once we actually know what we're doing. The other side says: you can't just go messing with ecosystems you don't understand, nature isn't ours to manage, and half the interventionist examples are cartoonishly naive about how ecology actually works. And yet both sides will agree that suffering matters and ecosystems are complicated and fragile. So what's actually going on here? My hunch is this is one of those disagreements where the real fight isn't about facts at all—it's that one side thinks moral urgency means you have to act even under uncertainty, and the other side thinks moral caution means you don't touch a system you can't model. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a genuine empirical question buried in there that one side is just way more confident about than the other. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move this, or is it basically stalemate until someone figures out something neither side has thought of?
