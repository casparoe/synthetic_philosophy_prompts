I've been reading around the Kuhn debate about incommensurability for a few months now, and I'm starting to feel like the whole thing has been stuck in the same place since at least the 1980s. Here's my rough picture: Kuhn says that when you switch paradigms — say from Newtonian to Einsteinian mechanics — the meanings of terms change so fundamentally that there's no neutral language in which to compare the two theories. "Mass" in Newtonian physics isnt just a different quantity; it's embedded in a different conceptual web. So the two paradigms are incommensurable. Critics immediately jumped on this as relativism: if you can't compare paradigms, you can't say one is better, and science becomes a matter of mob psychology. Kuhn then spent years clarifying that he didn't mean incommensurability as incomparability — he meant something more like untranslatability without remainder, or that there's no algorithm for theory choice. But the critics kept saying the same things, and the defenders kept saying the critics were attacking a straw man, and here we are, fifty years later, with the same positions.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really one disagreement but two nested ones. The surface disagreement is about whether Kuhn's account entails relativism. But underneath that, there's a deeper disagreement about what counts as a rational comparison between theories in the first place. The critics want a formal, maybe even algorithmic, account of theory comparison — something like Bayesian confirmation theory or a set of shared methodological rules. The defenders think that's a pipe dream and that Kuhn was actually describing how rational comparison works in practice: through values like simplicity, scope, and fruitfulness, which are shared but applied differently. If that's right, then the debate hasn't moved because the two sides are answering different questions — one about what rationality ought to be, the other about what it is.
