I was talking to a friend about animal ethics and he gave me what he said was Kant's position, roughly like this: animals are, morally speaking, just things for Kant, since only rational beings can be ends in themselves, so we have no direct duties to them. The only reason cruelty to animals is wrong is that it hardens the person who does it—it deadens the sympathy that normally carries over into how we treat other people, so someone cruel to animals becomes more likely to be cruel to humans. That means the wrongness is really about effects on your own character and on your future conduct toward people. And so, he concluded, if someone tortures a cat in private and it genuinely leaves how they treat people untouched, Kant has to say they've done nothing wrong. Something about this feels too neat to me, but I cant put my finger on where it goes wrong. Can you go through it claim by claim and say which parts a careful Kant scholar would accept, which are overstatements or conflations, and whether that last conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts?
