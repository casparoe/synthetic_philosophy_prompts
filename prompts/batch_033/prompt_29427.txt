The debate over whether money-pump arguments establish that rational preferences must be transitive has been running since at least the 1950s, and despite increasingly sophisticated formal work, the two sides seem as far apart as ever. On one side, advocates argue that intransitive preferences make an agent vulnerable to a series of trades each of which she prefers, but that collectively leave her worse off by her own lights — she pays to go in a circle and ends up back where she started, poorer. This is supposed to show that such preferences are practically irrational: an agent whose attitudes open her to a sure loss has made a mistake by her own standards. On the other side, critics have multiple replies: the agent can foresee the pump and decline the initial trade; preferences may be context-dependent and shift between choice nodes in ways the formal argument ignores; being exploitable by a cunning trader is not the same thing as being irrational; or the argument simply begs the question by assuming that vulnerability to a sure loss constitutes irrationality, when that is precisely what needs to be established for the intransitive agent who denies that transitivity is a requirement of reason.

The formal literature has gotten progressively more refined — pumps have been made more robust against foresight objections, distinctions have been drawn between different kinds of money-pump arguments, and attempts have been made to show that the real problem is not the pump itself but the availability of a dominated alternative. And yet the core standoff persists: one camp thinks a money pump is a devastating _reductio_, the other thinks it is a clever trick that doesn't touch the normative question.
