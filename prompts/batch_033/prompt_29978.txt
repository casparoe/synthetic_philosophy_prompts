I'm working on a longform piece about the foundations of probability, and I keep coming back to Bertrand's paradox as the problem that resists settlement. Before I interview any more researchers about their preferred resolutions, I want the specification of what a resolution is actually supposed to deliver. Could you write me the desiderata: what any adequate treatment of Bertrand's paradox — and by extension, any reformed version of the principle of indifference — must accomplish?

I need the list of requirements, with the case or consideration behind each. A resolution might need to say why the three classical chord-selection methods yield different answers without simply stipulating that one parameterization is correct. It might need to generalize beyond the circle to other geometric settings where the same indeterminacy arises, or to non-geometric uses of the principle of indifference — partitioning outcome spaces in discrete probability, assigning priors over competing models. It might need to respect that the principle does genuine normative work in some contexts and cannot be abandoned wholesale without leaving a gap in how we justify prior distributions. It might need to distinguish between cases where indifference is merely a convenience and cases where it is epistemically mandatory, and to say what marks the boundary.

I am especially interested in which requirements are non-negotiable and which reflect the commitments of particular camps — objective Bayesians, proponents of symmetry-based or group-invariance approaches, those who take the real lesson to be that probability is inherently model-relative, and so on. And if some subset of the requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — if, for instance, any rule that picks a unique distribution in the chord problem necessarily fails to handle other paradoxes of the same structure, or if satisfying both geometric generality and normative force requires a move that undercuts the principle's original motivation — that is the part I most need flagged. That would mean the desiderata list itself is the result, and the field's disagreement is not about which resolution is correct but about which requirement to drop.

A table format would work well if it helps, but prose is fine. The weight should fall on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing proposals — though a brief note on which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard resolutions would not go amiss.
