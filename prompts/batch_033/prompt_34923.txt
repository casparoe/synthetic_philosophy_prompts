I am the director of a small geopolitical forecasting unit within a policy institute. Twice a year, we produce probability estimates for a set of events — elections, armed conflicts, economic indicators — and these estimates feed directly into our government clients' contingency planning. Our process is inherently forward-looking: we assign a credence now, but we also know that in three months our team will have new sources, new analytical frameworks, and quite possibly a different view. The question I keep wrestling with is: when should I trust my future team's judgment over my current one, and when should I regard the anticipated shift as a sign that something has gone wrong with our present reasoning?

I have read a little about Van Fraassen's Reflection principle, which says roughly that your current credence in a proposition should equal your expected future credence in it — that you should treat your future self as an expert. But I also know there are cases where this breaks down: you might know you will be drunk, or sleep-deprived, or that you will have forgotten some crucial piece of evidence you now possess. And there are the more subtle puzzles — the Sleeping Beauty problem, cases where you will gain evidence whose character you cannot now anticipate, or cases where you know you will be subject to a systematic bias. I need a procedure my team can actually run, not a theorem.

Please write me a decision procedure for determining when to defer to an anticipated future credence and when to override it. The procedure should be a checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure says "defer," where it says "do not defer," and where it hands the decision to a human because the question cannot be settled by the procedure. The procedure must be usable by analysts with no formal training in epistemology, in under half an hour, for a single forecast. It should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then apply it to three worked examples: (1) a forecast about an election outcome where we expect to gain high-quality polling data between now and the next review; (2) a forecast where we know the team will be under severe time pressure and fatigue at the next review, and we anticipate that their credence will shift accordingly; (3) a case where the team's current estimate is 70% but we expect the future estimate to be 40% solely because we anticipate a change in team composition — a respected contrarian is joining next month. For each, show what the procedure yields and flag any step where the procedure's answer is one the team will not like.
