I'm a PhD student in political science working on the methodology of social explanation, and I've been reading a lot of macroeconomics lately. There's a practice in that field that I can't quite get a handle on philosophically, and I'd like your help reconstructing what it presupposes.

Since the 1970s, the dominant norm in macroeconomics has been that models require "microfoundations": you cannot simply posit a relationship between aggregate variables — say, between the interest rate and total investment — because that relationship is not a stable law of nature. It summarizes the decisions of many individuals, and when policy or circumstances change those individuals' incentives, the aggregate relationship may shift. So the norm goes: you must derive all aggregate behavior from the optimizing choices of individual agents. This is widely treated as a commitment to methodological individualism.

But here is the practice that puzzles me. The standard workhorse model that satisfies this requirement is the representative agent model, which treats the entire economy as a single infinitely-lived household. The representative agent's consumption, labor supply, and savings are, by construction, identical to the per-capita aggregates. The model then explains the time path of GDP, employment, and the price level by solving this one agent's optimization problem, given exogenous shocks and a policy rule.

What I cannot figure out is what this practice actually presupposes about the relationship between individual-level facts and social-level facts. On one reading, the representative agent model seems to presuppose that the social level is nothing over and above the individual level — that the economy really is, somehow, like an individual scaled up. But that is a very strong metaphysical claim, and I doubt most macroeconomists would endorse it if pressed. On another reading, the model is just a convenient fiction: we do not actually believe the economy is a person, but models that treat it as one happen to fit the data well enough. But if that is the case, the microfoundations requirement looks hollow — it is satisfied by a modeling trick that assumes away the very aggregation problem the requirement was supposed to solve.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of social ontology that this practice presupposes. In particular: (1) What are the different things "microfoundations" could be — a metaphysical thesis about what social facts consist in, an explanatory norm about what counts as a good explanation, or a pragmatic modeling constraint? (2) On which of these readings does the representative agent framework actually satisfy the requirement, and on which does it fail? (3) Is the practice internally consistent, or does it presuppose one thing about the individual–social relationship when it demands microfoundations and a different thing when it deploys the representative agent? (4) If the practice were reformed to take its own commitments seriously, what would have to change — would macroeconomics need heterogeneous agent models, or would it need to abandon the microfoundations requirement, or is there a third option?
