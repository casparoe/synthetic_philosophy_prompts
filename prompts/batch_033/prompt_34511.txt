I'm a philosophy professor working through the spectrum arguments against the transitivity of 'better than,' and I find my own credences less structured than they should be. I'd like your help.

Please give me your credence that the relation *all-things-considered better than* — for a single agent's prudential good, not interpersonal moral betterness — is genuinely, metaphysically transitive. That is: for any three outcomes A, B, C, if A is all-things-considered better for an agent than B, and B is all-things-considered better for that agent than C, then A is all-things-considered better for that agent than C. I am asking about the relation itself, not about whether our judgments reliably track it, nor about whether 'better than' claims are sometimes context-sensitive in ways that create merely apparent intransitivities.

Decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions, assigning a rough probability to each and briefly justifying it:

1. That at least one spectrum argument — e.g., Quinn's self-torturer, a hedonic continuum where adjacent pairs are indiscriminable but the endpoints clearly differ, or a series of small-diminishment cases about a life's overall quality — successfully describes a genuine intransitivity in all-things-considered betterness, as opposed to revealing only the limited resolution of our discriminative capacities, vagueness in the underlying comparatives, or a flaw in how the spectrum was constructed.
2. That, conditional on (1) being false — i.e., conditional on the standard spectrum arguments failing to establish genuine intransitivity — there is nevertheless some other reason to think all-things-considered betterness is intransitive: for example, from the structure of pro tanto reasons combining into an intransitive overall ordering, from the possibility of rational cyclic preferences that are not straightforwardly irrational, or from arguments about parity or incomparability generating intransitive chains.
3. That, conditional on all-things-considered betterness turning out to be intransitive in at least some cases, we can recover a principled, non-ad-hoc restriction on the scope of the intransitivity — for instance, that transitivity holds between outcomes that differ by more than a 'just-noticeable difference,' or that it holds for 'significantly better than' even if not for 'marginally better than,' or that the intransitivity is confined to artificially constructed spectra and does not threaten the rationality of maximizing under ordinary choice conditions.

Then combine these into your overall credence, stating clearly what conditional-dependence structure you assume. Finally, tell me which sub-question your overall credence is most sensitive to — where a shift in your probability would move the final number the most — and what kind of argument or evidence would most likely shift that sub-question for you.

Throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled among philosophers working on this topic from what remains contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
