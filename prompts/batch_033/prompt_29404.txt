I keep arguing with a friend about what makes a right action morally worthy — praiseworthy in the full sense, not merely correct — and I've found that my gut verdicts on cases don't obviously hang together. Here they are. A grocer who charges fair prices only because honesty is good for business does the right thing, but I don't think his action has moral worth. A woman who visits her sick friend in the hospital out of duty, though she finds it draining and takes no pleasure in it, does act with moral worth. But I also want to say that a woman who visits the same friend simply because she loves her and would be miserable staying away acts with moral worth too, and not in some second-best sense. A man who donates to an effective charity only because he wants to impress a colleague seems to me to lack moral worth, even though the charity does a lot of good. So far I can summarize myself as: the agent's motivating reasons matter, and not just the outcome. But then there is the case I can't get around: a boy helps an enslaved person escape because he has come to care about him as a friend, while sincerely believing that what he is doing is wrong — that he is stealing someone's lawful property. I find it very hard to deny that his action has moral worth. Yet my tentative principle — an action has moral worth just in case the agent does it because she takes it to be right — says exactly that he lacks it, since he takes himself to be doing wrong. I would rather keep the verdict than the principle.

My tentative principle also seems too strict for the woman who visits out of love: she is not visiting because she judges it right; she is visiting because she loves her friend. So I suspect the principle needs to be something like: an action has moral worth when the agent acts for the features that actually make the act right, whether or not she conceptualizes those features under the heading of rightness. But I am not sure that formulation is precise enough, and I want it tested.

Please do this for me. (1) State the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts; be precise about what "acting for the reasons that make the act right" amounts to, and show how it handles the grocer, the two visitors, the donor, and the boy. (2) Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, or can accommodate only by becoming gerrymandered, and whether you think I should overturn that verdict rather than complicate the principle. (3) Give me verdicts on some cases I have not listed: a person who acts from a consciously held correct moral principle that she originally adopted only because her parents drilled it into her and she wanted their approval; a person who does the right thing from duty, where the duty she takes herself to have is actually mistaken, so that she happens to do what is right but for a reason that would usually point elsewhere; and a person who acts from warm inclination to do something that is in fact wrong, and it happens to come out right only by luck. Finally, (4) separate what is settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
