In my decision theory class we've spent three weeks on the Allais paradox and I'm struck by how completely stuck the whole debate is. The facts aren't in dispute: people (including me, when I'm honest) tend to pick the sure $1m over the 89/10/1 gamble in the first choice, and then take the 10% shot at $5m over the 11% shot at $1m in the second, even though each pair differs only by a common branch, so the independence axiom says that branch can't be doing any work and the same underlying preference should show up in both choices. But then the class splits exactly down the middle and nobody moves. One side says the choices reveal a real normative problem with independence: certainty has a special status, and a theory that calls me irrational for treating it as special just doesn't respect what certainty is like. The other side says the axiom is a coherence constraint on preferences, not a psychological prediction, so my Allais choices just show I made a mistake I should correct on reflection, like someone who takes both sides of a bet. I've started to suspect its not really a disagreement about which theory is right, but about what decision theory is even for: a normative standard for choosing, a descriptive model of how people actually choose, or a set of coherence constraints. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, and tell me whether anything could move it, or whether the two sides are just never going to be arguing about the same thing.
