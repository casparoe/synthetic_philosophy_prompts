The exam question was: "According to Kant, what gives an action moral worth, and why is the case of Huckleberry Finn — as discussed by Nomy Arpaly — often taken to pose a problem for Kant's criterion? Can an account of moral worth in terms of acting for the right reasons handle that case better?"

The student wrote:

"Kant thinks an action has moral worth only if it is performed from duty, meaning the agent does it because she sees it as the right thing to do, and not because she happens to feel like it. The shopkeeper who charges fair prices only to protect his reputation acts in accordance with duty but not from duty, so his action has no moral worth. Likewise the sympathetic person who helps others because she enjoys it acts from inclination, and Kant says this action, however amiable, also lacks true moral worth. So for Kant, inclinations are at best morally irrelevant.

Huckleberry Finn seems to be a problem here because Huck helps Jim escape slavery out of friendship and sympathy, not out of duty — in fact Huck believes he is doing something wrong. So Kant's criterion would have to deny that Huck's action has moral worth, but intuitively it clearly does. Arpaly's 'right reasons' account fixes this: Huck's action has moral worth because he acts from inclination, specifically a sympathy that tracks the right-making features of the situation, namely Jim's humanity and his claim not to be treated as property. This shows that moral worth really depends on the agent's motives being good, not on the motive being duty in particular. So Kant's view and the right reasons view mostly agree whenever the agent is moved by the features that make the act right; the only real difference is that the right reasons view lets inclination count."
