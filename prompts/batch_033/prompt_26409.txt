I'm a journalist who covers AI and cognitive science, and I've been tracking a peculiar asymmetry in how we reason about artificial and biological minds. I don't mean the familiar question of whether machines can think — I mean something narrower, a structural feature of the debate itself that I keep seeing across op-eds, panel discussions, and even some philosophy papers.

Here's the pattern. When someone lays out a mechanistic account of how a large language model produces fluent text — token prediction, attention weights, training on web-scale corpora — many commentators treat the very existence of that account as a reason to deny that the system understands anything. The reasoning seems to go: we can explain how it works in reductive terms, therefore its outputs aren't genuine. But when someone offers a mechanistic account of how a human produces fluent text — neural firing patterns, statistical learning during development, articulatory motor programs — nobody treats that as undermining the conclusion that the human understands language. In the human case, having a reductive explanation is treated as scientific progress; in the machine case, it's treated as debunking. I've noticed the same dynamic around other capacities: when AlphaGo made its famous move 37, much of the commentary emphasized that it was "just" trained via self-play, as if that settled the question of creativity; when a human grandmaster makes a surprising move, the fact that it can be explained in terms of pattern recognition built over thousands of hours of study doesn't seem to trouble anyone.

So the asymmetry isn't about the explanatory power of the mechanistic accounts — it's about whether the entity being explained is one we antecedently assume has a mind. The availability of a reductive explanation counts against attributions of understanding in one case but not the other, and the difference seems to be tracking our prior commitments, not anything internal to the explanations themselves.

I'm trying to figure out whether this specific asymmetry has been isolated and named in the philosophical literature. It isn't quite the "AI effect" — the familiar phenomenon where successes in AI get redefined as not-really-intelligence the moment they're achieved. That's about shifting the goalposts for what counts as intelligence. What I'm describing is different: it's about how the very possession of a reductive explanation gets treated as epistemically discredentialing when the subject is artificial but not when it's biological, even when the explanations are structurally analogous. Is there an established term for this? If not — and this is what I'm really after — how would you characterize its boundaries precisely? Does it collapse into some more general bias like in-group favoritism, is it a special case of genetic reasoning (valuing a trait differently depending on its origin), or is it a distinct epistemic phenomenon with its own structure? I'm less interested in a label than in a clear account of exactly what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges lie.
