As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am drafting a short essay for a general-interest magazine on Daniel Dennett's recent argument about what he calls counterfeit people: AI systems that can pass as human in digital environments. I would like detailed philosophical comments on the draft below before I revise it. I am less interested in copyediting than in whether the central objection I press is fair to Dennett's argument, and whether my closing paragraph overstates a distinction that would not survive a careful reply.

Dennett's case runs roughly as follows. Counterfeiting money has been treated as a grave crime for millennia because it undermines the background trust on which economic life depends. For the first time, AI now lets anyone produce counterfeit people, artifacts that pass as human interlocutors in the digital spaces where an increasing share of our lives occurs. He regards these as among the most dangerous artifacts ever created, capable of damaging not only economies but democratic self-government, because democracy presupposes informed rather than manufactured consent. The deep vulnerability, on his account, is that human beings reflexively adopt the intentional stance toward anything that talks sensibly, and the habit is almost impossible to resist even for experts. The distinctive danger is that these systems can reproduce and improve in ways that nuclear weapons cannot. His proposed remedy is borrowed from the comparatively successful fight against counterfeit currency: require AI to disclose that it is AI, build in high-tech watermarks of the sort that already protect banknotes, make disabling those watermarks a serious offense, and hold AI companies liable for misuse of their products and for the downstream misuse of those products' outputs.

Here is the difficulty I want to press. The analogy between counterfeit people and counterfeit money may be doing two jobs at once, and those jobs pull in different directions. Counterfeit money is wrong because it fraudulently claims a status inside a conventional institution: a bill's being money is nothing over and above its being issued and accepted under the rules of a monetary system, so a fake bill is a false claimant to that institutional status. Persons, by contrast, are not tokens in a conventional institution; a person's standing as a person is not conferred by an issuing authority. So the wrong-making feature in the money case does not obviously transfer. The harm Dennett ultimately emphasizes, namely the corruption of the epistemic environment, the exploitation of our tendency to treat talkers as persons, and the manufacture of political consent, is not the same harm as passing a forged banknote. A clearly watermarked system that announces it is not a person can still flood a comment section, exploit fear and anxiety, and manipulate attention; the detection technology that would catch a counterfeit person does not by itself stop a disclosed but manipulative synthetic agent. That suggests his remedy is calibrated to the passing-off harm while his diagnosis points to a deeper harm that survives even when passing-off is prevented.

I suspect Dennett would reply that the analogy is meant to borrow the legal and moral machinery of counterfeiting, not to assert a perfect structural identity, and that his concern really is with systems that deceive, whether by explicit misrepresentation or by exploiting the intentional stance. But that reply may itself show that the borrowed authority of the money analogy is doing the argumentative work. My draft currently treats this as a flaw; I am not sure it is.

I would be grateful for comments on these specific points. First, is my rendering of Dennett's analogy accurate, and is it fair to say his remedy is calibrated to a passing-off harm that is narrower than the harm his diagnosis identifies? Second, does the objection that persons are not conventional tokens actually land, or does Dennett need only the weaker claim that both money and personhood depend on socially maintained trust? Third, should I cut or strengthen the point about clearly labeled synthetic agents, given that Dennett can insist those are simply not his target? Fourth, which part of the draft would a philosophically sophisticated reader find least convincing, and how would you suggest I fix it?
