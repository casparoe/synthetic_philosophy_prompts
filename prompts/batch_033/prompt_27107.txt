Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have long argued past each other about the Platonia dilemma and the broader class of one-shot symmetric dilemmas it exemplifies. The first camp is the superrationalist, in the tradition associated with Hofstadter: in a symmetric dilemma, a rational player should ask which strategy they would want every player in the same situation to adopt, and should adopt that strategy, because each player's reasoning is the common output of a single situation. The second camp is the causal decision theorist: a player should choose the act that maximizes expected utility given their credences about the causally independent choices of the others, and no argument from symmetry can substitute for a causal or strategic link between players.

Begin by fixing the shared case. Let there be N players, symmetrically situated and unable to communicate. Each must independently choose whether to send a message; a player wins a large fixed prize if and only if exactly one player sends, and no one wins anything if zero send or more than one send. This is the Platonia dilemma. Both camps accept this description as neutral. The Luring Lottery, a distinct but related superrationality experiment with a different payoff structure (a fixed prize divided among all entrants and awarded by random draw), may be brought in where it illuminates the cruxes, but the primary case is Platonia.

Then produce the document such a collaboration would yield:

(1) A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. These should include the arithmetic and game-theoretic facts: that sending weakly dominates not sending for each player taken individually; that if every player independently sends with probability p, each player's expected payoff is p(1−p)^(N−1) times the prize, maximized at p = 1/N; that the only symmetric Nash equilibrium is universal sending, which yields everyone nothing; that among pure Nash equilibria, the only ones in which anyone wins are those with exactly one sender, and nothing in the symmetric situation singles out which player that should be; and that the superrational mixture is not a Nash equilibrium. The list should also include any further non-vacuous claims the two sides discover they can jointly affirm about what the dilemma shows and what it does not show.

(2) A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement, with the superrationalist's formulation and the causal decision theorist's formulation each phrased so that its own side would sign it. At least these disagreements should appear: (a) whether the object of rational choice is the individual act or the common strategy; (b) whether the weak dominance of sending retains its normative force in a case where universal compliance with dominance reasoning yields everyone nothing while a symmetric non-equilibrium mixture yields each a positive expected payoff; (c) whether the original dilemma is relevantly equivalent to the case in which each player chooses a single decision algorithm that every copy will run; and (d) how much of the dispute is substantive rather than verbal.

(3) A crux section. For each substantive crux, give the case or argument that both camps accept as bearing on it, and record in advance each side's prediction or diagnosis of that case. At minimum, address these cruxes. First, the algorithm-selection case: all players are told that each is running a copy of the same decision algorithm, which takes only the game description and a private random seed as input; does this make the superrational strategy rationally mandatory, and does the original dilemma differ from this case only in degree of transparency? Second, the sealed-choice case: each player is told that the other players' choices have already been made and sealed, so that the player's choice cannot influence them; what does each camp say, and does the agreement or divergence here reveal what is doing the work in the original case? Third, the iterated case: if the same players face the same dilemma repeatedly with full feedback, does the dispute survive, and what would each side predict about the emergence of turn-taking or mixed strategies? Fourth, the Luring Lottery variant: does the superrationalist's reasoning carry over cleanly to the Luring Lottery's different payoff structure, and does the causal decision theorist's objection shift in force or kind?

(4) A closing note separating the verbal from the substantive. State precisely which portion of the dispute, if any, is a disagreement about the word "rational" rather than about what players should do, and which portion would remain even if both sides were forced to use the other's vocabulary.

Throughout, write the document as something both camps could actually put their names to: the agreed claims should be neither vacuous nor slanted, the disagreement formulations should be ones each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view, and the predictions should be concrete enough that a variant experiment could confirm or disconfirm them. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the collaboration is over the structure of the dilemma itself, not over who said what.
