I'm a PhD student working on the epistemology of testimony, and I keep tripping over something from a neighbouring corner of epistemology. A friend of mine who does formal epistemology tried to explain the bootstrapping problem to me over coffee, and I wrote down his gloss. It went roughly like this: the bootstrapping problem shows reliabilism is in serious trouble, because a reliabilist has to say that if your colour vision is in fact reliable, then looking at a red wall gives you knowledge that the wall is red. But from that knowledge you can infer that your colour vision got this particular case right, and after enough such cases you can infer that your colour vision is reliable in general. Since that whole route is circular — you're using your colour vision to vouch for your colour vision — it can't give you genuine justification, so reliabilism, or any view that lets perception justify you without you already knowing your senses are trustworthy, is refuted. He added that the 'easy knowledge' problem is just another name for the same thing.

I suspect this runs together a few different claims and overstates what the argument actually establishes, but I don't know the literature well enough to say exactly where. Could you go through it bit by bit and tell me what a careful epistemologist would accept, what they'd want to qualify, and wether the conclusion really follows? In particular, are bootstrapping and easy knowledge genuinely the same problem, or is that itself one of the conflations? And is the circularity as obviously fatal as my friend made it sound, or are there respectable responses that bite that bullet?
