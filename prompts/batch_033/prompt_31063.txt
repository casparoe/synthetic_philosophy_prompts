I am giving a departmental colloquium next month in which I defend a hinge-epistemological account of our most basic certainties, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis, stated plainly: the commitments that make inquiry possible — that there is an external world, that my senses are not systematically deceptive, that the past did not begin five minutes ago — are neither ordinary empirical beliefs nor conclusions justified by evidence. They are hinge commitments: they are not themselves up for assessment in the ordinary course of inquiry, not because evidence happens to be lacking, but because they are what we rely on in order to assess evidence at all. From this I draw two negative conclusions. First, the Cartesian skeptic's demand for evidence that these commitments are true is not a demand we are unable to meet but a demand that has stepped outside the practices in which evidence, doubt, and justification do their work. Second, G. E. Moore's display of his hands and his claim to know an external world on that basis misdescribes the situation, because his perceptual claim is not functioning as an evidence-answerable premise but as something whose standing is already presupposed by the practice of taking anything as evidence. I will present the view as a live epistemological position, not as Wittgenstein exegesis, though I will acknowledge the debt to On Certainty.

My positive argument, in compressed form, is this: any challenge to a belief must rely on grounds that are not themselves in question in that context; a fully general skeptical challenge therefore presupposes the very kind of standing commitments it purports to call into question; the skeptical demand is thus not an unanswered question but a demand made from outside the practice of asking; and Moore is right that the hinge is more certain than the skeptic's premises but wrong to treat that certainty as a piece of empirical knowledge, because hinges play a normative, rule-like role rather than a descriptive one.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium of roughly twenty-five people, mostly epistemologists. Two are contextualists in the Lewis/DeRose style, who think knowledge ascriptions are sensitive to conversational stakes and that skeptical and ordinary ascriptions can both be true relative to different standards. One is a safety theorist who thinks knowledge is true belief that is safe from error in nearby worlds and is broadly sympathetic to external-world knowledge. Two are evidentialists who think justification is a matter of the subject's evidence and that any commitment held without evidence is thereby unjustified or arational in a way that should make us nervous. One senior person is a committed Moorean: he thinks the skeptic's premises are less plausible than the claim that he knows he has hands, and that any theory that cannot secure that knowledge has already refuted itself. There are also several graduate students with no fixed allegiance, and one philosopher of language who thinks the interesting questions here are semantic rather than epistemological and will press on what it could even mean for a commitment to be exempt from assessment. They are polite but exacting, and they will notice if a reply quietly changes the subject from knowledge to warranted assertibility or from justification to psychology.

Draft my prep sheet. For each objection, give me: who in the room is most likely to raise it, how they would phrase it in their own vocabulary, how damaging it really is to my position, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is softest. Order the objections by roughly how likely they are to arise combined with how much damage they could do. Flag which objections I should concede up front rather than resist, and which I must hold. Include the question I should be most hoping nobody asks, and say why it is dangerous and what, if anything, I can say if it comes.
