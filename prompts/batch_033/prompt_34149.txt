Write a sonnet about Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument against the thesis that mental states are luminous — that is, that whenever you're in a given mental state, you're in a position to know you're in it. The octave should build the intuitive appeal of luminosity: the thought that if you're in pain, or feeling cold, you can't be wrong about it, because what could possibly stand between you and that knowledge? The volta should introduce Williamson's sorites-style reductio: consider a slow, imperceptible warming from cold to comfortable. If feeling cold were luminous, you'd know you feel cold at each moment you do. But adjacent moments in the series are indiscriminable, so your confidence couldn't drop sharply between them. That forces you either to claim you know you're cold even when you plainly aren't, or to admit you didn't know you were cold even when you were — either way, luminosity fails. The closing couplet should capture what the argument really shows: that knowledge requires a margin for error, and that our mental lives are less transparent to us than we supposed. The poem should be accurate enough that an epistemologist would recognize Williamson's specific argument rather than a generic skepticism about introspection. Please write the answer in American English.
