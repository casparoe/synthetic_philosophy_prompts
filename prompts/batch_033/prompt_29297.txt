I am a novelist. My current novel turns on a decades-long rivalry between two game theorists: one who has spent his career defending the Nash bargaining solution — the point maximizing the product of the parties' utility gains over the disagreement point — as the correct account of cooperative bargaining, and one who has come to regard the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution — the maximal feasible point on the segment from the disagreement point to the utopia point of the parties' best feasible payoffs — as the right one. Late in the novel, the two men agree to an adversarial collaboration and produce a single joint statement they can both sign. I need that document written in full, because the novel will quote from it, and it has to be rigorous enough that a game theorist would believe both men put their names to it.

Act as an expert in cooperative game theory and axiomatic bargaining. Think carefully, and before you draft the statement, spend some time in private preparation: restate each solution concept precisely, lay out the axiomatizations, including where Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives and Kalai–Smorodinsky's individual monotonicity part ways, and work through at least two numerically specified bargaining problems on which the two solutions diverge. Use that preparation to calibrate the document; then present only the joint statement itself.

The document should contain the following:

1. A numbered section titled "Claims Both Parties Accept": substantive claims about the setting (a compact, convex feasible set of utility pairs and a disagreement point), the shared axioms, the cases in which the two solutions coincide, and what each solution is trying to capture.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one exact point of divergence, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position — in particular, the Nash side's commitment to independence of irrelevant alternatives and the Kalai–Smorodinsky side's commitment to individual monotonicity. The table should also flag any apparent disagreements that are merely verbal.

3. A section titled "Cruxes": the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of the two men.

4. For each crux, a concrete bargaining problem with actual numbers that both parties agree bears on that crux, together with each side's prediction, recorded in advance, of what the correct solution is for that problem and why, and what each side would count as evidence against its own view.

The joint statement should be neither vacuous nor slanted: the agreed claims should be substantive, and each side's statement of its own view should be one its own camp would sign, not a caricature. It should read like a document written by two people who respect each other and are not pretending to agree. Do not include references or citations; these two men are writing for each other, not for a journal.
