I'm an undergrad taking a course on Mill this semester, and I also sit on my university's student conduct board. We handle complaints about student speech—posters, chalking, social media posts, flyers, things said at events—and it always comes down to whether the speech is protected or whether it can be restricted because it harms people. The university policy says speech can be restricted only to prevent harm, and the board keeps arguing in circles about what even counts as harm. I want something I can actually use in hearings.

Please write me a decision procedure for whether a speech complaint should move forward under the harm principle. It has to be something a panel of non-philosophers can run in maybe half an hour: questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to the panel because it's a question of values rather than facts. Make it faithful to Mill's actual view, not the pop version. In particular, deal with the hard distinctions: harm vs. mere offense, speech that directly incites harm vs. speech that only makes harm more likely, and whether a hostile or degrading social environment counts as harm. Then run the procedure on three cases: a student group putting up flyers for a speaker that many students say makes them feel unsafe; a student newspaper column arguing that a campus minority group is, on average, less intelligent; and an anonymous social media post urging people to physically confront a named student. I want a committed answer: actually draw the lines and give your reasons, not a menu of considerations, and tell me where you think the procedure is least defensible.
