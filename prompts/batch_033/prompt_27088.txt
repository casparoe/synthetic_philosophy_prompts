I have recently started working through the foundations of statistical mechanics in earnest, and I have been surprised to find that the debate over the direction of time is far less settled than the textbooks suggest. The dispute has had a stable shape for decades, and I would like a diagnosis of why it does not move. Everyone agrees that the fundamental dynamical laws are, so far as we can tell, time-symmetric, and that the manifest world is not: we remember the past and not the future, causes precede their effects, and the future strikes us as open in a way the past does not. Everyone also agrees, at least as a starting point, that these asymmetries have something to do with the thermodynamic arrow—the fact that entropy was low in the past and tends to increase. Beyond that, the positions appear frozen. One camp holds that the thermodynamic arrow is the fundamental asymmetry, and that the psychological, causal, and epistemic arrows all reduce to it; on this view, a low-entropy initial condition plus statistical mechanics does the explanatory work, and whatever remains is a technical matter. A second camp argues that the thermodynamic arrow is not mind-independent in the way the first camp assumes: entropy increases only relative to a choice of coarse-graining and a probability distribution over microstates, and those choices are licensed by our epistemic situation or our capacity to intervene, which already presupposes the very temporal orientation they are meant to explain. A third camp concludes that time-symmetric dynamics plus a de facto boundary condition cannot explain a lawlike asymmetry, and that we should therefore posit some time-asymmetric ingredient in the laws themselves, in quantum mechanics, or in causation. Each side can state the others' views with ease, and none of them moves.

My working hypothesis is that this is neither an empirical disagreement nor a merely verbal one, but a disagreement about what an explanation of an asymmetry must do: the first camp accepts a boundary condition plus statistics as explanatory, the second refuses to accept posits that smuggle in the asymmetry being explained, and the third insists that a lawlike fact requires a lawlike source. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is my hypothesis right, or is there a hidden empirical or formal question at stake, or are the camps answering different questions under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a theorem, a conceptual distinction, a reformulation of the question—or should we expect the impasse to persist? Please engage the positions as I have described them and be direct about whether my hypothesis is defensible.
