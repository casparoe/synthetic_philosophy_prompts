I'm a retired geologist — spent forty years mapping strata and testing hypotheses about depositional environments, so I'm pretty comfortable with the idea that good science makes risky predictions. I've recently gotten into philosophy of science, reading Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos, the usual suspects, and I now sit on the board of a small private foundation that funds "basic scientific research." Our charter says we only fund work that is empirically testable — no armchair stuff.

We've got a proposal that's split the board. A young evolutionary biologist wants to test competing adaptive hypotheses for why paternal care evolved in a clade of poison frogs (the idea being that in species where males guard egg clutches, predation pressure on unattended eggs should be higher, and she's got a nice comparitive design with field sites across three countries). One board member — a retired engineer who's read just enough Popper to be dangerous — says we should reject it. His argument: Popper himself showed that Darwinism is unfalsifiable, a metaphysical research programme not a real scientific theory, and adaptionist storytelling in evolutionary biology is just that — stories. Another board member (a working ecologist) points out that Popper later recanted that view, and anyway the proposal states specific, falsifiable predictions about predation rates and phylogenetic signal.

I've been asked to draft the decision memo for our meeting next month. It should recommend fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with clear reasoning, and it should engage the Popper objection seriously rather than just citing his recantation and moving on. Also, I'd like the memo to include a short section — maybe a paragraph — saying what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding would flip the recommendation, and where you think the reasoning is most likely to go wrong. (I'm new enough to philosophy to know I should be nervous about my own confidence.)

Oh, and please dont make it sound like a philosophy seminar paper — the board needs something they can actually vote on.
