I teach an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I am putting together a handout for a week on concept learning. In the previous session we contrasted two pictures: on one, a concept is a Boolean combination of primitive features, a checklist of properties that each object has on its own; on the other, many of the concepts people actually acquire are irreducibly relational, concerning how the elements of a scene stand to one another rather than what each element is like by itself. I want to close the week with a diagnostic Bongard problem, the kind with a left panel of six boxes that all satisfy a hidden rule and a right panel of six boxes that all violate it. The exercise should be engineered so that a learner whose hypothesis space contains only Boolean combinations of unary shape properties is forced to misclassify a held-out item, while a learner that can represent binary relations among shapes classifies it correctly. The point is not to win a rhetorical point by stipulation; it is to make students feel, from the inside, that learning a concept from a handful of positive and negative examples is not a single ability, because what you can induce from six and six depends on which hypotheses you can even formulate.

Please construct the problem to the following specifications, and then demonstrate that it meets them. The hidden rule must be genuinely relational — adjacency, containment, alignment, relative size, same-kind contact, or something of that sort — and it must not be statable as any Boolean combination of unary properties, where the available unary properties are shape type, size, orientation, filled or unfilled, and location rounded to a coarse three-by-three grid. Each panel has exactly six boxes; each box contains two to four simple figures (circles, squares, triangles); and everything must be specified in words or ASCII so that the problem is fully determinate without an image. In the write-up, state the rule in one sentence, confirm that each of the twelve boxes has the status the rule assigns it, and then give two ordinary held-out boxes (one positive, one negative) and one probe box that does the diagnostic work. The probe should be paired with a comparison box such that the two boxes agree on the entire distribution of the specified unary features but differ relationally, so that the feature-based learner must classify the pair alike while the relational rule separates them. Then walk through the verdict each learner gives on the probe, explain why it gives that verdict, and identify the feature of the case that is doing the work; say what the example collapses into if that feature is removed. Finally, say how a determined defender of the feature-based view would resist — for instance by enriching the feature set, by rejecting the probe as an unnatural rule, or by denying that the pair is a fair test — and assess whether that defense can succeed without quietly smuggling relational predicates back in under another name, or whether it simply concedes the lesson the handout is meant to teach.
