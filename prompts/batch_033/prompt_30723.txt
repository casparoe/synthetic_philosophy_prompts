I keep noticing a pattern in ordinary 'if' talk that I don't have a word for. Someone will say, perfectly naturally:

'If Alice had come to the party, it would have been a great party. But if Alice and Bob had both come, it would have been a disaster.'

Both halves go through fine, and the pair feels coherent in that order. Now flip it:

'If Alice and Bob had both come, it would have been a disaster. But if Alice had come, it would have been a great party.'

The second sentence now sounds like a retraction — like the speaker is taking back the first claim — even though it's the same two sentances as before. Not a word changed.

Here's a future-tensed version, same shape: 'If it rains tomorrow, the match will be cancelled; but if it rains and the ground staff cover the pitch, the match will go ahead.' Fine in that order. Reverse it, and the rain-only sentence sounds like the speaker forgott what they just said.

So: adding detail to the 'if' part flips the conclusion, and the pair is only comfortably assertable in one direction. I half-remember from a logic course that 'if' doesn't permit adding extra conditions to the antecedent the way 'all' does. But I'm not after the inference rule — I'm after the order-sensitivity itself. Is there an established name for this? And if the standard name belongs to the backward-looking 'would have' cases, is the future-tensed version the same phenomenon or a different one? If the asymmetry has no name of its own, I'd want a term that captures exactly this: the weaker conditional is assertable after the stronger one only when it's understood as a correction or retraction, never as a plain addition.
