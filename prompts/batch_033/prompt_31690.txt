Imagine a behavioral geneticist and a philosopher of science who have, over the course of a year, argued about what heritability coefficients do and do not show. I want you to draft the joint statement they could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The two positions are these. The first researcher — call her the evidential-weight theorist — holds that a high heritability estimate for a trait such as general cognitive ability, in a specified population and range of environments, is a substantial and robust finding: it shows that genetic differences among individuals are a major source of the observed variation in that population, and that purely environmental accounts of individual differences are incomplete. The second researcher — call him the deflationary theorist — holds that heritability coefficients, while technically well-defined, are systematically overread: they are local descriptive statistics, tied to a particular population and a particular range of environments, that neither measure how genetic a trait is nor rank the causal importance of genes against environments, and that support no direct conclusions about the likely effects of interventions or about differences between populations.

Please produce the document as follows.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both researchers can sign. Cover at least: the formal definition of heritability as a ratio of variances; the distinction between narrow-sense and broad-sense heritability; the point that heritability is a property of populations, not of individuals; the point that high heritability does not imply immutability or the futility of environmental intervention; the point that within-population heritability does not by itself license inferences about the sources of between-population differences; and the dependence of any estimate on the range of environments sampled.

2. Disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give a neutral formulation of the question, the evidential-weight theorist's position phrased as she would accept it, and the deflationary theorist's position phrased as he would accept it. Include at least these three: whether the proportion of phenotypic variance associated with genetic differences is an acceptable measure of the causal or explanatory importance of genes; whether a coefficient that shifts when the environment changes can support conclusions extending beyond the studied range; and whether high heritability in one population provides any non-negligible evidence about the likely magnitude of genetic influence on differences between populations.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, and indicate which side would have to move if the crux resolved against them.

4. Tests. For each crux, specify a concrete empirical case, intervention study, or body of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that evidence will show.

5. Verbal-disagreement assessment. State which parts of the dispute, if any, are best explained by different uses of terms such as importance, genetic, or cause, and which parts would remain substantive under shared definitions.
