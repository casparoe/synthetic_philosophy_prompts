The many-worlds interpretation tells us that every quantum measurement outcome occurs in some branch, which makes the Born rule — the rule that assigns probabilities to measurement outcomes — look like a category mistake. If everything happens, what are probabilities probabilities of? Two prominent lines of response have emerged. The decision-theoretic program, associated with David Deutsch and David Wallace, attempts to derive the Born rule from rational-choice axioms, arguing that a rational agent who knows they will branch must act as if they assign credences equal to the squared amplitudes. The self-locating uncertainty approach, developed in different forms by Lev Vaidman and by Charles Sebens and Sean Carroll, instead treats branching as creating a situation of self-locating ignorance: before measurement you should be uncertain about which branch you will find yourself in, and the squared amplitude tells you how to distribute that uncertainty. I want to understand how these two strategies differ at the architectural level — not just their conclusions, but what each asks you to accept about the nature of probability, about what a rational agent in a branching universe should care about, and about what the Born rule is even a theory of. Where do they genuinely conflict on the physics or the epistemology, and where are they answering different questions that happen to share some vocabulary?
