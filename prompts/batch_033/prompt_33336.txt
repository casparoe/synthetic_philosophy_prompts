Two friends of mine spent an entire dinner arguing about the Sleeping Beauty problem, and when they asked me to referee I realized I couldn't. The setup: Beauty goes to sleep Sunday. A fair coin is flipped. If heads, she's woken once, Monday. If tails, she's woken Monday and Tuesday, but the Monday waking is erased from her memory. On each waking she's asked for her credence that the coin landed heads.

Sam said it's obviously one half. The coin is fair and Beauty knew before she slept that she'd wake at least once which ever way it landed. Waking is something she was certain would happen under heads and under tails, so it's no evidence at all. Her Sunday credence of one half should just carry over.

Dana said no — waking is self-locating evidence. When Beauty wakes she doesn't know whether it's Monday or Tuesday. There are three waking episodes she could be in: heads-Monday, tails-Monday, tails-Tuesday. Her experience can't distinguish them, so she should give each an equal share, and heads gets only one of the three. So one third.

Sam came back: that's double-counting. The coin toss doesn't create three possible worlds, it creates two. In the tails world she wakes twice, but the Tuesday waking isn't a second outcome of the coin — it's the same tails outcome again. You're treating centered possibilities as if they were possible worlds, but probability lives on worlds. So one half.

Dana said: then why does Beauty go broke if she bets on heads at even odds every time she wakes? Across many runs, two thirds of all wakings are tails-wakings. If she pays fifty cents, every waking, for a ticket that pays a dollar on heads, a bookie will clean her out. A credence of one half commits her to a systematically exploitable price, and that's the standard test of irrationality.

Sam said that bookie is just exploiting the fact that she bets twice under tails. If she makes one bet per run — say only the Monday bet counts — then one half is exactly the fair price. So the Dutch book changes the payoff structure, not her evidence. All it shows is that per-waking and per-run betting questions have different fair odds, so betting can't settle what she should believe.

Dana said: okay, but the problem isn't asking what odds govern per-run bets. It's asking how confident she should be, on a given waking, that the coin landed heads. She's in a particular waking episode. Run the experiment many times and look at all the waking episodes: one in three is a heads-waking. If her credence should track the frequency of situations like the one she's actually in, it should be one third. If you think it shouldn't track that frequency, tell me why not.

Sam said: because her situation isn't just "a waking episode"; it's "a waking episode in a run whose coin is fair." The fraction of runs that land heads is one half. Which frequency she should track depends on whether she's estimating the coin or estimating the waking, and the problem asks about the coin.

Dana said: that can't be right, because it has no limiting case. Make tails a thousand wakings instead of two. You'd still say one half? Surely when she wakes she should be almost certain the coin was tails. The original case is just the small version of that.

Sam said the thousand-waking version actually proves my point, because it shows the problem is ambiguous. "What's the chance the coin landed heads?" is still one half; "what fraction of all wakings are heads-wakings?" is one in a thousand and one. People's intuitions flip because they slide between those two questions. The original question is the first one.

Dana said: no, the slide is yours. On Sunday, "what's the chance the coin lands heads" is a question about the coin. On waking, the same words are a question about her current centered situation, because she has to locate herself among possible wakings in order to answer it. And your earlier no-evidence rule — don't update on something that's certain under both hypotheses — assumes the evidence is the same proposition in both hypotheses. But "I am awake now" isn't the same proposition across the two worlds; "now" picks out a different time in the heads world than in the tails world. That's why the rule doesn't apply here.

Sam said: but on Sunday Beauty is certain she'll wake up with amnesia and be asked for her credence. She can predict the whole thing. If she's rational she should follow a policy she'd endorse on Sunday, and Sunday Beauty says the coin is fifty-fifty. She would not sign off on a plan to wake up and say one third without having learned anything, and she can't say on Sunday what information she'll have gained.

Dana said: there's no requirement that Sunday Beauty be able to state the information in advance. What she gains is self-locating information — that a waking is happening now — and Sunday Beauty can't express that de se fact about Monday or Tuesday until she's actually there. Sunday Beauty can predict that Monday Beauty will say one third and still keep her own Sunday credence at one half, because they're answering different questions on different evidence.
