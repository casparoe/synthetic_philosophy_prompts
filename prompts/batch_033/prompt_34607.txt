I have been puzzling over a kind of sentence that sounds as though the speaker has contradicted herself, even though the sentence can plainly be true, and I cannot find a satisfying account of what kind of failure it is or where its boundaries lie. The cleanest case is something like this: It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining. There is no contradiction there, since the weather and my state of mind are different facts, and a person can easily be ignorant of the weather. Yet the sentence seems impossible to come out with in the first person, and the wrongness feels different from ordinary surprise or bad manners. What puzzles me more is how narrow the wrongness is. Shift the tense and it vanishes: It was raining, but I did not believe that it was raining is a perfectly ordinary confession of past ignorance. Shift the person and it also vanishes: It is raining, but she does not believe that it is raining is just a report of someone else's ignorance. Only the first-person present-tense case has this quality.

A second construction seems related but perhaps different. Consider: It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining. This too can be true, since beliefs can be false, and yet it is just as unsayable. But here the speaker is not merely ignorant of the fact; she attributes to herself a positive belief that conflicts with it. I cannot tell whether this is the same underlying phenomenon as the first case or a distinct one, and whether either case, or the tense-and-person asymmetry itself, has an established name of its own. I am less interested in a label than in the carving: what exactly goes wrong, whether it is a logical contradiction, a conversational or pragmatic rule, or something about self-knowledge, and where the boundaries of the phenomenon lie. For instance, the sentence I do not know whether I believe that it is raining does not seem to have the same unsayable quality, and I would like to know why it escapes the difficulty.
