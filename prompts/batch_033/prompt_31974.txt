Analyze the 2000 US presidential election in Florida as a case study for the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. I already know the formal result: with three or more candidates, any non-dictatorial voting rule that always selects a winner and lets every candidate win under some preference profile is manipulable, so strategy-proofness and non-dictatorship can't coexist. What I want is the careful mapping. Who are the relevant voters, what are the alternatives (Bush, Gore, Nader; and where do the butterfly ballot and the recount fit — are they part of the rule or part of the environment?), whose sincere preferences were what, and who actually voted strategically or was pressured to. Then say honestly what the theorem explains and what it doesn't: is Nader's spoiler effect an instance of manipulability, or is it a coordination problem the theorem is silent about? And what does Florida do to the theorem — does it vindicate it, or does it show that 'strategy-proofness' in the theorem's sense is too narrow to capture what actually went wrong? Pin the model down and tell me where it stops.
