I cover emerging technology for a science and ideas magazine, and I'm writing a feature on mind uploading and cryonics. In nearly every interview, when I ask whether the upload or the revived patient would really be the same person, I get some version of the line that identity is not what matters — what matters is psychological continuity, and Parfit settled this with the teletransporter. The conversation then moves straight to the practical payoff: uploading is survival enough, cryonics is a rational bet, and a person scanned into two or more copies has nothing to fear, because each copy is psychologically continuous with the original.

I've come to distrust this move, but I can't articulate where it goes wrong — or whether it goes wrong at all. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that "identity is not what matters" could be making, from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false, and state what practical conclusion it would actually license. Then tell me which reading, if any, would actually support the inference from "the copy is psychologically continuous with me" to "the copy is my survival in the sense that matters" — and which readings my sources are most likely sliding between when they use the slogan to dismiss my question. I want a menu I can take back into the interviews, not a single settled interpretation.
