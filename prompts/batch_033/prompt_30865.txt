I am a novelist, and I am at work on a novel whose secondary character, Miriam Hart, is a retired competition economist who for thirty years wrote a fortnightly newspaper column in which she ruled, case by case, on whether some situation amounted to a genuine race to the bottom — the kind of self-defeating competition sometimes called a Moloch problem. I have drafted the record of her published rulings, but I have deliberately left her underlying principle unstated, because a late scene turns on her being consulted about a new case. I would like you to help me make that principle precise enough that the scene will feel earned rather than arbitrary.

Her published rulings, as I have drafted them, are these.

1. A coastal town's fishing boats overfished their shared bay until the stock collapsed and the local fleet was ruined. She called this a textbook race to the bottom and later endorsed the quota scheme the town adopted.

2. Two neighbouring states drifted into a naval arms race that ended in a war neither government had wanted, with heavy civilian losses. She called it the purest multipolar trap she had ever written about.

3. A school was caught up in an annual competition among pupils over increasingly expensive branded trainers; each cohort tired of the game within a term or two, and nobody outside the competition was much affected. She wrote that it was wasteful and mildly absurd but not, in her sense, a race to the bottom.

4. The shops on a town's high street each extended their opening hours until all of them were open around the clock; takings did not rise, the shopkeepers were exhausted, and the hours could have been wound back within a week by a simple agreement. She declined to support a by-law and said it was the shopkeepers' own sleep to lose.

5. Two rival software firms in the same city bid up one another's salaries for engineers until both firms' margins were squeezed, with the engineers as the obvious beneficiaries. She ruled that this was competition doing its ordinary work rather than a race to the bottom.

The new case I have planned is this. The shops on the same high street as case 4 have lately taken to amplified street advertising: each shop plays recorded messages through a loudspeaker, each turns up the volume to be heard over the others, no shop gains custom from the practice, and the noise has become a serious nuisance to the residents who live above and behind the shops. A single by-law, or a voluntary agreement among the shopkeepers, could end the practice overnight at negligible cost.

Would Miriam Hart call this a race to the bottom, and would she support the by-law? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the principle she seems to be running, the other principles that fit her published rulings just as well, and which further case would best tell those principles apart. Please set out the candidate principles, say what each predicts about the new case, give your best guess about her ruling with your reasons for preferring that principle, and specify the further case that would discriminate most cleanly.
