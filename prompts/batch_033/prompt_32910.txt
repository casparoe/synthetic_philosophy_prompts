Consider Kavka's toxin puzzle: an eccentric billionaire offers you one million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a mildly nauseating but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money is transferred the moment you form the intention; whether you actually drink the toxin tomorrow is irrelevant. You know tonight that tomorrow you will have no reason to drink it — the money is already secured, the sickness is guaranteed, and there is no further incentive. Yet you also know that you can only receive the money if your intention tonight is genuine.

Please give me your credence that a perfectly rational agent can, under these conditions, form the intention to drink the toxin. Decompose your estimate into at least the following sub-questions: (1) whether intentions are a distinct kind of mental state that cannot be formed by purely theoretical deliberation about what one will have reason to do, or whether they reduce to beliefs-plus-desires such that knowing the future payoffs settles the question; (2) whether the rationality of an intention is constrained by the rationality of the action intended, so that intending to do what you know you will lack reason to do is itself irrational; (3) whether the rational agent has access to self-binding strategies and, if so, whether their availability changes what counts as a rational intention in this case. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question and show how they combine into your overall credence. Then identify which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and describe what argument or evidence would most shift it.
