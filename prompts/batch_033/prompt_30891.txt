I've spent the last six months researching a novel about an evolutionary biologist who gets drawn into a public debate with an intelligent design advocate, and somewhere along the way I read Dennett's Darwin's Dangerous Idea and became kind of a convert to the whole crane-vs-skyhook picture. You know: cranes are actual explanatory mechanisms that build complexity from simpler parts through cumulative steps, and skyhooks are fake explanations that just assume the thing they're supposed to explain — like saying consciousness exists because of an immaterial soul that magically does the consciousness part. And his related point about greedy reductionism — that it's a real mistake to say things like "love is just oxytocin" as though the higher-level pattern doesn't matter — this also seemed right to me. But here's what's been nagging at me. In fiction, I use skyhook-y explanations constantly. A character does something inexplicable and I gesture at "the mystery of human nature" and the reader accepts it. A plot turns on a coincidence so perfect it feels meant, and no one complains. So I've started wondering whether the crane/skyhook distinction is as sharp as Dennett makes it sound, or whether it's more of a rhetorical hammer for hitting creationists than a real piece of philosophical machinery. What I want to know is: what would actually count against this framework as a general thesis about what makes an explanation good? What would a genuine counterexample look like — a domain where a skyhook-style explanation is the right kind of explanation, not just a placeholder for one we haven't found yet? And is greedy reductionism a position anyone actually holds, or is it a strawman that makes Dennett's own reductive project look moderate by comparision? I want the defeaters laid out clearly, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want an honest answer about whether the framework has been insulated against falsification — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view that's more methodological than empirical.
