I run a small AI safety reading group, and last week we discussed how one should evaluate a proposed alignment technique. Two of the members, Mara and Jonah, got into a long argument that none of us could referee. I'll reproduce the exchange as best I can, because I'd like you to adjudicate it.

Mara opened with the claim that when we ask whether an alignment method is safe, the only standard that matters is worst-case behavior. Deployment is adversarial, she said: if there is any input or environment where the system misbehaves, someone or something will eventually find it, and an average-case guarantee is a statement about the world staying friendly, which is exactly what alignment cannot assume. Jonah replied that for learned systems the worst case is not something we can compute or even approximate; the space of environments is too large and the model's behavior too opaque, so a "worst-case guarantee" is either vacuous, holding only for a toy formalization, or it is a claim about a finite test suite, which is just a small-sample estimate of average behavior. Mara answered that our inability to compute the exact worst case does not license retreating to the average; it licenses trying harder to approximate the worst case, through adversarial training, red-teaming, and expanding the set of hard cases, whereas lowering the bar to typical behavior gives the appearance of rigor while ignoring the part of the distribution that matters. Jonah shot back that adversarial training and red-teaming are themselves average-case evaluations over the distribution of attacks we happened to generate: we have replaced the original distribution with a slightly different one and then reported typical performance under it, so calling the result "worst-case" is a relabeling rather than a different kind of guarantee.

At that point Jonah shifted to a cost argument: no useful learned system will ever satisfy a true worst-case requirement, so demanding one means refusing to deploy anything, and that refusal is itself a risky decision. A system that is safe in almost all realistic cases, with monitoring and the ability to shut it down, can be better than the perfect system that does not exist. Mara replied that this only works if the remaining cases are minor; if the tail contains catastrophic failures, the average can look excellent while the policy is reckless, and "realistic cases" is exactly the set an adversary or a distribution shift will leave. For sufficiently high stakes, she said, a small probability of disaster dominates the calculation. Jonah then pressed on the notion of a worst case itself: once an adversary can optimize against the deployed system, there is no maximally bad input, just an escalating arms race, so the only meaningful question is how robust the system is across a distribution of plausible shifts, which is an average-case question about the shift distribution. Mara denied that this was average-case reasoning; it is worst-case over the adversary's capabilities rather than over nature's distribution, she said, so Jonah had moved the guarantee up a level while keeping its structure, which was exactly her point.
