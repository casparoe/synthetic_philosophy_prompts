A colleague and I sat in on a physics department colloquium about the arrow of time, and afterwards two of the speakers got into an argument at the reception that I've been turning over ever since. Neither of them is a crackpot; both know the literature. I'll reconstruct their exchange as faithfully as I can.

(1) The first said that the thermodynamic arrow simply *is* the direction of time: the future is the direction in which entropy increases, and the second law tells us why processes unfold one way and not the other. To ask for an arrow of time beyond that is to ask for a explanans that has no possible explanandum.

(2) The second replied that this conflates an asymmetry *within* time with an asymmetry *of* time. The second law says that entropy increases toward the future, but that statement already uses the word "future" — it presupposes a temporal direction rather than supplying one. If all you had was a four-dimensional block universe and a distribution of entropy values, you couldn't pick out which direction was which without already knowing which way time points.

(3) The first answered that this is no objection at all: you define the future direction operationally as the direction of entropy increase in any local region, and then the second law becomes a substantive claim that this direction is globally consistent — that the same orientation works everywhere. That's a non-trivial fact about the world, and it's precisely what we mean by there being an arrow of time.

(4) The second pushed back: the real puzzle is why the early universe was in such a low-entropy state. The underlying microphysics is time-symmetric, so the second law is a statistical regularity, not a fundamental law. But statistical reasoning alone doesn't give you the arrow: if you take the current macrostate and run the time-symmetric dynamics forward and backward, the most probable past is also a higher-entropy past — which is the wrong answer. To get the right answer you need the Past Hypothesis, the claim that the universe began in a special low-entropy condition. And that just *is* the arrow of time, smuggled in as a boundary condition. The thermodynamic arrow doesn't explain it; it restates it.

(5) The first responded that this is exactly how explanation works in physics: you explain the general pattern from a lawful boundary condition. We don't say Newton's laws fail to explain planetary orbits because we still need initial positions and velocities. The Past Hypothesis is a boundary condition, and the second law is the dynamical consequence. Together they explain the arrow. The demand that the boundary condition itself be explained by something else is a demand for a different kind of explanation — a cosmological one — not a failure of the thermodynamic one.

(6) The second said the analogy fails because in Newtonian mechanics the boundary condition doesn't secretly contain the very thing to be explained. The Past Hypothesis says "entropy was low at the temporal boundary we call the beginning." But "beginning" already distinguishes that boundary from the other one. You can try to state it in a time-neutral way — "there is a boundary of spacetime on which the entropy is low" — but then you face the question: why does that boundary play the role of the past in every other temporal asymmetry (causation, memory, deliberation)? If the answer is "because it's the low-entropy boundary," then you've just defined the past as the low-entropy boundary, which is exactly the operational definition we started with — and we've gone in a circle.

(7) At this point they were interrupted and the argument never resumed. I need someone to adjudicate this move by move. Rate each of the six moves (the interruption precludes a seventh from the first speaker) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say who has the better overall position given where the argument stood when it broke off.
