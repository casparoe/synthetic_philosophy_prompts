I'm taking a course on classical Indian epistemology, and we've spent the last three weeks on the Nyāya school's theory of the pramāṇas — the instruments of valid cognition. The professor has been unusually reluctant to give us a crisp statement of the theory; instead she has been working through cases and announcing which pramāṇa she takes to be operative in each. So far her ledger looks like this:

- Looking out the window and seeing that it is raining: *pratyakṣa* (perception).
- Seeing wet pavement and concluding that it rained earlier: *anumāna* (inference).
- A neighbour telling you the post office is closed: *śabda* (testimony).
- Having a gavaya described to you as 'like a cow but with a shorter muzzle and a hump', then later encountering one at a wildlife sanctuary and recognising it: *upamāna* (comparison).
- Spotting a bird and identifying it as a kingfisher from its plumage: *anumāna*.
- Tasting a dish and recognising the flavour as cinnamon: *pratyakṣa*.
- Reading a notice that says 'Bridge closed' and thereby knowing the bridge is closed: *śabda*.

She has told us that the next case will be: hearing a voice on the telephone and recognising it as one's mother's voice. We are to predict her classification before the session.

I am trying to work out what rule or rules she might be running, because her verdicts so far look consistent with more than one plausible way of drawing the boundaries between the pramāṇas. Why, for instance, is recognising a kingfisher from its plumage classified as inference, while recognising cinnamon from its taste is classified as perception? Perhaps the distinction turns on whether the recognition is mediated by attention to specific marks — plumage pattern, beak shape — or is instead immediate and unreflective, as the taste of cinnamon seems to be. Or perhaps it turns on whether what is recognised is a natural kind (kingfisher) versus a sensory quality (cinnamon). Or perhaps on something else entirely: maybe the kingfisher case involves an explicit step of reasoning from visible features to an unobserved category, whereas the cinnamon case is a direct sensory judgement that resists further analysis. What I want to know is: what are the candidate rules that fit her ledger so far, what does each rule predict about the mother's-voice case, which prediction is the best bet and why, and — if the mother's-voice case does not settle matters — what further case would cleanly separate whichever rules are still in the running?
