I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad seminar called "Concepts and Categories" — about 18 students, mostly philosophy and cognitive science majors, one 80-minute session. We're about to start a unit on Douglas Hofstadter's work on letterforms and the whole "what makes an 'a' an 'a'?" question — the idea that there's some abstract essence of a letter that survives across wildly different fonts, handwriting, even partial degradation, but it's almost impossible to pin down in terms of necessary and sufficient conditions. I want them to feel this problem in their bones before we read any of the theoretical stuff.

So I'm looking for a hands-on classroom activity — something where they're drawing, sorting, arguing about borderline cases, and gradually discovering that they can't actually articulate what rule they're using to recognize 'A's even though they're perfectly reliable at doing it. I'd like the activity to have a few distinct phases that build on each other, with clear instructions for each phase and approximate timings. The debrief should map what happened in the activity onto the philosophical issues: the tension between rule-based and exemplar-based categorization, the problem of borderline cases, the role of context and style, and why this isn't just a quirk about letters but a deep puzzle about concepts in general.

Materials should be simple — paper, markers, maybe some pre-prepared ambiguous shapes for the boundary-pushing phase. No computers or special equipment. And please tell me what'll predictably go wrong: I've run enough of these to know that the first time never goes smoothly, and I'd rather know the failure modes in advance. Also, one constraint: about a third of my students have some background in graphic design or typography, and I don't want them to dominate the discussion with technical vocabulary (x-height, stroke weight, etc.) — the activity should be accessable to everyone while still giving the design-minded students something to chew on. Oh, and I'd love it if the activity itself surfaces the fact that context matters — like, the same squiggle might be an 'a' in one word and not in another — without me having to lecture about it.
