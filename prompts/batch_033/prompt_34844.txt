I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I am still not sure I got the core point across — that you cannot locate a subject who suffers the harm. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of death: fourteen students, one ninety-minute session. I want to design a structured activity that makes them feel the force of the Epicurean argument before we read the primary texts. They should discover the puzzle themselves rather than having it explained to them.

Design a classroom activity — a game with rules and payoffs, a role-play, a structured deliberation, or a staged thought experiment with real choices — that forces participants to confront the question of when exactly the harm of death occurs and who experiences it. The activity should be arranged so that they run into the problem: every candidate answer for the timing of the harm (before death, at the moment of death, after death) runs into trouble, and the position that death is not bad for the one who dies begins to look like a genuine challenge rather than sophistry. The facilitator should not name the Epicurean argument or its components until the debrief; the activity itself should do the work.

I need the setup and any materials, the rules and sequence of the activity, a timeline for the ninety minutes, and a detailed debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the components of the Epicurean argument — the "no subject" argument, the Lucretian symmetry argument, the deprivation account and its difficulties — and should say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Be honest about which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and what I will need to supply in discussion afterward.
