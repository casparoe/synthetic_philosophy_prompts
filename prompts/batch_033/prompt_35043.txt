I was covering a conference on comparative cognition last fall, and during a break I overheard two researchers arguing about Dennett's taxonomy of minds — specifically, where the line between Skinnerian and Popperian creatures actually falls. I wrote down their exchange as best I could remember it, and it's been bothering me ever since. Here's roughly how it went:

Speaker 1: "Take the New Caledonian crow that bends a straight wire into a hook to retrieve food from a tube. That's a novel solution to a novel problem, produced without visible trial and error. If that isn't Popperian — internal hypothesis testing, letting possible actions die in one's stead — I don't know what is."

Speaker 2: "I don't think that follows. The crow has a lifetime of manipulating twigs and wire-like objects. Each component motor routine could have been shaped by reinforcement. The novelty is in the combination, and novel combinations of reinforced behaviors don't require internal simulation — they just require generalization. Skinnerian creatures can generalize."

Speaker 1: "But the crow isn't just generalizing across stimuli. It's producing a highly specific shape that fits the causal structure of the problem. Generalization from past reinforcement doesn't explain why it bends the wire into a hook rather than a loop or a straight probe. It seems to anticipate the functional requirements of the task."

Speaker 2: "'Seems to anticipate' is exactly the problem. We're imputing internal simulation from the behavior alone, but the behavior is equally consistent with a sophisticated Skinnerian architecture that has learned causal contingencies through extensive object interaction. The diagnostic test for Popperian cognition is whether the creature can reject a solution without enacting it. Tool manufacture doesn't demonstrate that."

Speaker 1: "Then your criterion is so strict it's almost unfalsifiable. By that standard, nearly no nonhuman animal could qualify as Popperian, because we can always tell a Skinnerian just-so story about how the behavior was shaped."

Speaker 2: "That's not a bug — it's a feature. The taxonomy is meant to be a ladder of genuine cognitive innovations. If we lower the bar for 'Popperian' every time we're impressed by an animal, we lose the explanatory power of the distinction."

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. At each turn, who is on stronger ground, and why? Are there conceptual distinctions available to either speaker that neither made explicit? And finally: is there a principled way to resolve the underlying disagreement about what counts as evidence for Popperian cognition, or does the taxonomy itself need refinement at this boundary?
