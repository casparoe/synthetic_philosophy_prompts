I've been reading discussions about machine consciousness and AI moral patienthood on LessWrong, the EA Forum, and adjacent spaces for years now, and I keep noticing a dialectical pattern that I don't have a good label for. It shows up whenever someone tries to state a criterion for which computations or systems matter morally — which ones are conscious, sentient, or otherwise deserve moral consideration.

The pattern runs roughly like this. Someone proposes a functional or computational criterion: a system is conscious if it has high Φ (integrated information), or a global workspace, or succeeds at certain self-report tests, or instantiates a particular predictive processing architecture. Then a critic offers a counterexample that satisfies the criterion but seems intuitively non-conscious — a giant lookup table, a billion people passing notes, a very slow hand simulation, a rock whose microphysical states happen to map onto the right causal structure. The defender then adds a condition to exclude the counterexample: the computation must be implemented "in the right way," the causal structure must be "intrinsic" rather than merely ascribed, the system must be "unified" in some sense beyond the functional specification. But when someone asks what this new condition actually amounts to — what property it picks out in the world — one of three things tends to happen. Either the condition can't be stated without appealing to the concept of consciousness it was meant to naturalize, or the condition turns out to also rule out human brains (once you ask the same question of them), or the condition just pushes the counterexample one step back without resolving the underlying difficulty.

What interests me is that this doesn't feel like ordinary goalpost-moving or ad hoc theory patching. The defenders aren't acting in bad faith, and the new conditions often feel genuinely motivated — there does seem to be something wrong with the lookup table that isn't wrong with the brain. But the pattern repeats across very different proposed criteria — IIT, global workspace, higher-order, predictive processing, and so on — and seems to reveal a structural difficulty: any criterion stated in purely functional or computational terms appears vulnerable to counterexamples that exploit the gap between "satisfying the criterion" and "being the kind of thing that could be conscious," and the only way to close the gap is to add conditions that are themselves consciousness-involving. I'm aware of Block's work on "the harder problem of consciousness" and Chalmers on the combination problem, but I'm not asking about the metaphysical problem itself — I'm asking about this specific dialectical pattern of criterion-proposal, counterexample, and condition-addition-that-circularly-requires-the-concept. Does it have an established name in the literature? Is it just an instance of something more general (the problem of the criterion, a Lakatosian degenerating research program, the problem of deviant realizations in functionalism), or does it have its own treatment? And if it doesn't have a name, what is the cleanest way to characterize what's going on here, and what cleanly distinguishes it from those nearby phenomena?
