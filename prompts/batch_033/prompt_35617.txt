I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language. In a recent seminar on sortals and kind terms, a colleague appealed to the old line "a white horse is not a horse" from the White Horse Dialogue to support the claim that adjectival modification does not always produce a subtype of the head noun's extension. I know the line has a long history in the Chinese School of Names and that it has been read in radically different ways — as a thesis about reference, about the distinction between intension and extension, about the semantics of the copula, about how names pick out individuals versus kinds, and even as a claim about the metaphysics of identity. I would like to get clear on what the precise theses are that someone could be asserting when they say "a white horse is not a horse." Could you lay them out as a menu of distinct claims, from the trivial to the radical, and say for each whether it is defensible and which (if any) would actually support my colleague's use of it in the sortals debate? Please also indicate how confident you are in your analysis and single out the point you are least sure of.
