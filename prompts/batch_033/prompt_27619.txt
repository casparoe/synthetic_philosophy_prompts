I teach an undergraduate seminar, and last semester I spent more hours than I want to admit staring at two papers trying to decide whether one deserved a B+ and the other an A−. Around three in the morning I found myself constructing the following argument, and now I cannot see where it fails — but it has to fail, because its conclusion is that there is no such thing as a grade.

(1) Take any two papers that differ by exactly one sentence — say, the second is identical to the first except that it includes one additional sentence of ordinary quality, neither brilliant nor disastrous. If the first deserves an A, the second also deserves an A: a single sentence cannot be the difference between deserving an A and deserving something less than an A. If it could, we would be saying that an entire semester's assessment can turn on one sentence, which is incompatible with how we actually think about evaluation.

(2) Now take any paper that clearly deserves an A — a flawless, penetrating essay — and any paper that clearly deserves an F — say, a single sentence reading "I didn't do the reading." There exists a sequence of papers connecting them, each differing from the previous by exactly one sentence, where we gradually degrade the excellent paper one sentence at a time, replacing one of its sentences with a slightly weaker one at each step, until we arrive at the failing paper.

(3) By repeated application of (1), the failing paper deserves an A.

(4) By the same reasoning in reverse, the excellent paper deserves an F.

(5) So grading distinctions collapse entirely: every paper deserves every grade.

(6) But this conclusion is false. Teachers distinguish A papers from C papers all the time, students and employers rely on those distinctions, and I myself am confident that some of my students wrote better papers than others — often much better.

(7) Therefore at least one of the steps above must be rejected.

I want a diagnosis: which step would you reject, why did that step look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? Make the cost concrete — I need to know what the diagnosis means for someone who will actually sit down next semester and write letters on a stack of papers. And I would like you to tell me which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about the diagnosis: where is your own reasoning about this most exposed?
