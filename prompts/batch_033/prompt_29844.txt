I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about Lewis Carroll's tortoise, and I've come away convinced of a position I want to test. My view, roughly, is this: the regress Carroll identifies is genuine and shows that the rule of inference in modus ponens cannot be captured as an additional premise — it must be something the reasoner does, not something the reasoner believes. Deduction therefore rests on a kind of practical ability or implicit know-how that is not reducible to propositional knowledge, and attempts to justify deduction by adding more premises simply mistake the kind of thing a rule of inference is.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not just the standard objections I've already heard — that the regress is benign rather than vicious, that we can simply take the rule as a permission rather than a premise, that Carroll was making a narrower point about the difference between premises and rules — but a fuller map of its exposure. Which observations about actual reasoning, which formal results, which arguments in the philosophy of logic would a committed defender of this view have to concede as counting against it? Are there ways the view could be shown to collapse into something trivial, or to rest on a distinction between doing and believing that cannot be sustained? And is the view insulated in a way that should worry me — have I qualified it into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind?
