I've been in a philosophy of biology reading group for the past year, and the function debate is driving me up the wall in a very specific way. Not because the positions are murky, but because nobody has moved in what feels like fifty years. The selected-effects camp says the function of a trait is the effect it was selected for: the heart's function is to pump blood and not to make a thumping noise, because pumping is what hearts were selected for. That's also why a malformed heart that can't pump still has the function of pumping, and why 'malfunction' makes sense. The causal-role camp says the function of a thing is just its contribution to some capacity of a containing system that you've chosen to analyze: relative to circulation the heart functions to pump blood, but relative to the system that makes heart sounds it functions to make thumping noises. No history, no selection, no spooky normativity.

Their replies to each other are equally familiar. Selected-effects people say causal-role is too liberal — it can't distinguish function from lucky side effect. Causal-role people say selected-effects is too historical: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a human with no evolutionary past still has a heart that pumps blood, so it seems to have a function even though nothing selected for it, and anyway physiologists and engineers talk about function all the time without caring about selection. Both camps have had these moves for decades and the dialectic is basically static; every argument has a well-known counterargument and nobody budges. I care partly because I keep seeing people try to use 'function' to naturalize mental content, and the whole project seems to ride on wich notion of function is the real one. My working theory is that this is two different questions sharing one word — one side is asking 'why does this trait exist?' and the other is asking 'how does this system work?' — but I'm not sure, because they also seem to disagree about whether functions can be normative, and that doesn't look merely verbal.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, substantive, two explanatory projects talking past each other, a hidden empirical question about whether biologists actually use a unified concept of function, or bedrock intuitions about normativity that neither side can argue for? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a better taxonomy, an empirical study of scientific usage, or just agreeing to drop the word? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the literature.
