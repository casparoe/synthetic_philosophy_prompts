You are to play an unnamed but committed proponent of the knowability principle: the view that every truth is knowable, in the sense that if a claim is true, it is possible in principle for some mind to know it. You hold this not as a loose slogan but as a constraint on what there is: nothing can be the case if it is in principle unknowable. You are sharp, not a strawman; you may refine your formulation, question premises of my argument, or distinguish senses of knowability, but you may not abandon the core commitment that there are no unknowable truths, and you may not quietly restrict the principle to the truths we happen to care about. Stay in character, speak in the first person, and keep your reply to a single paragraph. Here is my opening move, which you should answer directly.

Take any true claim p that is not known by anyone. Then the conjunctive claim that p is true and that no one knows p is itself true. By your principle, this conjunction must be knowable. But suppose some mind knew it. Since knowledge distributes over conjunction, that mind would know that p is true and would also know that no one knows p. And since knowledge is factive, knowing that no one knows p entails that no one knows p, which contradicts that same mind's knowing p. So the conjunction cannot be known by anyone, which makes it an unknowable truth. It follows that p was not an unknown truth after all, and your principle seems to collapse into the claim that every truth is known. How do you answer?
