I'm the director of grants at a mid-sized philanthropic foundation with roughly £40 million in annual disbursements. For the past three years we've been making funding decisions largely by intuition and board consensus, and while that's produced some good bets, it's also produced a portfolio that's hard to defend to our trustees: we're funding a mix of arts education, global health, climate advocacy, and criminal justice reform with no clear story about why these and not others. We've been reading about the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework used in effective altruism and want to adopt something like it, but the advice we've found is mostly high-level — "rate each cause on these three dimensions" — with no guidance on what to do when the dimensions pull in different directions, or how to get from ratings to a decision when the ratings themselves are uncertain. I'd like you to write a decision procedure that a programme officer at a foundation like ours could actually run on each prospective cause area. The procedure should be a sequence of questions, with clear stopping rules (when does a cause fail at the first hurdle?), and it should specify what counts as a sufficient answer — what standard of evidence is needed at each step. It should also flag the points where the procedure hands the decision to a human because the question is fundamentally one of values rather than facts. The procedure needs to be usable by someone with a background in programme management but no formal training in philosophy or economics, and it should take no more than a day of desk research to complete for a given cause area.

Once you've laid out the procedure, run it on three candidate cause areas to demonstrate how it works in practice: (a) funding research into a vaccine for a neglected tropical disease that affects roughly 200,000 people annually in low-income countries, where several other major foundations are already active; (b) funding advocacy for electoral reform in a stable democracy, on the grounds that better electoral systems produce better policy outcomes across all domains; and (c) funding a pilot programme to improve end-of-life care in UK hospices. Please make sure that at least one of these applications yields a result that a typical foundation officer would find uncomfortable — either a rejection of something that feels obviously worthy, or an endorsement of something that feels questionable — and explain why the procedure's answer is correct even if it's counterintuitive. Also, please note what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of cause areas it will systematically misjudge, and the decisions it must leave to judgement.

Please write in British English throughout. Start by brainstorming the key challenges in operationalising the ITN framework before settling on a final procedure. You should approach this as an expert in philanthropic decision-making and the philosophy of effective altruism, and you should think carefully about the structural tensions between the three criteria before committing to a specific procedure.
