A familiar narrative has it that Libet-style experiments show the brain decides before the agent does: the readiness potential ramps up before subjects report the urge to move, so the conscious intention looks like an afterthought, with the only remaining role for the will being a last-moment veto. Later work complicates the picture — the reported time of the urge is itself a judgment that may be reconstructed after the fact, and at least one reanalysis models the readiness potential as a threshold-crossing accumulator whose early phase need not be a decision at all. Suppose you are writing a piece that will try to get philosophers and legal scholars to update on this literature in the right directions rather than the usual caricatured ones. Give a defended ranking of the claims people draw from this work — that conscious intentions are epiphenomenal, that the conscious self retains a real veto, that our sense of deciding is systematically confabulated, that retributive blame and punishment need revision, and anything else you think belongs on the list — from the ones that are genuinely supported to the ones that are overread or do not follow at all. For each claim, say exactly what the experiments would have to establish (about the timing judgments, about the relation between the readiness potential and the action, and about what "free" would need to mean) for the inference to go through, and identify the further experiment or analysis that would most change its standing. I want the dependency structure and the standards of evidence more than the list itself.
