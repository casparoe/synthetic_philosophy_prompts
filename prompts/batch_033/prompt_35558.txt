Please argue that Jeffrey conditionalization, far from solving the problem of uncertain evidence, merely relocates it. The standard motivation for Jeffrey conditionalization is that we rarely if ever learn any proposition with certainty — the evidence we receive from perception, testimony, or measurement is always tinged with uncertainty. Strict conditionalization, which requires P(E) = 1 after learning E, is therefore an idealization that never applies. Jeffrey conditionalization relaxes this: experience directly shifts your credences over some partition {E_i}, and you then update the rest of your credences by averaging your conditional probabilities over that partition, weighted by the new credences.

The problem is that Jeffrey conditionalization treats the new credences P'(E_i) as primitive inputs to the updating process, but it says nothing about where they come from or what constrains them. If the original complaint was that strict conditionalization demands an unrealistic certainty about the evidence, then Jeffrey conditionalization demands something equally mysterious: that the agent simply find herself with new credences over the partition, with no account of how experience produced them. The uncertainty has been pushed inward — from uncertainty about the world to uncertainty about what the experience even licenses — but the epistemological problem has not been solved; it has been hidden inside a black box labeled "the agent's new credences."

Develop this argument in detail. In particular, address the following: if we try to open the black box and give a model of how experience produces the shift from P(E_i) to P'(E_i), do we not find ourselves needing something like strict conditionalization on a higher-order event (e.g., "I had an experience with such-and-such character")? And if so, does Jeffrey conditionalization collapse into strict conditionalization at a higher level, making it a convenient shorthand rather than a genuine alternative? If you think the collapse can be resisted, explain how.
