I'm a retired physicist who's been reading a lot of philosophy of cosmology lately, and I keep noticing a specific argumentative move in the Fermi paradox literature that I can't find a name for. Here's the move: Someone proposes a solution to the paradox — say, that all technological civilizations inevitably destroy themselves before they can colonize the galaxy. The obvious objection is that it only takes *one* civilization that doesn't destroy itself to fill the galaxy, and with a hundred billion stars and billions of years, a universal tendency seems wildly implausible. The response is always: "Well, maybe the probablity of surviving is just astronomically small — one in 10^20 or whatever." And then the debate stalls because we have no way to estimate that number.

This same structure shows up for lots of proposed solutions — the "they're all hiding" solution, the "they all ascend to a higher plane" solution, the "they all decide expansion isn't worth it" solution. The move goes: universal claim → objection from diversity → retreat to an extreme probability claim → stalemate.

What I'm wondering is whether this three-step retreat has a name in philosophy of science. It's not quite an ad hoc modification, because the probability claim is a genuine weakening of the original. It's not quite unfalsifiability, because in principle you could get evidence about the probability. It reminds me of the way some theodicy arguments work — "maybe God has reasons we can't fathom" — but applied to aliens. Is there an established term for this pattern, or at least a literature that analyzes it? And if there isn't a name, what's the closest thing and how does this differ from it?
