Compare al-Ghazālī's treatment of causation in The Incoherence of the Philosophers (Tahāfut al-falāsifa) with Ibn Rushd's reply in The Incoherence of the Incoherence (Tahāfut al-tahāfut), focusing on their disagreement about the cotton-and-fire case. Both thinkers accept that fire and burning are constantly conjoined in experience; they disagree about whether this conjunction reveals a necessary connection or only God's habitual way of acting, and about what each alternative would imply for the possibility of demonstrative knowledge. Spell out, as precisely as possible, where the two arguments part company. In particular: does al-Ghazālī's denial of necessary connection in nature leave him without any ground for expecting the future to resemble the past, or can he legitimately appeal to God's wisdom and custom to secure reasonable expectation? And does Ibn Rushd's appeal to natures and causal powers do more than reassert the necessity that al-Ghazālī denies? Finally, judge which position is in the better dialectical position once the question is framed as one about the justification of inductive inference rather than about the metaphysics of causation.
