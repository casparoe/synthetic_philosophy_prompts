I am working on a project about how different theories of token causation treat redundant causal processes, and I would like a thought experiment constructed to the following specifications.

The two theories should be characterized as follows. The first is a simple Lewis-style counterfactual theory: a token event C caused a token event E only if, had C not occurred, E would not have occurred, with the counterfactual read non-backtrackingly in the standard way. The second is a Woodward- and Halpern–Pearl-style interventionist theory of actual causation: C caused E if there is a possible intervention on C that changes whether E occurs, when some set of off-path variables is held fixed at values that need not be their actual values, subject to the usual minimality requirement.

Specifications:

1. The scenario must be ordinary and realistic — a hospital or a workplace — and no longer than one paragraph. It should not simply be a relabeled version of the standard two-thrown-rocks example.

2. C and E must be positive events; the case must not involve omissions, failures to act, absences, or negative events.

3. The two theories must diverge in the following direction: the simple counterfactual theory says C did not cause E, while the interventionist theory says C did cause E.

4. The interventionist verdict must be generated by an explicit contingency: state which off-path variables are held fixed, at which values, and show how an intervention on C then changes E. The contingency should be essential to the verdict — if off-path variables could be held only at their actual values, the interventionist would also deny causation.

5. The case should isolate a single structural feature rather than any special subject-matter detail. The point is to show what the interventionist's allowance of non-actual contingencies buys in token-causal judgments.

After the scenario, provide the following. (a) Lay out the relevant variables, their actual values, and the structural equations or a precise counterfactual structure. (b) Show why the simple counterfactual theory denies causation, tracing the relevant counterfactual and explaining why the but-for test fails. (c) Show why the interventionist theory affirms causation, giving the contingency and the resulting difference. (d) Identify the structural feature doing the work, and explain what happens if it is removed — in particular, whether the case collapses into one on which the two theories agree. (e) Say how a defender of the simple counterfactual theory would attack the case, and whether amending that theory in the direction of Lewis's later influence-based account would change its verdict.
