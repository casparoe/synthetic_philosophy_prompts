I am a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I sit on the grants committee of a small effective-altruism-aligned donor fund. We want to decide a new proposal consistently with our own track record. Our five past decisions were:

1. Funded a mass deworming program with multiple randomized controlled trials and a very low cost per quality-adjusted life year.
2. Rejected a community mental-health counseling program that had moderate-quality studies, because its outcomes were too heterogeneous and difficult to quantify against our usual metrics.
3. Funded, with a publication condition, a speculative AI safety research group that had no near-term measurable outcomes, on the grounds that the tail risk was severe enough to justify funding despite weak measurability.
4. Rejected an arts education program with strong anecdotal reports but no controlled evidence.
5. Funded a policy advocacy group working on air pollution, on the strength of quasi-experimental evidence that its campaigns produced measurable health improvements.

The new proposal is from a criminal justice reform organization working to reduce pretrial detention and incarceration. It has a plausible causal mechanism and two credible quasi-experimental studies, but its outcomes are mixed: some are measurable (days incarcerated, rearrest rates), while others are not easily quantified (dignity, family stability, trust in institutions). It does not fit our health-metric template, but the harms it addresses look large.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Which of our past decisions is the closest analog, and why? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so, and explain what principle we would be adopting with each live option. I care less about the bare fund/reject answer than about the candidate rules we might be running, which rules the track record rules out, and which rule you would recommend and why.
