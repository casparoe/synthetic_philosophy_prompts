I'm on the university's risk and compliance committee. We've been using an AI-based system to screen grant proposals for compliance with export control regulations — it flags proposals that might involve restricted technologies or sanctioned countries. Six months in, the system cleared a proposal that absolutely should have been flagged; the PI is now under federal investigation, and the university faces potential fines. The compliance officer who signed off says she trusted the system because it had been accurate for the first five months and she was processing three times the normal volume. The vendor says the system's overall accuracy is 97%, which meets the contract specs, and that no system is perfect. The general counsel wants to know who's responsible so we can assign liability and figure out what to change.

Here's the wrinkle: the more I look at this, the more I think the "who's responsible" question might not have a clean answer. The compliance officer was acting reasonably given the workflow we imposed; the vendor delivered what they promised; the system itself isn't a moral agent; and the committee that approved the system (us) relied on aggregate accuracy metrics that masked the distribution of errors. It feels like a textbook responsibility gap — the kind people have been writing about with autonomous systems — where the usual conditions for holding someone accountable (control, foreseeability, reasonable alternatives) are distributed across too many actors to land on any one of them. But I can't just write a memo saying "it's a philosophical puzzle, good luck."

I need to draft a memo for the committee that (a) recommends concrete next steps — disciplinary action or not, contract renegotiation, workflow changes, whatever — and (b) honestly engages with the possibility that we're facing a genuine responsibility gap, not just a failure to find the right person to blame. What does it mean for institutional policy if some outcomes of autonomous systems are literally nobody's fault in the way our accountability frameworks assume? And what do we do about the PI and the federal investigators in the meantime? I'm not asking for a philosophy paper — I need a document the committee can actually act on — but I don't want the memo to dodge the hard part by pretending we can just find the negligent party if we look harder.
