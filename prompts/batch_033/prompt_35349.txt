Been thinking about eliminative materialism lately — you know, the Churchland line that beliefs and desires as folk psychology conceives them are on a par with phlogiston and caloric fluid, and mature neuroscience will simply stop talking about them. A friend who's into it challenged me to say exactly what's wrong with it, and I came up with this argument, but now I'm worried it proves too much and I can't spot where it goes off the rails.

(1) If eliminative materialism is true, there are no propositional attitudes. No beliefs, no desires, no intentions — the whole lot is empty.
(2) If there are no propositional attitudes, there are no truth-evaluable mental states — states that can be correct or incorrect depending on how things are.
(3) If there are no truth-evaluable mental states, there is no reasoning. Reasoning is precisely the process of transitioning among truth-evaluable states in ways that tend to preserve truth or achieve epistemic goals.
(4) If there is no reasoning, there is no such thing as being convinced by an argument.
(5) If there is no such thing as being convinced by an argument, the practice of philosophy — which is in the business of convincing people by argument — is built on a mistake.
(6) Eliminative materialism is itself a philosophical thesis, advanced by arguments intended to convince.
(7) So eliminative materialism, if true, makes the activity of arguing for it impossible. It saws off the branch it's sitting on.

Now, I think the eliminativist grabs (2) or (3). They'll say neural patterns can be truth-evaluable without being propositional attitudes — truth-evaluability is just a matter of carrying information and being deployable in ways that succeed or fail. But then I worry: by that standard, a thermostat carries information about room temperature and succeeds or fails at triggering the heating, and nobody thinks a thermostat is reasoning. So the eliminativist either has to swallow the conclusion that reasoning doesn't exist (which seems mad) or find a principled line between thermostat-style information-processing and reasoning — and drawing that line looks suspiciously like smuggling propositional attitudes back in.

What I really want to know: which step is actually the weakest, what does the best eliminativist response concede, and does this argument prove too much — i.e., would a parallel argument also take down other revisionary metaphysical positions (moral error theory? mereological nihilism?) that we might not want to lose?
