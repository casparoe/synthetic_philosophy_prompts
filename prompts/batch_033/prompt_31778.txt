I'm developing a teaching handout for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mind. I'd like you to construct a taxonomy of positions on the question: "In virtue of what features does a physical system count as sentient — and thereby as a bearer of moral status — rather than as a mere mechanism?"

The taxonomy should be a tree in which each branching node is labeled by a yes/no question that splits the logical space. The questions should be phrased so that someone can answer them without knowing the names of the positions they lead to — the tree should sort by commitments, not by labels.

Please ensure the following positions appear as leaves (or, if the tree cannot sharply distinguish two of them, flag that pair and explain why):

- Eliminativism or strong illusionism: no physical system is genuinely sentient, or the concept of sentience is irredeemably confused.
- Biological chauvinism: only carbon-based neural systems can be sentient.
- Substrate-neutral functionalism: any physical system with the right causal-functional organization is sentient, regardless of its material composition.
- Integrated Information Theory: sentience is a matter of a system's having a high value of Φ, a measure of causal integration that depends on the specific physical structure of the system and cannot be read off from its abstract input-output profile alone.
- Abstract computationalism: implementing the right abstract computation suffices for sentience, regardless of the physical details of the implementation.

Where the tree's branching structure leaves a combination of answers that no major position occupies, briefly note that cell and diagnose whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The audience is philosophy graduate students who know the terrain; the taxonomy should not re-teach basics but should reveal the structure of the debate — which commitments force which others, where the genuine fault lines lie, and where positions that sound different collapse into the same leaf.
