The classic money pump is used to defend the transitivity axiom: if an agent has intransitive preferences — preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A, all at the same time — a bookie can extract money from them by offering a sequence of trades, each of which the agent accepts given their current preferences, cycling them back to their starting endowment minus a fee at each step. This argument targets a synchronic defect: the preferences are all held simultaneously, and the defect is a purely structural one in the agent's preference ordering.

But money pump arguments are also wielded against dynamically inconsistent agents — agents whose preferences shift over time in a predictable way, such that a plan they make at t₁ for what to do at t₂ is abandoned when t₂ arrives. The bookie here exploits the temporal shift: the agent pays at t₁ to commit to a course of action, then pays again at t₂ to undo it. This targets a diachronic defect: a mismatch between earlier and later selves.

My question is whether these are genuinely two different arguments with different structures and different normative targets, or whether the diachronic money pump is just the synchronic one applied to a temporally extended agent. Some philosophers seem to treat the synchronic case as the paradigm; others, particularly those working on dynamic choice and the status of resolutions, suggest that the diachronic case introduces considerations about the relationship between temporal selves, the authority of past commitments, and the nature of agency over time that have no analogue in the synchronic case.
