I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been writing a book whose protagonist is an entomologist who has drifted into pest-control work and cannot stop noticing the moral texture of what he does. In trying to write him honestly, I have collected my own first-order judgments about a series of cases, and they refuse to settle into a single rule.

If my apartment has bedbugs, I have no hesitation about heat-treating the flat so that every last one of them dies. If a cockroach appears in the kitchen at night, I will put down bait that poisons the colony, with only a faint twinge. If a wasp gets into the bedroom of a child with a severe sting allergy, I kill it without a second thought. Yet when ants find their way onto the counter in spring, I wipe them away and seal the crack, and I will not put down ant poison that would kill the nest; I tolerate the scouts instead. A single moth that comes in through an open window I catch and carry outside, and I could not bring myself to kill it — but if I find pantry moths in a bag of flour, I throw the flour out and set traps that kill the adults, and I feel almost nothing. I think a malaria-control program does the right thing when it sprays the inside walls of houses to kill Anopheles mosquitoes, but I think a farmer does something wrong when he sprays a flowering crop with a broad-spectrum insecticide that kills honeybees and wild pollinators in the surrounding hedgerows. I find a bug zapper in a garden on a summer evening ugly and somehow wrong, even though it kills mostly the same mosquitoes I kill indoors. And a child pulling the wings off a fly strikes me as clearly wrong, though I cannot honestly say whether it is wrong because of the fly or because of the child.

I can see some of the fault lines myself. I am willing to exterminate every bedbug in my home but not to poison a nest of ants, though both mean killing an entire infestation that has crossed my threshold. I release a single moth but kill the same species by the dozen once it is in my flour. I kill a wasp that threatens an allergic child but cannot decide about a wasp nest that only frightens people. I keep going back and forth on two further cases: a wasp nest under the eaves of a house where no one is allergic but the residents are afraid of being stung; and a proposal to release gene-drive mosquitoes that would drive a local mosquito species to extinction in order to eliminate malaria. Is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? Please state it as precisely as you can, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, tell me what it implies for the two cases I cannot settle, and propose one further case of your own that would most sharply test it.
