I've been trying to reverse-engineer a friend's views on discounting the future. She's a careful thinker — not an economist, but someone who arrives at her positions by reflecting on cases rather than applying a formula. Over several months of conversation I've collected the following verdicts:

She'd rather have $100 today than $120 in a year, but she'd rather have $120 in eleven years than $100 in ten years. She says the difference between "now and later" feels much larger than the difference between "later and slightly more later," and she's untroubled by the fact that this pattern would, if she made the first choice now, lead her to reverse it in a year. On a policy question involving lives: she supports spending $10 million today to save 100 lives today, but opposes spending $10 million today to save 105 lives in one year. Yet she supports spending $10 million in fifty years to save 105 lives in fifty-one years over spending $10 million in fifty years to save 100 lives in fifty years. She's aware of the pattern and says it doesn't feel like a contradiction: the further out you go, the less the exact timing matters. She also thinks we should care about the welfare of people in 500 years roughly as much as we care about people in 100 years, and she's frustrated by standard cost-benefit analyses that discount the far future to nearly nothing. But she admits she doesn't feel the same urgency about a preventable death in 200 years that she feels about a preventable death next year. I once asked her to compare two policies: one prevents a catastrophe in 30 years, the other prevents the same catastrophe in 130 years. She said she'd prefer the earlier one, but only weakly — she'd trade it off for a very small increase in the number of lives saved.

I can see at least three candidate rules that fit everything she's said. One is quasi-hyperbolic discounting (the β-δ model), with a steep present-bias parameter β and a δ close to 1 — the sharp drop in weight happens right after the present moment, and everything in the future is discounted at nearly the same low rate. A second is a "near/far" threshold view, on which there's a high discount rate for the first few decades and a low, maybe zero, rate beyond that, with the cutoff somewhere in the mid-future rather than at the present moment. A third is that she doesn't discount time itself at all, but she discounts for the probability that the promised good will actually reach its intended beneficiary — and she implicitly thinks that probability decays quickly over the near term and slowly over the long term.

The new case I want to predict: I ask her to compare Policy A, which prevents 1,000 deaths in 5 years at a cost of $10 million today, with Policy B, which prevents 1,000 deaths in 55 years at a cost of $10 million in 50 years. Both involve a 5-year gap between cost and benefit, but one starts now and the other is shifted 50 years into the future. What does she say? And if her answer to this case doesn't settle the matter, what single further case would most cleanly tell the three candidate rules apart?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
