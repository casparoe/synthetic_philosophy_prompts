Here's something I've been turning over for a while and I'd like you to think through it with me. In most of the United States, if a gas station triples the price of bottled water the day after a hurricane, that's price gouging and its a crime. But if an airline triples the price of a seat the week before Christmas, or a ride-share app doubles fares during a snowstorm, nobody treats that as a crime — it's just dynamic pricing. On the surface both are a seller raising prices sharply when demand spikes and the buyer's alternatives are lousy. So what theory of value, or of commodification — of which goods may properly be priced — does the anti-gouging rule presuppose? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then separate what the law is really committed to from what it merely fits with. Are the airline and surge-pricing cases consistent with that theory, or does the law's own logic actually condemn them too — or is the whole pattern underdetermined, so several different theories could license it? And if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforwardly welfarist one, where prices are just information about scarcity and efficiency is the main test, what would have to change: would we repeal the gouging laws, or would we have to start prosecuting airlines too? Please give me your answer in American English.
