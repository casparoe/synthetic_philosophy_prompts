I chair the appointments committee for a senior research professorship in my university's Faculty of Social Sciences. We shortlisted two candidates and, as is customary, commissioned written assessments from two external assessors. One assessor — the person most of us would regard as the leading scholar in the relevant field — has submitted a detailed and forceful report ranking candidate A clearly above candidate B on originality, rigour, and likely future contribution. At the end of the report, however, she has added an unusual paragraph. She explains that she was completing the assessment while recovering from a serious illness and under significant sleep disruption, that she has independent and long-standing reason to distrust her comparative judgements in such states, and that she therefore asks us to treat her confidence as materially less reliable than usual. She nonetheless reports that her honest first-order judgement remains that A is the stronger candidate by a wide margin.

The committee is now split. One camp says the caveat is straightforwardly relevant: it is higher-order evidence that the report's ranking is less reliable than it would otherwise be, so we should discount the ranking accordingly — perhaps treating the two candidates as effectively level, or commissioning a further assessment. The other camp says this is incoherent: the caveat is itself a product of the very cognitive state it warns us about, so we cannot coherently privilege it over the first-order judgement. On this view the assessor remains the best-calibrated instrument we have, and the only defensible move is to take her expert ranking at face value; to do otherwise is to defer to the self-report of an instrument she herself has told us is unreliable. Our regulations allow us to commission a further external assessment only if we record a formal reason for doing so, and we may not simply set aside a report without stating grounds, so the reasoning has to be set out in writing whichever way we go. I have read enough around the debate on epistemic akrasia to suspect we are staring at a practical instance of it, and my own prior keeps shifting, which is part of why I am asking for the memo rather than drafting it myself.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next week's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — discount the report, disregard the caveat, seek a further external assessment, or something else — with reasons the rest of the committee could actually act on. It should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than caricature it. If you think the task as posed is confused — for example, that there is no coherent distinction between using the report and using the caveat, or that a committee cannot rationally be asked to adjudicate between them — then say so plainly and tell me what we should do instead, rather than writing a memo that papers over the problem.
