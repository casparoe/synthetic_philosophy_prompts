I am a retired natural scientist who now runs a philosophy reading group, and we have spent several months on the debate about what egalitarians should equalize when they call a distribution unjust: welfare, resources, capabilities, or something else. I would like to give the group a sorting quiz of the kind magazines use, but built to do genuine philosophical work. Please write it for me.

The quiz should be titled something like Which currency of egalitarian justice do you actually hold? It should contain eight to ten questions. Each question should present a short, concrete case—expensive tastes, disability, adaptive preferences, unequal natural talent, voluntarily chosen work, differences in how efficiently people convert income into functionings, and the like—with two or three answer options. The options must be written so that a reader can answer from their reaction to the case without already knowing the names of the positions, and no question may simply ask which label the respondent prefers.

The scoring key should sort respondents into at least these positions: subjective welfare egalitarianism, Dworkinian equality of resources, Sen-style capability egalitarianism, Arneson's equal opportunity for welfare, and Cohen's equal access to advantage. Add a residual leaf for a sufficientarian or threshold view if the questions naturally generate one, and mark any leaf as unnamed if no one in the literature defends exactly that combination.

I want the instrument built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Begin by identifying the axes on which these positions genuinely come apart—for instance, whether the currency is a mental state, a bundle of transferable resources, or a set of real opportunities; whether expensive tastes are compensable and, if so, only when they were not chosen; whether adaptive preferences reduce the injustice of a deprivation; whether the ideal is ex post or ex ante. Then write questions that vary one axis at a time. In the scoring key, locate each position in that space, explicitly flag any pairs of positions the questions cannot separate, and note any combinations of answers that correspond to no defended position, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

The audience is scientists who are new to philosophy, so the cases should be vivid and the respondent-facing instructions short; the scoring key, however, should be rigorous enough that a professional philosopher could object to it productively.
