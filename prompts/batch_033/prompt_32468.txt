I've been reading about pain asymbolia — the condition where patients can locate and describe a painful stimulus ("it's sharp, it's in my hand") but insist it doesn't bother them. They don't flinch, they don't withdraw, and they don't want it to stop. This has been messing with my view of why pain is bad, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove that pain's badness is entirely a matter of my attitude toward it. I'm fairly sure that's wrong, but I can't find the flaw.

(1) Pain asymbolia shows that the sensory component of pain (intensity, location, quality) and the affective component (unpleasantness, aversiveness, the "ouch" quality) are dissociable — they can come apart entirely.

(2) If the sensory and affective components are dissociable, then the sensory component alone is not sufficient for pain to be bad. After all, asymbolics have the sensory component and don't seem to be undergoing anything bad.

(3) What remains to make pain bad is the affective component. So the badness of pain is entirely a matter of the subject's negative affective response to the sensation.

(4) But a negative affective response is, at bottom, a kind of aversion or dislike — a state of the subject directed at the sensation.

(5) If pain's badness consists entirely in the subject's disliking it, then pain is bad for someone only because and to the extent that they dislike it.

(6) Now generalize: the same dissociation is possible in principle for any unpleasant experience — nausea, grief, the sensation of suffocation, fear. In each case, what makes the experience bad is the subject's negative affective response, not anything intrinsic to the experience itself.

(7) But that can't be right. If someone inflicted severe pain on you but also somehow suppressed your capacity to dislike it, they would still be harming you. It would still be wrong for them to do it. The badness of agony isn't just a matter of my attitude toward it.

The argument seems to show that if we could just change our attitudes, we could make torture harmless. But torture isn't harmless. Where does the argument go wrong? If I have to give up a premise, which one costs the least, and what do I have to accept instead?
