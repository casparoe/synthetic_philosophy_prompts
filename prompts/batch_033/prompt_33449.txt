I cover philosophy and the behavioral sciences for a general-interest magazine, and recently I sat in on a small workshop about rational choice. One session turned into a protracted argument between two participants — call them A and B — about whether incommensurable values make certain choices rationally underdetermined. I took notes, but I've been unable to decide who had the stronger case, and I'd like a point-by-point adjudication.

A kicked things off by arguing that the standard model of rational choice treats all reasons as commensurable — as though every consideration can be placed on a single scale and weighed. But in a hard choice — say, between a career in law that serves justice and a career in music that creates beauty — the values at stake don't share a common measure. They aren't equal, and neither is better. So it's not a matter of discovering which option is superior; it's a matter of committing to one, and in doing so, creating the reasons that retrospectively justify the choice. B pushed back: if the options are genuinely incommensurable before the commitment, then the initial act of committing is itself unmotivated. You're picking one arbitrarily, and calling the result "rational" because you then live consistently by the choice looks like a bait-and-switch. A replied that B was begging the question by assuming that rationality requires a pre-choice reason that tips the balance — that's exactly the commensurability assumption under dispute. B said no: the problem isn't the absence of a tipping reason, but the absence of any explanation for why you went one way rather than the other. If your "commitment" is indistinguishable from a coin flip at the moment of decision, then the subsequent reasons you generate are downstream of an arbitrary event, and a chain is only as rational as its weakest link. A countered that this conflates the rationality of the selection mechanism with the rationality of the life you lead afterward — and that for hard choices, the relevant standard of rationality is about how you integrate the choice into your identity, not about the procedure that selected it. B shot back that this move would rationalize literally any post-hoc conversion: join a cult at random, live devoutly by its rules, and call the whole sequence rational. At that point the moderator stepped in, and the argument never got resolved.

I'd like you to go through the exchange, move by move, and say who is right about what — where each side lands a genuine point, where each overreaches or misses a distinction, and whether there's a resolution available that neither of them articulated.
