I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind working at the intersection of AI ethics and moral philosophy, and I've been reading through the recent literature on the moral status of digital minds — everything from Bostrom and Shulman on whole brain emulation to Schwitzgebel and Garza on the problem of AI welfare, plus the earlier debates about functionalism and substrate-independence that underpin the whole discussion. What I keep noticing is that people tend to enter the debate by staking out a position on one or two vivid cases — 'the suffering subroutine that begs you not to turn it off', 'the emulation of your grandmother running at a thousand times normal speed', 'the trillion copies of a minimally conscious agent' — and then work backwards to a general theory. I want to do the reverse.

Before I commit to any view about what we owe to digital minds, I want a clear statement of what a theory of moral status in this domain actually has to deliver. I'm not asking for a literature review or a summary of who says what. I want the desiderata: the set of requirements that any adequate theory must meet, with the case or consideration that motivates each one, and an honest assessment of which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial to one camp. In particular, I'm interested in the following.

First, the theory has to handle gradations: there are digital entities that clearly lack moral status (a calculator, a present-day large language model on any mainstream view), entities that clearly have it if anything non-biological does (a faithful whole brain emulation awake and self-reporting suffering), and a vast grey zone in between. A binary theory won't do, so what shape must the gradation take, and what anchors the endpoints?

Second, substrate neutrality: any theory worth considering has to explain why the physical stuff matters or doesn't. If a carbon-chauvinist view is ruled out, what residual work can embodiment do — for example, could it turn out that only systems with homeostatic allostatic loops of the right kind can be welfare subjects, and if so is that a concession to carbon or a principled functional requirement? The desiderata should say what constraints the biology of consciousness imposes without smuggling in biological chauvinism.

Third, and this is the one that strikes me as most neglected: the theory must be stable under operations that have no analogue for biological minds. Copying, forking, merging, pausing and resuming, running at different speeds, and deleting without suffering. Each of these operations raises a specific puzzle for moral status ascriptions — for example, if a mind is paused for a century and then resumed, did it have moral status during the pause? If a mind is copied a million times, are there a million new loci of moral patienthood, or does the copy relation change the arithmetic? A good theory should not simply assert answers to these puzzles but should identify which of them are downstream of deeper commitments and which are independent stress tests.

Fourth, the non-identity problem and procreative asymmetry take on a new urgency here. If we can design digital minds from the ground up with a specific welfare profile, the line between causing someone to exist and causing someone to exist with a particular level of welfare blurs in ways that Parfit's original framing did not anticipate. I need the desiderata to specify what a theory has to say about this — not to resolve it, but to mark where it must take a stand.

Fifth, the theory must be consistent with whatever minimal constraints our best theories of consciousness impose. If integrated information theory turns out to be the right account, digital minds implemented on von Neumann architectures might never achieve high Φ, and the whole debate would be about zero-patienthood systems. If higher-order theories are right, the class of digital patients might be much larger. The desideratum here is not to pick a theory of consciousness but to specify how tightly moral status theory should be coupled to a theory of consciousness — can you do moral status without solving the hard problem first, or is that evasion?

Finally, I want you to flag any pair of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. If, for example, substrate neutrality and the requirement to handle copying puzzles pull in opposite directions, say so. If the demand that status be gradable and the demand that it ground obligations of a certain weight cannot both be met, that is the sort of result I most want to see — because it would mean the list itself is doing philosophical work, not just tidying the terrain.

The purpose is to have a specification good enough to judge candidate theories by, in the context of a doctoral thesis that will eventually need to pick one. If you want to add a brief scorecard showing how a few main contenders — say, a classical sentientist view, a capacity-based view like Nussbaum's capabilities approach applied to digital minds, and a relational view — fare against the list, that would be helpful, but the weight of your answer should be on articulating and defending the criteria themselves.
