In my philosophy of translation seminar last week, a student trotted out the old line that a translation, like a woman, is either faithful or beautiful, never both. She then used it to argue that Hofstadter's project in Le Ton beau de Marot — dozens of versions of one tiny Marot poem, many of them under strict formal constraints — is basically a catagory mistake, because you can't keep the rhyme and meter and still be faithful to what the poem says. You have to choose, and choosing form is choosing beauty over fidelity.

I don't want just a feminist takedown, or at least not only that; I want the analogy audited as an analogy. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the woman, what fidelity corresponds to, what beauty corresponds to, and what the either/or is actually asserting about translation. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, what the analogy quietly imports about meaning — presumably that sense is something seperable from form and that fidelity means fidelity to literal content — and whether the student's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there's a better analogy for what formal constraint does in translation, I'd like that too.
