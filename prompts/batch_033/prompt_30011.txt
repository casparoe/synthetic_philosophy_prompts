I've been kicking around an essay idea about epistemic dependence on AI and I'd love some honest feedback on whether the core argument actually holds water. The gist is this: people often say that offloading cognitive work to AI is no different in kind from offloading memory to books or navigation to maps — we've always used external tools. But I think there's a difference that gets missed, and it has to do with the structure of the feedback. When you rely on a map, you're still doing some of the orienting work yourself; you have to match what you see on the map to what's around you, and if you get lost, the mismatch is salient. When you ask ChatGPT to summarize a paper for you, the output just arrives — clean, confident, no seams showing. There's no friction that forces you to engage with the underlying material, and no natural checkpoint where you'd notice if the summary was subtly wrong. So the worry isn't just that we're delegating cognition, it's that we're delegating it in a way that systematically atrophies the very skills that would let us notice when the delegation fails. I'm trying to work out whether this is actually a novel point or just a dressed-up version of the old "Google is making us stupid" argument. Also, I'm not sure how to handle the obvious objection that lots of people do in fact catch AI errors — does that undermine the claim, or just show that the erosion hasn't fully set in yet? Would appreciate comments on whether the argument is worth developing, where the weakest link is, and whether I'm missing an obvious counterexample that would force me to qualify the whole thing.
