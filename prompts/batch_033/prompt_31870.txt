Aquinas’s position on tyrannicide in De Regno is often summarized as: a tyrant by usurpation may be killed, while a tyrant by oppression should ordinarily be tolerated unless a public authority has judged against him. Please unpack the conceptual machinery behind this asymmetry. In particular, explain what makes someone a tyrant in each case, why the distinction between usurpation and the oppressive exercise of legitimate authority matters for determining who may act, and whether Aquinas’s view is best read as treating the killing of a usurper as a kind of execution of a public judgment already made by the community, rather than as a private right of resistance. Also address a possible internal tension: if a legitimate ruler becomes a tyrant by oppression, why is the common good served by tolerating him until a public authority intervenes, and what exactly counts as public authority for Aquinas in this context? Keep the exegesis tight: distinguish what the text explicitly says from what it leaves underspecified, flag where later commentators have arguably filled gaps rather than interpreted a determinate doctrine, and do not treat the De Regno position as if it simply repeats what Aquinas says elsewhere. Avoid citations and bibliography; this is a request for conceptual clarification, not a literature review.
