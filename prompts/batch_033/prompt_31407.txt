Below is a question from an exam, followed by a student's answer. Grade the student's answer out of 10, and justify the grade with point-by-point comments rather than a general impression. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The first item should state the grade; the remaining four items should give the point-by-point comments that justify it.

Question: A surgeon tells a patient that a proposed operation has a 70% chance of success. Explain how a frequentist, a subjectivist, and a propensity theorist would each interpret the surgeon's "70%", and state which of the three interpretations (if any) treats the statement as a claim about this particular operation.

Student answer: The frequentist reads the 70% as a long-run relative frequency: in a large sequence of operations on relevantly similar patients, about 70% would succeed. Because this is a single case, the frequentist must place it in a reference class—patients of the same age, diagnosis, and health status—and the 70% is simply the observed success rate in that class. The subjectivist reads the 70% as the surgeon's degree of belief that this operation will succeed, as measured by her betting dispositions; two surgeons with the same evidence could assign different probabilities without either being irrational. The statement therefore reports the surgeon's degree of belief about the operation, not an objective feature of the operation. The propensity theorist reads the 70% as an objective tendency of this particular patient–operation pair to produce success, analogous to the bias of a coin. Since propensities attach to single cases, the surgeon needs no statistical evidence to assert one; she is asserting the physical disposition directly. Of the three, only the propensity interpretation takes the statement at face value as a claim about this particular operation.
