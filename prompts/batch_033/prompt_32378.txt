In my criminal law class the lecturer keeps saying that sentencing is about giving people what they deserve — the punishment should fit the crime and nothing else should move it. Then a week later we covered the early guilty plea discount, which is completely standard: plead guilty at the first opportunity and the same offence, same harm, same record, gets a sentence reduced by as much as a third in some jurisdictions. The official justifications are usually that it spares victims the ordeal of a trial, saves public money, and shows remorse or acceptance of responsibility. I can see the practical appeal, but I can't see how the philosophy hangs together. If the original sentence really tracked what the crime deserved, how can it shrink because the defendant said guilty early? That looks like the system is treating part of the sentence as a bargaining chip, which would mean it wasn't deserved in the first place. But if the sentence is only a tool for deterrence or incapacitation, why do judges talk about remorse as if it matters in itself, and why is the discount sometimes refused for defendants who pleaded guilty but clearly feel nothing? The practice seems half retributive and half consequentialist, and I want to know whether that is a coherent position or just an uneasy compromise.

So: what theory of punishment does the early guilty plea discount presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with or leaves open — for instance, could a retributivist or a pure consequentialist also accept it, and on what grounds? Then tell me what would concretely have to change about the practice if we replaced its implicit theory with a thoroughgoing retributivism, and seperately with a thoroughgoing consequentialism. I am less interested in the standard one-line contrast between the two camps than in what this particular practice reveals when you push on it.
