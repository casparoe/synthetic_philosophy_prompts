I'm taking a philosophy of science course this term and we just spent a week on Hempel's raven paradox. I can follow the formal point — the equivalence condition plus the Nicod criterion means a green sock confirms "all ravens are black" — but it still feels like a party trick, and I'm pretty sure most of my classmates feel the same. I help run a discussion section for the course, about 16 undergrads, 60 minutes, and I want to run an activity that makes them actually feel the force of the thing instead of just nodding along.

Could you design a runnable in-class activity or game for that setting? What I want them to come away seeing is that you can't dismiss the green sock for free: you have to give up or weaken either the equivalence condition (that a hypothesis and it's contrapositive are confirmed by the same evidence) or the Nicod criterion (that a positive instance confirms a universal generalization), and each option costs you something. But I don't want to tell them that up front — the rules, roles, or incentives should push them into the inconsistency themselves, and we only name it in the debrief.

Some constraints: no real money, and materials should be simple (cards, slips, a whiteboard, some kind of points scheme). The activity should include a moment where they have to commit — like sorting cards as evidence for/against/irrelevant, or betting on the rule after each draw — and then have to reconcile what they said with a logically equivalent rule. Include black non-ravens as a distractor category too, because under the standard account those are neutral and that also surprises people. The debrief should map the moments in the activity onto the formal bits: the Nicod criterion, the equivalence condition, why a non-black non-raven is a positive instance of the contrapositive but feels irrelevant, and why black non-ravens are neutral. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the paradox the activity can't reproduce and I'd have to supply myself.

And if you can work in the "but green socks are way more common than ravens" move — the thought that the background distribution makes the sock irrelevant — and why that either is or isn't a way out, that'd be great, because someone always bring it up.
