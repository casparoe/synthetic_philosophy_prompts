I need a thought experiment built to order. The target is the structural difference between prioritarianism and the maximin principle (the view that only the welfare of the worst-off matters). Both views give extra weight to the worse-off, so they often travel together in policy debates, but they come apart because prioritarianism is additively separable across persons while maximin is not: prioritarianism sums a concave function of each individual's welfare, so gains to any person count for something, whereas maximin simply compares the worst-off positions and ignores everything else. I want a case that isolates this additive-separability feature and forces the two views to diverge cleanly.

Specifications:

- The case involves a choice between two policies. Each policy affects exactly three groups. All three groups' outcomes differ between the two policies. The identity of the worst-off group must be the same under both policies — the divergence must not come from one policy changing who occupies the worst-off position. Use concrete stakes drawn from an ordinary policy domain (health, housing, education, income) rather than abstract utility numbers.
- Prioritarianism must oppose the policy that maximin favors. The prioritarian verdict must be robust: it should hold for a wide and plausible range of concave transformation functions (square root, log, and similar), not merely for a single cherry-picked parameter value or an artificially extreme one.
- The scenario must be realistic — the sort of trade-off a government agency, hospital, or institution might actually face — and must fit in one paragraph.

After presenting the scenario, do the following:

1. Walk through the reasoning of maximin and prioritarianism on the case, showing exactly why they diverge. Include a rough numerical demonstration with at least one standard concave function so I can see the mechanism.
2. Identify the structural feature of the case that does the work — what exactly about the pattern of gains and losses across the three groups makes additive separability bite here.
3. Explain what happens if that feature is removed: do the two views converge, and if so, on which verdict?
4. Describe how a defender of prioritarianism might resist the case. For instance, could they argue that the setup implicitly assumes something a prioritarian should reject about the concavity function, or about how groups are individuated, or about the relationship between the stakes chosen and the underlying welfare metric? Give the strongest version of the resistance, not a straw man.
5. Finally, step back and say whether this case reveals a genuine vulnerability in prioritarianism, or whether it merely illustrates a feature — the willingness to trade off gains to the worst-off against sufficiently large gains to others — that prioritarians already acknowledge and are happy to live with. If the latter, what exactly does the case teach us that wasn't already obvious from the formalism?
