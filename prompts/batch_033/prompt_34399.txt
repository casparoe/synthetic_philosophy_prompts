ok so i've been going down a rabit hole about causation by omission — like when does *not* doing something actually cause something to happen — and i've ended up with a handful of views that i'm not sure all hang together. i'm a software engineer by trade so some of this comes from thinking about bugs and blame, but bare with me.

here's my current position:

(1) omissions can be genuine causes. if i promise to water my neighbor's plant and don't, my failure to water it caused the plant to die. that's real causation, not just a figure of speech.

(2) but an omission only counts as a cause when there's some norm, duty, or expectation that the person should have acted. the promise is what makes my omission causal; without it, i'm just one of billions of people who also didn't water the plant.

(3) the best test for whether an omission caused something is the counterfactual test: would the outcome have happened if the omitted action had been performed? if no, then the omission was a cause. this is clean, handles edge cases, and matches how i debug.

(4) in code, a missing null check causes the null pointer crash. but if a program crashes on malformed input, the absence of input validation that i never thought to write is *not* a cause of the crash. somehow these two absences feel different.

(5) causation by omission is symmetric with causation by action. once we admit omissions are causes, we shouldn't treat them as second-class or weird. they're just causes.

so that's the set. i've got a nagging feeling that (2) and (3) are in tension — the counterfactual test doesn't seem to care about norms — and maybe (5) makes that worse. and (4) might be pulling against (3) too. but i can't tell which of these are actual conflicts and which just look like conflicts until you stare at them. can you go through these, flag the real tensions, say which are merely apparent, rank them by severity, and tell me what the cheapest fix is?
