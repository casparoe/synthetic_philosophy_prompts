Ok so I'm in this philosophy of language course and we just finished the bit on Kripke's Wittgenstein on rule-following, and I'm trying to sort out what I actually think instead of just nodding along with the professor. The skeptic says there's no fact of the matter about what I mean by 'plus' — any finite set of past uses is compatible with infinitely many functions (like quaddition, where x quus y = x+y if both are below some huge number I've never encountered, and 5 otherwise). And I find that conclusion completely nuts, but I'm having trouble saying exactly why without begging the question.

Here are my gut reactions on a bunch of cases, and I want to know if there's a single account of meaning determination that gets all of them, or close, and which ones I might have to give up. (A) A kid learns addition up to 100, then on 57+68 says '5'. I say he's wrong — he meant addition, not quaddition with a cutoff at 57. The fact that he made a mistake doesn't mean he was following a different rule all along. (B) A solitary person, Crusoe-style, grows up on an island with no language community and develops his own symbols, including '+' for combining piles of shells. I think he means addition, even though there's no community to check him. So community agreement can't be what *constitutes* meaning. (C) But then: a whole community uses 'green' in the normal way, and over a century it gradually drifts to include some blue things. I want to say the meaning *changed*. But if there's no fact about what individuals meant in the first place, how can I say the communal meaning changed? That seems like I'm treating community usage as determinative here but not in Case B, and the inconsistency bothers me. (D) Someone sincerely says 'I've always meant addition by +' and we scan their brain and find their dispositional profile is actually quaddition-ish (they'd systematically say 5 for sums over some huge number they've never computed). I think they're wrong about what they meant — sincere avowal doesn't settle it. But then what *does*? (E) If dispositions are what matter, I have a disposition to make mistakes when I'm tired or the numbers get huge. I don't want to say 'plus' means whatever my error-prone dispositions would produce. So there's a normativity issue — meaning seems to be about how I *should* go on, not how I *would* go on. (F) Here's the one where I actually feel the skeptical pull: a mathematician introduces a new operation, @, defined only for inputs 1 through 10, and then dies. The community later extends it to 11 in a way that seems natural to them. But a different community could have extended it differently. I'm genuinely unsure whether there's a fact about what the mathematician 'really meant' for 11, and this feels different from the addition case though I can't articulate why.

So: is there a principle (or a small set of candidates) that captures these verdicts? Be precise about what each clause of the principle is doing — which case motivates it. What does the principle say about cases I haven't listed? And which of my verdicts, if any, do I have to abandon? I'm especially worried about the tension between B and C, and about whether F forces me to accept more of the skeptical conclusion than I want to.
