In my graduate seminar on practical reason this term, a student has been pressing a version of the view that hard choices are rationally underdetermined. His argument leans on the familiar apples-and-oranges idiom. When a person must choose between two options that draw on deeply different kinds of value — a demanding surgical career versus staying near home to care for an aging parent, say — the options are, he insists, like apples and oranges: there is no common measure in which both can be weighed, so no amount of further deliberation could make one option more rational than the other, and the very words better and worse stop applying. I suspect the idiom is doing much of the argumentative work for him, and I want to make that work explicit. Please audit the analogy in detail. First, give the correspondence table: what in the hard choice corresponds to the apples, the oranges, the missing scale, and the person trying to compare them. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely incidental. In particular, address what the analogy quietly imports about what comparison requires — for instance, whether comparing two things really requires a single common metric, given that we compare apples and oranges without difficulty once a purpose (dessert, price per pound, vitamin content) is fixed. Finally, assess whether his conclusion — that choices between incommensurable values are rationally arbitrary — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and say whether some better analogy would carry whatever truth the idiom is reaching for.
