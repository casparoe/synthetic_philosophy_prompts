I'm trying to work out what I actually think about moral worth — when an action is creditworthy in a way that reflects well on the agent, not merely right by coincidence. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. A shopkeeper gives a child the correct change. He does it because he believes honesty is his duty, and he wants to do his duty. The fact that honesty is good for business never crosses his mind. Verdict: the action has moral worth.

2. A different shopkeeper gives a child the correct change, but he does it solely because he believes a reputation for honesty will bring repeat customers. He doesn't care about duty one way or the other. Verdict: the action lacks moral worth, even though it's the right thing to do.

3. A passerby sees a stranger collapse on the sidewalk. She rushes over, calls an ambulance, and stays until help arrives. She acts out of immediate sympathy and concern for the person's suffering. The thought "this is my duty" never occurs to her. Verdict: the action has moral worth — and I find myself wanting to say it has at least as much as the first shopkeeper's.

4. A different passerby sees the same collapse. He feels no sympathy at all — he finds the stranger somewhat repellent. But he believes firmly that one has a duty to help those in need, and he acts solely from that belief. Verdict: the action has moral worth, but it feels somehow thinner than the sympathetic helper's, though I can't articulate why.

5. A third passerby sees the collapse. She helps, but her motivation is that onlookers will think badly of her if she doesn't. She correctly judges that helping is the right thing to do, but that judgment plays no role in moving her. Verdict: the action lacks moral worth, or at best has very little.

6. Someone wants to poison his business rival. He puts what he believes is arsenic in the rival's coffee. Unbeknownst to him, the substance is a life-saving antidote to a rare condition the rival has, and the rival recovers fully. Verdict: no moral worth whatsoever. He didn't perform the act of saving a life; he performed the act of attempted murder.

7. Someone volunteers at a homeless shelter every week. She does it partly because she cares about the guests and partly because she believes she has a duty to help the disadvantaged. Either motive on its own would have been sufficient to get her to volunteer. Verdict: the action has moral worth, and the presence of the duty motive doesn't diminish it.

8. A father believes, on the basis of what he takes to be sound moral reasoning, that he ought to push his child toward a high-status career. But whenever he tries, he finds he can't bring himself to do it — something in him recoils. Instead he supports his child's choice to study poetry. As it happens, supporting the child's autonomy is the right thing to do, though the father doesn't believe this. Verdict: I want to say the action has some moral worth — his emotional responsiveness is tracking something real — but I'm uneasy, because he's acting against his own best judgment.

Is there a single principle of moral worth that delivers all or most of these verdicts? If not, which verdicts would you overturn, and what's the best principle that handles the rest? I'm especially interested in what the principle says about a case I haven't listed: someone who acts rightly from a settled disposition of character — say, honesty — that was itself cultivated for purely self-interested reasons long ago, but now operates without any thought of self-interest. Does that action have moral worth?
