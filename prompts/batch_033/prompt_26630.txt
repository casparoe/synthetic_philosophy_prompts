I've been rereading L.A. Paul's *Transformative Experience* alongside rewatching Denis Villeneuve's *Arrival* (based on Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life"), and I want to think through how the film engages with Paul's central argument. Paul's claim is that some choices are "transformative" in a specific sense: making the choice changes who you are—your preferences, your values, even your epistemic standpoint—such that you cannot rationally evaluate the choice from your current perspective. You can't know "what it will be like" from the inside, and you can't even trust your current preferences as a guide, because those preferences may change as a result of the experience.

*Arrival* seems to dramatize exactly this. Louise Banks learns Heptapod B, and the language rewires her cognition so that she experiences time non-linearly. Once transformed, she knows her daughter will die young, and she chooses to have her anyway. But the film's structure makes it ambiguous when the "choice" happens—or whether it's a choice at all, given the non-linear experience of time. This seems to put pressure on Paul's framework: if the transformed Louise can't not-know the future, is she making a decision in Paul's sense? And if pre-transformation Louise can't possibly evaluate what it would be like to live with foreknowledge of her child's death, does the film suggest the choice is simply inscrutable, or does it offer resources Paul doesn't?

I'd like a reading that takes both the film and Paul's argument seriously. Where does the film illustrate Paul's claims about the limits of rational decision-making? Where does it complicate them—for instance, by introducing the question of whether a transformative experience can change not just your values but the structure of agency itself (if you experience time as a block, can you "decide" anything)? And does the film ultimately vindicate Paul's view that some choices can't be rationally assessed from the outside, or does it suggest something else—that the very idea of "choosing" to undergo a transformative experience is confused in ways Paul doesn't fully address?

If my framing is off—if I'm misreading Paul, or if the film is doing something that doesn't map onto her argument the way I think—please say so. I'd rather get the philosophy right than force the parallel.
