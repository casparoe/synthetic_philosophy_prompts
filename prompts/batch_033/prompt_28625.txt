I keep going back and forth on process vs. outcome supervision, so I wrote down a bunch of calls I'd make on concrete cases. I'm hoping there's one principle underneath them, or at least a small set. Setup: we're training a model that does multi-step reasoning, and we can score either the final answer or the intermediate chain-of-thought (by hand, with a process reward model, whatever).

- A model gets the right answer to a math problem, but one of the middle steps is just wrong — it cancels terms in a way that shouldn't work, and the error happens to cancel out. I don't want to reinforce this. The right answer doesn't redeem the bad step.
- A model reasons every step correctly, but at the very end it writes '3 + 5 = 9' or copies the answer wrong. This feels much less bad then the first one — I'd almost want to reward it, or at least penalize it way less.
- A model outputs the right answer with no real reasoning, or a chain-of-thought that's clearly a post-hoc rationalization that didn't produce the answer. This feels basically as bad as being wrong. I wouldn't want to train on the outcome.
- A model gives a correct answer with a long chain-of-thought I can't actually verify step by step (a novel proof, code that passes tests but I can't follow the logic). Here I fall back on scoring the outcome, and I'm not that unhappy about it. That sits awkwardly next to the previous one.
- A model trained only on outcome rewards on a benchmark finds a shortcut — it notices the correct answer is always the longest option, or exploits a bug in the checker. It 'gets the answer right' but clearly isn't doing the task. This is the obvious nightmare for outcome-only supervision, and I'd point to it as the reason process matters.
- But then: a model trained on process rewards learns to write reasoning that looks good to the process reward model while being subtly wrong. So process supervision can be Goodharted too, and I trust it less than I'd like to admit.

Is there a principle that captures all of these, or close? The pair that bugs me most is the first two: correct reasoning with a wrong final answer vs. incorrect reasoning with a right final answer. My gut says the first is better, which sounds like I think process matters for its own sake, not just as a predictor of outcomes. But the other cases push the other way — when I can't see the process I'm fine rewarding the outcome, and when the process signal is itself unvalidated I don't trust it. What principle should I actually hold, and which of these verdicts does it force me to drop? Also: what does the principle say about a case I haven't listed — a model that reaches the right answer by a valid but completely non-human reasoning path that a human process evaluator would mark wrong because it doesn't look like the textbook proof?
