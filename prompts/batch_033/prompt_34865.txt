**Question (10 points):**

State Sen's liberal paradox precisely: which conditions are involved, what does the impossibility result assert, and what does it *not* establish about the relationship between individual rights and the Pareto principle? Use the Lady Chatterley's Lover case to illustrate the structure of the conflict.

**Student Answer:**

Sen's liberal paradox, introduced in his 1970 paper "The Impossibility of a Paretian Liberal," demonstrates that three conditions on a social choice function cannot be jointly satisfied: unrestricted domain (U), the weak Pareto principle (P), and minimal liberalism (L). Minimal liberalism requires that there exist at least two individuals each of whom is decisive over at least one pair of social alternatives, capturing the idea that every person has a protected personal sphere. The result is an impossibility: no social welfare function can respect all three conditions for every logically possible profile of individual preferences.

The standard illustration is the Lady Chatterley's Lover case with two individuals, Prude and Lewd. Prude prefers that no one reads the book; if someone must, he would rather read it himself than let Lewd read it. Lewd wants both to read it, and especially wants Prude to read it. Each is assigned a right: Prude decides whether *he* reads it, and Lewd decides whether *he* reads it. Given these rights, the following cycle emerges: Prude-reads-and-Lewd-doesn't is Pareto-superior to neither-reads; Lewd-reads-and-Prude-doesn't is Pareto-superior to Prude-reads-and-Lewd-doesn't; and neither-reads is, by Prude's right, preferred to Lewd-reads-and-Prude-doesn't. No alternative is best, so U, P, and L cannot all hold.

The paradox thus shows that liberalism and the Pareto principle are logically incompatible. Sen later extended the result by demonstrating that the paradox persists even when rights are formulated as alienable—that is, when individuals may waive their decisiveness over their private sphere. The only way out is to abandon one of the three conditions, and most liberals would abandon P rather than L.
