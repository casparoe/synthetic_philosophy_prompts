Two people in my philosophy of mind reading group got into a long argument after the seminar on dreaming, and neither would concede. Iris had been reading Malcolm and kept pressing a grammatical point; Peter had been reading Dennett's paper on the cassette theory and kept insisting the question was empirical. I wrote down the exchange as it happened and now want someone to referee it, because I can see force on both sides.

(1) Iris opened: you can't have an experience while you're sound asleep, because being sound asleep just is not being conscious, and an experience is something a conscious subject undergoes. So the idea of a private inner show running through the night is a confused picture. What the word "dream" actually attaches to is the waking report — saying "I dreamt that..." is the criterion for having dreamt, not a description of an episode that occurred during sleep. (2) Peter replied that this was verificationism: there is a perfectly good empirical question she was trying to dissolve by grammar, namely whether the brain generates a temporally extended experience in real time during REM or simply inserts a memory of one at the moment of waking. Those are different hypotheses about what happens during the night. (3) Iris answered that his two hypotheses are indistinguishable from the dreamer's point of view — the report and the later memory are the same either way — so talk of an experience during sleep is doing no work; it's an idle wheel. (4) Peter said they are not indistinguishable from the third-person point of view. The REM findings — eye movements that track the reported content of the dream in real time, and the correlation between reported dream duration and REM duration — are evidence that the content is being generated during the night rather than confabulated all at once at waking, so the cassette hypothesis does not get to sit alongside the received view for free. (5) Iris came back that the REM correlations were calibrated against waking reports in the first place. The only access anyone has to the content of a dream is the report given on waking; the physiology is a correlate of the report, so you can't use the correlate to get behind the report to some inner experience the report allegedly describes. (6) Peter said that is far too strong: a correlate can be evidence, and here the temporal tracking is exactly the kind of evidence that distinguishes a real-time process from a memory dump. Unless she wants to deny that third-person evidence can ever bear on consciousness — a much more sweeping claim than anything she had defended — she should admit the question is open and empirical.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it as clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, or clearly right, and justify each rating in a sentence or two. Then give the strongest version of each person's overall position, identify the points both of them are missing, and finish with an overall verdict on who is more right and where the dispute actually bottoms out.

Structure your response in four sections, in this order: (1) Move-by-move adjudication, roughly half the response; (2) The strongest case for each side, roughly a quarter; (3) What both are missing, roughly an eighth; (4) Overall verdict, roughly an eighth.
