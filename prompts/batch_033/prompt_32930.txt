I've been teaching a graduate seminar on infinite ethics this term, and one of my students presented an argument last week that I haven't been able to shake off. It has the form of a reductio against the claim that we inhabit a spatially infinite universe, but I think it proves too much — or at least, I suspect it does, though I can't quite locate the misstep. I'd like you to diagnose it.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) If the universe is spatially infinite, then (assuming matter is roughly uniformly distributed) there are infinitely many beings capable of suffering and flourishing — call them moral patients.

(2) Any finite act — saving a life, writing a cheque to a charity, breaking a promise — affects at most finitely many moral patients.

(3) In a set of infinite cardinality, any finite subset is of measure zero. So from the standpoint of the total set of moral patients, the subset affected by any finite act has measure zero — it is, in a precise sense, vanishingly small relative to the whole.

(4) If an act affects only a measure-zero subset of moral patients, then, from the impartial perspective that ethical theory demands, the act makes no difference to the overall moral state of the universe.

(5) If two acts make no difference to the overall moral state of the universe, they are morally equivalent.

(6) Therefore, all finite acts are morally equivalent. Saving a drowning child is morally equivalent to letting her drown. Donating a kidney is morally equivalent to selling one on the black market.

(7) The conclusion is absurd. So one of the premises must be false.

The student's own view was that we should reject (1): the universe is not spatially infinite, or at least we have no reason to believe it is. But I'm not satisfied. The argument seems to get its force from a kind of aggregationist reasoning that would cause trouble even if the universe were merely very large rather than strictly infinite — "very large" and "infinite" might not differ in the relevant way for the measure-zero intuition to do its work. And more importantly, I suspect the problem lies earlier, perhaps in the move from (3) to (4), or in the very idea that the impartial perspective requires us to take the measure of the whole set of moral patients. But I haven't been able to articulate exactly what's wrong without giving up something I'd rather keep.

So: please tell me which premise or inferential step you would reject, explain why it looked plausible, and spell out what rejecting it costs. In particular, I'd like to know whether rejecting a given step forces us to give up on any standard commitments in ethical theory or decision theory, and whether the alternative positions — finitism about the universe, revisionary approaches to infinite aggregation, abandoning the impartial perspective, and so on — each carry their own hidden costs.
