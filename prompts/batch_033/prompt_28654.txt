I work at a product design firm, and I've been clashing for months with a senior designer who has a very particular philosophy about how we should evaluate features. Whenever we review a design, he insists on identifying the function of every single element — every button placement, every color choice, every interaction pattern, every spacing decision. If someone can't articulate what specific user need a given feature serves, he argues it should be cut. His view is that good design is purposeful design, and that leaving something in without a clear rationale is just laziness.

I've pushed back a few times, pointing out that some features might be side effects of other design decisions — for instance, a particular spacing might just be what falls out of the grid we committed to, not something we chose for its own sake. Or a visual artifact might be an unintended consequence of how our component library composes elements. He dismisses this as sloppy thinking and says that assuming something is functionless is just giving up on understanding it. If it's there, he says, it does something, and our job is to figure out what.

I feel like he's wrong, but I'm having trouble making the case in a principled way. Is there a rigorous argument for why not every feature of a designed system needs to serve a distinct function? And does the fact that designed systems have intentional creators change the situation compared to, say, biological organisms, where we might be more willing to accept that some traits are byproducts of structural constraints rather than adaptations? I'm looking for a committed answer that takes a clear position on whether my colleague's approach is defensible or whether it rests on a mistake, and why.
