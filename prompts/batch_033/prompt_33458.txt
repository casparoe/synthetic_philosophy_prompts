I'm a journalist covering science and tech, and I keep running into the same pattern in differnt guises and I'm wondering if philosophy or cognitive science has a name for it.

Here's the pattern. You have some agent — could be an animal, a person following a proceedure, an AI system, a bureaucracy — doing something that looks smart, adaptive, flexible. It's responding to its environment in ways that seem intelligent. But then you change one small thing about the situation, something that any creature that actually understood what it was doing would handle fine, and the whole thing goes off the rails. It doesn't degrade gracefully; it just keeps executing the same subroutine as if nothing changed, even when the subroutine now produces nonsense. And the moment you see that, you realize the apparent intelligence was an illusion — it was just a fixed routine that happened to be well-matched to the normal environment, and the "flexibility" was really just the environment's regularity, not the agent's.

The classic example I've heard is a wasp that drags its prey to the entrance of its burrow, goes inside to inspect, comes back out, and drags the prey in — but if you move the prey a few inches while the wasp is inside, the wasp comes out, drags the prey back to the entrance, and goes inside to inspect again, and you can do this indefinitely. The wasp never thinks "hey, I just inspected." It's locked into a fixed sequence.

But I've been seeing the same shape in much more modern contexts. A customer service chatbot that handles 90% of queries beautifully but then when you ask it something that's one step outside its training distribution it loops through the same script pages forever. A corporate policy that works great until some edge case hits and then the organization does something absurd because the policy has no exception-handling. The way some arguments play out online, where someone has a canned response that works against the standard version of an objection but when you tweak the objection slightly they just repeat the canned response as if you'd said the standard thing.

What I'm after is: does this pattern have an established name? I know "brittleness" is in the ballpark but that feels too broad — brittleness is just the property of breaking, not the specific thing where the breaking reveals the underlying rigidity. I've heard the word "sphexish" or "sphexishness" floating around, from the wasp example, but I don't know if that's a real term of art or just a clever coinage, and if it is a term, I don't know whether it covers all the cases I'm describing or only the animal-behavior case. Also, I want to know what the edges of this concept are. Is it the same thing when a human does it (out of habit, or because they're following a procedure mindlessly) versus when an insect does it (because it's literally wired that way) versus when an AI does it (because it's trained on a distribution)? Or are those differnt phenomena that just look similar? And is there a precise way to define the pattern so you can tell whether a given failure is an instance of this thing or just a regular mistake?

I'm not just looking for a label — I want the concept carved at the joints. If there's an established term, tell me what it covers and what it doesn't, and whether my examples all fit under it. If there isn't, coin one and define it precisely enough that I can use it in my writing without being sloppy. Take a definite position rather than just surveying what people have said. And write in American English.

One last thing: could you put the bottom line in a machine-readable form at the very end? Something like \answer{the term} or if there's no good term, \answer{no established term, proposed: your-term-here}.
