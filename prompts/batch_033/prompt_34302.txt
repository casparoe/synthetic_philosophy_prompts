Consider the following five candidate criteria for distinguishing a moral preference — a substantive valuation that an agent may legitimately hold — from a moral bias: a distortion in moral judgment that the agent ought, on reflection, to correct. Rank them from most successful to least successful at drawing this distinction in a principled way. For each criterion, provide a sentence or two explaining where it succeeds and where it fails, and in particular whether its failures are mere edge cases or whether they reveal a deeper structural problem with the criterion.

1. **The reasons-responsiveness criterion**: A moral preference is a bias if the agent would resist abandoning it even when presented with what they themselves would acknowledge, in a calm and reflective moment, as sufficient countervailing reasons.

2. **The genealogical criterion**: A moral preference is a bias if its causal origin lies in a process that is generally unreliable at tracking moral truth — for instance, unreflective cultural transmission, self-interest, or an affective mechanism shaped by evolutionary pressures insensitive to moral distinctions.

3. **The consistency criterion**: A moral preference is a bias if the agent applies it selectively to some cases but not to others that they cannot articulate a relevant difference between, or if the pattern of application tracks a non-moral feature — such as social proximity or in-group membership — that the agent would disavow as morally irrelevant.

4. **The consensus criterion**: A moral preference is a bias if it would be rejected by the majority of agents who are well-informed, dispassionate, and competent at moral reasoning — or, in a stronger version, if it would be rejected from a suitably idealized standpoint such as a contractualist original position.

5. **The phenomenological criterion**: A moral preference is a bias if the agent experiences it as an involuntary, visceral reaction — a "yuck" response, a flash of outrage — that precedes and resists deliberative articulation, rather than as a judgment reached through and sustained by reflection.

In your ranking, you may find that some criteria are best understood as proxies for a deeper criterion you have ranked higher, or that two criteria collapse into each other on closer inspection; note this where it occurs. You may also conclude that no single criterion is adequate and that the distinction itself is unsustainable; if so, explain whether the ranking exercise nonetheless reveals something about which criteria get closest.
