I've spent the last year reading about the argument that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that human minds aren't mechanical — the argument J.R. Lucas made in his 1961 paper "Minds, Machines and Gödel" and that Roger Penrose later revived in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind. The core idea, as I understand it, is this: take any consistent formal system that a purported mind-mechanism would correspond to. By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, there is a sentence — the system's Gödel sentence — that is true in the standard model of arithmetic but unprovable within the system. Yet we, the human mind, can see that this sentence is true. So we are not that formal system. And since the argument applies to any candidate formal system we might propose, the mind is not a formal system at all.

The counterarguments have been around almost as long. Perhaps we cannot know that we are consistent, and the argument requires that we do. Perhaps we cannot actually "see" the truth of the Gödel sentence for a system complex enough to be us — the act of surveying an entire formal system is not something any actual human can perform. Perhaps we are a formal system, but one we cannot state, survey, or identify from the inside. Perhaps the argument smuggles in an idealized "we" that does not correspond to any real cognitive agent. Perhaps the whole move from a theorem about formal arithmetic to a claim about minds is a category mistake.

What puzzles me is that this debate has been running for over sixty years and the positions seem frozen. The same arguments appeared in the 1960s, resurfaced in the 1990s, and reappear today in discussions of AI and consciousness, with little apparent progress. I am trying to understand what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a substantive formal disagreement — something that a sharper logical analysis could in principle settle? Is it a disagreement about what an explanation of the mind must look like — one side insisting that any adequate account must be reducible to a formal system, the other side denying that this is the right demand? Is it a disagreement about whether Gödel's theorems have any relevance outside mathematics at all? Or is it ultimately a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for: the intuition that the mind must be something more than a mechanism, versus the intuition that it must be something that fits into the physical world?

I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, not a verdict on who is right. And I want to know: given your diagnosis, is there anything — a theorem, an experimental result, a conceptual distinction — that could actually move the debate forward, or is the question itself ill-posed in a way that means we should expect the stalemate to persist indefinitely?
