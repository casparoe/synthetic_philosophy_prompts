As a novelist, I have spent years watching how serious readers, reviewers, and editors actually apportion their praise, and I have come to find one pattern philosophically puzzling. A novel that ends in irreparable loss, moral failure, or unrelieved grief is routinely described as honest, brave, and unflinching; a novel that allows its characters a stable happiness is apt to be called sentimental, escapist, or too neat. Yet the same people who use this vocabulary do not treat the devastating books as a duty. They speak of loving them, of staying up all night with them, of pressing them on friends, of rereading them. They seek the sadness out and appear to find something in it that they value, even though they also report that a sad film or a news story can feel like a cost they are too tired to pay. The practice, then, seems to be: in serious fiction, suffering is treated both as a mark of artistic seriousness and as a source of something readers actively want, while in other contexts the same suffering would be treated as an unwelcome burden.

What theory of why we enjoy sad art does this evaluative practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can. Does the practice presuppose that the sadness itself is the source of the enjoyment, that the enjoyment arises despite the sadness, or that the sadness is a vehicle for something else—insight, a feeling of contact with reality, mastery of difficult emotion, or the formal beauty of the telling? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and say where it is underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same behavior. Finally, tell me what would have to change in how such readers and editors judge novels if they replaced their implicit theory with a straightforward hedonism about art—the view that a work's value consists in the pleasure it produces.
