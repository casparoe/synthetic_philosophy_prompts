I'm on the governance committee of a DAO that has been running futarchy-style votes for a couple years now, and we've build up enough of a track record that the decisions are starting to feel precedential. The basic setup: proposals pass or fail based on what conditional prediction markets say about a success metric, and our committee is supposed to keep the choices coherent across cases. Here's the record so far, stripped of details:

- We approved a protocol upgrade when the market predicted +12% token price over six months.
- We rejected an influencer marketing spend when the market predicted no measurable price effect.
- We approved a compliance overhaul that the market said wouldn't move price, but that cut the probability of a catastrophic regulatory fine from about 20% to 5% over a year; we treated that as an expected-value win.
- We rejected a treasury diversification into stables when the market predicted much lower volatility but flat-to-down expected return.
- We approved a bug bounty when the market predicted no price effect but a drop in the probability of a critical exploit from 10% to 6%; we treated that as a cheap expected-value win.

Now we have a proposal to spend treasury funds on a one-year insurance policy covering a catastrophic smart-contract failure. The market on token price over the next year predicts -2% (the premium). The market on 'probability of a catastrophic exploit in the next 12 months' predicts a drop from 5% to 1%. We didn't commission a market on overall expected token value, and the proposer argues the exploit-probability market is the relevant one because the whole point is tail risk. The two of us on the committee disagree: one says we should approve whenever the market says the proposer's chosen metric will improve; the other says we've always implicitly required an improvement in expected token value, and tail-risk reductions only count if they pay for themselves. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own record? Which precedent is the closest analog, and if the record really underdetermines this, what principle are we adopting under each choice? And is there a further case we could put to the markets that would split those two principles more cleanly than this one?
