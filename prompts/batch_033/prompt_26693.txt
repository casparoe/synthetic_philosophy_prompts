I've been covering a moral philosopher whose work extends T.M. Scanlon's contractualist framework to practical ethics. Over several interviews, I've collected her verdicts on cases that test how harms to some should be weighed against harms to others. For her, an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle that no individual could reasonably reject. Here are four verdicts she has given:

(1) You can save one person from a burning building or five from another. She says: save the five. Each of the five has a claim to be rescued equal in strength to the one's, so the principle favoring the greater number cannot be reasonably rejected by anyone.

(2) A technician is trapped in a broadcast tower with a broken leg and is in severe pain. Rescuing him immediately requires taking the transmitter offline for an hour, causing millions of viewers to miss the final hour of a championship match. She says: rescue him. No individual viewer's frustration is serious enough to ground a reasonable rejection of a principle requiring them to bear that cost.

(3) A factory can install filters that prevent one worker per year from developing a permanently disabling respiratory illness, at a cost that adds a few cents to the price of each unit sold to millions of consumers. She says: install the filters. An individual consumer's burden of a few cents is too trivial to ground a reasonable rejection, given what is at stake for the worker.

(4) Ten people are adrift in a lifeboat that will sink unless one is thrown overboard, killing that person but saving the other nine. She says: it is wrong to throw anyone overboard. The one's complaint against being killed as a means is of a fundamentally different kind from the others' complaints about the risk of drowning, and a principle permitting such sacrifice can be reasonably rejected by the one person asked to bear it.

Now a new case has come across her desk, and I want to predict her verdict before she issues it. A public health agency must choose between two equally costly programs. Program A cures one person of a severe, lifelong debilitating illness involving constant serious pain and total immobility. Program B prevents a thousand people from developing a moderately serious chronic condition: persistent knee pain that significantly limits mobility and daily activities but is manageable with medication and is not debilitating. Neither the one nor the thousand are identifiable in advance.

What would she say? I am less interested in the bare answer than in identifying the principle or principles that can jointly generate all four of her earlier verdicts, determining whether those principles converge or diverge on this new case, and—if they diverge—naming the further case that would best tell them apart. I also want to know whether the Scanlonian contractualist framework as she applies it forces a particular answer here, or genuinely underdetermines it.
