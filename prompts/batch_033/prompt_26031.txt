I am presenting a paper at a jurisprudence workshop next month and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The workshop is held at a law school whose faculty trained almost entirely in the Hart/Raz positivist tradition. The audience will be roughly twenty analytic legal philosophers, most of whom are committed to some version of the separability thesis, plus two Dworkinians who reject exclusive positivism but also reject Fuller's position, and one natural law theorist who will probably be sympathetic but who will press me on whether I have gone far enough.

My thesis is that Lon Fuller's argument from the inner morality of law — the eight procedural desiderata he sets out in *The Morality of Law* — establishes a necessary connection between law and morality that withstands the standard positivist objections. I argue that a legal system systematically failing the desiderata (one that is entirely secret, purely retroactive, impossible to comply with, and arbitrarily enforced) does not merely produce bad law; it fails to produce law at all. The inner morality is therefore not a set of mere efficacy conditions for legal systems but a genuine morality — a morality of purposive social ordering — and this means the separability thesis, understood as the claim that law can have any moral content whatsoever, is false. In the paper's second half, I argue that Hart's minimum content of natural law in *The Concept of Law* is a concession that brings him closer to Fuller than Hart acknowledged, and that the real dispute between them narrows to whether the connection between law and morality is one of rational necessity or merely of natural fact.

Please prepare me for the Q&A along the following lines:

1. The eight or so objections I am most likely to receive, ordered by how damaging each would be to my argument, phrased the way an analytically trained legal positivist would phrase them.
2. For each objection: a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest.
3. Which objections are most likely to come from the Dworkinians, which from the positivist, and which from the sympathetic natural lawyer.
4. What I should concede up front, in my opening remarks, to disarm the most predictable objections before the Q&A begins.
5. The one objection I should hope nobody thinks to ask, and why it would be especially hard to answer.
