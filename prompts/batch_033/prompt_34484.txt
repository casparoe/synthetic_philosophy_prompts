I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been asked to run a guest session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consciousness — about 18 students, 80 minutes. The topic is Frank Jackson's knowledge argument. The students will already have read the Mary's room paper and at least one standard physicalist reply (likely Lewis's ability hypothesis or Churchland's). I'd like to design a structured in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the knowledge argument before we discuss the replies — something that reproduces, in a compressed and imperfect way, the structure of Mary's situation. The idea is that they should acquire a body of third-person knowledge about a sensory experience they have not had, make predictions about what the experience will be like on the basis of that knowledge, and then undergo the experience and discover for themselves whether they learn something new. The activity should be runnable with ordinary classroom materials and should not assume any particular dietary restrictions or sensory impairments beyond what is easily accommodated. Please give me: the rules and sequence of the activity, a timeline, what materials I need to prepare, a debrief that maps what happened onto the structure of the knowledge argument, an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce (and what the facilitator must supply), and a list of things that predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle them.
