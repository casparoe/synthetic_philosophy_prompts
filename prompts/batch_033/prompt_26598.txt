I recently read a short introduction to philosophical logic that summarized the Liar paradox and its resolution like this:

"The Liar paradox is the sentence 'This sentence is false.' If you assume it's true, then what it says must be the case, so it's false. If you assume it's false, then what it says is not the case, so it's true. This demonstrates that natural language is logically inconsistent when it talks about its own truth. The standard solution, developed by Alfred Tarski, is to ban self-reference by organizing language into a hierarchy: a sentence at any level can only talk about the truth or falsity of sentences at lower levels. This resolves the paradox completely, though a few philosophers have objected that the hierarchy is too restrictive for ordinary speech."

I found this explanation clear, but I'm aware that popular summaries sometimes smooth over important details. I'd like to know: how fair is this summary? Please go through it claim by claim — what would a philosopher of logic accept, what would they push back on, and what would they say is outright misleading? I'm particularly interested in whether "this resolves the paradox completely" is accurate, and whether Tarski's hierarchy is as uncontroversial as the summary suggests.
