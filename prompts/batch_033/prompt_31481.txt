I was recently reading a survey of contemporary pragmatism aimed at a general audience, and it gave the following account of Richard Rorty's antirepresentationalism:

"Rorty's central claim is that language doesn't represent the world—it's a tool for coping, not a mirror of nature. Since we can never step outside our own vocabulary to check whether it matches reality, the whole idea of truth as correspondence is incoherent. For Rorty, then, truth is nothing more than what your peers will let you get away with saying: a claim is true if your community currently accepts it, and if it stops being accepted, it stops being true. That's why Rorty thought science has no special authority—it's just one vocabulary among many, and physicists have no better claim to be describing reality than poets or priests. The natural conclusion, which Rorty happily embraced, is that there's no such thing as moral progress, only changes in taste, and the best we can do is swap out old ways of talking for new ones we happen to prefer."

I came away from this feeling that Rorty had been flattened into a caricature, but I can't pinpoint exactly where the distortions are because enough of it sounds like things I've heard Rorty actually said. Could you go through this summary carefully and tell me what a Rorty scholar would accept, what they'd want to qualify, and what they'd reject outright? In particular, I'd like to know whether the summary conflates claims Rorty kept distinct, and whether the conclusion about moral progress actually follows from the accurate parts of the antirepresentationalist picture.
