After three decades in molecular biology, I retired two years ago and began reading philosophy with the same appetite I once brought to journal clubs. I have been working through the literature on promissory obligation — the question of why a promise binds, what kind of normative force it carries, and to whom the wrong of breaking one is ultimately done. I find myself oscillating between two families of view. According to the first, promising is a social practice constituted by a system of conventional rules: to utter the words in the right context is to make a move within that practice, and the obligation to keep one's word is grounded in the value of having the practice itself — roughly as the obligation not to castle the king through check derives from the value of chess rather than from any independent wrong done to the pieces. According to the second, the obligation is irreducibly interpersonal: when I promise you something, I voluntarily induce in you a warranted expectation that I will perform, and the wrong of breaking the promise is the wrong of disappointing that expectation — of inviting your reliance and then betraying it. On the first view, the victim of a broken promise is, in the first instance, the institution; on the second, it is the person.

I would like you to write a dialogue between a proponent of the practice-based account and a proponent of the assurance-based account. Both characters should be competent and well-informed; neither should serve as a foil. I want to see genuine engagement — each responding to the other's actual arguments rather than offering prepared speeches. Push them on the cases that most clearly separate the two views: promises to the dead, where there is no living promisee whose expectations can be disappointed; promises extracted under duress, where the practice may still classify the utterance as valid but the interpersonal wrong seems absent; promises whose fulfillment has become pointless because the promisee no longer cares; and promises one makes to oneself. Let the dialogue reveal where the two theories diverge in their verdicts and where they might converge for different reasons. I am less interested in a winner than in a clear map of the fault lines.
