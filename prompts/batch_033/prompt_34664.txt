I'm a third-year PhD student in moral and political philosophy, and I'm trying to settle on a dissertation topic. I've been drawn to the debate between egalitarianism and prioritarianism — specifically, the claim that prioritarianism avoids the leveling-down objection that plagues telic egalitarianism (the view that inequality is intrinsically bad). I'm persuaded that the leveling-down objection has real force against views that treat equality as a non-instrumental good, and I'm inclined to think prioritarianism captures what's attractive about egalitarian concern without the counterintuitive implications. But I'm less sure about where the interesting philosophical work now lies.

The way I see it, the terrain includes at least the following clusters of questions: (1) Whether prioritarianism really does avoid the leveling-down objection, or whether structurally analogous problems arise for the priority view under different descriptions — for instance, does the requirement to give extra weight to the worse off generate its own paradoxical implications about trade-offs between small gains to the badly off and large gains to the moderately well off? (2) How to specify the priority weighting function: is there a principled way to determine how much extra weight to give to the worse off, or does prioritarianism turn out to be a family of views with no non-arbitrary way to select among them, and if so, how damaging is that? (3) The relationship between prioritarianism and sufficientarianism — whether the most defensible version of prioritarianism collapses into a sufficiency view (if the priority function is sharply concave) or whether the two are genuinely distinct in their foundational commitments. (4) The ex ante versus ex post distinction — whether prioritarianism should be applied to prospects or to outcomes, and what hangs on this choice, particularly for cases involving risk and uncertainty across persons. (5) Whether prioritarianism's advantages depend on a welfarist framework, and what happens to the view if we adopt a capabilities or resourcist metric of distributive justice instead — does the leveling-down objection resurface in a different form? (6) How prioritarianism handles questions in population ethics — the non-identity problem, the repugnant conclusion, and variable-population cases — and whether it fares better or worse than egalitarian alternatives, and whether the priority view needs to be supplemented or revised to handle these.

I have about three years of focused work ahead of me, and I need to pick one of these clusters (or something I've missed) as the core of a dissertation. I'm not asking for a reading list. What I want is a reasoned judgment about which of these open questions are most worth spending years on — which are upstream of the most other questions, which are tractable enough to yield a dissertation's worth of progress, and which would make the biggest difference to how we evaluate prioritarianism as a competitor to egalitarianism if they were resolved one way rather than another. I'd also like to know which clusters you'd deprioritize, and why.
