I run a reading group on metaethics — about 12 people, mostly grad students but some faculty, 90-minute sessions. I want an activity that makes them feel, not just discuss, the problem of distinguishing genuine moral preferences from biases. The idea is: by the end, they should be genuinely uncertain whether some of their own moral convictions survive the debunking challenge, and they should see why the distinction is so slippery.

Here's what I'm picturing: you give them a set of moral cases (maybe 5-6) and they privately record their judgments. Then you reveal information about each case — framing effects, cultural variation data, evolutionary explanations, or evidence that their judgment flips under irrelevant changes. At each stage they have to say whether they still endorse their original judgment and why. The debrief should map their experience onto the philosophical terrain: evolutionary debunking, the reliability challenge to moral intuitions, the difference between explaining and explaining away, and the problem that there's no agreed test for when a moral conviction is a bias rather than a preference.

Rules, materials, timeline for 90 minutes, and an honest note on what the activity can't capture — I'm guessing it can make the problem vivid but can't resolve it (obviously), and it might tilt people toward skepticism more than the arguments warrant. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong and how to handle it.
