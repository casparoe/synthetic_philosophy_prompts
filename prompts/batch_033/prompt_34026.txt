Lately I've been going back and forth about gender-neutral language rules — you know, style guides banning generic "he," "mankind," "chairman," and requiring singular "they" or "firefighter" and "humankind." I was reading Douglas Hofstadter's old satire "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" (written under the pen name William Satire), where he flips race and gender: "white" is the word for all human beings, "black" is the marked category, and the pronouns are "whe" and "ble." A cranky columnist defends the convention by insisting that "chairwhite" obviously includes black people, that "All whites are created equal" is more elegant than "All persons are created equal," and that singular "they" would wreck the grammar. The joke is that these are just the standard arguments for generic "he" and "chairman" with the categories swapped.

What I can't figure out is what the actual reform practice presupposes about language and thought. It looks like it has to presuppose something Whorfian — that the words we use shape how we think, so changing the default word for humanity will change who counts as the default person. But a lot of people who insist on gender-neutral language would also deny being linguistic determinists. So can the practice be motivated without any claim that language shapes thought — say, purely as a matter of respect, or of refusing to encode one group as the default? And what does the satire itself presuppose about how analogies work, given that it's supposed to make you recoil at "chairwhite" and thereby change your mind about "chairman"? I'm trying to pin down which rival views of the word–concept relationship the practice quietly rules out, where it's actually compatable with several, and what would have to change about the reform project if the presupposed view were swapped for a different one.
