I've been going back and forth with some friends on the ACX subreddit about whether it's ever rational to hold religious beliefs, and someone linked me to a blog post that summarised the James–Clifford debate like this:

"W.K. Clifford argued that it's morally wrong to believe anything without sufficient evidence — his famous line is 'it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence.' His go-to example is a shipowner who convinces himself his leaky ship is seaworthy because he doesnt want to pay for repairs; the ship sinks and everyone dies. Clifford says the shipowner is guilty even if the ship had made it, because the way he formed his belief was irresponsible. William James responded that Clifford's principle is self-defeating: if you apply it to itself, there's insufficient evidence that we should only believe on sufficient evidence, so the principle undermines itself. More importantly, James argued that there are cases where we have to believe something before the evidence is in — like whether other people will like us, or whether a relationship will work — because the belief itself helps make it true. He called these 'genuine options' and said that when an option is live, forced, and momentous, and the evidence doesn't settle it, you're permitted to believe on passional grounds. Basically, James thought Clifford was demanding an impossible standard of proof that would paralyse us in everyday life, and that faith in things like God or moral progress is rational because the evidence only comes after you commit."

I've read both essays but it was years ago, and this summary doesnt quite match my memory — I recall James being more careful than this, and I don't remember the self-defeat point featuring prominently.
