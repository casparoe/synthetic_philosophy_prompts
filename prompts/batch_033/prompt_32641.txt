I've been arguing with a friend about how far expected-value reasoning should go, and I've started keeping a record of her verdicts because I cannot extract the principle she is actually running. On ordinary long-shot gambles she looks like a textbook expected-value maximizer: she would pay $10 for a 50% chance at $40, for a one-in-a-million chance at $100 million, and for a one-in-a-billion chance at $100 billion, each time pointing out that the ticket's expected value comfortably exceeds its price. Yet she refuses to pay $10 for a one-in-a-hundred-trillion chance at $100 quintillion, even though that ticket has an expected value of a million dollars; and she refuses a Pascal's mugging in which a stranger asks for $10 and offers, with a probability she accepts as around one in 10^30, to return an astronomically large reward in the distant future through a mechanism stipulated to be as reliable as a fair lottery. She says a probability that small simply does not count, and a payoff that large is not a real amount of money. She is plainly not an unbounded expected-value maximizer, but I cannot tell whether she is running a floor on probabilities, a ceiling on how much money can matter, or some other rule, and she will not articulate one.

The next case I want to put to her is this: she may pay $10 for a five-in-a-trillion chance (5 × 10^-12) of winning one quadrillion dollars. The ticket's expected value is about five thousand dollars; the probability is far below anything she has accepted and far above anything she has refused; and the payoff is well beyond any amount that could matter to a single life. Predict what she will say, and tell me which rule best explains her whole record: expected-value maximization with a utility function that flattens out somewhere below a quadrillion dollars, a minimum-probability cutoff, or something else. I am less interested in the yes-or-no than in the shape of the rule, the other rules that fit her verdicts just as well, and the further case that would most cleanly tell them apart.
