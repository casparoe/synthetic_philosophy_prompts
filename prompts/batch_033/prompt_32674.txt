I cover the science of mind for a general-interest magazine, and the piece I am planning turns on a puzzle one level up from the usual consciousness debate. It is not the hard problem itself — how physical processes give rise to experience — but what has come to be called the meta-problem of consciousness: why we so readily judge that there is a hard problem, why the explanatory gap feels unbridgeable even to people who accept a physicalist metaphysics, and why that conviction survives increasingly detailed neural explanation. I do not want a survey of proposed solutions. I want the specification against which any proposed solution should be judged: the desiderata for an adequate theory of the meta-problem.

Write that specification. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a preference of one camp, and say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are contested. The purpose I am fixing for adequacy is this: a theory good enough to let an educated lay reader see which proposed explanations are doing real work and which are only relabeling the phenomenon.

The list should go beyond the obvious. It should, for example, distinguish explaining a subject's verbal problem report from explaining the underlying problem intuition; explaining why the intuition arises from explaining why it persists after physicalism is accepted; and explaining why it attaches to sensory and affective experience rather than to, say, syntactic processing or the regulation of blood glucose. It should also say which empirical constraints a theory must respect — developmental, clinical, or comparative — and flag where the empirical record genuinely constrains the options rather than merely decorating them.

The part I most want is the diagnosis of internal tension. I suspect there is a conflict between explaining the intuition in fully mechanistic terms and preserving its claim to point at a genuine explanatory gap, but I want to know whether that conflict is real or merely apparent, and whether it is the deepest one. Are any two or three requirements jointly unsatisfiable, such that no theory can meet the entire list and the list itself becomes the finding? If a short scorecard of the main existing approaches sharpens the criteria, include it; otherwise keep the weight on the requirements.
