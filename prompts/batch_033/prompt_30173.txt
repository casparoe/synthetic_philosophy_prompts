I am a philosopher of mind preparing a paper on the temporal structure of conscious experience. Over the years I have collected my intuitive responses to a range of experimental cases where the apparent timing of a conscious percept and the timing of the relevant neural processing seem to diverge. I would like you to help me extract the principle—or small set of principles—that best systematizes these verdicts, and to tell me honestly which verdicts the principle forces me to abandon.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher of mind would: begin by working through each case to clarify what exactly my verdict commits me to, then look for a unifying principle, test it against the cases, and adjust. I am less interested in a label for my view than in a precise statement of what distinguishes the cases where I judge that a stimulus was never consciously experienced from those where I judge that it was experienced but the memory or report of it failed, and from those where I judge that the question itself rests on a mistake.

Here are the cases:

1. **Color phi.** Two dots, red and green, are flashed 150 ms apart at locations 4° apart. Subjects report a single moving dot that changes from red to green roughly at the midpoint of its trajectory. My verdict: It is a mistake to ask whether the subject first consciously experienced a red dot and a green dot and then retrospectively revised the memory (the "Orwellian" story), or whether the conscious experience itself was of a moving, color-changing dot from the outset (the "Stalinesque" story). The distinction presupposes a Cartesian theater in which experiences occur at definite moments, and that picture is what should be rejected.

2. **Cutaneous rabbit.** Five rapid taps at the wrist, then three near the elbow, then two on the upper arm. Subjects report taps hopping at regular intervals up the entire arm, including unstimulated intermediate locations. My verdict: The taps at unstimulated locations are genuinely experienced—part of the conscious percept itself, not a false memory reported after the fact. The brain constructs the experience of taps at those sites, and that construction is the conscious experience.

3. **Backward masking (metacontrast).** A disk is flashed for 20 ms, followed after 50 ms by a ring surrounding the disk's location. Subjects report only the ring and cannot identify the disk's features even under forced choice. My verdict: The disk was never consciously experienced. The mask prevented the conscious percept from arising; this is not a case of experiencing and then immediately forgetting.

4. **TMS to visual cortex.** A TMS pulse applied 80–100 ms after a visual stimulus eliminates awareness of the stimulus; the same pulse at 40 ms or 130 ms leaves awareness intact. My verdict: At the critical window, the stimulus was never consciously experienced. The neural process was interrupted before it could generate a conscious percept.

5. **Attentional blink.** In RSVP at 10 items/second, subjects identify T1 but miss T2 when T2 appears 200–500 ms after T1, though they report T2 at shorter or longer lags. My verdict: T2 was never consciously experienced during the blink window—the conscious percept never formed.

6. **Libet-style subjective timing.** Direct cortical stimulation of somatosensory cortex must continue roughly 500 ms to produce a reportable sensation, yet subjects date the sensation to the moment stimulation began. My verdict: There is no determinate fact about when the sensation was consciously experienced. The temporal judgment is itself a construction, and the question "when exactly did it occur in consciousness?" has no precise answer.

7. **Sperling partial report.** A 3×4 letter array is flashed for 50 ms. Subjects report only 3–4 letters on whole report but can report any cued row nearly perfectly if the cue arrives within roughly 300 ms. My verdict: All 12 letters were consciously experienced. The limitation is in memory consolidation and report, not in the conscious percept itself. This is an Orwellian case: rich experience, rapidly decaying memory.

I can already see tensions. In case 1 I reject the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction as resting on a bad picture, yet in cases 3 and 4 I freely deploy "Stalinesque" reasoning, and in case 7 I label the case "Orwellian." In case 2 I say the constructed experience is the real experience, which seems to reject the distinction in a different way. In case 6 I am skeptical about determinate timing facts, as in case 1, but for what feel like different reasons.

Your task: (a) State the principle or principles that best capture these verdicts. (b) Identify which verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to revise. (c) Apply the principle to a case I have not listed: the flash-lag illusion, where a moving object is perceived ahead of a briefly flashed stationary object even when they are physically aligned. (d) Advise me whether the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction, on your reconstruction, earns its keep or should be abandoned.
