Analyze the founding and constitutional design of the United Nations through the lens of Kant's essay on perpetual peace. I already know the broad outlines of both, so do not summarize them; I want the analysis itself. On Kant's side, I have in mind the preliminary articles — for instance, the provisions about standing armies, national debts contracted for foreign purposes, and forcible interference in another state's constitution — together with the definitive articles: a republican civil constitution for every state, a pacific federation of free states rather than a single world state, and a cosmopolitan right of hospitality. On the UN side, I have in mind the Charter's general prohibition on the use of force, the collective security powers of the Security Council, the permanent-member veto, the General Assembly's one-state-one-vote equality, the absence of a standing UN army, and the postwar human rights instruments. Give me a careful mapping: which elements of the UN realize Kant's articles, which depart from them, and which fall outside his categories altogether; do not force a fit where the essay is silent. Then give a definite verdict on what the episode shows about Kant's theory. Is the UN best understood as a Kantian pacific federation, as something the essay's argument would have predicted, or as an institution whose actual operation — especially the veto and the persistence of war outside the Council's reach — tells against the essay's central claim that peace must be instituted through law rather than left to prudence or good will? Take one position, defend it, and say explicitly where your mapping is most contestable.
