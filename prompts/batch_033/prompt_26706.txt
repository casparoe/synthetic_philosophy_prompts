I am a retired geologist, and I now sit on the ethics advisory panel of a national body that assesses proposals for deep geological repositories for radioactive waste, with design lives measured in tens of thousands of years. Having recently begun reading philosophy, I have been struck by Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, the claim that many of the things we value, and many of our reasons for acting, depend on an implicit background confidence that humanity will continue after we die. This seems directly relevant to our panel's work, because we frequently see proposals that are justified as protecting future generations, while their safety cases quietly assume that future generations will exist in sufficient institutional continuity to maintain, monitor, and eventually seal the repositories. Scheffler's argument suggests that this dependence is not a mere technical detail but a condition of the proposal's intelligibility as something worth doing at all.

I would like you to turn this insight into a decision procedure we can apply to any proposal in roughly forty-five minutes. Please present it as exactly five numbered steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each step should be a question we ask about the proposal, with enough guidance that a panel member who has not read Scheffler can recognise a satisfactory answer and know what to do when the answer is unsatisfactory. The procedure should distinguish a proposal that merely assumes humanity will continue, which may be benign, from one whose justification would be undermined if that assumption could not be relied upon, which may not be. It should also flag the point at which the panel must make a value judgement rather than a factual determination, and it should be candid about at least one respect in which the procedure is likely to misfire.
