I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and a recurring part of my beat is the politics of classification in medicine and psychiatry: a condition added to a major diagnostic manual, a syndrome that patient groups are campaigning to have recognised, a long-standing diagnosis that a professional body wants to retire, or a statistically common trait newly described as a risk factor. I write several of these stories a month, and the framing decision I make each time — whether to present the change as medicine discovering something about the world or as medicine revising a value-laden decision — has real consequences for readers, patients, insurers, and the people to whom the label is applied. My editor signs off on every piece, so the procedure should be something I can defend in a short editorial conversation.

Philosophers of medicine mark this terrain with a distinction between naturalist and normativist accounts of health and disease. As I understand it, naturalists hold that disease is a matter of biological or psychological dysfunction that can in principle be identified without moral or social value judgements, while normativists hold that judgements of health and disease are partly evaluative: they encode views about which states are harmful, undesirable, or departures from how a life should go. I do not need a survey of this debate. I need it translated into an editorial procedure.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my desk can run on any proposed or contested classification. It should consist of questions to ask in order, and for each question it should say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a discovery framing, where it stops with a decision-and-values framing, and where it hands the matter to a person because the question is one of values or unresolved fact rather than something a checklist can settle. The procedure must be usable by a reporter without medical training in about ninety minutes, using only sources a working journalist can realistically obtain, and it must say what to do when the empirical evidence is thin, contested, or unavailable. It should also be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as tests.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a condition newly added to a major diagnostic manual on the strength of expert consensus but with disputed biological markers; (b) a long-standing diagnosis that a professional body proposes to remove on the grounds that it is now better understood as normal variation; and (c) a patient-led campaign to have a distressing but statistically common condition recognised as a disease so that treatment will be funded. For each case, show which questions carry the weight and where the procedure declines to give a verdict. Include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer my editors are likely to resist, and close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Please write the answer in American English.
