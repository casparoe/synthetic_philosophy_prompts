The International Astronomical Union's 2006 demotion of Pluto is usually told as a story about taxonomy; I want it analyzed as a real case of Kripkensteinian rule-following, meaning the skeptical reading of Wittgenstein's rule-following remarks. I know the standard story: the discovery of Eris and other large trans-Neptunian objects forced astronomers to make explicit what the term planet had always been taken to mean, and the Prague vote adopted a definition under which Pluto failed the requirement that a planet have gravitationally cleared its orbital neighbourhood, so Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet. I am not asking for the astronomy or the institutional history; I want the philosophy of meaning. Begin with a disciplined mapping: who the players are (the IAU as rule-giver, the wider astronomical community as the practice, Pluto and the newly discovered bodies as the disputed next cases), what the pre-2006 rule for applying the term planet actually consisted in, what the options were, and what counts as evidence about the term's prior meaning. Then run the Kripkenstein skeptic's challenge on the case: say why no fact about past usage, past dispositions, or past criteria determined the correct answer once the rule ran out, and whether the vote is best understood as discovering the term's real extension or as stipulating a new rule and projecting it backward. Then say which features of the episode the skeptical-solution picture — meaning as settled by communal agreement in practice rather than by a fact — explains well, and where it misdescribes or goes silent, especially on the difference between an explicit, dated, written vote by a designated body and the tacit agreement of a shared form of life. Close by saying what the episode in turn shows about the Kripkenstein picture: a confirmation of the skeptical solution, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated because it was built for an individual speaker extending an arithmetic function rather than a community legislating a kind term.

Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Let the points run: the mapping; the skeptic's challenge applied; what the skeptical solution explains; where it misdescribes or goes silent; and the verdict on the theory.
