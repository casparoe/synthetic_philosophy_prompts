I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and twice a year I run a public event — an "ideas evening" — in a rented bookstore space. The audience is about thirty people, mostly curious nonspecialists, and we have a little under two hours. The format is that I don't lecture; I stage an activity that gets people into the grip of a philosophical puzzle, and then we talk through what happened.

For the next event I want to tackle Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle. The puzzle, as you know, runs like this: a billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you violently ill for a day but has no lasting effects. The money is deposited the moment you form the intention — you don't have to actually drink the toxin to get paid. You know all of this. The question is: can you form the intention? You want the money, you have every reason to intend, but you also know that once you have the money, you will have no reason to drink the toxin. And you know that you know that. So how could you possibly intend to do something you know you won't do?

I want to put the audience through a live version of this structure, with real but small stakes, so that they feel the bind in their own decision-making before I name the puzzle or mention Kavka. The stakes should be tangible enough that they care about the outcome, but nothing that would make anyone genuinely distressed. I need a clean design: rules, materials, timing, a procedure for verifying intentions without relying on self-report alone, and a debrief sequence that maps what happened in the room onto the philosophical puzzle, including the distinction between intending and merely expecting or wanting, and the question of whether rationality requires you to be able to form the intention or whether it requires you to be unable to.

Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — which step will confuse people, which move will feel artificial, what participants will try to game — and how to handle it. And be frank about which feature of the philosophical puzzle the live activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in the debrief.

Finally, I want you to flag the part of your design you are least confident about, and tell me what kind of feedback from a trial run would change your mind about it — what you would need to see to abandon that element and try something different.

I'd like the whole thing in continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
