I got into an argument with a friend who does public finance about safe Pareto improvements — the idea that you can identify policies that make some people better off and no one worse off without committing to a full utility function for each person, because you only count changes that would be improvements under every preference relation consistent with each person's observed choices. After reading around, I've come away with a fairly deflationary view: once you take behavioral evidence seriously — intransitivities, framing effects, present bias, social or moral motives tangled up in market choices — the safe set shrinks to nearly nothing, and the framework only gives you interesting results when you quietly smuggle back in the rational-choice structure it was supposed to avoid needing. I now think safe Pareto improvements are rare enough that the idea is mostly empty for the policy cases people actually argue about.

By that I mean the actual considerations — empirical regularities, theoretical moves, formal results — that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. Which findings would show the safe set is larger then I think? Which moves could expand the set without begging the question? Which of my assumptions is doing the most work, and what breaks if you relax it? Distinguish decisive from merely awkward, and don't only give the critics' line — say what a defender of safe Pareto improvements would themselves concede counts against them. If you think my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, say so and whether that matters here. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the specifics and single out the point you're least sure about.
