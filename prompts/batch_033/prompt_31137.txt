I've been working through my own intuitions about intentional action after reading some experimental philosophy on side-effect cases, and I'm writing a short piece for a seminar. I want to make sure I'm not just rationalizing a mess of case-specific reactions, so I'd like help extracting the principle behind my verdicts, if there is one. Act as a philosopher of mind and action who works on intentional action and the methods of experimental philosophy. Think through each case individually first, then brainstorm candidate principles before settling on the best one or two, and write in American English.

Here are the cases, with my honest verdicts. (1) A vice president is told that a new manufacturing plan will increase profits and, as a side effect, damage a nearby river. He says, "I don't care about the river; launch the plan." The plan is launched and the river is damaged. I say he damaged the river intentionally. (2) A different vice president is told the same plan will increase profits and, as a side effect, clean up a nearby river. She says, "I don't care about the river; launch the plan." The plan is launched and the river is cleaned up. I say she did not clean up the river intentionally. (3) A third vice president is told the plan will increase profits and damage the river. She says, "I hate that it will damage the river. Go ahead, but minimize the damage and document everything for remediation." I find myself not wanting to say she damaged the river intentionally, or at least far less than in (1). (4) A surgeon performs a novel operation to save a patient's life, knowing that as a side effect it will leave a distinctive scar pattern that colleagues will immediately recognize as her handiwork and that will enhance her reputation. She is indifferent to the reputation. I say she left the scar intentionally. (5) An office manager installs new software knowing it will speed up billing and, as a side effect, shift which department handles late accounts. She has no views about the shift either way. I say she intentionally shifted which department handles the accounts. (6) A junior manager is ordered by the board to implement the plan that will damage the river. He objects, but is told he will be fired if he refuses. He implements it, knowing the damage will occur. I say he did not damage the river intentionally, or at least I am much less willing to say he did than in (1).

Is there one principle that gets all six of these, or close? I can see at least two places where my verdicts pull against each other: (1) and (6) differ even though the foreseen harm is the same, and (2) and (4) differ even though both involve a good side effect the agent doesn't care about. A simple "foreseen equals intentional" rule fits some cases but not others; a simple "bad side effect equals intentional, good side effect equals not" rule fits (1) and (2) but not (3), (4), or (5). State the best candidate principle precisely, show how it handles each case, say what it predicts about cases I haven't listed (for example, a harmful side effect the agent didn't know about, a good side effect the agent actively wanted, a bad side effect produced clumsily, a neutral side effect the agent regrets), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which one or two I would have to revise under it. Please also separate what the empirical literature on folk judgments actually shows from what is a contested philosophical interpretation of it, and don't present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
