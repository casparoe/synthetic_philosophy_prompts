I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on philosophical approaches to translation, and I also work as an assistant editor at a small literary press that publishes poetry in translation. The two roles keep colliding: at the press, we argue every month about whether a submission that keeps a source poem's rhyme and meter while taking liberties with literal meaning is faithful or a betrayal, and in class we've been reading Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot*, which is almost entirely an extended argument-by-example about exactly that tension — built around dozens of renderings of a single short French lyric by Clément Marot. I'd like to turn what Hofstadter actually commits himself to into something our editorial team can use, because the senior editors won't read the book and I can't adjudicate every submission by intuition.

Please write a reusable decision procedure for evaluating a constrained, form-preserving translation when it sacrifices some literal or semantic fidelity. The procedure should be one a non-philosopher editor can run in about twenty minutes on a single submission. It should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "revise" or "reject," and where it must hand the decision to a human because the question is one of judgment rather than fact. Ground the steps in the distinctions the book actually turns on — for instance, the difference between copying a formal feature mechanically and recreating an analogous constraint in the target language, and the question of which formal features are constitutive of a particular poem rather than merely ornamental — and be explicit about which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions: (1) a translation of a French villanelle that keeps the villanelle's rhyme scheme and refrain structure but shifts several images in order to keep the rhymes; (2) a translation of a sonnet that is semantically close but drops the rhyme scheme entirely; and (3) a translation that keeps meter and rhyme and reads beautifully in English, but turns the original's grave, ceremonious register into something jokey and colloquial — the sort of version our team would be tempted to accept on readability alone. For each, say what the procedure outputs and why. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle and where an editor's taste inevitably re-enters. End with a one-sentence summary of the decision rule as the final line, in the form \answer{...}.
