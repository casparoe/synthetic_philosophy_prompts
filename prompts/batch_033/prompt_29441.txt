I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation this term, and my study group keeps discovering that we disagree about particular cases long before we disagree about any named theory. I'd like you to write a sorting quiz for the group — a "Which theory of causation do you actually hold?" quiz — with eight to ten questions and a scoring key.

The quiz should sort people by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they prefer, so no question should simply ask whether the student thinks causation is a matter of counterfactual dependence. Each question should instead present a specific case or a sharp methodological claim, and offer two or three answer options that are first-order verdicts or reasons for a verdict, phrased so someone can answer without already knowing which position the answer points toward.

The seminar has been built around the contrast between counterfactual theories (roughly, Lewis's analysis of token causation in terms of counterfactual dependence between events, including his later influence-based refinement) and interventionist theories (roughly, Woodward's account, on which causal claims concern relationships among variables that are invariant under interventions). I want the quiz built around where those two come apart, but not reduced to a two-way choice. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these leaves: a Lewis-style counterfactual theory, a Woodward-style interventionism, a regularity/INUS view in the Mackie tradition, and a process view in the Salmon–Dowe tradition. You may add a probabilistic leaf if the questions genuinely earn it; if they do not, say so rather than forcing it.

Please make sure the following cases or claims appear somewhere in the quiz, because these are the ones our seminar has actually argued about: early preemption; symmetric overdetermination; causation by omission; double prevention; and a proportionality case (for example, a pigeon trained to peck at red things that pecks at a scarlet target). At least one question should also probe whether the student is willing to let an account of causation use irreducibly causal notions such as intervention or manipulation, since that is the fault line between reductive and non-reductive approaches in our reading.

The scoring key should give, for each leaf, one sentence describing the case or question that view handles most awkwardly. It should also flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot reliably separate — I suspect, for instance, that Lewis's influence account and Woodward's fine-grained variable account will converge on the preemption case, and that a purely probabilistic view may be indistinguishable from a regularity view on these questions. Where an answer pattern has no natural leaf, note the empty cell and say briefly whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. The audience is a small group of philosophy graduate students, so the questions can assume familiarity with the standard cases and terminology.
