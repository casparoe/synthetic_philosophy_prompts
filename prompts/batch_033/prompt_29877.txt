I am a teaching assistant for an upper-division undergraduate course in contemporary political philosophy, and I am calibrating my grading for a question on Rawls's account of public reason. The exam question was as follows:

In Political Liberalism and the later essay "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited," Rawls develops an account of public reason. Explain what public reason requires of citizens when religious or other comprehensive doctrines figure in their political advocacy. In particular: (i) distinguish the public political forum from the background culture; (ii) explain the proviso and the wide view of public political culture; and (iii) say whether public reason is equivalent to secular reason.

One student submitted the answer below. I would like a careful second opinion before I assign a mark. Please grade the answer on a 0–10 scale and provide a point-by-point assessment that justifies the grade: go through the main claims in the answer, note which are correct, and identify any errors, conflations, or unsupported inferences, however subtle, explaining in each case what the more accurate Rawlsian position would be. I am looking for specificity rather than a general impression.

Student answer:

Rawls's central claim is that when citizens deliberate about constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, they should offer reasons that all reasonable citizens can accept, and these reasons cannot rest on any particular comprehensive doctrine. Religious doctrines are comprehensive doctrines, so in the public political forum religious reasons are generally excluded from public justification. Rawls is careful to say that this is not because religious reasons are false or irrational; the difficulty is that they cannot be shared by citizens who hold different comprehensive doctrines. He distinguishes the public political forum, where public reason governs, from the background culture—churches, universities, families, and voluntary associations—where citizens remain free to reason from their comprehensive doctrines. He also describes a moral duty of civility: citizens should be able to explain to one another how the positions they support can be justified by public reasons.

In the later wide view of public political culture, Rawls relaxes this restriction through the proviso: citizens may introduce religious or other comprehensive reasons into public political discussion, but only after they have translated those reasons into secular terms that all citizens could share. This shows that public reason is, in the end, a form of secular reason: the point of the proviso is to ensure that only secular considerations do justificatory work in public political debate. Rawls is therefore not hostile to religion as such, but he does require religious citizens to refrain from bringing their faith into public debate except insofar as it can be restated in a secular idiom.
