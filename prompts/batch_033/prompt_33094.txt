In a discussion group I follow, someone defended panpsychism against the combination problem by saying the worry about many small minds becoming one big mind is no more troubling than the fact that a picture on a screen is made of many small colored squares that are not themselves pictures of the whole scene. Just as the image is nothing over and above the arrangement of pixels, he argued, a human mind could be nothing over and above the arrangement of micro-experiences. I want to know how far this analogy actually holds.

Lay out the correspondence in detail: what in the pixel case is supposed to correspond to micro-subjects, to their experiences, to the arrangement relation, and to the unified macro-subject. Then identify where the analogy breaks. In particular, pixels each have a color but no perspective on the whole image, while micro-subjects, if panpsychism is true, are supposed to have their own point of view; does that difference matter for whether the analogy can carry the conclusion? Also, an image looks like one thing only because an outside viewer surveys all the pixels at once, whereas in the mind case there is by definition no outside viewer to take in the separate micro-experiences; so where does the unity come from? I would like the disanalogies separated into those that are merely cosmetic and those that are doing the real philosophical work.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy was being used to support — that the combination problem is overstated and an arrangement of micro-experiences can constitute a unified human subject — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly importing the very unity it was supposed to explain. If there is a better analogy available, say what it is and why it is better.
