I've spent years in the rationalist blogosphere and have internalized a strong version of the Aumann-inspired norm against persistent disagreement. My position, roughly, is this: when two agents share common priors and their posterior probabilities for a proposition are common knowledge between them, their posteriors cannot differ. I take this to have genuine normative force—not merely as a formal curiosity, but as a practical guide to how I should respond to disagreement. When I find myself in sustained disagreement with someone whose reasoning I respect, after we've exchanged all our evidence and walked through each other's models, I treat the residual disagreement as strong evidence that at least one of us is processing information irrationally. I've changed my mind on substantial questions this way—AI timelines, the interpretation of certain cause-prioritization estimates, and more mundane things—and I credit the norm with making me more accurate.

But I want to stress-test this. I want to know what would count against the position—not the standard objections I've already heard a hundred times (common priors are descriptively false, we never achieve literal common knowledge, computational boundedness breaks the proof, etc.). I've absorbed those and they haven't moved me, because I think the position survives them as a regulative ideal in roughly the way that Bayesian epistemology survives the fact that no one is a perfect Bayesian. If the ideal is unreachable, that doesn't make it useless as a compass.

What I'm after is a map of the position's genuine exposure: what observations, arguments, formal results, or edge cases would actually force me to abandon or substantially revise the view that persistent disagreement after full disclosure is a reliable signal of irrationality? I want defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be—fatal, major revision required, merely awkward. I want to know which defeaters a committed defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss as irrelevant. And I'd like an honest assessment of whether the position, as it's actually held in the rationalist community (with all the standard caveats and idealizations), has been insulated against falsification to the point where that very insulation is a problem. If the position can't lose, does it say anything?
