I've been turning this over ever since my brother started sending me links to things last year. At first it was the standard stuff — hidden cancer cures suppressed by pharma, that sort of thing — and I dismissed it. But then I caught myself nodding along when a friend said it was obvious Epstein's network was being protected, and I realized I had no clear basis for treating these cases differently. I've been trying to reconstruct my own epistemology of suspicious beliefs ever since, starting from my gut reactions to concrete cases rather than from some principle I already hold.

Here are my verdicts. I think the reporters who pursued Watergate were reasonable to believe there was a cover-up before the tapes surfaced. I think the Tuskegee whistleblowers were reasonable to believe the government was deliberately letting Black men go untreated, even before the story broke. I think people who believed, before the Church Committee, that the CIA was running domestic mind-control experiments were reasonable — MKUltra sounded like science fiction but the belief was warranted. I think people who believed the tobacco companies knew they were selling a carcinogenic product and hid it were reasonable long before the documents were unsealed. On the other side: I think people who believe the moon landing was faked are unreasonable, and the same for flat earth, and for the claim that the Sandy Hook shooting was a hoax staged with crisis actors. I think the 9/11 controlled demolition theory is unreasonable. I think the belief that vaccines cause autism is unreasonable. Here is where it gets uncomfortable for me: I think the early COVID lab leak hypothesis was not unreasonable to entertain in 2020, even though it was widely dismissed as a conspiracy theory at the time, but I also think people who now insist it was definitely a bioweapon are being unreasonable. I think believing that Epstein's network involved powerful people who were protected was reasonable long before the details emerged, but believing that every public figure you dislike was on the island is unreasonable. And here is the case that bothers me most: I think the belief that the 2020 U.S. election was stolen through systematic fraud is unreasonable — but I struggle to articulate what makes it different in kind from Watergate, where I said the journalists were reasonable before the proof was in. Both involve claims that powerful actors subverted democratic processes and covered their tracks. Both were pursued by people who cited anomalies and insider testimony. Yet my verdicts point in opposite directions, and I cannot name the feature that does the work.

I am trying to find out whether there is a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures this whole pattern. I am especially interested in what it would say about a case I keep going back and forth on: the claim that evidence of non-human intelligence has been actively suppressed by a faction within the military-industrial complex. And I would like to know which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to abandon — and whether the Watergate/election pair can be kept intact or whether one of those verdicts has to give.
