The debate concerns whether the badness of pain is intrinsic to pain itself. The central piece of evidence is pain asymbolia, a dissociative syndrome in which patients can locate, classify, and rate a noxious stimulus — reporting, for example, that it is sharp, hot, on the left hand, and intense — while denying that the stimulus is unpleasant or that they want it to stop. They typically do not withdraw, grimace, or otherwise avoid the stimulus. This syndrome is commonly taken to indicate that pain involves at least two components: a sensory-discriminative component, which registers the stimulus and its features, and an affective-motivational component, which makes the experience unpleasant and drives avoidance. The syndrome is distinct from insensitivity to pain, in which the noxious stimulus is not registered as such in the first place. The philosophical disagreement is over what this dissociation establishes. One side holds that the badness of pain is intrinsic to the pain state itself, so that a state with the relevant sensory character but no unpleasantness is either not really pain or is being misdescribed. The other side holds that the badness is a distinct affective-motivational component that is normally but only contingently attached to the sensory component, so asymbolia shows the sensory component occurring alone.
