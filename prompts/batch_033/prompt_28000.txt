I'm drafting a short essay, aimed at a LessWrong-style audience, on what counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail jointly show about decision theory, and I'd like detailed comments on the notes below before I write it up. Please comment on four things: (a) whether my characterizations of the causal, evidential, and updateless responses to the two cases are accurate; (b) whether the asymmetry I propose — that the mugging rewards a policy that is not weakly dominated, while the blackmail punishes one that is — is the right way to separate the cases, or whether it papers over the hard part; (c) whether the point that XOR blackmail is misnamed does real work or is a distraction; and (d) whether these notes would make a good essay and what should be cut. Lead with your overall bottom line in the first sentence, then give your comments, with no preamble and no restating of the prompt.

Notes for the essay:

Counterfactual mugging. Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, Omega asks you for $100; if it lands heads, Omega gives you $10,000, but only if it predicted that you would have paid in the tails case. You find yourself in the tails case, facing the request. A causal decision theorist refuses: the coin has already landed, and paying now has no causal effect on any payoff. An evidential decision theorist also refuses: conditional on being asked, paying is not evidence for anything worth $100. But Omega predicted this, so in heads worlds such agents receive nothing. An updateless reasoner evaluates the policy "pay if asked" across both branches, sees it as trading $100 in tails for $10,000 in heads, and pays. The standard lesson is usually put as: choose policies rather than actions, and do not update away the information that your action in one branch is correlated, through the predictor, with payoffs in another branch.

XOR blackmail. Omega, a perfect predictor, commits to sending you a message exactly when your house has termites XOR you pay Omega $1,000. Termite damage would cost you $1,000,000. A causal reasoner refuses: paying does not causally prevent termites. An evidential reasoner pays: conditional on receiving the message, paying makes termites impossible and refusing makes them certain. But compare the two policies globally. If you are predicted to pay, the message arrives exactly when there are no termites, so you pay $1,000 in the termite-free worlds, while termite worlds get no message and you still suffer the damage. If you are predicted to refuse, the message arrives exactly when there are termites, and you suffer the damage there; termite-free worlds get no message and cost you nothing. Refusing therefore weakly dominates paying: it does no worse on termites and avoids the $1,000. An updateless reasoner refuses. The lesson I want to draw is that conditioning on "the message arrived" is itself the mistake, because that observation is downstream of the predictor's model of your policy.

The proposed asymmetry. Both cases have a predictor, a hidden state, and an action with no local causal effect on that state, yet the correct policy pays in the mugging and refuses in the blackmail. My way of stating the difference: in the mugging, the policy "pay if asked" is not weakly dominated by "refuse if asked" — it does worse in tails but much better in heads — so a global expected-utility comparison selects it. In the blackmail, the policy "pay if message" is weakly dominated by "refuse if message," so no global evaluation should select it, however good paying looks conditional on receiving the message. The joint lesson would be: evaluate whole policies across all branches, respect weak dominance at the policy level, and treat observations produced by the predictor's model of your policy as evidence about your policy rather than as information to update on before choosing.

Points I am unsure about. Is weak dominance at the policy level the cleanest way to separate the two cases, or does it smuggle in a causal notion that needs independent justification? Does the point that XOR blackmail is misnamed matter for the diagnosis — would rewriting the problem as genuine blackmail (Omega reveals the termites unless you pay) change the correct policy? And is the pair better read as one argument for updatelessness, or as an argument that updatelessness alone is insufficient and must be supplemented by an account of which policy-level correlations count as consequences of your decision procedure?
