Two friends of mine, Sara and Tom, got into an argument after our decision theory seminar and neither would back down, so I'm hoping you can referee it. The whole thing was about the old Death in Damascus problem—the one from Gibbard and Harper 1978 where Death is a perfect predictor of where you'll be, and if you stay in Damascus Death meets you there, and if you flee to Aleppo Death meets you there instead. Their argument went like this.

Sara started by saying that causal decision theory gives the right answer: since your act doesn't causally affect where Death is, and you have no prior about Death's location, staying and fleeing have the same expected utility, so neither is rationally required. It's just a case where you're indifferent and you pick. Tom replied that this is unstable, because the moment you actually decide to stay, you become confident Death is in Damascus, so staying now looks like certain death and fleeing looks better—and the same reversal happens if you decide to flee. So a theory that says "you're indifferent" is giving advice you can't actually follow.

Sara came back by saying Tom was mixing up the standpoint of choosing with the standpoint of having chosen. Before you've settled on an act, you haven't conditioned on your own choice, so the expected utilities really are equal. Conditioning on the act you're about to perform is exactly the mistake evidential decision theory makes, and it's why evidentialists get Newcomb problems wrong. Tom said that's too quick, because deliberation itself is a source of evidence: as soon as you lean toward staying, you learn something about your own dispositions—that you're the kind of agent who will probably choose staying—and that's evidence about where Death is. So a correct theory has to take that dynamic into account, not just the static ex ante utilities.

Sara then said fine, take the dynamics into account: you'd need a ratifiability condition, and in Death in Damascus no pure act is ratifiable, since after choosing either one you'd prefer to have chosen the other. But that doesn't mean the theory is broken; it means this problem has no stable rational resolution, and we should just accept that. Tom said that's a cop-out. Decision theory is supposed to tell you what to do, even in weird cases. If it says "nothing is rationally permissible," it's admitting defeat. He thought the real lesson of Death in Damascus is that the problem is badly posed: a perfect predictor of your act is impossible in a way that matters, so the thought experiment shouldn't be taken as a counterexample to anything. Sara said that's question-begging—we stipulate the predictor precisely to test our theory, and a theory that can only handle cases by denying their coherence is worse than one that handles them head-on.

So the question: can you adjudicate this move by move? I don't just want a verdict on who wins overall. I want to know, for each specific point in their exchange, who's right about that point and why, and whether there's a way both of them are talking past each other.
