I'm an undergrad and I help run our university's student prediction market, the one where people buy and sell contracts that pay a dollar if some campus event happens. I'm the only person on the team who has taken Decision Theory, so I'm the defacto odds person: each week I post the opening prices before trading opens, and we act as the counterparty, meaning we'll buy or sell a contract at the listed price until the order book takes over. The markets are small real-money things (capped stakes) like whether the library renovation finishes during fall term, whether the dinning-hall strike lasts past March, who wins the student election, whether the provost resigns. Last week we got taken apart on a library market: someone bought a combination of our contracts on 'finished during fall term,' 'finished during spring term,' and 'not finished by the end of spring term' that paid out no matter what, and it wasn't luck — our prices summed to more than a dollar across three outcomes that should sum to exactly a dollar. My co-managers just said the guy was sharp and we should post better prices, but I'm pretty sure we were Dutch-booked because our prices were incoherent. In class we proved the Dutch book theorem — a set of prices that violates the probability axioms is exploitable for sure loss — but going from the theorem to something I can actually run at 11pm before the market opens is a lot harder than I expected.

So write me a procedure, a checklist I can actually use and hand to my co-managers who haven't taken the course, for deciding whether a set of prices we're about to post is coherent and won't expose us to a sure loss. Don't just say 'make them satisfy the probability axioms'; I need the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says stop and fix prices, where it says don't open the market at all, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a judgment call. Then run it on at least these cases: (1) the library market from last week with prices 50¢, 30¢, and 25¢ on the three outcomes above; (2) a runoff-election market where we want to post 'A wins outright' at 60¢ and 'A makes the runoff' at 40¢; (3) a market everyone's been asking for on which professor gets tenure, where the outcomes aren't mutually exclusive and one of them is basically unverifiable — I suspect the procedure will say don't open it, which my co-managers will hate, but I need to know. Also be honest about what the procedure can't catch: correlated outcomes, people who know more than we do, thin markets, that kind of thing.
