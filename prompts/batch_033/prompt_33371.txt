I'm a TA grading a midterm in a philosophy of language course, and I'd like a second opinion on one answer before I put a number on it. The exam question was: 'Epistemicism and supervaluationism are two leading accounts of vagueness. Explain how each view treats a borderline sentence like "Alice is bald," and identify one important difference between them.' Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Epistemicism, defended by Williamson, says vagueness isn't a semantic deficiency but a kind of ignorance: 'Alice is bald' has a definite truth value, and there is a sharp cutoff between the bald and the not-bald, but we can't know where it is. Supervaluationism, by contrast, says the meaning of 'bald' doesn't fix a single cutoff. A sentence is true if it's true on every acceptable way of making the term precise, and false if it's false on all of them; borderline sentences are true on some precisifications and false on others, so they receive a third truth value, neither true nor false. Because of this, supervaluationism gives up the law of excluded middle for borderline cases: 'Alice is bald or not bald' is neither true nor false when Alice is borderline, since neither disjunct is true. So the main difference is that epistemicism keeps bivalence and locates vagueness in our knowledge, while supervaluationism is a three-valued logic that locates vagueness in meaning."
