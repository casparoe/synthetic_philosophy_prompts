Question: "Present the two-envelope paradox and explain what you think the mistake in the reasoning is."

Student answer: "You're shown two envelopes, one has twice the money of the other. You pick one. Before opening it, you're offered the chance to switch. You reason: let the amount in my envelope be X. The other envelope has either X/2 or 2X, each with probability 1/2. Expected value of switching: (1/2)(X/2) + (1/2)(2X) = 5X/4, which is greater than X. So you should switch. But by symmetry the same reasoning applies after switching, so you'd switch forever, which is absurd. The mistake is that the calculation implicitly assumes a uniform distribution over all possible dollar amounts — an improper prior. For any proper prior distribution, the expected gain from switching before you open the envelope is zero, not positive. And this holds even after you open the envelope: once you've opened it and seen, say, $100, you still have no reason to switch, because the prior washes out the apparent 25% advantage. The paradox just comes from using an ill-defined probability distribution."
