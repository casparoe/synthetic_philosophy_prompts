I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on just war theory and I've been deep in Anscombe's stuff — 'Mr. Truman's Degree,' 'War and Murder,' and *Intention*. Something's been nagging at me. She died in 2001, right before the U.S. drone program really took off. I'm trying to figure out what she'd say about targeted killing by drone.

On one hand, you could argue drones are *better* than what she condemned. She went after Truman for intentionally killing innocents as a means — the civilians at Hiroshima and Nagasaki were the target, not collateral. Drone strikes at least *aim* at specific militants, and the civilian deaths are (supposedly) foreseen but not intended, which her own double-effect framework should in principle allow. So maybe she'd see drones as a move in the right direction.

But then I think about her insistence that you can't just redescribe what you're doing to make it sound better — the intention is in the action itself, not in some private mental act you can point to later. And when you look at how drone programs actually operate — signature strikes where you don't even know who you're killing, 'combatant' defined so loosely it covers any military-age male in a strike zone — it starts looking a lot like the kind of indiscriminate killing she thought was absolutely ruled out. She was pretty clear that if you're willing to kill the innocent as a means, the whole moral framework has already collapsed.

So what do you think? Would Anscombe condemn the drone program in the same terms she used against Truman, or would she see a morally relevant difference? And does the physical distance of the operator matter to her framework at all, or is that just a red herring?
