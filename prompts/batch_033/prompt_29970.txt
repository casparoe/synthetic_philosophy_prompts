I'm teaching a graduate epistemology seminar and want a handout that sorts students into positions on the dogmatism paradox by what they say about cases, not by which label they recognize. The paradox, as I will set it up: a person knows P on good evidence, and knows that if P is true then any evidence against P is evidence for something false and therefore misleading; she then seems to be in a position to know of any particular piece of alleged counterevidence, before examining it, that it is misleading — which appears to license dismissing counterevidence unread. Nearly everyone finds that conclusion intolerable, but it is unclear which step to give up. I want the handout to be a decision tree with six to eight leaves, in which every branch node is a question a student can answer from a concrete case or a plainly stated claim without knowing the names of any positions. Use at least these two diagnostic cases, and add one or two of your own only if the tree needs them: (1) a defense lawyer who knows from a definitive DNA test that her client is innocent, and then receives a sealed witness statement placing him at the scene; (2) a physician who knows a diagnosis from a conclusive test and then receives a less reliable test result pointing the other way.

The leaves should include, in whatever arrangement seems most faithful to the logical space: a closure-denying response; a response on which knowing that the evidence is misleading does not by itself license ignoring it because the justification for that second-order belief is merely propositional rather than doxastic; a level-splitting or higher-order-evidence response; and a concessively dogmatist response that accepts the license to ignore in at least some cases. If you think one of these collapses into another, or needs to be split into two leaves, say so. Each leaf should carry its standard label where one exists (or be marked unnamed), a one-sentence statement of the view, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then, separately: flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate and explain what further question would be needed; identify any combination of branch answers that no actual position occupies, and say for each whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; and tell me which single question in the tree is doing the most classificatory work and whether its wording is fair to a student who has not yet taken a side.
