At a small philosophy discussion group I attend, two members got stuck on Searle’s distinction between original and derived intentionality. Maya opened with Dennett’s wandering two-bitser thought experiment, described roughly like this: a vending machine built to accept U.S. quarters is shipped to Panama, where quarter-balboas have the same weight, diameter, thickness, and metal composition as U.S. quarters but different markings, and the machine happily accepts them. She said the machine’s coin-detector state doesn’t really mean U.S. quarter in the way her thought about a quarter does; its aboutness is borrowed from the engineer who built it, so it is a clear case of derived intentionality. Tom replied that if borrowed from the engineer is enough to make something merely derived, then human thoughts are also merely derived: our neurons were not designed by engineers, but they were shaped by natural selection, and selection can fix what a state is for just as much as an engineer can. Maya shot back that selection doesn’t have intentions or purposes; it is an algorithmic process with no designer, so it can’t lend content to our brain states the way an engineer lends content to a coin detector. Tom said that is a double standard: function can arise from natural selection even though selection itself doesn’t intentionally design things, and if function is what grounds the vending machine’s derived intentionality, then evolutionary function can ground human intentionality too, unless Maya can point to a difference that does the real work. Maya then brought up the Chinese Room: a person following written rules can pass for a Chinese speaker without understanding Chinese, so mental content cannot be reduced to functional or syntactic competence; there is a first-person residue that the two-bitser lacks. Tom replied that the man in the Chinese Room is not the whole system, just as the coin detector is not the whole vending machine, and once you grant that the room as a whole can have intentionality in some derived sense, Searle’s own picture has no principled way to deny that it has the same kind of intentionality humans have, unless he is simply helping himself to a “real” understanding that the thought experiment was supposed to establish. They turned to me because I was the only one not arguing, and I honestly couldn’t tell who was right where. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who is more right overall, if anyone.
