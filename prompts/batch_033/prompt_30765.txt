I am in my penultimate year of secondary school, and I have become puzzled by a marking practice I used to take for granted. At my school, every assessed group project is given a single shared mark: each member of the group receives exactly the same mark, regardless of who contributed what, and teachers defend this by saying that it mirrors how teams operate outside school and that it teaches us to hold one another accountable. I have recently read about the prisoner's dilemma, and the scheme now looks to me like a one-shot dilemma in which the individually rational move is to let the others do the work; if everyone reasons that way the project collapses, yet in practice most groups do finish their projects. I would like a short written analysis of what theory of co-operation, self-interest, and motivation this practice quietly presupposes, distinguishing what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and noting where several different theories would each license the same practice. In particular: does the practice presuppose that the students are in a repeated interaction with reputational effects, rather than a single one-off game, even though each project is assessed once and never revisited? Does it presuppose that groups will informally punish free-riders, and if so, what does that presuppose about the punishers' own incentives? Which rival views of co-operation does the practice rule out, and is it compatible with several different theories (for instance, that the group reasons as a single agent, or that people have an unselfish taste for punishing slackers)? Is the practice internally consistent, given that its justification seems to rely on ongoing relationships while its incentive structure is one-shot? And what would the marking scheme have to look like if the school instead assumed that students are purely self-interested in a one-off setting, or that co-operation needs formal enforcement rather than informal pressure? Please write the answer in British English, as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Make the fifth item a statement of how confident you are in the overall reconstruction and which of the other four points you are least sure of.
