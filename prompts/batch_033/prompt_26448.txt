I've spent a lot of time thinking about dreams — not from reading philosophy, just from paying attention to my own dreams and trying to figure out what's actually happening when I sleep. I've ended up with a set of views, but I'm not sure they all fit together. I'd like you to go through them carefully and tell me where the tensions are.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher would: take each commitment seriously, trace out what it implies, and flag any pairs or groups that can't all be true at once. For each tension, tell me how severe it is and what I'd have to give up or revise to resolve it. Also, if any pairs only look like they're in tension but actually aren't, point that out — I want to know which problems are real and which ones dissolve on closer inspection. Before giving your final assessment, work through the commitments systematically; it may help to go pair by pair and ask what each pair jointly entails before deciding whether they conflict.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) It seems obvious to me that I have experiences while dreaming. When I wake up from a nightmare, the fear was real, the images were vivid — I was experiencing something, not just waking up with a story in my head.

(2) I believe that for any genuine experience, there must be something it's like for the subject at the time the experience occurs. If there's nothing it's like to be me at 3 AM, then I'm not having an experience at 3 AM, whatever I say when I wake up.

(3) I don't think the inability to report an experience while it's happening shows that it isn't an experience. People in flow states, infants, and locked-in patients all have experiences they can't report in the moment, and we don't deny they're having them.

(4) But I do think there's a real difference between having an experience at time T and merely being caused, by events at T, to be disposed to tell a certain story later. If dreaming is nothing more than the process of waking up primed to narrate, then I wasn't having experiences during sleep — I was just a storytelling mechanism that activates upon waking.

(5) Neuroscience seems to settle this: during REM sleep, the visual cortex, amygdala, and other regions show activity patterns very similar to waking perception and emotion. That looks like experience happening in real time, not just a dormant disposition that waits until morning.

(6) However, I worry that this neuroscientific argument might be circular. We only know that certain brain activity correlates with experience because waking subjects can tell us what they're experiencing. During sleep, we have no such reports — so we might be assuming that similar brain activity means similar experience, which is exactly what's at issue.

(7) Lucid dreaming seems to break through the whole problem. When I'm lucid, I know I'm dreaming, I make decisions, I have a stream of conscious thought. That's undeniably an experience. And since lucid and non-lucid dreams involve largely the same brain states — just with or without the prefrontal activity that supports meta-awareness — it seems arbitrary to say only lucid dreams are experiences.

Please go through these and tell me: does this set hang together? If not, which commitment is causing the most trouble, and what's the least costly way to fix things?
