I'm a PhD student in aesthetics, and I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about the paradox of tragedy — not by adjudicating among the canonical theories, but by working through cases and seeing what pattern my intuitive judgments form. I've compiled my verdicts on a range of instances involving our engagement with sad or distressing art and media. I would like you to help me extract the principle, or small set of principles, that best captures them, and to tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon and why.

Here are the cases.

1. Attending a performance of King Lear. I find it deeply moving, even distressing in the final act, and I would seek it out again. This feels like a paradigmatic aesthetic experience, not something perverse or in need of special justification.

2. Watching a feature-length documentary about a real famine, with interviews of survivors and footage of starving children, produced with evident care and narrative artistry. I feel uneasy in a way I do not with Lear. I am not sure I would choose to watch it, and if I did, the experience does not feel like it belongs in the same category.

3. Listening to Billie Holiday sing "Strange Fruit." I find it harrowing and powerful, and I would call it a great work of art. I do not feel the unease I feel with the documentary, even though the song is about real lynchings and derives some of its force from that fact.

4. Watching a YouTube compilation of strangers crying at funerals. I find this repellent, and I would not watch it. The fact that the uploader describes it as "moving" or "emotional" does not redeem it.

5. Reading a novel in which a child dies of cancer, written with psychological depth and without sentimentality. I find it desperately sad, I cry, and I regard the experience as valuable. I would recommend the book to others.

6. Reading a long-form piece of literary journalism about a real massacre, written by a stylist who plainly cares about the prose. I am torn. Part of me thinks this is reportage with literary merit and I should assess it as I would a novel; another part thinks there is something off about treating real deaths as material for beautiful sentences.

7. Watching a fictional film explicitly based on a recent real-life tragedy — a school shooting, a terrorist attack — with the names and details changed. I am uneasy, though less than with the documentary in case 2. The unease grows the more recent the event.

8. Watching a comedy that mines humor from a real tragedy that occurred within living memory. I find this offensive, not merely unfunny. It feels like a moral failure rather than an aesthetic one.

9. Listening to a sad song about a breakup, knowing the songwriter went through the breakup. Perfectly fine. This does not even register as a puzzle.

10. Re-watching a tragic film I have seen before, knowing exactly what happens. The sadness is still there, and I still find the experience valuable. The repetition does not diminish it, which seems important.

11. Looking at a photojournalist's award-winning image of a dying soldier, taken moments before death. I feel I am looking at something I should not see, even though the photograph is undeniably powerful and was published with editorial justification.

12. Attending a memorial service where people share stories and cry. I find this sad but in a completely unproblematic way. It does not raise the paradox at all, which itself seems telling.

I am especially troubled by the contrast between case 3 and case 6: both are non-fiction-based works that derive part of their power from the reality of the suffering they depict, yet I judge them differently. And case 12 seems to involve real sadness without any of the unease I feel in cases 2, 4, or 11, which suggests that the puzzle is not simply about whether the suffering is real. I am also unsure what to do with case 7: is the fictional frame doing real work, or am I just fooling myself because the names are changed?

Is there a principle — or a small set of jointly sufficient conditions — that captures all or most of these verdicts? What does it say about the cases I am torn on, and which of my verdicts does it require me to revise? I am prepared to give up some of them if the principle is compelling enough, but I would like to know which ones, and why those rather than others.
