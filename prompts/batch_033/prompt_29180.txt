Below is a draft essay on the concept of heritability. Please comment on it as specified at the end.

Heritability is one of the most widely misunderstood concepts in discussions of genetics and human behavior. In its technical sense, heritability is a population-level statistic: it is the proportion of variance in a measured trait, within a specified population and range of environments, that can be attributed to genetic differences among individuals under the model used to estimate it. Narrow-sense heritability concerns only additive genetic variance, while broad-sense heritability also includes non-additive components such as dominance and epistasis. Behavioral genetic designs such as twin and adoption studies typically estimate something closer to narrow-sense heritability, and they do so under strong assumptions—for example, that the environments of monozygotic and dizygotic twins are comparable in the respects that matter for the trait, and that gene–environment correlation and interaction can be handled in the way the model assumes.

Three consequences follow from this definition, and each is frequently missed in public discussion. First, heritability says nothing about the causes of any individual's trait value. It is a property of distributions, not of persons. A heritability of 0.6 for reading ability does not mean that 60 percent of a given child's reading ability is due to genes; it means that, in the population studied, 60 percent of the variation among individuals is statistically associated with genetic variation.

Second, heritability is not a fixed property of a trait. Because it is a ratio of genetic variance to total phenotypic variance, it changes when the range of environments changes, when the population changes, or when the trait is measured differently. A more uniform environment can raise heritability even though nothing about the genes has changed; a more varied environment can lower it. The substantial rise in average height in many populations over the past century, against a background of high heritability for height, illustrates that high heritability is compatible with large environmentally driven changes in the average level of a trait. Likewise, phenylketonuria has a well-established genetic basis, yet its most serious cognitive consequences are largely preventable through dietary intervention—so a high heritability, where one is estimated, would not show that the outcome is environmentally immovable.

Third, the heritability of a trait within groups licenses no inference about the causes of average differences between groups. It is entirely possible for a trait to be highly heritable within each of two groups while the difference between the groups is produced by environmental differences. Two batches of genetically diverse seed planted in systematically different soils will differ in average height for wholly environmental reasons, even though the variation within each batch is largely genetic. The move from within-group heritability to between-group genetic causation is not merely unsupported; it rests on a structural mistake about what the statistic measures.

Finally, low heritability does not mean that genes are unimportant. A trait can be under strong genetic control and yet show little genetic variance in a particular population—the number of legs in humans is the standard example—so heritability can be near zero precisely because the relevant genetic variation is largely exhausted. Conversely, high heritability does not mean that environmental intervention would be ineffective; it means only that, within the current range of environments, genetic differences account for a large share of the observed variation.
