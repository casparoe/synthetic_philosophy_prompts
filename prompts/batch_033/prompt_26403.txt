I'm a graduate student writing a paper on the contractualist tradition, and I've been wrestling with a problem in T.M. Scanlon's moral theory that I can't seem to resolve on my own. In *What We Owe to Each Other*, Scanlon argues that an act is wrong if it violates a principle that no one could reasonably reject — where reasonable rejection is assessed by comparing the burdens that different principles would impose on different individuals, taken one at a time. The puzzle I keep coming back to is why this individualist framework seems to struggle so much with aggregation: cases where many people each bear a relatively small burden, and we have to decide whether those small burdens can collectively outweigh a very large burden borne by a single person.

The standard example is Scanlon's own case of the transmitter room. An electrician, Jones, is being painfully electrocuted while repairing a transmitter during a World Cup broadcast; we could save him by shutting off the transmitter, but doing so would deprive millions of fans of the enjoyment of watching the match — a very minor inconvenience for each, but spread across an enormous number of people. Scanlon argues that we should save Jones, because no individual fan's complaint (a brief interruption to their viewing) is weighty enough to reasonably reject a principle that prioritizes preventing severe harm. The fans' complaints don't "add up" — each one is individually too trivial. So far so good. But then Scanlon also wants to say that in other kinds of cases — for instance, choosing which of two groups of strangers to rescue from drowning — the numbers do matter, and we should save the larger group. And he tries to give a principled account of when aggregation is and isn't permitted, turning on whether the relevant individual complaints are "comparable" in the right way.

What I can't get straight is whether this distinction really holds up. What exactly makes the fans' complaints in the transmitter room case fail to be "comparable" to Jones's complaint, such that they can't be aggregated, while the complaints of the members of the smaller rescue group *are* comparable to those of the larger group? Is the contractualist forced to appeal to something like a substantive theory of well-being to sort complaints into comparable and non-comparable buckets — and if so, does that undercut the claimed neutrality of the contractualist framework? Or are there more promesing lines of defense I'm missing? I'd appreciate a careful walkthrough of how the aggregation problem arises within Scanlon's view, what the main responses are, and whether any of them genuinely resolve the tension without quietly abandoning the individualist starting point that makes contractualism distinctive in the first place.
