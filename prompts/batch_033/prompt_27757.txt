Question: State the conceivability argument against physicalism as carefully as you can, explain the phenomenal concept strategy as a reply, and assess whether that reply succeeds.

Student answer: Philosophical zombies are creatures physically, functionally, and behaviorally identical to ordinary conscious beings, yet with no phenomenal consciousness — there is nothing it is like to be them. The conceivability argument runs as follows. First, zombies are conceivable. Second, if zombies are conceivable, then zombies are metaphysically possible. Third, if zombies are metaphysically possible, then physicalism is false, because physicalism requires the physical facts to necessitate all other facts. Therefore, physicalism is false.

The phenomenal concept strategy responds that phenomenal concepts are special. They are concepts we deploy from the first-person perspective in having experiences, and they are conceptually isolated from physical and functional concepts. Because of this isolation, we can appear to conceive of a being that satisfies every physical description while lacking what our phenomenal concepts pick out. The strategy therefore shows that zombies are not really conceivable: the appearance of conceivability is an artifact of the gap between phenomenal and physical concepts, not a guide to how the world could be. Once we see that a phenomenal concept and a physical concept can pick out the same property despite being conceptually independent, the argument's second premise is undermined, since conceivability does not entail possibility in this case. Physicalism is left untouched.
