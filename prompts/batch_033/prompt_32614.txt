I have spent my career writing novels. Over the years, I have arrived at a view about mind and meaning that I want to subject to serious scrutiny — not to have it defended, but to have its vulnerabilities mapped honestly. I will state the view, then ask what would count against it.

The view begins with something I accept. Daniel Dennett, drawing on a nineteenth-century critic of Darwin, identified two "strange inversions of reasoning." The first is Darwin's: "In order to make a perfect and beautiful machine, it is not requisite to know how to make it." Blind variation and natural selection produce the appearance of design without a designer. The second is Turing's: "In order to be a perfect and beautiful computing machine, it is not requisite to know what arithmetic is." Rule-governed symbol manipulation produces the appearance of thought without a thinker. I accept both inversions. They are among the most important insights in intellectual history, and I have no interest in disputing them.

But I believe they leave something out. The view I hold is that there is a difference between a process that produces competent outputs and a process that comprehends what it is doing, and that this difference is not itself exhausted by the Darwin–Turing inversion. In particular, I believe that the creation of literary fiction — the kind of novel that earns its place in a tradition, that does something morally and aesthetically serious over time — requires comprehension. A system that generates text by optimizing for some objective, without understanding the world the text refers to or the human situation it speaks into, may produce superficially competent prose. It may even produce passages that move readers. But it cannot produce a novel that matters in the way that certain works of literature matter: works that are answerable to lived experience, that take risks that can only be taken by someone who knows what is at stake, and that sustain, over hundreds of pages, a coherent pressure of moral attention. Competence is not the same thing as art, and the difference is not merely in the eye of the beholder.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for the standard AI-skeptic talking points; I already know those. I want an honest map of the position's exposure. What empirical developments would be decisive counterevidence? If a language model, trained only on text and lacking anything we would recognize as comprehension, were to produce a novel that is widely and durably received as a serious literary achievement — received, that is, by readers who do not know its origin — would that count? If so, what features would the novel need to have to distinguish a genuine counterexample from a case of readers being fooled by superficial fluency? What conceptual arguments would show that the distinction I am drawing is either incoherent or does no work? Are there arguments from the philosophy of art — about the intentional fallacy, about the death of the author, about the role of the reader in constituting meaning — that undercut the very idea that comprehension on the part of the creator is required for serious art? And what would the defenders of my position themselves have to concede as counting against it, even if they ultimately maintain the view?

Finally, I want to know whether the position, as I have stated it, has been qualified in ways that make it unfalsifiable — and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, or whether some views about mind and meaning are simply not in the business of making empirical bets. If the position can be endlessly adjusted to accommodate any new evidence, I want to know that, and I want to know whether that is a defect or a feature.
