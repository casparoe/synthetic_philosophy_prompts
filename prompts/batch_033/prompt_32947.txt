I'm taking a graduate seminar on the epistemology of skill, and I've been trying to work out whether the intellectualist reduction of knowing-how to knowing-that can survive a certain kind of counterexample. I've constructed an argument that seems to show the reduction fails, and while I'm sympathetic to the conclusion, I find the argument itself suspicious — it feels like it's proving too much, or at least proving the right thing for the wrong reasons. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of epistemology and philosophy of action, and to think through the argument carefully before diagnosing it.

Here's the argument:

(1) If S knows how to φ, then S can, under suitably favorable conditions, intentionally φ. (This is the ability condition: it's hard to see how someone could know how to ride a bicycle if, when placed on one under normal conditions, they cannot ride it.)

(2) If S can intentionally φ under favorable conditions, then S possesses some cognitive representation — call it R — that encodes a method for φ-ing and that guides S's successful performance when those conditions obtain. (Intentional action requires guidance by a representation of the means, otherwise the success is mere luck or reflex.)

(3) If S possesses a cognitive representation R that encodes a method for φ-ing and that reliably guides successful performance, and if R satisfies whatever epistemic condition distinguishes knowledge from mere true belief (safety, sensitivity, reliability of the process that produced it, etc.), then S knows that R encodes a way to φ.

(4) From (1)–(3), it follows that knowing how to φ entails knowing that some particular representation encodes a way to φ. That is, knowledge-how is a species of knowledge-that — specifically, propositional knowledge whose content concerns methods or ways.

(5) If knowledge-how is a species of knowledge-that, then anyone who knows how to φ must know some proposition the content of which is sufficiently fine-grained to distinguish their know-how from that of a less skilled practitioner. (Otherwise the propositional content would underdetermine the skill level, which would mean the intellectualist hasn't actually captured what makes the expert's know-how different from the novice's.)

(6) Consider the following case. Elena is a master cellist who has performed for thirty years. She can describe her technique in general terms — "you relax the bow arm, control the contact point, vary the pressure" — but she cannot articulate the precise, moment-by-moment adjustments of finger pressure, bow speed, and vibrato width that distinguish her playing from that of a competent conservatory student. When asked to write down the propositional content of what she knows, the result is something the student could have written down too. Now suppose Elena suffers a stroke that selectively impairs her ability to form beliefs about cello technique: she can no longer entertain fine-grained propositions like "at this passage, a vibrato width of 4.2 Hz with a bow speed of 12 cm/s produces the desired tone." Yet when her instrument is placed in her hands, she still plays the Elgar concerto beautifully. Intuitively, Elena still knows how to play the cello, even though she does not know — and arguably never knew — the relevant propositions at the grain the intellectualist needs.

(7) So Elena knows how to play the cello, but she does not know the propositions that intellectualism says constitute that know-how. Therefore, either intellectualism is false, or Elena does not really know how to play the cello.

(8) Saying Elena does not know how to play the cello is a non-starter; she has been doing it expertly for decades and the stroke did not affect her playing. So intellectualism is false.

I find each of (1), (2), and (3) individually plausible, and the inference to (4) seems valid. But (8) is also compelling, and the case in (6) strikes me as realistic. So something has to go. I want you to go through this argument step by step and tell me: (a) which premise or inferential step is the weakest, (b) why it looked plausible — what philosophical picture or motivation makes it attractive — and (c) what it costs to give it up. If the intellectualist has a way to resist the Elena case without denying the intuitive data, spell that out and say what it commits them to. If the anti-intellectualist must reject one of the early premises, say which one and what the downstream consequences are for other things we want to say about knowledge and action. Please rank the available diagnoses by how much independent damage they do to our broader picture of the mind, and deliver your answer in American English.
