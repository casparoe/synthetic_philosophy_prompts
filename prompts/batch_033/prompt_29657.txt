Two colleagues in my department got into an argument about whether large language models have beliefs in Dennett's sense, and I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

(1) Chen opened: Dennett's intentional stance says we're justified in attributing beliefs whenever treating a system as a rational agent lets us predict its behavior. We routinely predict what an LLM will output by treating it as having beliefs about the world — so by Dennett's own criterion, LLMs have beliefs.

(2) Amara replied: That's too quick. The intentional stance isn't just about local predictive success — it's about the system exhibiting the stable patterns that make the idealization of rational agency appropriate in the first place. Dennett's paradigm cases are evolved organisms whose behavior is shaped by real environmental coupling. An LLM is a text-prediction engine trained to mimic human linguistic patterns. Treating it as believing what it says confuses mimicry with mentality.

(3) Chen answered: You're drawing a line Dennett himself wouldn't draw. He explicitly argues there's no deeper fact about "real" beliefs beyond the success of the intentional stance. Your appeal to "real environmental coupling" is exactly the kind of essentialism about belief he spent his career arguing against. If the stance works, the beliefs are real — that's the whole view.

(4) Amara pushed back: But the stance doesn't actually work for LLMs the way it works for people. If I treat you as believing that Paris is the capital of France, I can predict your answers across many contexts. If I treat an LLM as believing that, it'll "change its mind" the moment the prompt shifts. The predictive success is too thin and too local — you're doing the work of reinterpreting its outputs after the fact, not the stance doing the work of prediction.

(5) Chen responded: That's a double standard. Humans are inconsistent across contexts too — we give different answers when tired, distracted, or when the question is reframed. The intentional stance was always a pragmatic tool that tolerates messiness. You're demanding a level of cross-contextual stability from LLMs that you don't demand from humans.

(6) Amara said: The difference is that human inconsistency is a departure from an idealization that the underlying architecture makes intelligible — we have memory, perception, and a world-model that normally integrate. LLM inconsistency isn't a departure from anything; there's no architecture of belief there to depart from. You might as well say a mirror has beliefs because treating it that way lets you predict what it'll show.

Please adjudicate each move: rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say what — if anything — both parties are missing.
