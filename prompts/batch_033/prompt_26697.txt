Next month I'm leading a philosophy-and-literature discussion on Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, and I want to bring in Taurek's argument that the numbers don't count. I keep going back and forth on wether the story is a perfect test case for him or a completely different kind of case. On one side, Omelas looks like pure aggregation: one child's misery is traded for everyone else's happiness, and the people who stay are basically doing the utilitarian math. On the other side, Taurek's whole point is that you can't add up separate people's gains and losses that way. He says that if you can save one stranger or five strangers, the fact that five is more doesn't by itself give you a reason to save the five, and flipping a coin is a perfectly acceptable way to decide. The child in the cellar isn't being saved in either scenario, and the ones who walk away aren't choosing between one and five, so maybe the story assumes the very aggregation he's denying, or maybe it's doing something else entirely.

I'd like an actual reading, not a summary. Where does the story dramatize Taurek's point, and where does it resist it? Does it take a stance on whether the numbers count, or does it stack the deck by making the one child's suffering concrete and everyone else's happiness vague? Would Taurek have to say the people who stay are reasoning badly, or even doing something wrong, or just that their the-numbers-settle-it reasoning is doing more work than it can bear? I'm also curious what the story shows that his argument doesn't — about complicity, about the difference between choosing and just living inside a choice someone else already made, about why walking away doesn't solve anything either. Please be honest about where the reading breaks down rather than forcing the story and the argument to line up. Also, separate what's actually settled about Taurek's view from what's still contested among people who work on this stuff, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
