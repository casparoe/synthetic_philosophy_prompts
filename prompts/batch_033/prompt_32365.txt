You are a philosopher of biology with a strong background in philosophy of mind. Please think this through carefully, and begin by distinguishing the different things someone might mean by "reason" in the cases I describe.

I keep noticing a pattern in evolutionary explanations that I don't have a stable way to describe, and I want to know whether it has an established name — and, more importantly, whether the underlying way of talking is legitimate. A cuckoo chick shoves the host bird's eggs out of the nest. When a biologist explains this, they may say the reason the chick does it is to remove competitors for the food its foster parents will bring. But the chick does not represent that rationale; it has no idea why it does what it does. Similarly, when an insect's body mimics a leaf, we say the reason it looks that way is that resembling a leaf fools predators — yet no designer had that reason in mind, and neither does the insect. In both cases the "reason" seems to carry real explanatory weight, but there is no reasoner anywhere in the picture.

Is there a name for this — reasons that are genuinely explanatory without being reasons for any agent? I know this is somewhere in the neighborhood of biological functions, "as if" explanation, and the idea that natural selection acts like a designer, but none of those labels captures exactly the combination of realism and mindlessness I'm pointing at. I'm not just after a term, though: I want to understand what exactly the phenomenon is, how it differs from a mere manner of speaking, and where its edges are. For example, does it extend to a rock falling "in order to" reach the ground, or to a trait whose benefit is only visible in retrospect? If there is no exact term, what would you call it, and how would you define it so that it doesn't collapse into metaphor or ordinary teleology?
