Two people in my philosophy of mind seminar got into an argument about original and derived intentionality, and after an hour neither had moved. I took notes and want to know who was right about which moves. Here is a reconstruction of the key exchanges, in order.

**Marian** opened with a version of Searle's Chinese Room. A computer running a program can pass the Turing Test for Chinese, but the man inside (or the processor) is just pushing symbols around according to syntactic rules. The symbols aren't about anything to him. So whatever intentionality the system appears to have is derived from the programmers who interpret the outputs. Brains, by contrast, don't need an external interpreter — their mental states are intrinsically about things. That's original intentionality.

**Rafael** replied that this distinction only goes through if you can tell, in principle, whether a system's intentionality is original or merely derived. He brought up Dennett's two-bitser example: a vending machine equipped to detect US quarters. Take the same machine to Panama, where the quarter-balboa coin is physically identical to a US quarter. The machine's internal state doesn't change, but now it "means" quarter-balboa instead of quarter. The meaning of its state is entirely a matter of how we — or the broader context — treat it. That's derived intentionality. But, Rafael asked, what test distinguishes a brain's "original" intentionality from a sufficiently sophisticated version of the same thing? If there's no test, the distinction is idle.

**Marian** answered that the distinction isn't supposed to be settled by a behavioral test; it's causal. Neurons have specific causal powers — the ability to produce consciousness and intentionality — that silicon transistors don't. A computer simulation of a brain no more has original intentionality than a computer simulation of a thunderstorm makes you wet. The Chinese Room makes this vivid: the man has no access to the meanings, only to the syntax. Adding more rules or more speed doesn't introduce semantics where none was present.

**Rafael** said this was pushing the mystery into "causal powers" without explaining what those powers are. He argued that if the claim is that biological carbon has a special property that silicon can't replicate, that's either a form of vitalism or it's an empirical claim for which we have no evidence. If it's a conceptual claim — that intentionality is just conceptually tied to biology — it begs the question against anyone who thinks intentionality is a functional property. He added that the "system as a whole" reply to the Chinese Room is exactly right: the man doesn't understand Chinese, but the man-plus-rulebook-plus-paper does, just as no single neuron understands English but the brain does.

**Marian** responded that the system-as-a-whole reply concedes her point: the intentionality of the overall system is still derived from the programmers who set up the rules and the man who interprets the inputs and outputs. The brain, by contrast, has no external interpreter. The two-bitser machine is a perfect illustration of derived intentionality — it doesn't mean anything on its own. Its internal state only means "quarter" or "quarter-balboa" relative to an interpreting community. The brain doesn't need a community to assign meaning to its states; it's the source of the meaning.

**Rafael** countered that the two-bitser is meant to make the opposite point. If you can't tell the difference between a "real" quarter-detector state and a "merely derived" one except by pointing to its history or its designers, then the distinction between original and derived intentionality is like the distinction between a genuine US quarter and a perfect counterfeit: it's real only if you care about pedigree, but it doesn't track any difference in what the system does or how it works. Brains, he said, are just vastly more sophisticated two-bitsers — shaped by evolution and learning to track features of the world — and there's no principled line where derived intentionality magically becomes original.

At this point the seminar ended and neither had budged.

Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them missed.
