I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar called "The Normativity of Contracts," and we've spent the past several weeks on the question of why promises bind. We've worked through Hume's convention-based account in the Treatise, Rawls's practice-based view from "Two Concepts of Rules," and Scanlon's expectation-based account from What We Owe to Each Other. Our professor has asked the class to help design an in-class activity for next week's session that will make the rest of the students — about eighteen of us — feel the force of the central puzzle experientially rather than just reading about it. The session is eighty minutes, and the room is a seminar room with movable chairs and a whiteboard.

The puzzle we keep circling around is this: when you break a promise, are you doing something wrong primarily because you've harmed the specific person you made the promise to — disappointed their expectations, betrayed their trust, taken advantage of their reliance — or because you've undermined a valuable social practice that everyone benefits from and you're free-riding on that practice? Or is there some third thing, something about the exercise of normative powers or the transfer of authority, that neither of these quite captures? The readings pull in different directions, and the discussion keeps stalling out because we don't have a shared experience to anchor the disagreement.

I'd like to propose a structured activity — ideally a game with real but small stakes, like candy or extra-credit points — that forces participants to confront this question from the inside. The activity should have two distinct phases. In the first phase, promises are embedded in a clearly beneficial social practice: making and keeping promises visibly improves everyone's outcomes, and breaking them visibly harms the practice for everyone. In the second phase, promises are made one-on-one in isolation from any broader practice — no reputation, no group benefit, no third-party awareness — and participants have to decide whether they still feel bound. The transition between the two phases should be jarring: participants should walk out of the first phase convinced that the practice explains everything, and then find themselves in the second phase feeling that something is still missing. I need: (a) rules and materials for both phases, including a payoff scheme that doesn't require real money; (b) a timeline for the full eighty-minute session; (c) a debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical accounts we've read, shows where the practice-based view gains traction and where it loses it, and introduces the voluntarist or authority-based alternative at the right moment; (d) an honest note on what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run — especially the risk that participants in Phase 2 will simply import the norms from Phase 1 and not feel the tension; and (e) a suggestion for a follow-up discussion question or short reading that ties the experience back to the texts.
