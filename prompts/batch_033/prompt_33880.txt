I spent forty years as an experimental physicist — the kind of person who thinks you don't really know something until you've built an apparatus and measured it. So it's been a bit of an adjustment, in retirement, to find myself reading philosophy of science and getting genuinely tangled up over something I used to take completely for granted: thought experiments.

Here's what I mean. Galileo drops two unequal weights from the Leaning Tower — except he doesn't, he just imagines it, and somehow that imagined scenario tells him something real about how the world works. Einstein chases a light beam on a bicycle in his head and ends up with special relativity. In my own career I used thought experiments all the time to reason about detector setups before we built them. But now that I'm actually thinking about it philosophically, I can't shake the question: how on earth does this work? You sit in an armchair, you picture a scenario that has never occurred and in some cases could never occur (Maxwell's demon, Schrödinger's cat), and you come away with new knowledge about the physical world. That seems almost like magic, and it sits very uneasily with the empiricism I spent my career practicing.

I've read the standard moves — that thought experiments just make implicit knowledge explicit, or that they're really arguments dressed up in narrative form, or that they work by triggering intuitions that evolution or experience have tuned to track reality. But none of these fully satisfy me. The "making explicit" account seems to deny that thought experiments ever teach us anything genuinely new, which just feels false to the history of physics. The "they're just arguments" line seems to miss something about the imaginative, almost sensory quality of running a thought experiment — when I was reasoning through that detector setup in my head, it felt like something different from just chaining propositions together. And the intuition story raises the question of why armchair intuitions should be reliable about regimes far outside our ancestral environment, like relativistic velocities or quantum superpositions. It seems like a hell of a coincidence if the cognitive machinery we evolved for dodging predators and throwing spears happens to be trustworthy when we're thinking about reference frames moving at 0.9c.

So I guess what I'm asking is: can someone give me a account of how thought experiments in science yield knowledge that (a) takes seriously the fact that they seem to produce genuinely novel insights, not just clarifications, (b) doesn't reduce them to deductive arguments with decorative imagery, and (c) explains why we should trust them, especially when they concern domains where our everyday intuitions have no business being reliable? I'm not looking for a bibliography — I want the conceptual architecture of the best answer out there, laid out for someone who's comfortable with technical reasoning but new to the philosophical terrain.
