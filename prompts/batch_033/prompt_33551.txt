John von Neumann died in 1957, just as the digital computer was becoming a real object of study and as the first formal models of neural function were being proposed. He left behind an unfinished lecture manuscript comparing the brain with the computing machines of his day — a short work in which he discussed the brain's logical depth, the reliability of its components, its parallel operation, its remarkably low energy budget, and the likelihood that its internal code is not primarily mathematical. Since his death, several research programs have emerged that he could not have witnessed: deep neural networks trained by backpropagation, the framework of the brain as a hierarchical Bayesian inference engine, and the proposal that whole-brain emulation might one day be feasible. Suppose von Neumann had lived to see these developments. What would he likely have made of them? Which of his stated views would he have regarded as vindicated? Which would he have revised? And where would he have pushed back against the assumptions embedded in the newer frameworks? Ground the prediction in the positions he actually expressed, and be explicit about the points at which the historical evidence runs out and one can only speculate.
