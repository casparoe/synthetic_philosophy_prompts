I co-organize a discussion series at my company — a mid-size AI lab where most of the audience are engineers and research scientists, not philosophers. We do an interview format: I sit down with a guest for about an hour, and then we open it up to questions from the room. Next month we're hosting a researcher who has argued, across several papers and blog posts, that the alignment problem should be understood narrowly as intent alignment. On their view, the problem is getting AI systems to robustly do what their human operators intend, and this should be distinguished from value alignment — the much thicker project of getting AI systems to act in accordance with human values or promote human flourishing. Their central claim is that value alignment is philosophically intractable (we don't agree on what human values are, and we may never converge) and that intent alignment is the only version of the problem we can make genuine engineering progress on. They also argue that intent alignment is sufficient: if we succeed at it, we can use intent-aligned systems to help us with whatever further normative questions we face.

I've been asked to draft the interview questions, and I want to go beyond having them restate the position — our audience already knows the broad strokes from internal discussion. I want to pressure-test the sufficiency claim from several angles. Could you draft eight to ten questions that do the following: (1) press on what intent alignment prescribes when the operator's intentions are malicious, internally inconsistent, or based on false beliefs; (2) explore whether an intent-aligned system would resist being shut down or repurposed when the operator changes their mind, since the system is trying to do what the operator intended at deployment time; (3) ask whether the notion of "what the operator really intends" smuggles in normative content that starts to look like value alignment by another name; and (4) probe what empirical or conceptual evidence would actually count against the narrow view — what would make its defender change their mind? For each question, include a follow-up that anticipates the reply they're most likely to give, so I'm not left flat-footed if they give the obvious answer.
