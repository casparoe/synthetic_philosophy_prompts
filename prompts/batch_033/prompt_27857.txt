Act as a professional philosopher who works on causation by omission and on the role of norms, expectations, and defaults in causal judgment, and who has practice turning subtle philosophical distinctions into decision procedures that non-philosophers can run. Before writing the procedure, think through the main candidate accounts of when an omission is a cause of an outcome, and what each would say about the three cases below; then state which account you are operationalizing and why it is the right one for this setting.

I chair the adverse-event review committee of a 350-bed teaching hospital. We meet twice a month to review cases of serious patient harm and death, and in each case we must decide whether a clinician's failure to act — not ordering a test, not escalating a finding, not checking a pending result, not calling a consultant — caused the harm. The classification matters: if we record the harm as caused by an omission, the event is classified as preventable, triggers a formal investigation, and can follow the individual clinician's record; if we record it as not caused by omission, it is filed as non-preventable and the process ends. The medical director signs off on our classifications and expects the procedure to be consistent and defensible.

Our recurring problem is that the bare counterfactual test — would the harm have occurred if the person had acted? — is far too permissive. In nearly every death, some nurse could have called an hour earlier, some consultant could have returned a page sooner, some lab could have phoned a result instead of posting it. If we use that test literally, dozens of people caused every death, and the distinction between the failure that killed the patient and the background of ways things might have gone better disappears. I know the philosophy here only in rough outline: some views tie causation by omission to what would normally happen, or to what a person in that role was supposed to do, or to a relevant default, and some deny that omissions are causes at all. I want that work turned into something my committee can actually use.

Write me a reusable decision procedure, a sequence of questions to run in order, that the committee can apply to any case in roughly twenty minutes. The procedure should say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a definite conclusion that the omission did not cause the harm, where it stops with a definite conclusion that it did, and which step or steps require a judgment of professional standards or institutional values rather than a finding of fact — and who should make that judgment and how it should be recorded. Throughout, keep the causal question distinct from the question of blame or competence, because the committee tends to run them together and I need the procedure to force them apart. Committee members are clinicians, patient-safety staff, a risk manager, and one lay member; they will not read the philosophical literature, so each question should be phrased in ordinary clinical language, with a sentence or two of rationale connecting it to the philosophical idea it operationalizes.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases:

1. A 42-year-old with no relevant history presents with pleuritic chest pain, normal vital signs, and a normal examination; the physician diagnoses musculoskeletal pain and does not order imaging or a D-dimer. Two days later the patient dies of a pulmonary embolism. Had a CT been ordered at the first visit, the embolism would almost certainly have been found and treated. At the time, however, the presentation fell well below the threshold at which the relevant clinical decision rules recommend imaging.

2. A laboratory posts a critically abnormal potassium value of 6.8 to the electronic record, but the system does not route it to the covering physician, who never sees it. The patient has a fatal arrhythmia later that night. Checking for pending results is possible but is not a specified part of the covering physician's workflow, and the hospital has no rule about who must monitor un-routed results after hours.

3. A nurse observes clear, documented deterioration in a patient over several hours but does not call the rapid response team, because on that unit such calls are treated as over-reactions and discouraged. The patient arrests. Calling the team would have been within the nurse's role and would, on the balance of probabilities, have prevented the arrest.

One of these should produce an answer the committee will not like; do not soften it, and say explicitly which case it is and why the committee will resist it. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of case it will misclassify, the questions philosophy has not settled that the procedure treats as settled, and the point at which the hospital simply has to choose a standard and live with the consequences.

Your answer's final line must be a one-sentence summary of the procedure.
