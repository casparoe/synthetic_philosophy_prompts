I spent my career in the earth sciences, where maps and navigation were part of the craft, and I have recently been reading philosophy of technology — Winner, Ihde, Verbeek. I would like to work through something concrete that has been bothering me.

Consider the design of a modern turn-by-turn GPS navigation system. The interface gives you one instruction at a time: "Turn left in 500 feet." It does not, by default, show you the overall route in a way that invites study. It does not tell you that you are heading northwest; it tells you to turn. If you miss the turn, it does not leave you to reorient yourself; it silently recalculates and issues a new sequence of commands. The map is, in principle, available, but the interface makes it effortful to consult and effortless to ignore. The system is designed so that you can reach your destination without ever forming a mental model of where you are.

What I want to understand is: what philosophical commitments about human agency, knowledge, and competence does this design presuppose? In particular:

Does it presuppose that the driver is, at the relevant moment, a passive executor of instructions — that the competence that matters is following commands rather than building and maintaining a mental representation of the terrain?

Does it presuppose a view of knowledge in which knowing-that (the system knows the route) can substitute for knowing-how (the driver knows how to get there), and if so, does it treat this substitution as costless?

And is the presupposition merely descriptive — "drivers, as a matter of fact, do not build mental maps, so we design around that" — or is it normative — "building a mental map is unnecessary, and a driver who insists on doing so is wasting effort that the system has made obsolete"?

I am also interested in whether the design is philosophically underdetermined. Could you hold a completely different view of human agency — say, that navigation is a skill worth cultivating and that technologies should scaffold it rather than replace it — and still design the same interface for purely pragmatic reasons? Or does the interface inevitably encode and promote one view at the expense of others, whatever the designer's intentions?

Finally, if the design does encode a particular view of the human agent, does the widespread adoption of GPS then train drivers into that view, making the presupposition self-fulfilling? I am trying to work out whether this is a case where an artifact has what Winner would call a politics, or whether the politics lies entirely in the use and the design is neutral.
