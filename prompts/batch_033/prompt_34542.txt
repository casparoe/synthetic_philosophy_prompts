I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when automation undermines the meaningfulness of work, going by my gut on a range of cases. Here are my verdicts:

1. A furniture maker who used to do everything by hand now uses CNC machines for cutting and joinery, but still designs each piece and does the finishing by hand. I think the work remains meaningful in the same way it was.

2. A radiologist whose diagnostic work is taken over by an AI that reads scans more accurately than any human; the hospital keeps them on to convey results to patients and coordinate follow-up care, but they no longer exercise the diagnostic skill they spent a decade acquiring. I think their work has been hollowed out — it has lost something essential.

3. A farmer who used to plow behind a mule now drives a tractor with GPS-guided steering. I do not think the automation has made the work less meaningful. If anything, it has freed them to manage the farm more thoughtfully.

4. An accountant whose reconciliation and bookkeeping are fully automated; they now spend their time on strategic tax planning and advising clients on business structure. I think their work has become more meaningful, not less.

5. A poet who uses a language model to generate first drafts and then spends hours revising — cutting lines, reshaping stanzas, rewriting the ending. I go back and forth on this one. Sometimes I think it is like the furniture maker (automating the mechanical part, keeping the creative part); other times I think it is closer to the radiologist (the machine is doing the thing that was the core of the work).

6. A factory worker whose entire assembly-line job is eliminated by robots; they are retrained as a robot maintenance technician. The new work feels meaningful, but in a different register — more about technical mastery than about making the product.

7. A teacher who used to develop lesson plans from scratch now uses an AI system that generates them; the teacher still delivers the lessons, runs discussions, and adapts in real time to how students are responding. I think the work remains meaningful.

Is there a principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? If so, what does it say about the poet case I keep going back and forth on? If no single principle gets all of them without becoming ad hoc, which verdict would you overturn, and why?
