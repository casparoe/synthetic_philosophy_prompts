I'm writing a paper on moral worth — specifically on the question of what must be true of an agent's motivating reasons for a right action to count as creditworthy, as reflecting well on her in the sense distinctive of moral assessment. The debate I'm engaging with has at least three positions I need to pull apart cleanly. The first is a strict Kantian view: an action has moral worth only if it is done from the motive of duty, where the duty motive must serve as the agent's primary determining ground, not merely co-present with an inclination that happens to point in the same direction. The second is Barbara Herman's neo-Kantian refinement: the duty motive need not be primary; it can function as a "limiting condition" — it operates in the background, ruling out impermissible courses of action, while the agent's primary motive can be something like sympathy or love, as long as the agent would not have acted on that sympathy had doing so been wrong. The third is the "right reasons" view associated with Nomy Arpaly and Julia Markovits: an action has moral worth when the agent acts for the features that in fact make the action right, regardless of whether she conceptualizes those features under the description of duty or rightness — what matters is responsiveness to right-making considerations, not the de dicto commitment to doing what is right.

I need a thought experiment built to isolate where these views come apart. Specifically, I want a pair of cases. In both cases, an agent helps a stranger in evident distress, and in both the agent's primary felt motive is genuine concern for the stranger's welfare — not a cold sense of obligation. In Case 1, the concern operates directly and unmonitored: the agent sees the need and responds, without any deliberation about permissibility at all. In Case 2, the agent's concern is subjectively very similar in its felt quality, but it is motivationally downstream of an implicit, counterfactual‑sensitive recognition that helping is permissible; had the agent believed — even fleetingly — that helping in this situation would wrong some third party, the concern would have been silenced rather than outweighed. The outward behavior and its consequences are identical across the two cases. Construct the two scenarios with enough psychological detail that the theories can genuinely render verdicts rather than having verdicts stipulated for them. Then walk through what each of the three views says about each case and why, identify which structural feature of Case 2 does the work in splitting the verdicts, and tell me what happens if you remove that feature — does the disagreement collapse back into something more familiar, like a dispute about whether the duty motive must be occurrent or can be merely dispositional? Finally, if one of the theories cannot be made to deliver a clean verdict without adding tendentious assumptions, say so honestly and explain what the impediment is.
