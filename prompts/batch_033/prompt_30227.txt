I'm on the test-ordering committee at my clinic and, since my husband came home with a direct-to-consumer health report he half-understood and panicked over, I've been thinking a lot about whether more information is actually good for patients. At Thursday's meeting we have to decide whether to add a new, basically costless blood panel to the standard workup for every adult patient: it estimates ten-year risk of developing Alzheimer's, but our clinic can't offer the new disease-slowing drugs, so a positive result would mainly change monitoring and planning. One committee member keeps citing Good's theorem, saying costless information can never make an expected-utility maximizer worse off because you can always ignore it. The other side says that's exactly wrong in practice: patients and doctors won't ignore a scary number, and we'll get anxiety, unnecessary scans, and worse care. Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting, recommending adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions. It should have a concrete, actionable bottom line and should engage the strongest version of the loosing side's argument rather than just calling it irrational.
