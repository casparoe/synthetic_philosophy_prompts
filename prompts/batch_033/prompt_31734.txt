I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums (LessWrong and its orbit, mostly), and I keep running into the phrase "defer to the objective chance" used as if it settles a discussion by itself. I've started to notice that it gets used to do very different jobs in different arguments, and I want to separate the readings before I push back in a thread.

Here are three places I've seen it recently:

1. In a discussion about whether a one-off event — a specific AI system failing during a particular deployment — "has a probability," someone ended the argument by saying that once you know the objective chance of the event, you simply defer to it: your credence should be whatever that number is.
2. In a different thread about a coin that was flipped and then hidden, someone said my credence that it landed heads should be 1/2, because the objective chance of heads was 1/2, and any other credence would be a refusal to defer to the chance — even though the flip is over and the outcome is already fixed.
3. In a third discussion, about a forecasting model that outputs a number for some geopolitical event, someone claimed that rational agents should adopt the model's number wholesale, because the model's probability just is the objective chance and rational credences defer to objective chances.

I know there's something in formal epistemology called the Principal Principle in this vicinity, but I've never seen its possible readings laid out side by side, and I don't trust my memory of the exact formulation.
