I'm a retired physicist, got into philosophy a few years ago, and I've been chewing on this thing called the unilateralist's curse — the idea that when any one person in a group can act on their own, the group's fate is determined by the most optimistic or reckless member. I've been collecting cases and writing down my gut reactions, and I'm trying to figure out if there's a principle that ties them together.

Here's my tentative stab at a principle: a unilateral action is wrong when (a) the stakes are high, (b) the effects are irreversible or very costly to reverse, and (c) the people who bear the risk didn't consent to the process. But I keep finding cases where this doesn't quite work.

The cases:

1. A virology grad student, working alone, discovers a method to make a known pathogen more transmissible in mammals. He publishes the full protocol without telling anyone. Verdict: wrong. Full stop.

2. Same scenario, but this time he emails the draft to his advisor first. Advisor doesn't reply for three weeks. Student publishes anyway. Verdict: still wrong, but less blameworthy. But I'm bothered that the wrongness seems to depend on the advisor's inbox habits.

3. A climate scientist wants to run a small-scale stratospheric aerosol injection experiment — a few kilograms of sulfates, effects dissipate in weeks. The international community is paralyzed, so she just does it. Verdict: I think this is fine, maybe even good. The data is valuable, the risk is negligable.

4. Same scientist, but now she scales up to a level that measurably shifts rainfall patterns in a neighboring country for a season. Still unilateral. Verdict: not fine. But where exactly is the line between case 3 and case 4? I can't articulate it.

5. A nation-state unilaterally deploys full-scale solar geoengineering, overriding near-universal international opposition. Verdict: terrifying and wrong. But is the difference from case 3 just a matter of scale, or is there something about states vs. individuals that changes the analysis?

6. A security researcher finds a zero-day in a widely-used encryption library. She publishes it immediately, no patch available. Verdict: wrong. But if she had given a 90-day disclosure window and the maintainers ignored it, I'd say it's right. So the process matters — but in case 2, going through a process and getting ignored didn't make it right. Why does process matter here but not there?

7. A neighbor unilaterally chops down a shared tree that was blocking his solar panels. No discussion. The tree can be replanted. Verdict: wrong, but it's a small wrong. The curse applies, but the magnitude is so low that I don't think it's a moral crisis.

8. A doctor in a rural clinic has a patient with a novel infection. The standard protocol says to wait for lab results before starting antibiotics. The doctor is pretty sure waiting will kill the patient, so she starts a broad-spectrum antibiotic immediately. Verdict: clearly right, even though it's unilateral, breaks protocol, and the downside of unnecessary antibiotics (resistance) is borne by others. So sometimes unilateralism is justified.

Here's my question: can you extract a principle from these verdicts that isn't just a laundry list of conditions? Something that explains why case 3 is okay and case 4 isn't, why case 8 is okay and case 6 isn't, and why the process-defect in case 2 doesn't exculpate but the process-defect in case 6 does? And what does the principle say about a case I genuinely can't resolve: an AI researcher at a small lab builds a model that passes a capability threshold most of the field considers dangerous, and releases it open-source because she thinks the democratization benefits outweigh the risks and the big labs are sitting on a consensus that's actually self-serving. Is that more like case 3 (deadlocked process, valuable information) or case 1 (irreversible harm, no consent)?

If I have to drop one of my verdicts to get a clean principle, which one should go?
