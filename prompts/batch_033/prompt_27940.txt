Next week I'm giving a talk at my lab's alignment reading group, and I'm already dreading the Q&A. The claim I'm defending is that we should mostly stop using process-based supervision — rewarding each step of reasoning — as our default for training models on hard tasks, and go back to outcome-based supervision, rewarding only the final answer, for alot of what we do. My argument, roughly: process reward models are themselves just models trained on human judgments about intermediate steps, and the whole point of the hard tasks we care about is that the model is better than the person judging those steps. So process supervision systematically punishes the model for finding weird-but-correct reasoning paths that don't look right to the step-judge, and rewards confident-looking reasoning that matches what the judge expected. That's a recipe for training models to be sycophantic about their reasoning rather than actually right.

The audience is mostly RLHF engineers — several of them built our current process reward model and think of it as the fix for reward hacking — plus two safety researchers who worry that outcome supervision is exactly how you get reward hacking. I know the obvious objections ('final answers are too sparse,' 'process supervision is just regularization,' 'you can have the model judge its own steps,' 'we already debiased the process reward model'), but I want to be ready for this room, not for the internet.
