I cover artificial intelligence and the ideas behind it for a scientifically literate general readership, and I am preparing a long piece on whether the internal workings of large neural networks can be said to have representational content. The case study I keep returning to is the research practice of mechanistic interpretability. The field has now settled into a stable set of working methods. Researchers fit linear probes and sparse autoencoders to the hidden activations of large models, label the resulting directions or dictionary elements with ordinary concepts, test those labels by activating, suppressing, or patching the components, and then report that they have identified the feature or the circuit responsible for a class of behaviour. An interpretation is generally treated as good when it buys predictive and interventional control: when it lets you steer the model's outputs in the expected direction and when it generalises to inputs the interpreter has not seen.

At the same time, the field's methodological commentary is full of deflationary caveats. Researchers routinely say that the labels are convenient shorthand, that a given direction is only one of many possible descriptions of what the model is doing, and that one should not reify the named concept as something the model literally contains. Yet the day-to-day research reports seem to do exactly that.

I want to understand what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes, because I suspect the answer is not obvious. Does mechanistic interpretability, as actually practised, presuppose realism about neural network representations — the view that a model's internal states can genuinely stand for features of its inputs, and that interpretation is a matter of discovering those states — or can the entire practice be reconstructed on an instrumentalist view on which features and circuits are merely useful predictive artefacts imposed by the researcher? Which of these views does the practice strictly require, as opposed to merely fitting comfortably with? Are the field's official caveats consistent with its discovery-talk, or is there a real tension between them? And what would have to change in how interpretability research is done, evaluated, or reported if instrumentalism — or a purely causal account on which the model's internal states have no semantic content at all — were accepted?

I would be grateful if the answer were written in British English.
