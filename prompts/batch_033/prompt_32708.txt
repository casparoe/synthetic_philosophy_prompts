I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, working on a dissertation chapter about the normative structure of epistemic rationality. Lately I've been reading through the debate between the so-called "akratic" and "anti-akratic" positions on higher-order evidence, and I'm starting to wonder whether I'm missing something fundamental — or whether the literature is.

The puzzle, as I understand it, goes like this. You've examined some first-order evidence — say, a set of statistical studies — and you've reached a credence of 0.8 in some proposition P. Then you receive higher-order evidence: you learn that you were severely sleep-deprived while reviewing those studies, and that sleep-deprived subjects in your reference class make reasoning errors of the relevant kind at a substantially elevated rate. The question is whether you can rationally maintain your 0.8 credence in P while simultaneously believing that your total evidence — the studies plus the fact about your sleep deprivation — does not support a credence of 0.8. The anti-akratic camp says no: this combination of attitudes is structurally irrational, a kind of epistemic analogue of weakness of will. The akratic camp says yes: sometimes you can be rationally stuck in such a state, because your first-order reasoning still compels you even after you learn it was unreliable.

But here's what I can't shake. It seems to me that the entire problem dissolves if you simply model the situation as a standard Bayesian network from the start. You have a prior over the joint distribution of P, your evidence E, and your reliability R. You observe E, update on it, and then observe some signal about R — say, that you were sleep-deprived — and update again. The resulting posterior credence in P already reflects everything the higher-order evidence tells you. There's no separate step where you "adjust" your credence to "match" your higher-order assessment; the two aren't independent quantities that need reconciling. The only reason an akratic tension appears is that people are implicitly modeling the epistemic situation in two stages — first-order reasoning, then higher-order correction — and then treating the outputs of those stages as competing claims on your belief. But that two-stage picture is just a bad model of evidence. If you write down a proper prior and conditionalize, the supposed conflict never arises.

So my question is: what exactly is the residual puzzle? Is there a substantive normative question here that survives the Bayesian reformulation, or is the epistemic akrasia literature — for all its sophistication — really just a long detour driven by an artifact of how people chose to frame the problem? I want a committed answer: if I'm wrong, I want to know exactly where the Bayesian dissolution fails and why the two-stage picture is forced on us rather than optional.
