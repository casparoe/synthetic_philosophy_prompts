I'm a retired physical chemist — I spent most of my career doing spectroscopy and then interlaboratory comparisons for a standards institute, so I'm comfortable with the machinery of measurement scales, calibration against a reference standard, reproducibility, drift, and the difference between ordinal and interval/ratio scales. Recently I've gotten into philosphy and I keep tripping over the debate about interpersonal utility comparisons. The usual setup is something like: Ann gains more from getting the apple than Bob loses from not getting it, so a policy that gives the apple to Ann is a net improvement. One side says comparisons like that are meaningful and indispensable if we're going to talk about welfare or justice at all. The other side says utilities are just rankings of options with no unit attached, so comparing Ann's gain to Bob's loss is like comparing two thermometers that were never calibrated against a common standard: you can say each one goes up, but you can't say one went up more than the other.

I'd like you to restate the main positions in that debate in my vocabulary — measurement scales, calibration, reference standards, inter-instrument comparability, reproducibility, maybe drift and systematic error — position by position, as faithfully as you can. For each position, tell me which parts translate cleanly, which parts you had to fudge to get them into the vocabulary, and which parts have no expression in it at all. Then give me the residue report: is what doesn't translate a sign that my measurement vocabulary is missing something real, or a sign that the philosophical claim was doing more work than it was entitled to? I have a suspicion that the whole thing comes down to whether there's any procedure for putting two people on a common utility scale, and I want to know whether that suspicion survives contact with the actual arguments. Also, please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would make you come out the other way, and where your translation is most likely to fail.
