For over fifty years, the debate about the definition of death has been stuck. On one side, defenders of a brain-based criterion argue that death occurs when the brain — specifically the brainstem or the whole brain, depending on the formulation — irreversibly ceases to function, because this marks the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness and the loss of the organism's ability to integrate its own functions. On the other side, defenders of a circulatory-respiratory criterion argue that death occurs when the heart and lungs irreversibly stop, because an organism with a beating heart, circulating blood, and cellular metabolism throughout its tissues simply is not dead — it is dying, perhaps, or being kept going artificially, but it is not a corpse. Both sides agree on the underlying physiology: they agree about what happens in a brain-dead body on a ventilator (hormonal regulation, wound healing, cellular respiration, even gestation in pregnant patients). They agree that the brain-dead patient will never regain consciousness. They agree, for the most part, that it is ethically permissible to remove life support and, with consent, to harvest organs from brain-dead patients. And yet neither side has persuaded the other, and the debate recurs in essentially the same form decade after decade — in philosophy journals, in medical ethics committees, in courtrooms, and in public controversies over individual cases.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a verdict on which side is right. My own hypothesis is that the debate is not really about biology at all: it is a disagreement about which concept of death we have reason to adopt, given that the pre-theoretical concept is indeterminate between an organism-centered and a person-centered reading, and that practical considerations — organ procurement, resource allocation, the timing of grief — pull in different directions. But I am not confident in this diagnosis, and I want it tested against the actual structure of the arguments. Is this a substantive factual disagreement in disguise, a verbal dispute, a clash of incommensurable frameworks, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what — if anything — could move the debate forward? A new piece of physiological evidence? A conceptual distinction that both sides could accept? A change in transplant technology that alters the practical stakes? Or is this simply a debate that cannot be resolved because it is not, at bottom, a disagreement about facts at all?
