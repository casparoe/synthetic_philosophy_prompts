so here's something that's been bugging me. my country lets me vote in national elections even though i've lived abroad for over a decade — i don't pay taxes there, i'm not subject to most of its laws, the policies don't really affect my daily life. meanwhile my neighbor here, who's been a legal resident for fifteen years, pays full taxes, sends her kids to local schools, is subject to all the same laws i'm not, but can't vote because she never naturalized. and it's not just my country — lots of democracies do versions of this: lifetime voting rights for expats, sometimes even for their descendants who've never set foot in the country, while denying the vote to long-term resident noncitizens.

what theory of who belongs to the demos does this pattern presuppose? it seems like it can't be an "all-affected interests" principle because the resident noncitizen is clearly more affected than i am. it can't be an "all-subjected persons" principle for the same reason. so is it just blood-and-soil ethnic nationalism dressed up in democratic language? or is there a coherent democratic theory that actually justifies privileging the absentee citizen over the resident foreigner?

and i want to push on this a bit. if the theory is something like "citizenship is a permanent relational property, not a matter of current stakes," then what does that imply for people who renounce citizenship — are they making a metaphysical mistake about what citizenship is? and if we took the theory seriously, what else would it commit us to — like, should emigrants get to vote on tax rates they don't pay? should immigrants get voting rights after some number of years regardless of naturalization? i'm trying to figure out whether the practice is just an awkward historical compromise or whether there's a philosophically respectable position underneath it, and if there is one, whether anyone actually believes what it implies.
