I accept the position that the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness show that the standard group-level fairness criteria — roughly, calibration, error-rate balance across groups, and demographic parity — cannot in general be satisfied simultaneously, except in special cases such as perfect prediction or equal base rates across groups, and that algorithmic fairness is therefore not a matter of finding a procedure that is fair in every relevant sense but of making and defending trade-offs among criteria that each capture something real but partial. I want to know what would count against this position: which observations, formal results, reformulations of the criteria, or changes in how the decision problem is set up would have to be true for the trade-off conclusion to be wrong or importantly misleading, and what the position predicts that rival readings of the impossibility results do not. Distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward; say which of the escape routes (richer decision rules, causal rather than observational criteria, individual rather than group fairness, rejecting the criteria as misformalised, showing the conflicts are negligible in realistic settings) are live rather than merely logically possible; and say what a defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against it. I would personally be most moved by a demonstration that the conflicting criteria do not track distinct ethical values but are artefacts of one contested formalisation, or that a single criterion can do the work of all of them once the prediction target and decision rule are specified properly. Finally, assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind. Please answer in British English.
