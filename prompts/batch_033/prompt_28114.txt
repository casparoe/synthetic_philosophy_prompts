I am a high school senior, and in two weeks I will present my position in the semifinal round of a regional high school Ethics Bowl. I am defending a version of hedonism: pleasure is the only thing that is good for its own sake, and pain is the only thing that is bad for its own sake, with "pleasure" understood functionally rather than as a single felt quality. My central claim is that the heterogeneity objection — that the pleasure of finishing a hard proof, the pleasure of a warm bath, and the pleasure of a rival's deserved misfortune share no common felt quality — fails, because what unifies pleasures is not how they feel but a shared role: while having the experience, the subject wants it to continue for its own sake and, absent countervailing considerations, endorses it. I will argue that this shared role is enough to do the work hedonism needs.

The judging panel for this round is unusually well matched to the topic. One judge is a philosophy teacher and a value pluralist who believes that knowledge, friendship, and pleasure are each good in their own way and will resist any attempt to reduce them to one thing. Another judge is a psychology teacher who teaches motivation and emotion and is careful about the experimental finding that wanting and liking are separable, so he will distrust any account that defines pleasure by an attitude of the moment. The third judge is a debate coach and a committed consequentialist who favors preference satisfaction over hedonism, and he will press me on whether my view delivers the practical guidance I claim for it.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me exactly five objections, numbered and ordered by how likely they are to be raised and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way one of these three judges would actually phrase it. For each objection, give a short reply I could deliver in the time allowed and a one-sentence note on where that reply is softest. Include the question I most hope nobody asks, and mark it clearly. Format the answer as exactly five numbered entries, with no introduction or conclusion, each entry consisting of three short parts: the objection (one or two sentences), the reply (one or two sentences), and the weak point (one sentence).
