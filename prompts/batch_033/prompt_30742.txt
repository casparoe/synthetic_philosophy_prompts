A friend who has read a lot of Hofstadter summarized his view on the persistence of the self as follows:

"Hofstadter's idea is that your 'I' isn't a thing, it's a pattern — specifically a strange loop of self-reference running on the hardware of your brain. And since it's a pattern, not a physical object, it can run elsewhere too. When people know you intimately, a lower-resolution copy of your self-pattern gets instantiated in their brains. So after you die, pieces of you literally survive — distributed across the minds of everyone who was close to you. That's why Hofstadter says he doesn't fear death: he'll go on existing in his wife's mind, his friends' minds, and so on. It's basically mind uploading without the computers — the same intuition that you're software, not hardware, and that survival just means the pattern persisting somewhere."

I find the conclusion hard to swallow, but I don't want to dismiss the view based on a friend's retelling. Could you go through this summary piece by piece and tell me what Hofstadter would actually endorse, what he'd qualify, and what he'd reject? In particular, does the phrase "literally survive" capture his position, or does the summary subtly slide from a modest claim about partial pattern-instantiation into something stronger that Hofstadter himself wouldn't sign off on?
