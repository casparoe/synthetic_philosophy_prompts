I am working through a passage from the second chapter of the *Zhuangzi*, the “Discussion on Making All Things Equal” (齊物論, *Qi wu lun*), in Burton Watson’s translation. Here is the passage in full, taken from Watson’s rendering as presented on the Golden Elixir website:

> “Once Zhuang Zhou dreamt he was a butterfly, a butterfly flitting and fluttering around, happy with himself and doing as he pleased. He didn’t know he was Zhuang Zhou. Suddenly he woke up and there he was, solid and unmistakable Zhuang Zhou. But he didn’t know if he was Zhuang Zhou who had dreamt he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming he was Zhuang Zhou. Between Zhuang Zhou and a butterfly there must be some distinction! This is called the ‘transformation of things.’”

The last sentence arrests me. What precedes it appears to be a classical sceptical move: the experience of dreaming is indistinguishable, from the inside, from waking experience, so one cannot know with certainty that one is not now dreaming. The text seems to grant this fully—Zhuang Zhou genuinely does not know whether he is a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. Yet immediately after that admission, the passage abruptly asserts that there *must* be a distinction between Zhuang Zhou and the butterfly, and then labels the whole episode the “transformation of things” (*wuhua* 物化).

I would like help understanding what philosophical work that final sentence is doing. Specifically: why, having just undermined any confident distinction between dream and waking, does the text insist that a distinction between the two terms nonetheless exists? And how does calling this a “transformation of things” address—or deliberately refuse to address—the sceptical worry the dream raises? I am not asking for a broad summary of Daoist metaphysics, but for a close analysis of why the passage needs that final turn and how it should shape our reading of the dream episode as a whole. Please write your answer in British English.
