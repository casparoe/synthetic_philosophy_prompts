I've been reading the fairness impossibility results in the algorithmic fairness literature — the ones showing that a risk score can't in general be calibrated across groups while also equalizing false positive and false negative rates, once the outcome's base rate differs between groups. I keep finding myself drawing a stronger conclusion from them than the papers themselves seem to state, and I want to check whether that conclusion is actually licensed. My current reading is that these results don't merely identify a trade-off between two desirable properties; they show that the very idea of a fair classifier is internally inconsistent, in something like the way a no-free-lunch theorem shows there's no universally best learning algorithm. If that's right, then every proposed fairness metric is just one more arbitrary way of deciding whose errors count, and calling one model fairer than another is really only a report of a preference among incompatible error distributions rather than a judgment about any real property of the model. I'm aware I have a habit of reading impossibility results as deeper than they are, so I want a second look before I commit to this. Am I right to draw that conclusion, or am I committing the fallacy of treating an incompatibility among several formalizations as an impossibility for the underlying concept? If I'm overreading, I'd like the exact step where the inference breaks, and a precise statement of what the results do and do not rule out. And if the question itself is badly framed — if I've built a false presupposition into it — please say that plainly and tell me what I should be asking instead, rather than humoring the framing.
