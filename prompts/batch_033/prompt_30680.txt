I'm a software engineer and I've been going down a rabbit hole on cultural relativism after an argument with a friend, and I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe instead of just having vibes. I'm not trained in philosophy at all so I might be missing obvious distinctions or using terms wrong, but I wrote down my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and I want to know if there's a coherent principle behind them or if I'm just inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts:

1. There's an indigenous group that practices endocannibalism — they eat their dead as a funeral rite. The person died naturally, the family consents, and the practice is spiritually central to their way of life. I think this is fine. Not just "we shouldn't intervene," but actually not morally wrong.

2. A culture practices female genital mutilation on girls around age 10, without anesthesia, as a coming-of-age ritual. The parents and elders genuinely believe it's necessary for purity and marriageability. I think this is wrong, and outsiders are justified in trying to stop it.

3. A culture has a tradition of sati — widows are expected to immolate themselves on their husband's funeral pyre. Many widows defend the practice and say they go willingly. I think this is wrong, and the fact that they say they consent doesn't change my verdict. The social pressure is so overwhelming that I don't trust the consent.

4. A culture eats dogs. They raise dogs for meat in conditions comparable to how factory farms raise pigs. I feel viscerally uncomfortable about this but I don't think it's wrong in a way that justifies intervention. I eat pork, so I'd be a hypocrit to condemn it.

5. A culture practices arranged marriage where both parties are adults and have formal veto power, but there's enormous family and community pressure not to use it. I think this is... probably fine? Not ideal, but not something outsiders should intervene in. Though I'm less confident about this one than the others.

6. A culture has a hereditary caste system where certain groups are treated as "untouchable" — excluded from temples, wells, schools, and social life. This has been in place for centuries and many people across castes, including some in the lower castes, accept it as the natural order. I think this is wrong, and its longevity and local acceptance don't make it less wrong.

7. Here's the case I keep going back and forth on. A culture practices ritual scarification — elaborate facial scarring done to teenagers as a beauty practice and marker of adult identity. It's painful, it's permanent, it carries real risks of infection and keloid formation. But the people who undergo it overwhelmingly describe it as a source of pride, beauty, and belonging, and many say they'd feel incomplete or invisible in their community without their scars. Adults who've been through it choose it again for their own children. I can't settle on a verdict here. It feels different from FGM but I'm not sure I can articulate why, and I worry I'm just drawing the line based on what's more familiar or less viscerally horrifying to me personally.

So my question is: is there a principle or a small set of principles that captures all of these verdicts? If there is, what does it say about case 7? And if there isn't one, which of my verdicts should I reconsider?

I've also heard people mention Montaigne's essay on cannibals in these conversations. If you know it, I'd be curious whether his framework would agree with my verdicts or not, and where it would diverge.

Please think through this carefully — I want you to role-play as a professional philosopher who specializes in ethics and cross-cultural judgment. Start by brainstorming a few candidate principles that might be doing the work behind my intuitions, then test each one against all seven cases, and then give me your best account of what fits. Be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate and whether you think the problem is with the verdict or with the principle.
