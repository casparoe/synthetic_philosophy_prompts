I've been in a philosophy of science reading group for about two years, and the one topic we keep returning to without making any visible progress is the underdetermination of theory by evidence. The core claim — that any body of evidence is compatible with indefinitely many logically distinct theories — is something everyone in the room accepts in the abstract. But the moment we try to draw conclusions from it, the group splits into the same factions every time. One faction treats underdetermination as a decisive argument against scientific realism: if the evidence cannot force a unique theory on us, then our preference for the theories we actually believe — with their quarks, their natural selection, their tectonic plates — must be driven by something other than rational compulsion from the data, and we should be agnostic about the unobservable posits. Another faction insists that this is a philosopher's problem, not a scientist's: the rival theories that generate the underdetermination are gerrymandered, grue-like constructions that no one would ever entertain in practice, and the fact that they exist as logical possibilities tells us nothing about the rationality of actual scientific inference. A third faction, smaller but persistent, argues that the whole framing is wrong because it treats confirmation as a two-place relation between theory and evidence, when in fact background knowledge, explanatory coherence, and the track record of research programs do real epistemic work that the underdetermination argument simply ignores.

What I find striking is that these positions have not budged. We have read the classic papers, we have worked through the formal results, we have tried to construct empirically equivalent rivals to familiar theories — and still, at the end of every meeting, everyone walks out holding the same view they walked in with. My suspicion is that the impasse is not really about the logic of underdetermination at all. I think it is about a deeper disagreement over what epistemic standards apply when we move beyond direct observation: one side treats the gap between evidence and theory as a gap that needs to be closed by argument, while the other side treats it as the normal condition of empirical reasoning and thinks the demand for a deductive bridge is itself the mistake. But I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I would like an outside perspective.

Is there a hidden empirical claim about the history of science that would settle it if we had the right data? Is it a verbal dispute about what "rational" means, or about what counts as a genuine rival theory? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would genuinely count as progress — a distinction, a case study, a formal result — or is this the kind of debate where stasis is the rational outcome?
