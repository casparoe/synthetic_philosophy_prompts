So I'm a novelist — the kind who spends years putting characters in situations where they have to choose between pulling a lever and watching the trolley go by, metaphorically speaking. I write literary fiction where the moral weight of the whole thing often turns on whether someone acted or just... didn't. And over two decades of doing this, I've ended up pretty conviced that the doing/allowing distinction is real and morally deep. Not that doing is always worse — I'm not an idiot — but that there's a structural difference between you being the agent who brings harm about and you being the person who fails to stop a harm that's already in motion, and this difference isn't just about downstream effects or legal convenience or how we happen to feel about it. It's in the metaphysics of agency, or something like it.

Now I've read enough philosophy to know this view gets hammered. Rachels' bathtub, Singer's pond, the whole apparatus. But I want to know what I should actually be moved by. Lay out the defeaters for me. Which ones should make me abandon the view entirely, which ones should make me narrow it, and which ones are the kind of thing where a defender of the distinction would say "yeah that's awkward but here's why it doesn't sink the ship"? I want it ranked — fatal, seriously damaging, merely awkward.

And here's a thing I think about a lot: I've watched readers — ordinary readers, not philosophers — track this distinction with incredible reliability across my books. They'll forgive a character who lets something awful happen in a way they absolutely won't forgive a character who causes it, even when the outcomes are identical. They feel it in their gut. Is that data? Or is that exactly the kind of folk intuition we should be suspicious of? I honestly don't know the epistemology here.

Also, I worry that I've qualified my view into unfalsifiability without noticing. Like, whenever someone presents a case where letting-die seems clearly worse than killing, I just say "well I never said doing is always worse, I said it's structurally different." Is that a legit move or have I made the view vacuous? And if it is vacuous, is that a problem for a view of this kind — maybe some moral distinctions just don't admit of the kind of crisp empirical testability that would satisfy someone in the sciences, and that's fine. But if I'm just insulating myself from counterevidence I want to be told.
