I keep having the same arguement with a mate who's got involved in local politics. Every time there's a referendum or a close election he says the people have spoken, and that the job of government is just to carry out the will of the people. When I pushed him on it the other night he laid out his actual reasoning: he thinks a democratic public has a will in the same way an individual person does. Just as I have preferences, deliberate, make up my mind, and then act, the electorate has preferences, deliberates through campaigns and the press, makes up its mind in a vote, and the government then acts on it. On his view, complaining that 'the will of the people' is vague is nitpicking, because we don't demand that an individual's will be fully transparent before we treat them as a responsible agent either.

I want to know how far that analogy really holds, because it's doing all the work in his conclusion. Could you act as a political philosopher who works on collective agency and democratic theory and audit it properly? Start by brainstorming the correspondences — a correspondence table would help: beliefs, desires, changing your mind, weakness of will, self-deception, acting against your own judgement — and what the democratic equivalent of each would be. Then go through the breakdowns. Be specific about which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion and which are just surface differences (I suspect the lack of a single point of view is one, but I can't tell whether that's decisive or just a surface feature). The conclusion I care about is whether 'the people have spoken' can bear the weight he puts on it: that a government is entitled, maybe even obliged, to implement the will of the people without much further interpretation. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? And if it doesn't survive, say whether there's a better analogy in the vicinity and what that one would allow him to claim instead.
