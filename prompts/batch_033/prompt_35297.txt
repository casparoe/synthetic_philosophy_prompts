I'm a TA for an epistemology course and in two weeks I have to run a fifty-minute tutorial on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, taught together. The students are second-years who've done a bit of propositional logic but nothing in modal or probabilistic epistemology. I get the paradoxes myself — what I need is a teaching plan. Not an explanation of the paradoxes, but the order in which to build things up so the pair of them actually lands as a pair.

Here's what I'm worried about. The lottery paradox (you rationally believe of each ticket that it'll lose, but you also believe some ticket will win, and those can't all be true) seems to push you toward scepticism about rational belief, or toward denying that rational belief is closed under conjunction. The preface paradox (an author believes each claim in her book but also believes there's at least one error somewhere in it) seems structurally similar but the intuitive pull goes the other way — it feels fine to hold both, and that makes the lottery case look weird in retrospect. I want the students to feel that tension, not just learn the definitions. So: what has to go on the board first? I'm guessing I need the closure-under-conjunction principle stated explicitly before either paradox bites, but maybe there's something even more basic — like the difference between full belief and credence — that I should front-load. Should I start with the lottery paradox and then spring the preface paradox as a twist, or introduce them side by side? Is there a single running example that can carry both, or am I better off using the classic lottery setup and then pivoting to the book-preface scenario?

On misconceptions: I expect at least one student will say the lottery paradox dissolves because you shouldn't believe of any individual ticket that it'll lose — you only have high credence. Another will say the preface author doesn't really believe there's an error, she's just being rhetorically humble. Where in the sequence should I head each of these off — before the paradox is fully stated, or let them walk into it and then pull the rug? Are there subtler misconceptions I wouldn't anticipate? And what's one question I can ask in the last five minutes that'll tell me whether they've grasped the structural relationship between the two paradoxes, rather than just being able to recount each one separately?

Oh, and please write the whole plan in British English — the course is at a UK university and I want the terminology and spelling to match what they're used to seeing.
