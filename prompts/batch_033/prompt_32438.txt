"Self-deception seems to be a real phenomenon — people convince themselves their spouse isn't cheating, that they're more talented than they are, etc. But there's a deep puzzle about how it could even work. The paradox comes in two forms. The static paradox: if I deceive myself into believing P, then I must simultaneously believe P (since I'm deceived) and believe not-P (since I'm the deceiver and I know the truth I'm hiding). But that's contradictory. The dynamic or strategic paradox: if I intentionally set out to make myself believe P while knowing not-P, then my project is self-undermining — I can't succeed at getting myself to believe something I know is false, because the very act of trying keeps the truth in view.

I think self-deception is possible, and the paradox is only apparent. The best way to resolve it is to reject the idea that a single mind holds contradictory beliefs. Donald Davidson's view is that the mind is partitioned into semi-autonomous subsystems that can hold different beliefs. So one part of my mind knows my spouse is cheating, and another part believes she isn't, and the two parts are walled off from each other. This resolves both the static and dynamic paradoxes at once, since the contradiction is between different mental compartments, not within a single subject.

Alfred Mele offers a different approach. He argues that self-deception doesn't need to be intentional at all. On his deflationary view, a desire for P to be true causes me to form the false belief that P, without any intention to deceive myself. So there's no paradox because there's no deliberate deception going on — it's just a causal process where motivation biases my belief formation. This is basically the same thing as wishful thinking.

Between the two, I think Mele's account is more plausible because it doesn't require us to posit weird mental partitions. But Davidson's view captures something important: in genuine self-deception, the person seems to have some awareness of the truth they're avoiding, and Mele's account makes that hard to explain. So maybe the right account combines elements of both."
