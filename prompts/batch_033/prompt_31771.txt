In arguments for strong longtermism, a recurring analogy is that temporal distance is morally on a par with spatial distance. Caring about people alive today but not about people in the far future, the argument goes, is like caring about your neighbors but not about people in distant countries — both are just arbitrary ways of drawing a circle around a location in spacetime. The analogy is deployed to shift the burden of proof: if you already accept that spatial distance does not diminish moral importance, consistency demands that you treat temporal distance the same way.

Audit this analogy. Start by laying out what corresponds to what: what plays the role of the neighbor, the distant country, and the historical process of moral circle expansion? Then identify where the mapping breaks. Pay particular attention to features that the spatial case has but the temporal case lacks — for example, the symmetry of spatial relations (they are distant from us the same way we are distant from them), the possibility of two-way causal interaction, and the fact that spatially distant people already exist whereas future people do not. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the argument the analogy is meant to carry, or whether it is a surface-level mismatch that the argument can survive. Finally, assess whether the conclusion — that pure temporal discounting is unjustified — holds up once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether the analogy was doing genuine justificatory work or merely illustrating a conclusion reached on other grounds.
