I'm a department head at a mid-size architecture firm, managing about thirty-five people across four project teams. Over the past eighteen months I've found myself repeatedly in the same uncomfortable position: a team member raises a complaint about a colleague's conduct — missing deadlines without notice, speaking over others in client meetings, claiming credit for collaborative design work — and the colleague deflects not by denying the facts but by challenging the complainant's standing: "He's done the same thing," "She's not my supervisor," "That's rich, coming from her." I've handled these inconsistently. Sometimes I've dismissed the complaint because the complainant seemed hypocritical; other times I've insisted that a true criticism doesn't become false just because of who says it. Neither approach has felt satisfactory, and my inconsistency is starting to affect team morale. People are beginning to game it — preemptively digging up dirt on anyone who might criticize them.

I've since learned that philosophers have written extensively on what they call the "standing to blame" — roughly, the conditions under which a person is entitled to issue a moral criticism, considered separately from whether the criticism itself is accurate. I don't have the bandwidth to read the literature, but I need something I can use. Please adopt the role of a moral philosopher specializing in responsibility and blame. Your task is to design a decision procedure I can run, in under fifteen minutes, whenever a standing challenge comes up in my team. The procedure should be a sequence of questions I ask myself about the situation, ordered so that earlier questions screen out the clearest cases. For each question, tell me what a "yes" or "no" answer implies for how I proceed. Flag any steps where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and I simply have to exercise discretion. The procedure should be usable by someone without philosophical training and should be honest about its own limitations — where it will misfire, what it cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on two cases:
(1) A project lead who has a pattern of interrupting colleagues during internal meetings criticizes a junior architect for interrupting a client during a presentation.
(2) A senior designer criticizes a peer for presenting a shared design concept to the partners as though it were solely her own work; the critic herself did something similar three years ago, formally apologized to the team at the time, and has not repeated the behavior since.

Please deliver your response as exactly five numbered items, each two to four sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1 through 3 should present the decision procedure — the ordered questions, their implications, and the judgment calls. Items 4 and 5 should be the worked applications to the two cases. Think carefully through the underlying philosophical distinctions before constructing the procedure; I need this grounded in the actual literature on standing and hypocrisy, not in workplace folk wisdom. Write in American English.
