I keep runnning into the same slide and I don't have a word for it. Someone points out that the three-body problem has no general closed-form solution, or that a double pendulum blows up tiny differences in how you let it go, and then they conclude the system isn't deterministic, or that it can't be predicted even in principle. But when I sit with the actual equations, they're fully deterministic: the next state is fixed by the previous state. Sensitive dependence on initial conditions just means a measurement error too small to notice gets magnified over time — that's a fact about our instruments and our finite precision, not about the system itself. And "no closed-form solution" is a fact about the small list of functions we've given names to, not about whether the trajectory is pinned down. So the move from "no formula" or "small errors blow up" to "there's real chance in the world" looks like a fallacy to me. Is there a name for that specific slide, distinct from "chaos theory" or the "butterfly effect"? I'm not asking for those. If there's no established name, coin one and draw its edges: exactly which cases it covers, which nearby cases it doesn't, and how it differs from plain old mistaking our ignorance for randomness. Also tell me if I'm overcorrecting — is there any classical, deterministic system where an ideal predictor who knows the exact state and can compute without rounding still couldn't predict the trajectory, and if so, would that be a case of the system itself being unpredictable rather than just us? Keep your answer under 300 words.
