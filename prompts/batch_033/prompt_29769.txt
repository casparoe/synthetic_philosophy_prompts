This came up in my decision theory study group last week and I've been turning it over since, because the conclusion seems absurd but every step looks fine. The setup is the standard Ellsberg urn: 90 balls, 30 red, and 60 black-or-yellow in an unknown proportion. Bet A pays $100 if red comes out; bet B pays $100 if black comes out. Everyone we asked prefers A to B, because A has a known 1/3 chance and B has somewhere between 0 and 2/3. Now bet C pays $100 if red or yellow; bet D pays $100 if black or yellow. Again everyone prefers D to C, because D has a known 2/3 chance and C has somewhere between 1/3 and 1. So far so good.

Then the argument. (1) A and B agree on yellow: both pay nothing if yellow comes out. (2) C and D also agree on yellow: both pay $100 if yellow comes out. (3) So within each pair the only difference is the red-vs-black comparison, and the yellow payoff is just a common consequence. (4) By the Sure-Thing Principle, a common consequence can't flip a preference, so preferring A over B requires preferring C over D. (5) But we prefer D over C, so the Sure-Thing Principle fails. (6) If the Sure-Thing Principle fails, then changing what two options have in common — a payoff that is exactly the same either way — can reverse which one you prefer, and that looks like a pure framing effect. (7) So the whole risk/ambiguity distinction has no normative force: ambiguity aversion isn't a coherent attitude toward uncertainty, it's just a bias, like the gambler's fallacy.

He says the only way out is to deny the Sure-Thing Principle, and then your stuck with preferences that a common consequence can flip; he adds that the same move would force us to call the Allais common-consequence pattern a bias too, so the cost is higher than it looks. I'd rather keep the Sure-Thing Principle than accept (7), but I can't find the slide. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, say so and tell me what accepting the conclusion commits me to.
