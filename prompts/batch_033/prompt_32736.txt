I teach epistemology, and at last week's department reception I overheard two colleagues arguing about the dogmatism paradox. I didn't catch how it started, but here is the exchange as I recall it:

Elena: "Take any proposition you know — say, that you have hands. If you know that, then you also know that any evidence against it is misleading. So you can rationally dismiss counterevidence without examining it. That's the paradox: knowledge seems to license ignoring counterevidence."

Marcus: "But that can't be right. It would mean a scientist who knows some theory could rationally ignore every anomalous experimental result. That's plainly not how inquiry works."

Elena: "I'm not endorsing the conclusion. The paradox is that it follows from principles we otherwise accept. If you know p, then p is true. If p is true, evidence against p is evidence for a falsehood — it's misleading. And if you know in advance that some evidence is misleading, why should you have to examine it? So either we reject closure of knowledge under known entailment, or we reject the idea that you can dismiss evidence you know to be misleading, or we accept that knowledge really does license a kind of dogmatism. None of the options is comfortable."

Marcus: "The problem is that you're sliding between knowing p and knowing that you know p. If I merely know p, I may not be in a position to recognize that the counterevidence is misleading. The paradox only gets going if I know that I know p — and that's a much stronger claim, one the externalist can happily deny."

Elena: "I don't think the paradox needs the KK principle. All it needs is that if I know p, I can competently deduce from p itself that any counterevidence is misleading. The deduction runs: p; therefore not-p is false; therefore evidence for not-p is misleading. That's first-order reasoning from p, not from the premise 'I know that p.' The externalist can grant that the knower is capable of that deduction without granting that the knower knows that she knows."
