Okay, so I just spent like two hours arguing with my friend about reasons and motivation and I'm trying to sort out what I actually think. The argument was about whether someone can have a reason to do something even if there's no path from their current motivations to doing it — you know, the internal vs. external reasons thing. My friend is a hardcore Williams-style internalist and I kept pushing back, but now I'm not sure my own verdicts on cases hang together at all.

Here's where I land on a bunch of cases, gut-level:

1. Depressed guy who can't muster any desire to see his friends or get out of bed. I think he has a reason to get up and call someone, even if he can't feel it right now.

2. Woman raised in a cult who genuinely, wholeheartedly believes her highest calling is to be a submissive wife and she has no desire whatsoever for autonomy. I think she has a reason to leave, even if she'd look at me blankly if I said so.

3. Cruel person who takes real satisfaction in humiliating his coworkers. I think he has a reason to stop, full stop, even if he's never had a twinge of sympathy in his life.

4. But now: guy who's been told by his doctor that he needs to quit drinking, and he knows it, and he wants to be healthy, but every evening the craving wins and he pours himself a glass. I think he has a reason not to drink, and it's the same kind of reason as in the other cases, but it also feels different — like it's connected to something already in him, his wanting to be healthy, in a way that the cruel person's reason isn't connected to anything in him.

5. Here's a weird one: a guy who genuinely believes he's Napoleon. He wants to issue military orders. Does he have a reason to issue them? I want to say no, even though he has a motivation that would, if true, ground a reason. Something about the false belief poisons it.

6. And then: a teenager who has never heard a piece of classical music, never been exposed to it, has no desire to listen to it, but she's a brilliant pianist playing only pop songs and her teacher says she'd love Chopin if she just tried it. Does she have a reason to listen to Chopin? I honestly can't decide. Part of me says yes — there's this whole world of experience she'd genuinely value if she got there. But another part says: if there's literally no route from her current cares to putting on the Chopin, then calling it a "reason" is just nagging.

Am I just an externalist about some things and an internalist about others, and if so, is that even a coherent position? Or is there a single principle that sorts all of these? If you can find one, I'd also want to know what it says about the teenager case — because that's the one that keeps me up.

Don't write me a book — keep it to a page or so, maybe 500 words max. I want the principle, which cases it handles smoothly, which of my verdicts (if any) it has to reject, and what it predicts for the pianist.
