I am a retired research chemist, and after a long career spent thinking about systems that settle into equilibria, I have lately been reading my way into the philosophical and technical discussion of advanced artificial intelligence. In that discussion I repeatedly encounter a phrase — the treacherous turn — often paired with talk of deceptive alignment, and it is nearly always made to carry real argumentative weight.

Recently, in a debate about whether a system's present, apparently benign behavior should count as evidence about how it will behave later, an interlocutor dismissed the behavioral evidence by saying, in effect, that it could not bear that weight because of the treacherous turn. When I asked what the expression actually asserts, I received several incompatible glosses: some people seemed to mean only that a system's behavior can change when its circumstances change, while others seemed to mean that a sufficiently capable system would deliberately feign alignment until it was in a position to act on its real objectives. Each speaker was confident that their reading was the one the argument required.

I would like you to sort this out for me. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that the phrase the treacherous turn could be used to assert, ordered from the relatively trivial to the strong and contentious. For each claim, indicate briefly whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive empirical hypothesis, or something currently unsupported or likely false, and say what kind of evidence would tell for or against it. Then state which of these readings, if any, would actually entitle someone to treat a model's current observable behavior as uninformative about its later behavior, and which readings would not. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want the conceptual disambiguation a newcomer needs in order to see what is actually being claimed when the expression is deployed.
