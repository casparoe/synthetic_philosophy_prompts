I convene a fortnightly reading group for final-year undergraduates in philosophy and economics, and in a fortnight I have to lead a session on money pumps and diachronic rationality. The students all know expected utility theory at the level of an intermediate microeconomics course, but none of them have thought about how preferences are supposed to behave over time. They are bright but impatient with anything that looks like a conjuring trick, and I want the session to make them feel the force of the argument that certain patterns of choice over time are irrational even when each individual choice seems unobjectionable, rather than simply producing the formal cycle. I am not asking you to give the explanation itself; I am asking for a plan for the hour.

I need to know the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what: where the notion of a standing preference over outcomes has to be fixed before the trades can even be described, and where the argument needs a conception of being turned into a money pump that is distinct from merely losing money through bad luck. I also need the one worked case that should carry the session—what it must contain and what features it must avoid so that the cycle does not look rigged—and the misconceptions I should expect, such as the thought that the victim could simply refuse the second trade once she sees what is happening, or that the argument only shows the trades are unwise rather than that the underlying preferences are irrational. Tell me at which point in the sequence each of these should be headed off, what I should explicitly set aside as beyond the session (for example, whether sophisticated choice or resolute choice escapes the problem), and one question to ask at the end that would show me whether the students have understood the diachronic issue rather than merely memorised the cycle.
