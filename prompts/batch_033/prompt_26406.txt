so i'm working on a novel where the narrator is deeply compromised — he's been complicit in exactly the kind of thing he spends the book condemning, and i can't decide whether that ruins his moral authority or is somehow the whole point. i keep thinking about nick carraway in gatsby. he opens by telling us he doesn't judge people, then proceeds to judge absolutely everyone, and by the end gatsby's dead, the buchanans have retreated into their money, and nick is the one who got to write the story. 

what i want is a reading of gatsby through the lens of the philosophical literature on standing to blame and hypocrisy. the basic idea i've gathered is that you can be right about someone's wrongdoing and still lack the standing to call them out — if you're a hypocrite, or complicit, or it's none of your business. so: map that onto the novel. when does nick have standing to blame tom, daisy, jordan, even gatsby himself, and when does he lose it? is his affair with jordan, his arranging the reunion while knowing what daisy had done, his silence after myrtle's death — do any of those cost him standing, or are they the kind of involvement that actually gives him more rather than less? 

and here's what i'm really after as a novelist: does telling the story itself do something to standing? like, by the time nick has written the book, has he earned a kind of standing he didn't have while living through it? or is the whole thing a trick of narrative voice — we feel like he's earned the right to judge just because he's the one holding the pen? i want to know whether the philosophy has anything to say about that, or whether this is one of those places where fiction knows something the theories don't track.
