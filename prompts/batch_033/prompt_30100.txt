I'll reconstruct the exchange as best I remember it. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about which specific points, where each of them overstates, and what both are missing? I genuinely can't tell who's closer overall.

Lena: "Imprecise probabilities are a nonstarter. Being rational means having a single probability for every proposition, updated by Bayes' rule. If your credences are a set, then after you learn something you've got a set of posteriors — that's not a belief state at all, because it doesn't tell you how likely anything is."

Sam: "That just assumes the thing at issue. If I genuinely don't know a coin's bias, writing down exactly .5 for heads conflates 'the chance is known to be half' with 'I have no idea.' A set of probabilities keeps track of that difference. Ellsberg's subjects can tell the two apart, and it's not obvious they're wrong."

Lena: "But the Ellsberg preferences can't be represented by any single probability assignment. In the two-urn setup, preferring the known urn for red implies you assign less than .5 to red in the unknown urn, and preferring the known urn for black implies you assign less than .5 to black — but those have to sum to 1. So the subjects aren't revealing a new kind of rationality; they're violating the axioms of expected utility. Calling that 'risk vs ambiguity' is a redescription, not a defense."

Sam: "The axioms are exactly what's in dispute. Independence says a bet's value should be a weighted average of its state-by-state payoffs, with one set of weights — but the unknown urn is precisely the case where you don't have those weights. The known and unknown bets differ in exactly the way the axiom tells you to ignore. And the prior point still stands: in complete ignorance, any single number is arbitrary. What makes .5 a better representation of ignorance about a coin than .7? Symmetry is a substantive assumption, not ignorance. A set lets you represent what you know without adding made-up detail."

Lena: "But the arbitrariness washes out — any two reasonable precise priors converge after enough evidence, so the choice of prior stops mattering. And a set just relocates the problem: which set do you use? The most permissive set barely learns, and any narrower set smuggles in the same arbitrariness you accused me of. You haven't escaped arbitrary choices; you've hidden them in the choice of set."

Sam: "The permissive set does learn, in the sense that data eliminate members of it — see a tail and the two-headed prior is gone. What you don't get is a single number, and that's the point: the data haven't earned you one. And sometimes new information widens the set, as in dilation. That's not incoherent; it's the model telling you the information exposes how fragile your estimate was. A precise Bayesian can't even represent that."

Lena: "Dilation is the problem, not a feature. If more information can make you less certain, then information can have negative expected value — you could rationally pay not to learn something. And without a unique expected utility, how do you choose at all? You need a decision rule for sets, and any rule either stays incomplete or effectively selects a precise prior. So imprecision either collapses into ordinary Bayesianism or gives you no guidance."

Sam: "Incomplete guidance isn't no guidance. Sometimes the rational response to ambiguity is that several acts are permissible and none is uniquely best. And your 'pay not to know' point treats the Bayesian theorem that information is always weakly beneficial as if it were a general norm. Under ambiguity, avoiding information can be the right response to how little you know. Its a consequence of the view, not a refutation."
