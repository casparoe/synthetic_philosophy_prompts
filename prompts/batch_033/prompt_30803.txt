I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and on the side I teach a small evening epistemology seminar for adults who just like this stuff. Last week I set this question on Williamson:

"State Williamson's definition of luminosity, reconstruct his anti-luminosity argument (you can use the feeling-cold example), and then say whether that argument, on its own, refutes the KK principle — the principle that if a subject knows that p, she knows that she knows p. Be precise about the step from anti-luminosity to anti-KK."

One student wrote this answer:

Williamson defines a condition C as luminous iff whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. His anti-luminosity argument runs as follows. Take a subject at dawn, when she clearly feels cold, and follow her through a slow warming until noon, when she clearly does not feel cold. Index the moments t0 through tn. Because the change is gradual, adjacent moments are indiscriminable: if she knows at ti that she feels cold, then she must still feel cold at ti+1, since otherwise her belief at ti would be true only by luck, resting on a sensation she cannot tell apart from one at ti+1 where the same belief would be false. Now suppose feeling cold is luminous. At t0 she feels cold, so by luminosity she knows that she feels cold. By the indiscriminability step she feels cold at t1. By luminosity she knows it at t1, so she feels cold at t2, and so on. Iterating, she still feels cold at tn, contradicting the setup. So feeling cold is not luminous. Since the argument turns only on gradual change and the reliability condition on knowledge, it generalizes: no condition that can be gained or lost gradually is luminous. Hence the KK principle fails. Knowing that p is itself such a condition — it can come and go gradually as one's evidence shifts — so when you know p you are not always in a position to know that you know p, which is exactly the failure of KK.
