**Student Answer:**

The challenge from inverse akrasia targets Kant's thesis that an action has moral worth only if it is performed from the motive of duty. The paradigmatic case is Huck Finn, who helps the slave Jim escape despite believing—sincerely and on the basis of the moral views of his time—that he is doing wrong by stealing another person's property. Huck acts from friendship and sympathy, not from duty; indeed, his motives directly conflict with what he takes duty to require. Yet many of us have the strong intuition that Huck's action has moral worth—that he deserves moral credit for what he does. If this intuition is correct, then Kant's thesis is false.

Nomy Arpaly develops this challenge in her work on acting for the right reasons. On her view, Huck's action has moral worth because his desire to help Jim is responsive to the right-making features of the situation—Jim's humanity, his desire for freedom, the injustice of slavery—even though Huck's explicit moral beliefs tell him otherwise. What matters for moral worth, Arpaly argues, is not that the agent represents their action under the description "this is my duty," but that their motivating reasons align with the reasons that in fact make the action right. Huck's sympathy does align with those reasons, so his action has moral worth.

A Kantian can reply that this challenge rests on an uncharitably narrow reading of what it means to act from duty. Barbara Herman has argued that an agent can act from duty without consciously deliberating about the moral law at the moment of action. On her interpretation, what matters is that the agent's maxim is consistent with the categorical imperative and that the agent's underlying commitment to morality plays what she calls a "limiting condition" role—the agent would not have acted on that maxim had it conflicted with duty. Applied to Huck: his maxim of helping a friend in need is universalizable, and his evident moral anguish suggests that his commitment to doing what's right is genuinely operative in the background. So Huck might actually count as acting from duty on a suitably generous Kantian reading.

However, this Kantian reply fails for two reasons. First, if any action whose maxim happens to be universalizable counts as done from duty simply because the agent has some background commitment to morality, then the notion of acting from duty has been stretched past the breaking point. Kant's view was supposed to distinguish the person who acts from duty from the person who merely acts in conformity with duty, and Herman's interpretation collapses that distinction. Second, Arpaly's positive account explains Huck's case more straightforwardly: Huck's action has moral worth because his sympathy actually responds to what matters—Jim's freedom and dignity—not because of some counterfactual about what he would have done under different circumstances. The Kantian can accommodate inverse akrasia only by weakening the concept of duty until it no longer does the work Kant needed it to do, while Arpaly's account captures the phenomenon without such contortions.

I conclude that inverse akrasia cases do undermine Kant's account of moral worth, on any interpretation that preserves Kant's core claim that the motive of duty is uniquely suited to confer moral worth. The Kantian faces a dilemma: either maintain a robust notion of acting from duty and deny that Huck's action has moral worth (which is counterintuitive), or broaden the notion of duty to accommodate Huck and lose the very thing that made Kant's view distinctive.
