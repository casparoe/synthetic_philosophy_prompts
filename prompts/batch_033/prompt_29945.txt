Two engineers on my team got into a lunch argument that I couldn't referee on the spot, and I'd like a careful, move-by-move adjudication. The context: our company is clearing a patch of forest for a data-center campus, and the conversation turned to whether the clearing is, on animal-welfare grounds, a bad thing.

(1) Maya said the reflex that preserving forest is obviously good for wild animals deserves more scrutiny. Most wild animals live short, painful lives — the great majority die young from starvation, predation, disease, or exposure — so a smaller local population could mean less total suffering, and clearing the land might actually be good for the animals in the aggregate. (2) She added that even if the clearing itself kills or displaces animals painfully, that cost is paid once, whereas the reduction in the number of animals being born into short, painful lives compounds every year the land stays developed. (3) Dan replied that she was treating a deeply uncertain empirical claim — that wild lives are, on balance, not worth living — as if it were settled, and that the transition cost is not a one-time fee but a slow, ugly process in which displaced animals starve or are killed by competitors. He also said ecosystems recover, so unless the land is permanently developed, populations rebound, and you get the transition suffering without the long-run reduction. (4) Maya answered that the rebound point doesn't rescue the conservationist reflex; it just relocates the question. If populations rebound, the clearing causes a large pulse of death and displacement and then leaves the population roughly where it was, which is worse for the animals, not better; if they don't rebound, her original point about the ongoing reduction applies. So whether the clearing helps or harms depends on facts about rebound and wild animal welfare that the conservationist reflex simply ignores. (5) Dan said she had now retreated from "clearing is probably good" to "it depends," and that even the "it depends" version is loaded: in the no-rebound branch, her conclusion follows only if wild lives are net negative, which is exactly the claim in dispute. (6) Maya replied that this is where expected value does the work. If there is a non-negligible chance that wild lives are net negative, then reducing wild populations has a potentially large positive payoff, while preserving habitat for the animals' own sake pays off only under the optimistic assumption that their lives are good. (7) Dan ended by saying that by this logic one could justify all sorts of destructive interventions on the strength of a mere possibility, and that she was treating animals as interchangeable units of suffering rather than as individuals whose lives have value.

Please go through the moves one by one: say which claims are right, which are wrong or overstated, what both of them are missing, and who is more reasonable overall.
