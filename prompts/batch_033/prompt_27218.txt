Lately I have been taking seriously a broadly Quinean picture of moral values: the idea that moral judgments are not answerable to observation in the way scientific claims are, that our values are largely the product of natural selection and social conditioning, and that although moral language functions perfectly well within a community, it is not in the business of tracking an independent moral reality. On this view, when we disagree about whether some practice is wrong, we are not adjudicating between competing hypotheses about a moral fact; we are bringing different inherited or acquired evaluative standards to bear, and there is no external court of appeal beyond the standards themselves, or at least none with the kind of observational authority that science enjoys.

I want to know what would count against this position. Suppose I am not asking whether it is currently popular or whether it offends common sense, but what would actually be evidence against it. What findings in moral psychology, cross-cultural anthropology, or comparative cognition would put pressure on the claim that moral judgment does not track something? What philosophical developments—say, about the epistemology of observation, the nature of evaluative concepts, or the structure of practical reasoning—should make me revise the view rather than dismiss the objection as missing the point? I want the answer organized as a map of exposure: which challenges would be fatal if they succeeded, which are serious but likely answerable, and which are merely awkward for the view’s defenders. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the position can be modified to absorb almost any apparent counterexample by saying that the moral judgment in question was just another evolved or conditioned response. If it can, is that a sign that the view is unfalsifiable in a problematic way, or is that exactly the kind of flexibility one should expect from a grand thesis about the status of ethics? The kind of thing that would most move me is a careful description of a case or set of cases where moral inquiry apparently corrects itself by something other than appeal to already-held standards—not just a new coherent arrangement, but a genuine external check of the sort Quine denies ethics can have.
