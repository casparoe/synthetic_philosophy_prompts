I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been trying to make sense of a debate that seems to me to have stalled in a revealing way. The debate is about whether large language models understand the language they process, but I think the impasse is best understood through the lens of what Daniel Dennett calls the strange inversion of reasoning — the Darwinian and Turing-inspired idea that competence can arise without comprehension.

Here is the shape of the impasse as I see it. One side argues that when a system can reliably answer novel questions about a text, draw inferences, explain its reasoning, and apply concepts across contexts, the most parsimonious explanation is that it understands the content — that the burden of proof is on anyone who claims this is mere mimicry. The other side argues that this gets the lesson of Darwin and Turing exactly backwards: the whole point of the strange inversion is that spectacular competence can be produced by mindless, comprehension-free processes — natural selection in one case, symbol manipulation in the other — and that we should expect the same to be true of LLMs trained on next-token prediction, absent a positive reason to think comprehension has entered the picture.

What strikes me as odd is that both sides agree on the behavioral facts and on the training story, and both sides agree that Darwinian evolution and Turing machines are existence proofs of competence without comprehension — and yet they draw opposite conclusions about the case at hand. The debate has not moved in years, and the same arguments and counterexamples circulate without anyone changing their mind.

I have a tentative diagnosis, but I want it examined and refined. My diagnosis is that the debate is stuck because it is really a disagreement about the evidential standards for attributing comprehension: the first side treats behavioral competence as presumptive evidence of comprehension, with the burden on the skeptic to show why it isn't; the second side treats the training story — next-token prediction on a corpus — as presumptive evidence of mere competence, with the burden on the attributer to show what added ingredient turns statistical pattern matching into understanding. Since each side places the burden differently, the same evidence cuts in opposite directions.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of this debate that goes deeper than my burden-of-proof hypothesis. Specifically:

1. What kind of disagreement is this? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "understanding," a clash of methodological principles about where the burden of proof lies, a substantive disagreement about the functional architecture of comprehension that could in principle be settled empirically, or something else entirely?

2. If my burden-of-proof diagnosis is on the right track, what sustains it? Why hasn't either side been forced to shift its standards — what would it take for one side's standard to look unreasonable?

3. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Is there a kind of evidence, an experimental paradigm, a conceptual distinction, or a reframing of the question that could break the impasse? Or is this a case where we should expect the disagreement to persist because it reflects a genuine philosophical choice point that evidence underdetermines?

I am not asking for a literature review or a summary of who said what. I want the structure of the disagreement laid bare, and an honest answer about whether and how it could be resolved.
