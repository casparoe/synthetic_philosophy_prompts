I've been reading a popular introduction to metaethics (one of those illustrated guides they sell in museum gift shops) and the chapter on expressivism has a box summarising the Frege–Geach problem. I took a photo of it because I wanted to discuss it with my flatmate, so here's roughly what it says:

"Expressivists claim that moral statements like 'murder is wrong' don't describe facts or express beliefs; instead they express the speaker's disapproval of murder — roughly, saying 'murder is wrong' is more like saying 'boo to murder' than 'the cat is on the mat.' The Frege–Geach problem, named after Gottlob Frege and the contemporary philosopher Peter Geach, points out that this can't be right because we use moral language in all sorts of contexts where no disapproval is being expressed. For instance, in the sentence 'if murder is wrong, then getting someone else to murder is wrong,' the speaker isn't disapproving of murder — they're just considering a hypothetical. Since the meaning of 'murder is wrong' is clearly the same whether it appears on its own or inside a conditional, expressivism must be false: the meaning can't be an expression of disapproval, because in the conditional no disapproval is expressed. Most philosophers now regard the Frege–Geach problem as devastating for expressivism, and the few who don't have tried to rescue it by saying we have attitudes about attitudes, which most people find hopelessly ad hoc."
