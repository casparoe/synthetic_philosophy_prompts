I've been turning over Ursula Le Guin's story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas again, the one where the city's whole good life depends on keeping one kid miserable in a basement and everybody knows about it. I keep wanting to line it up against Peter Singer's drowning child example, the one where you walk past a shallow pond, saving the kid would just mean ruining your shoes, and Singer says that shows you have to give to famine relief even when the child is far away. But the two cases feel like they're doing different work, and I can't quite put my finger on what the difference is.

Could you read the story through Singer's lense for me? In the pond case the child is a stranger you happened across and the cost of helping is small and one-off; in Omelas the child's suffering is the thing the whole city is built on, everyone's happiness depends on it staying that way, and the cost of helping looks like it might be everything. Does Singer's argument still go through, or does the story show a limit in the analogy? And what about the people who walk away at the end, is that what Singer would tell them to do, or is it more like a way of feeling clean while not actually doing anything for the child? I'd like a reading that takes both the story and the argument seriously, and is willing to say both where the story complicates the philosophy and where the philosophy makes the story look a little too easy on itself.
