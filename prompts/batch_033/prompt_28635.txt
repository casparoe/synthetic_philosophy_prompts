So I've been in this ethics discussion group — mostly grad students, a couple professors — and for like the past year and a half we've been stuck on the torture-vs-dust-specks problem. You know the one: is it worse for one person to be tortured for an hour, or for a billion people to each get a single dust speck in their eye? The group splits almost exactly down the middle every time, and I've started to notice that we aren't even really arguing anymore, just repeating positions. The aggregators say "a billion is a lot, the math is clear." The anti-aggregators say "no number of dust specks adds up to torture, there's a lexical threshold." And then someone always says "our intuitions break at these scales so the question is meaningless." Then we all shrug and move on to the next paper.

What I want to understand isn't who's right — I've given up on resolving that — but what kind of disagreement we're actually having. It doesn't seem empirical: nobody disagrees about what dust specks feel like or what torture feels like. It doesn't seem like a disagreement about the non-moral facts at all. So is it a clash of foundational intuitions that can't be justified further? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "worse" — like, are we using different concepts of betterness without realizing it? Or is there some distinction (separateness of persons? continuity vs. discontinuity of value? something about the unit of moral concern?) that, if we all got clear on it, would actually break the stalemate? I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself, and an honest answer about whether anything could move it, or whether the right move is to stop treating it as a question with an answer.
