I've been thinking about a problem that's been bothering me for a while, and I can't find a way out of it. I don't have any formal training in philosophy — I work in software — but I've been reading about decision theory on my own, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove something I don't believe. I'm hoping someone can tell me where the reasoning goes wrong.

It started when I learned about expected value: you multiply the value of each possible outcome by its probability, and you pick the option with the highest sum. That seems perfectly sensible when you're deciding whether to bring an umbrella — a small inconvenience for a decent chance of staying dry. But then I started wondering how far the principle extends, and I built the following argument:

(1) If an action has positive expected value — that is, if the probability-weighted sum of all its possible outcomes is greater than zero — then a rational person should take it, provided there are no other actions with higher expected value available.

(2) For any finite cost, there exists a sufficiently large potential benefit and a sufficiently small nonzero probability such that the expected value of paying the cost in exchange for a chance at the benefit is positive.

(3) So if a stranger on the street asks me for $100, promising that he has a plan to bring about a trillion trillion happy lives and that my $100 will give his plan a one-in-a-trillion-trillion chance of succeeding, I should hand over the money. The expected value is enormous — one trillion trillion lives times one in a trillion trillion, minus $100.

(4) The same reasoning applies no matter how small the probability, as long as the promised payoff is scaled up to match. So I should also hand over the money if the probability is one in 10¹⁰⁰, with the payoff scaled to 10¹⁰⁰ lives, or one in 10¹⁰⁰⁰ with a payoff of 10¹⁰⁰⁰ lives, and so on without limit.

(5) But if this is true, then anyone can extract any amount of money from me by spinning a story about an arbitrarily large payoff at an arbitrarily small probability, as long as the expected value arithmetic comes out positive. I can't verify whether the stranger is telling the truth, but even if I assign a staggeringly low credence to his story, the numbers can be made large enough to overwhelm it.

(6) That conclusion is absurd. A principle of decision-making that tells me to empty my bank account for anyone with a sufficiently vivid imagination and a talent for multiplication is not a principle I can live by.

(7) So premise (1) must be false.

But if I reject (1), I'm left without a clear rule for when expected value reasoning applies and when it doesn't. Bringing an umbrella still seems right. Refusing to hand my wallet to a stranger still seems right. But I can't explain why both are correct without a principle that distinguishes them — and I can't find one. I've been turning this over for weeks and I can't tell whether the mistake is in one of the premises, or in the way I've moved from one step to the next, or whether I just have to accept a conclusion I find impossible to live with. Where does this argument go wrong?
