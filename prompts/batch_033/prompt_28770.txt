A popular science article I read summarizes the cognitive science of religion as follows: "Human minds come equipped with what researchers call a hypersensitive agency-detection device: we are built to over-detect intentional agents in ambiguous situations, because our ancestors could not afford to ignore a possible predator. Religious belief is what happens when this device, together with our ordinary social intelligence, is pointed at the world at large — we see purpose in storms, illness, and death, and we posit gods and spirits as the agents behind them. Religious concepts also have a built-in cultural advantage: they are 'minimally counterintuitive,' violating just enough of our intuitive expectations to be memorable while remaining easy to represent. On this picture, religion is a byproduct of cognitive machinery that evolved for other purposes, not an adaptation in its own right. And that is why religion has no function of its own — it is, in effect, a very successful glitch, and its persistence is explained by the architecture of our minds rather than by anything it does for individuals or societies."

I suspect the step from "byproduct" to "no function" is doing a lot of work, but I can't tell whether that's the only problem. How fair is this summary? Go through it claim by claim and say which parts a careful researcher in the cognitive science of religion would accept, which they would push back on, and which are overstated or misleading. In particular, does the conclusion that religion has no function follow from its being a byproduct? Please separate what is reasonably settled in the field from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
