So two people in my game theory reading group got into a long argument about the centipede game and I said I'd type it up and get an outside referee. The setup was the usual one: two players alternate, the pot starts at two dollars, and on each turn you can either take a little more than half for yourself and end the game, or pass, which doubles the pot and hands the turn to the other player; if everyone passes to the last node, the second player can take the big pot or pass and they split it. I forget how many rounds they used, doesnt matter for the argument.

Then the back and forth, as best I remember it:

(1) A said backward induction is airtight here. At the last node, player two should take rather than pass, because taking pays more than the even split. So at the second-to-last node, player one knows two would take, and so one should take. Roll that back and player one should take at the very first node. So the rational prediction is that the game ends immediatly with a tiny payoff.

(2) B said that's the standard line but it sits weirdly with what you'd actually think if the game kept going. If you found yourself at node 50, you'd know both players have passed many times already, which the backward-induction story says no rational player would do. So at node 50 you have strong evidence your model of the other player is wrong. If you then take because "he'll take at 51," you're ignoring everything you just saw. His passing so far is exactly the kind of evidence that he'll pass again.

(3) A said that's just off-path reasoning. Before the game, the plan "take at node one" is uniquely rational. If play somehow reaches node 50, that's an event that can only happen if someone already deviated from rationality, and you can't use an irrational deviation as evidence about what a rational player will do next. Once you're off the equilibrium path, backward induction has nothing to say—but that doesn't undermine the ex ante claim that taking at node one is the rational play.

(4) B said "off the equilibrium path" is doing all the work and maybe it's a cheat. At node 50 you're not choosing a plan from behind a veil; you're choosing now, with the information you now have. And your current best evidence is that the other player is a passer, not a taker. So the rational move now might be to pass and let the pot keep growing. And if that's right, it also matters ex ante: a rational player could anticipate that if the game reached node 50 he'd rationally pass, and that anticipation changes the node-one calculation.

(5) A said that's circular. You're using "he passed before" as evidence he'll pass again, but that's only evidence if you already take him to be a passing type rather than a rational taker who made a mistake. If he's a passing type, fine, but then you're not describing the game with common knowledge of rationality anymore. Backward induction was a claim about rational players with common knowledge of rationality, not about whatever type you discover mid-game.

(6) B said maybe that's the point: common knowledge of rationality is an idealization that breaks exactly when it's doing the interesting work. In a real game you don't know the other person is rational, you believe it to some degree and update. Backward induction is a theorem about an idealized setup, and the puzzle is that people read it as a prediction about actual players. If the idealized story can't even tell you what to do at node 50 without saying "that node can't happen," maybe the idealization is hiding the hard part.

They asked me to referee and I couldn't. Could you go through move by move and say who's right about what, who's more right overall, and what either or both of them are missing?
