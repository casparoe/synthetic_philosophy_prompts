I'm in an epistemology seminar, and last week a fellow student summarized the dogmatism paradox for me in a way that sounded suspiciously tidy. In his telling, it goes like this: "Take any ordinary piece of knowledge, say that your keys are on the table. Since you know it, you can deduce that any evidence against it must be misleading: if the keys are really on the table, then anything pointing away from that is pointing at a falsehood. By closure of knowledge under deduction, you therefore know, before even seeing it, that any counterevidence—a photo showing the table empty, for instance—would be misleading. So when you are actually shown such a photo, you can rationally refuse to change your mind on the grounds that you already knew it was misleading. The upshot is that fallibilism plus epistemic closure leads straight to dogmatism, and the only stable way out is to reject closure, because fallibilism is obviously non-negotiable."

I can't tell exactly where this gloss cuts corners, but I suspect it conflates a few things, especially around what closure actually gives you and whether the conclusion follows from the accurate parts. Could you go through his summary sentence by sentence and tell me what an epistemologist would accept, what they would want to qualify, and what is simply mistaken? In particular, does closure of knowledge under known entailment really allow you to know, in advance, that a particular piece of counterevidence would be misleading—or only something more generic or conditional? Does the paradox show that you can rationally dismiss actual counterevidence after encountering it, or only something weaker about your prior epistemic position? And is it fair to say the only stable way out is to reject closure, or are there coherent ways to preserve both fallibilism and closure while still explaining why the dogmatic reasoning goes wrong?
