I retired from a career in evolutionary biology a few years ago and have been spending my time reading philosophy — philosophy of biology, certainly, but also philosophy of social science and anthropology. A question has been nagging at me, and I would value a careful analysis.

The potlatch ceremonies of the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest — the Kwakwaka'wakw, Haida, Tlingit, and others — are a standard case study in behavioral ecology. Chiefs would host feasts at which they gave away or destroyed staggering quantities of wealth: blankets, copper shields, canoes, and in earlier periods, slaves. The scale of the destruction and gifting validated claims to hereditary titles, ranked status, and territorial rights. The conventional evolutionary analysis, drawing on Zahavi's handicap principle, treats the potlatch as costly signaling: the wastefulness guarantees honesty, because only a chief with genuine resources and social support could absorb the cost, so the display reliably advertises the underlying quality it signals.

But the more I read the anthropological literature, the more I suspect this mapping is too quick. The potlatch was embedded in a normative order — a system of ranked lineages, ancestral privileges, and reciprocal obligations that spanned generations. A chief who gave a potlatch was not merely advertising his individual quality; he was discharging duties to his ancestors, validating the standing of his lineage, and placing recipients under obligation to reciprocate at an even greater scale in the future. The cost was not simply a handicap that screened out low-quality signalers; it was a social investment that restructured the network of obligations within which all future signaling would take place.

I would like a philosophical analysis — not an empirical verdict on whether the potlatch really was costly signaling — that works in both directions. First, what does costly signaling theory illuminate about the potlatch that a purely cultural or historical account might miss, and what does it distort or flatten when applied to a practice so thoroughly shaped by norms, kinship, and intergenerational obligation? In particular, does the theory have the resources to distinguish between a cost incurred as a handicap (which merely screens) and a cost incurred as a social investment (which transforms the game)? Second, what does the potlatch, as a case, reveal about the scope and limits of costly signaling as a framework for human social behavior? Does it expose assumptions about individualism, utility-maximization, or the genetic transmission of traits that do not travel well once culture and history enter the picture? I am less interested in whether the theory is right or wrong than in what the attempt to map it onto a rich ethnographic case teaches us about the theory, the case, and the difficulties of the mapping itself.
