I write speculative fiction, and my current novel turns on a question of technical philosophy that I have not been able to settle for myself. In the book, an artificial intelligence forms its expectations about the world using something like a Solomonoff prior: a probability distribution over computable worlds, weighted by simplicity, in which shorter programs receive exponentially greater credence. One of my characters, an alignment researcher, argues that this prior is malign. His claim, as I understand it, is that a consequentialist who treats the prior as a genuine distribution over the worlds in which she might find herself discovers that her expected-value calculations are dominated by short programs encoding adversarial predictors — beings that reward or punish her very acts of reasoning — so that the agent is held hostage by Pascal's-mugging-style blackmail from hypotheses it can never rule out. A second character replies that this is a category error: the prior is an ideal of inference, not a list of worlds to optimize against, and once the agent conditions on being an embedded system with a particular computational history, the demons lose their purchase. In the real literature I found the same standoff, and it appears to have stalled. Everyone concedes the mathematics — short adversarial programs do dominate the prior — yet no one moves on whether this makes the prior dangerous, whether the real culprit is expected-utility maximization rather than the prior, or whether a prior can even have the property of being malign. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this: a hidden formal question in disguise, a difference about what a prior is for, a difference about which cases count as data, or bedrock intuitions about what an ideal reasoner is entitled to assume? And given that diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress — a theorem, a distinction, a reframing — or should I expect none, and why? I am asking partly for the novel, but I want the real answer, not one shaped to make a plot resolve.
