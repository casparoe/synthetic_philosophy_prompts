I have spent the past year defending prioritarianism in a reading group on distributive justice: the view that the goodness of a distribution of well-being is an additive function of individual well-being, with benefits to those who are worse off weighted more heavily, but with no independent value placed on equality as such. I was drawn to it because it seemed to preserve the core egalitarian impulse — that it matters more how a benefit affects the worse-off — while escaping the leveling-down objection, since on this view making the better-off worse off never improves a distribution when no one is made better off. Lately, though, I have begun to suspect that this immunity is too cheap, and I want a systematic account of what would count against the view rather than another restatement of its attractions.

What cases, arguments, or formal results should actually move a prioritarian? In particular: can the leveling-down objection be reframed so that it bites against prioritarianism after all — for example, by charging that a theory which refuses ever to level down has abandoned the thought that unfair inequality is itself a bad state of affairs, or that the priority weighting cannot be specified in a non-arbitrary way? Which of these are decisive and which merely awkward? What does prioritarianism rule in or out that its rivals — utilitarianism, egalitarianism, sufficientarianism — do not, and do any of those commitments expose it? I want to know what a committed prioritarian would themselves concede counts against the position, not only what critics say. And if the view turns out to be well insulated — if the flexibility of the weighting function lets it absorb every proposed counterexample — I want an honest verdict on whether that insulation is a strength or a defect. I would personally be most persuaded that something is wrong if prioritarianism turned out to be unstable: if, at one limit of the weighting, it collapses into utilitarianism and, at the other, into a leximin or maximin rule, leaving the supposed distinct middle ground without a principled foundation.
