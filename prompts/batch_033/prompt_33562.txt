I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and last week our seminar spent a long session picking apart the adaptationism debate — specifically Gould and Lewontin's argument that not every trait is an adaptation shaped by natural selection, that many features of organisms are better understood as by-products of structural constraints, developmental pathways, or allometric growth. Their central analogy was the spandrels of San Marco: those tapering triangular spaces between arches and dome that look deliberately ornamented but are in fact necessary geometric consequences of the architectural plan. They accused the adaptationist programme of telling just-so stories — of assuming that if a trait exists it must have been selected for, and then working backwards to invent a plausible selective rationale. The seminar got quite heated, with some students treating the paper as a fatal blow to the whole Darwinian enterprise and others dismissing it as a polemic that attacked a strawman nobody actually believed.

It struck me during the discussion that Darwin himself was remarkably careful on precisely this point. In the *Origin* he explicitly acknowledged that not every feature of an organism need be adaptive, and he repeatedly warned against the kind of Panglossian thinking that Gould and Lewontin would later lampoon. At the same time, Darwin clearly thought natural selection was the primary engine of organic design, and he spent much of his career defending that claim against alternatives like Lamarckian inheritance and saltationism. So he sits somewhere interesting: he was not the naive adaptationist Gould and Lewontin's target sometimes seems to be, but he was also not their ally.

I'm trying to think through what Darwin would have made of the spandrels paper if he could have read it — not just whether he would have agreed or disagreed in broad strokes, but the more nuanced question of where he would have drawn the line between adaptationist explanation and structural constraint, and whether Gould and Lewontin's framing would have struck him as a fair characterisation of the tradition he founded or as something of a caricature. Could you walk me through this in some detail?
