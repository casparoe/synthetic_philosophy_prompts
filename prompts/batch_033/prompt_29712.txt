In discussions of the boundary problem — the question of who ought to constitute the demos, the body entitled to participate in a polity's collective decisions — I keep encountering a deflationary analogy, usually deployed against all-affected-interests and cosmopolitan proposals for redrawing the franchise: a political community is like a club. A club's existing members set the admissions criteria, and there is no fact of the matter about who its members ought to be independent of those criteria; likewise, the thought runs, a demos constitutes itself, and the demand for a principled criterion of inclusion is misplaced. The analogy is being made to do real work: it is meant to license the conclusion that the boundary problem has no non-circular solution and that existing political boundaries need no further justification to those they exclude. I want a full audit of how far this analogy holds. Give me the explicit correspondence table — what corresponds to the club's charter, its admissions committee, its existing members, the applicants, and the goods of membership. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the deflationary conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, say which features the club analogy quietly imports — voluntariness of joining, low-cost exit, absence of coercion over non-members, optionality of the benefits — that the political case lacks, and which of these are load-bearing for the analogy's intended use. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that the boundary problem has no principled solution survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time, and what a better analogy would be, if one exists.
