So my dad died last month and I've been goin in circles on this thing I half-remember from a philosophy class years ago — Epicurus's argument that death can't be bad for the person who dies. The line is something like "when death is, we are not." I used to think it was just a clever puzzle, but now it's not so abstract.

Here's what I'm stuck on. The standard comeback is the deprivation account: death is bad for you because it deprives you of the goods you would've had if you'd kept living. Fine. But then there's the timing problem: *when* is this badness supposed to occur? It's not before death (you're still alive and haven't been deprived yet), it's not after death (there's no you to be the subject of the harm), and if death is instantaneous there's no moment *during* death either. Some people — Nagel, Ben Bradley — say the timing problem is a pseudo-problem. Maybe "bad for" doesn't need a temporal location; maybe the harm is located at the times you would have lived. But then I worry this opens the door to Lucretius's symmetry argument: if death deprives you of goods after you die, then by the same logic, the time before you were born also deprived you of goods — and nobody thinks their prenatal nonexistence was a harm. If the deprivation account can't block the symmetry, maybe it's not doing the work we thought it was.

I want an essay that works through this knot. Can the deprivation account handle the timing problem without collapsing into the symmetry problem? Or is the whole framing wrong — maybe the real issue is whether something can be bad for you without you ever experiencing it, and once you grant that it can, both the timing and symmetry problems dissolve? Weigh the arguments carefully, don't just summarize them. Reach a conclusion and defend it. I'm not looking for a "both sides have points" ending — pick a position and argue for it.
