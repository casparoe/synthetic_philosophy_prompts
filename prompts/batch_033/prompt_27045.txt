I've been trying to work out a naturalistic account of how thoughts get to be about things — the idea that a brain state means whatever it was selected to indicate, the way a smoke detector means smoke because smoke is what it is for. I hit an argument that seems to show the whole approach cannot make room for error, and I can't find the exit.

(1) What a state represents is fixed by its function: the condition it was selected to detect.
(2) A state's function is read off its selection history — it is whatever condition explains why states of that type were favored.
(3) Consider an animal with a detector that evolved to snap at flies. The detector also fires at small dark moving specks, such as pellets.
(4) The detector's function is to detect flies; pellets are just noise.
(5) But the selection history can't distinguish 'fly' from 'small dark moving speck.' In the animal's sensory environment, every fly that helped select the detector was also a small dark moving speck, so the same history is equally consistent with the detector's having been selected to detect either.
(6) The view therefore assigns no determinate content — or it assigns the disjunctive content 'fly or small dark moving speck,' in which case snapping at a pellet is not an error at all.
(7) Since any account of mental content must allow misrepresentation, the selection-based account fails.

I'd rather keep the selection-based picture than give it up. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
