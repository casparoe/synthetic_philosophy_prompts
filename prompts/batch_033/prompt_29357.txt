I accept the standard reading of Fitch's paradox of knowability: namely, that the derivation from the principle that all truths are knowable (∀p(p → ◇Kp)) to the conclusion that all truths are actually known (∀p(p → Kp)) is a sound argument, and that this result refutes any philosophical position committed to the knowability principle — most notably verificationist or anti-realist semantics that identify truth with the possibility of verification, proof, or warranted assertibility.

I want to know what would count against this assessment. Specifically, I want a map of the escape routes from Fitch's result, organized by how seriously they threaten the standard conclusion. For each route, I want to know: what would the world — or our best theory of logic, semantics, or knowledge — have to look like for it to succeed? Is it a live option or merely a logical possibility? And what would its proponents themselves concede as counting against it?

Please distinguish between the following families of response:

(a) Restriction strategies: approaches that preserve the spirit of the knowability principle by restricting its scope — for instance, to propositions for which the act of coming to know them does not undermine their truth. What would count against such restrictions? In particular, can the restriction be motivated independently, or does it simply carve out the problematic cases ad hoc? What would a principled criterion look like, and has one been supplied?

(b) Logical revision strategies: approaches that block the derivation by moving to a non-classical logic — for example, intuitionistic logic, in which the step from a contradiction involving negation to the corresponding positive conditional does not go through in the same way. What independent motivation would such a revision need in order to avoid the charge of being a purely defensive maneuver? Are there versions of the paradox that go through even in the revised logic?

(c) Strategies that accept the derivation but deny that the conclusion is absurd: for instance, by arguing that the conclusion that all truths are known is not obviously false once we attend carefully to what knowledge amounts to in this context, or by embracing a form of idealism on independent grounds. What would have to be true about the nature of knowledge or truth for this to be a serious option rather than a reductio of the response itself?

(d) Strategies that question the epistemic or modal principles used inside the derivation — for example, the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, the factivity of knowledge, or the closure principles governing the possibility operator. Which of these principles could coherently be denied, and what would the broader consequences be for epistemology or modal logic if one were denied?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of how exposed the standard conclusion actually is. Has it been qualified into unfalsifiability — are its defenders in a position to dismiss every objection as merely apparent? Are any of the escape routes genuinely threatening, or do they all come at costs that no one working in the debate would willingly pay? If the standard conclusion turns out to be well insulated, is that a problem for a thesis of this kind, or is it simply what we should expect from a result in philosophical logic?
