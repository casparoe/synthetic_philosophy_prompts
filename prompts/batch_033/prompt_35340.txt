I'm a graduate student in the history of philosophy, writing a paper on Hobbes's philosophy of geometry. Hobbes took geometry to be the paradigm of demonstrative science — it proceeds from clear definitions by strict deduction, and he thought all genuine science should emulate it. At the same time, he held that the objects of geometry are material bodies in space; he rejected the idea of abstract mathematical objects existing independently of matter. In his own lifetime he was already embattled, most famously in his dispute with Wallis over the adequacy of his own geometric proofs.

What I want you to do is this: predict, as rigorously as you can, what Hobbes's views would have been on three developments in geometry that occurred after his death — (1) the discovery of consistent non-Euclidean geometries in the 19th century, (2) Hilbert's formalist axiomatization of geometry, in which terms like "point" and "line" are treated as uninterpreted primitives defined only implicitly by the axioms, and (3) the empirical determination, via general relativity, that the physical space we inhabit is not Euclidean. For each, give me your reasoning from Hobbes's known commitments, note where those commitments pull in different directions, and flag where you think the prediction is genuinely underdetermined by the historical record. Finally, tell me whether Hobbes's philosophy of geometry, applied to these cases, would have forced him to revise any of his core views, or whether the framework can absorb them without fundamental change.
