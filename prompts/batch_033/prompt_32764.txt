So I've been reading the Dennett–Caruso exchange on free will and punishment — I'm a retired physicist and I've gotten into philosophy recently, so bear with me if I'm missing something obvious. As I understand it, they both agree that libertarian free will is a non-starter: we don't have some kind of contra-causal magic that makes us the "ultimate" authors of our actions. Where they part ways is on the implications. Dennett thinks we can still talk about free will and moral responsibility in a compatibilist sense, and that punishment can be justified on consequentialist grounds. Caruso says no — if libertarian free will is gone, then moral responsibility goes with it, and we should shift to a public-health model for dealing with harmful behavior.

Here's what I don't get. If they both accept the same underlying metaphysics — determinism, no libertarian free will — then isn't Dennett just engaged in a relabeling excercise? He's taking "free will" and redefining it to mean something like "the capacity to act on your desires without external coercion." That's a perfectly fine thing to study, but it seems like a different topic from what the traditional free will debate was about. Caruso, at least, is using the term in its traditional sense and concluding we don't have it. So isn't their disagreement ultimately just a verbal dispute about whether we should keep using the phrase "free will"? Or is there a substantive philosophical disagreement here that I'm not seeing?
