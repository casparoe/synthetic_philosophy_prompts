In my set theory class last week, the professor tossed off this line: "The axiom of choice is obviously true, the well-ordering theorem is obviously false, and who can tell about Zorn's lemma?" Everyone laughed, and I didn't. I know the three are equivalent over ZF, so I get that the surface joke is 'haha, our intutions diverge on equivalent statements.' But I've been turning it over since and I think there's more going on. When someone says AC is 'obviously true,' what exact claim are they making? That choice functions exist in some platonic sense? That AC is a good axiom to adopt pragmatically? That finite choice generalizes naturally? And when they say the well-ordering theorem is 'obviously false,' what's the precise intuition being appealed to — that you can't well-order the reals? that there's no *explicit* well-ordering? that well-ordering an uncountable set feels like pulling a rabbit out of a hat? I want to go through the different precisifications of 'obviously true' and 'obviously false' hiding in this one-liner and see which combinations are genuinely in tension given the equivalence, which are compatible, and whether the joke is actually making a substantive point about mathematical intuition or just getting a cheap laugh. Lay it out like a menu I can bring to office hours.
