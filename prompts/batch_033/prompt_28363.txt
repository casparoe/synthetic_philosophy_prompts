I am a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy late, and I am trying to get my views on the ethics of market exchange into order. I have found myself holding the following positions, each of which seems right to me on its own, but I have not worked through whether they fit together. I would like an audit rather than a survey: tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension, order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what the least costly revision would be.

(1) Voluntary, mutually beneficial exchange between competent adults is presumptively permissible, and legal prohibition of such exchange requires a strong justification.

(2) Buying goods produced in sweatshops is not wrong when the jobs are better than the workers' actual alternatives; refusing to buy in those cases would likely make the workers worse off.

(3) Price gouging after a natural disaster — charging sharply higher prices for water, fuel, or shelter — is wrong and should be legally prohibited.

(4) A transaction can be wrongfully exploitative even when both parties consent and both are made better off by it than they would have been without it.

(5) Some things should not be for sale even between consenting adults; I would place kidneys, votes, and jury service in this category.

(6) Consumer boycotts are a legitimate and sometimes admirable way to act on one's moral convictions.

Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose among: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
