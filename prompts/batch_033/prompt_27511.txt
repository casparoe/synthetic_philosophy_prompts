My epistemology professor has been giving us these little cases all term about people who get evidence about their own thinking, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer her rule before the final. Here's what I have so far.

- A student works through a proof, checks it twice, gets answer P. Then she's told, by a source she has every reason to trust, that she's been given a mild sedative that makes people a little overconfident on logic problems. Professor: she's still rational to believe P, just maybe a shade less confident.
- A guy has a strong gut feeling that a stock will go up, then learns his gut feelings about stocks are worse than chance. Professor: not rational to trust it.
- A radiologist glances at a scan, has a clear impression of a fracture, then is told the lighting is bad and radiologists in that lighting misread these scans a lot. Professor: she should suspend judgment.
- A student does a long arithmetic problem, gets an answer, then is told she was given a drug that specifically makes arithmetic reasoning unreliable. Professor: she should suspend judgment.
- A student solves a problem carefully, checks it, gets P, and is then told by a usually reliable source that she's a bit sleep-deprived, which can make people sloppy on long proofs. In fact the source is wrong and she's fine. Professor: her belief is rational.

Next week's case is this: a student solves a problem carefully, checks it, gets P, and is then told by a very trustworthy source that she's been given a drug that severely impairs exactly this kind of reasoning. In fact the source is wrong and she's perfectly fine. Does the professor say she's rational to believe P, or should she suspend?

I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rule she's running. I can sort of see two rules that fit all five earlier verdicts — something like 'weigh whatever evidence you have, including misleading evidence about your own reasoning' versus 'only actual reliability matters, so misleading higher-order evidence doesn't count.' I also keep going back and forth on wether a person can rationally believe P while also having strong evidence that their own reasoning is compromised. Which rule do you think she's actually running, what does each rule say about the new case, and what further case would settle which one she's using?
