I serve on my university's conduct appeals panel, and a case before us has split the panel. A graduate student, after a falling out with a classmate, logged into the university's shared virtual environment and destroyed the classmate's virtual home, workshop, and two years' worth of virtual objects she had built there; the logs confirm the destruction and the items cannot be restored. The conduct code prohibits destroying another student's property, and a hearing officer found him responsible. He appeals on the ground that virtual objects are not property and that the classmate's distress, however genuine, is distress over pixels rather than a real loss. Half the panel finds this persuasive; the other half says the loss is real and the code plainly reaches it. Please prepare the decision memo I will circulate before we deliberate: recommend whether to uphold or overturn the finding, with reasoning each side could recognize as engaging their position, and take seriously the strongest argument on the losing side rather than merely stipulating what counts as real.
