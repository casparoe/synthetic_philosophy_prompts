I'm facing a personal decision that's been nagging at me, and I suspect it has the shape of a deeper philosophical problem I've seen discussed in contexts like climate policy, but I can't tell whether my reasoning is sound or where exactly it goes wrong.

My wife and I are going through IVF. We have several embryos, and preimplantation genetic testing flagged one as carrying a variant linked to a condition that would make for a meaningfully harder life — not devastating, but the child would likely face recurring medical interventions, some social difficulties, and elevated risk of depression. The life would still be clearly worth living. We're trying to decide whether to implant this embryo or a different one without the marker.

The advice from our doctor and from family has been straightforward: choose the embryo without the genetic issue, because you want to give your child the best possible life. But I can't shake the feeling that this reasoning doesn't work the way people think it does. If we choose the other embryo, the child with the condition never exists at all. The child we'd have instead is a different person — different genome, different person. So the child with the condition isn't being made worse off by our choice; they simply wouldn't be here. If their life is worth living, how can we be acting against their interests by bringing them into existence? Who exactly is being harmed?

I recognize this structure from discussions of long-term policy — the idea that different choices lead to different people being born, so the usual "make things better for future generations" framing gets complicated. But I've never had to face it as a concrete decision, and I'm not sure whether the abstract argument I'm reaching for actually holds up when you're the one making the choice. Either my reasoning has a flaw I'm not seeing, or the standard advice has a flaw, and I can't figure out which. What's the right way to think about this?
