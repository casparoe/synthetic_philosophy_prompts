I teach a upper-level undergrad seminar called "Gödel, Turing, and the Limits of Mind" and I'm writing the final exam. We spent a solid three weeks on the Lucas-Penrose argument — the idea that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows human minds can't be Turing machines, because we can "see" the truth of the Gödel sentence of any formal system we're supposedly equivalent to, which the system itself can't prove. We read Lucas's 1961 paper, a chunk of Penrose's *Shadows of the Mind* (1994), and rebuttals by Putnam, Chalmers, and Feferman. The students can all recite the standard objections — "maybe we're inconsistent," "we can't prove our own consistency either," "the system might not be able to recognize its own Gödel numbering," etc. — but I want to know whether they actually understand *why* those objections work and whether they can spot the same move in a different costume.

So here's what I want: write me an essay question that presents a short, plausible-sounding argument that uses Gödel's theorems to reach a conclusion about AI alignment — something like: "Gödel showed that for any sufficiently powerful AI system, there will be truths it can't prove; a human overseer can in principle spot these blind spots; therefore human oversight of AI is necessarily superior to automated oversight." The argument should sound seductive but be vulnerable to the same (or analogous) objections that sink Lucas-Penrose. The question should ask students to evaluate the argument: where it goes wrong, what it assumes, and what a correct understanding of Gödel's theorems actually licenses us to say about AI systems.

Please give me the question itself, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme that allocates 25 marks across five or six specific moves a top answer must make. For each move, include a one-sentence note on how weaker students typically get it wrong. I want the marking scheme to be the kind of thing where a A student shows they can *apply* the technical objections rather than just name them.
