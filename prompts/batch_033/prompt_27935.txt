So here's where I'm at: a rigid designator picks out the same object in every possible world (where it picks out anything). "Aristotle" is rigid — it's that dude no matter what possible world we're in. "The teacher of Alexander" isn't rigid cause in some world somebody else taught him. Got it. And then there's these necessary a posteriori truths like "water is H2O" — necessary cause in every possible world water just is H2O, but a posteriori cause we needed to actually go do chemistry to figure that out.

Here's the thing that's messing me up. If "water" is a rigid designator, it designates H2O in every possible world. And "H2O" is also rigid — it designates that molecule in every possible world. So in every possible world, these two terms pick out exactly the same thing. But if that's true... what exactly did the empirical discovery *do*? Like, if the terms are already set up to co-refer across all of modal space, then the identity statement seems like it should be true just in virtue of how the terms work. Shouldn't we be able to figure out "water is H2O" from the armchair once we understand rigid designation? But that would make it a priori, which contradicts the whole point of the necessary a posteriori.

I have this nagging feeling I'm confusing the metaphysics of reference with the epistemology of how we *learn* the reference, but I can't quite put my finger on where the confusion is exactly.
