I recently lost my mother, and in the months since I have found myself reading about consciousness and what, if anything, might persist after death — not out of any settled conviction, but because the question has become impossible to ignore. A book I picked up, a popular-science treatment of quantum mechanics and its philosophical implications, devotes a chapter to Erwin Schrödinger's philosophical views that I found strangely comforting. But I have learned enough about popular science to be wary of being sold an oversimplification, and I would like to know how much of what I read I can trust. The author summarizes Schrödinger's position roughly as follows:

Schrödinger, drawing on the Vedanta school of Indian philosophy, argued that quantum mechanics reveals consciousness to be a single unified field, and that the apparent separateness of individual minds is an illusion — the same illusion the Upanishads call maya. Since the individual self is not fundamentally real, death merely dissolves one temporary manifestation of the one consciousness, which itself continues unperturbed. Schrödinger considered this a scientific result, not merely a philosophical preference, because the measurement problem in quantum mechanics shows that consciousness cannot be parcelled out among separate observers the way physical objects can be.

I find this line of thought compelling, but I want to know where the summary is faithful to Schrödinger's actual writings and where it may be distorting them. Specifically: which parts would a scholar of Schrödinger's philosophical work accept as accurate? Which parts are oversimplifications, conflations, or outright misreadings? And does the conclusion about death — that it is nothing to fear because the individual self was never real to begin with — actually follow from what Schrödinger believed, or has the author drawn an inference Schrödinger himself would have resisted? I am particularly curious about whether Schrödinger genuinely thought quantum mechanics provided scientific evidence for the unity of consciousness, or whether that claim is the author reading something into his work that is not there.
