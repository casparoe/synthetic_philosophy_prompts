I'm teaching a graduate seminar on metaethics this term, and we're about to spend three weeks on the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. Before my students get lost in the back-and-forth between Blackburn, Gibbard, Schroeder, and their critics, I want to give them a clear specification of what any adequate solution is supposed to accomplish — the desiderata. I've been working in this area long enough to list the obvious ones, but I worry I may be smuggling in assumptions that favor one theoretical camp over another, and I suspect some of the requirements I take for granted may be jointly unsatisfiable in ways that would reshape how we frame the problem itself.

You are an expert in metaethics and the philosophy of language, and you think carefully and systematically. Begin by brainstorming the full range of constraints that a solution to the Frege–Geach problem must satisfy — the logical phenomena, the semantic and metasemantic commitments that different parties bring to the table, the linguistic data, and the dialectical burdens expressivists face. Then produce a structured list of desiderata. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. For each, indicate whether it is non-negotiable — something any viable expressivism must deliver — or a preference of a particular version of expressivism or of its critics. Where you think a pair or subset of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied given existing results — for instance, from what we know about compositional semantics, logical form, or the semantics of propositional attitudes — flag that explicitly: that is the part I most want my students to see. Finally, state which single desideratum rules out the largest number of prominent attempted solutions, and briefly explain why. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a bibliography; the value is in the architecture of the requirements themselves and in the tensions among them.
