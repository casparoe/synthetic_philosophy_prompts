I'm preparing a philosophy of time unit for an undergraduate course and I want to use Denis Villeneuve's *Arrival* (2016) as a companion text to the presentism-versus-eternalism debate. The film's central idea is that acquiring the Heptapod language rewires Louise Banks's cognition so that she experiences time as a block — past, present, and future all equally real and accessible, which looks like a cinematic rendering of eternalism. But eternalism is a metaphysical thesis about the structure of time, not a claim about what human beings can perceive or know, and the film seems to slide between eternalism and a kind of fatalism about choice.

Read the film through the lens of this debate. Where does it genuinely dramatize what eternalism commits one to, and where does it trade on ideas that a philosopher of time would push back on — for example, conflating the claim that the future is real with the claim that one's choices are fixed, or treating foreknowledge as incompatible with agency? And what does the film's emotional arc — Louise's choice to have a daughter she knows she will lose — add to the philosophical discussion that an argument alone cannot? I want to use the film in class without misleading students, but I also want them to see what a narrative can do that a paper cannot.
