Take one of the standard spectra: a chain of outcomes A, B, C, ..., Z, where each adjacent pair is so close that you'd swear the earlier one is better than the next, but by the time you reach the end you'd swear A is worse than Z. Some philosophers say this shows 'better than' just isn't transitive, full stop: the pairwise judgments are the data and transitivity has to go. Others say transitivity is built into what 'better than' means, or is a requirement on rational evaluation, so at least one of those tiny-step judgments has to be false even though nobody can say which. Both sides feel the pull of the same pairwise judgments, nobody disputes the endpoint or the logic, so why hasn't this moved in decades? My working theory is that it's not really a first-order dispute about value at all — it's a clash about what's more negotiable, the pairwise intuitions or the formal constraint, and maybe a verbal dispute about whether its a comparative relation or something more like 'preferable all things considered.'
