I'm designing a half-day workshop for machine-learning engineers and institutional designers called "When Metrics Go Rogue," and I need the centerpiece: a diagnostic decision tree that takes a practitioner from "I have a proxy metric and I'm worried it will be gamed" to a recommended family of countermeasures. The audience is technically sophisticated but philosophically mixed — some will have read the deep-RL specification-gaming papers (the CoastRunners boat, the stacking robot that flipped the block), others will know Goodhart's law only as a proverb about Soviet nail factories, and a few will be economists who think of the problem through Holmström and Milgrom. I want the tree to sort them not by what label they like but by the structural features of their situation: whether they can query the true goal at all (even expensively), whether the agent can influence the metric-generating process, whether the metric is fixed by regulation or can be iterated, whether the distribution shift is anticipatable or will be genuinely novel, and so on.

The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of named solutions. Start with the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual approaches — adversarial specification, tripwire monitoring, side constraints, reward-model ensembles, impact regularization, human-in-the-loop oversight, satisficing with slack, and whatever else you think belongs — within that space. Flag any combination of answers that is logically possible but unoccupied: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it's merely unexplored? Also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate and explain what additional information would break the tie. Each leaf should carry a short name for the approach, the one-sentence rationale for why it matches that branch, and its most awkward commitment — the thing a practitioner adopting it has to live with that they'll wish they didn't.
