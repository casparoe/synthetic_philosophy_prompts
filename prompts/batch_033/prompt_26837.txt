I'm a philosophy professor and I sit on the graduate admissions committee for our PhD program, and we've been stuck for weeks on a fight about recommendation letters. The background is this: over the past decade letters have ballooned from a page to three or four pages, and every one of them now says the candidate is among the top one percent the writer has ever taught and shows unusual originality, and so on. Nobody on the committee actually believes any of it anymore, but the trouble is that if any individual advisor writes a short, sober, comparative letter, their student reads as weak next to the pile of superlatives coming out of other programs—so no one dares stop. A few of us have started calling it a Moloch problem or a race to the bottom. There's a proposal on the table that our department should unilaterally adopt a hard cap of, say, 500 words and a standard format for letters, and maybe include a line in each letter saying our department has a policy of calibrated, non-inflated language. The other camp thinks this is naive: if we disarm while everyone else keeps inflating, we just tank our own students' chances, and the honest move is to wait for a field-wide norm or push the disciplinary association to act. I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Could you draft the memo? It should recomend a concrete course of action with a time frame—adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions—and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously, rather than just insisting that short letters are more honest. I'd especially like it to address whether a single department's norm can do anything at all when the trap operates at the level of the whole field, and what we should tell faculty who worry they'll be personally blamed if a student doesn't get in.
