Two members of my philosophy reading group spent most of last Tuesday arguing about the Sleeping Beauty problem, and neither would concede. I took notes on their exchange, and I'd like someone to go through it and adjudicate point by point — who is right about what, where each argument goes wrong or right, and what both might be missing.

For context: in the Sleeping Beauty problem, Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday. A fair coin is flipped. If it lands heads, she is awakened on Monday only and the experiment ends. If it lands tails, she is awakened on Monday, then given a drug that erases her memory of the awakening, and awakened again on Tuesday. Each awakening is subjectively indistinguishable. When Beauty finds herself awake during the experiment, what credence should she assign to the proposition that the coin landed heads?

Here is their exchange, as close to verbatim as my notes allow:

**Elena (the halfer):** When Beauty wakes up, she has learned nothing new. On Sunday, she already knew with certainty that she would be awakened at least once. The fact that she is now experiencing an awakening was fully expected. Since no new evidence has been acquired, her credence in heads should remain at one-half — the prior probability of the fair coin.

**David (the thirder):** She has learned something new. She has learned the centered proposition "I am awake now." That proposition is true in more centered worlds under tails — two awakenings — than under heads — one awakening. Conditionalizing on that evidence moves her credence from one-half to one-third. The standard likelihood reasoning supports this: the probability of "I am awake now" given heads is proportional to one awakening, and given tails is proportional to two awakenings.

**Elena:** That likelihood reasoning is circular. You are treating the awakenings as if they were randomly sampled, but the setup does not randomly sample awakenings — it deterministically produces them. On Sunday, Beauty knows that in the heads world she will experience exactly one awakening and in the tails world exactly two. But she also knows that each awakening is a definite event she will experience. The fact that there are more of them in one world does not make that world more likely to be actual. If I know I am going to experience a headache tomorrow, and I also know that if I have a cold I will experience two headaches, the fact that I now have a headache does not make the cold hypothesis twice as likely — I knew I would have at least one headache either way.

**David:** That is a different case. In your headache example, you can distinguish the headaches temporally — you know whether this is the first or second headache of the day. In Sleeping Beauty, the amnesia makes the awakenings indistinguishable. The correct analogy would be: you wake up with amnesia and a headache, not knowing whether it is the first or second headache of the day. In that case, the fact that the cold hypothesis predicts more headache-episodes does shift your credence.

**Elena:** Fine, but consider this. Suppose Beauty is told, after waking, that today is Monday. What should her credence in heads be then? On my view, she starts at one-half and updates: P(Monday | Heads) is one, P(Monday | Tails) is one-half, so by Bayes she arrives at two-thirds. That makes sense — learning it is Monday eliminates the possibility that it is Tuesday, which only arises under tails, so heads becomes more likely. On your view, she starts at one-third and updates to one-half. So learning it is Monday makes her less confident in heads. That seems backwards: Monday is the only day compatible with heads, so learning it is Monday should favor heads, not disfavor it.

**David:** I do not think that is backwards at all. On my view, the shift from one-third to one-half is an increase in absolute credence in heads — it goes up. The fact that it lands at one-half rather than two-thirds reflects the fact that, before learning it is Monday, Beauty was already accounting for the fact that tails worlds contain more awakenings. When you strip away that self-locating uncertainty by revealing the day, the residual uncertainty about the coin is exactly one-half, which is just the prior. That is perfectly coherent.

**Elena:** Let me put the central objection differently. Suppose on Sunday night we tell Beauty the coin came up tails, so her credence in heads drops to zero. Then we give her the amnesia drug so she forgets this revelation. On Monday, she wakes up. On your view, her credence in heads is now one-third. So forgetting the evidence that the coin was tails caused her credence in heads to rise from zero to one-third. That seems like a violation of the principle that rational credences should not increase just because you forgot evidence against them.

**David:** But that is not just forgetting — it is being placed in a situation of genuine self-locating uncertainty. The amnesia does not merely remove evidence; it puts you in a state where you do not know which centered world you are in. And in that state, the correct credence is determined by the proportion of centered worlds compatible with your current evidence. That is a different kind of reasoning from ordinary conditionalization, and it is exactly what the problem is designed to test. If you insist on applying ordinary conditionalization, you are refusing to engage with the self-locating aspect of the problem.

**Elena:** I think the real issue is that your view treats centered worlds as the fundamental objects of credence, whereas mine treats uncentered worlds as fundamental and handles self-locating uncertainty through a different mechanism. The question is which framework gives the right answer in this case. Consider: if we run the experiment a hundred times, Beauty will be awakened about one hundred and fifty times, and about fifty of those awakenings will be in heads trials. So if she bets on heads at even odds each time, she will lose money. You take this to show that her credence should be one-third. But I say this just shows that the betting setup is unfair — she is being forced to bet twice when she is wrong. If she could bet once per trial, even odds would be fair.

**David:** She cannot bet once per trial — she cannot tell which awakenings belong to the same trial. The betting analysis that matters is the one from her perspective inside the experiment. And from that perspective, the fair betting odds track one-third. That is the operational content of credence: the odds at which you are indifferent to betting. If we define credence in terms of fair betting odds, the thirder position is correct by definition.

**Elena:** Then we have a deeper disagreement about what credence means. I think credence tracks the probability of the uncentered proposition "the coin landed heads." You think it tracks the probability of the centered proposition "this awakening occurs in a heads trial." Those are different propositions, and the answer depends on which one we are asking about.

**David:** But the problem explicitly asks: when Beauty finds herself awake, what credence should she assign to the proposition that the coin landed heads? That is a question about her epistemic state given her self-locating uncertainty. The uncentered proposition "the coin landed heads" is what she is uncertain about, but her evidence is centered. The whole point is to figure out how centered evidence bears on uncentered hypotheses.

At this point someone changed the subject, and they never resolved it. I would like you to go through this exchange and adjudicate it move by move. For each major argument, say whether it is correct, incorrect, or partially correct — and explain why. Then give an overall assessment: which position is better supported, and what, if anything, are both sides missing.
