The extended mind thesis has come to seem right to me. I mean the view, associated with Clark and Chalmers, that cognitive processes are not confined to the skull or even the body: under the right conditions, an external resource that is reliably coupled to an agent and plays a role that would count as cognitive if carried out internally can be partly constitutive of the agent's mind. I find the standard case persuasive: Otto, who reliably consults his notebook as his memory, has his memory partly constituted by that notebook, just as Inga's memory is constituted by her biological brain. I intend this literally, not as a metaphor or a loose way of speaking.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am after a map of its exposure: the observations, arguments, findings, and thought experiments that would genuinely threaten it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, together with an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. In each case I would like to know whether a defender of the extended mind would concede that the point counts against them, or whether they would dismiss it as question-begging, as a restatement of internalism, or as a cost they are willing to accept.

I have some candidate defeaters in mind, but I do not know how seriously to take them. One is empirical: if neuroimaging showed that the processes involved in using an external resource are of a fundamentally different kind from those involved in using internal memory — for instance, if internal recall and notebook consultation shared no relevant processing profile — would that matter, and what exactly would the imaging have to show? Another is conceptual: Adams and Aizawa have argued that the mark of the cognitive is non-derived content, and that external objects lack it. If that argument were vindicated, how much damage would it do? I am also unsure what to make of the coupling-constitution fallacy, the charge that defenders of the view confuse a causal coupling between an agent and a tool with the constitution of a cognitive system. Is that a real objection, or does it simply assert the boundary that is at issue? Finally, the view seems to imply that if I became deeply reliant on another person's brain for some cognitive task, that person's brain would be literally part of my mind. Some find this absurd. Should a defender of the extended mind treat this as an acceptable consequence, or is it a genuine difficulty?

I would also like to know what the thesis predicts or rules out that its rivals do not. If it makes no distinctive predictions, is that a problem for a claim of this kind, or simply the nature of a thesis about constitution rather than causation? And if the view has been insulated by qualifications — for example, by making the conditions on extension so demanding that nothing ever meets them, or so permissive that the claim becomes trivial — I want to know whether that insulation is a defect here, or merely a feature of careful philosophical theses.

Finally, I should say what I would personally find most persuasive. I suspect I would be moved by a demonstration that the parity principle — the idea that if a process would count as cognitive when done internally, it should count as cognitive when done externally — either begs the question or yields unacceptable results when applied consistently. But I am not certain I am right to weight that so heavily, and I would like to know whether defenders of the view have a good answer to it.
