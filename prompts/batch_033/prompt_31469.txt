I've been teaching a grad seminar on pain and value this semster, and I've been keeping a running list of my own off-the-cuff verdicts about whether a given pain is bad for the person undergoing it. I'm starting to wonder what principle I'm actually tracking. Here's my track record so far:

- Ordinary stubbed toe: bad.
- Pain asymbolia (the classic case where the patient says "it's sharp, it's in my hand, I know it's pain, but it doesn't bother me at all"): not bad.
- Someone on an opioid that kills the unpleasantness but leaves the sensory qualities intact ("I can feel the pain but it's like it's happening to someone else"): not bad.
- Nociceptive firing during general anesthesia, no conscious experience: not bad.
- A masochist who's trained themselves to genuinely want the painful sensations—they still feel the unpleasantness full force, they've just learned to want it: bad.
- An experienced meditator who reports feeling pain with all its sensory and affective intensity but says they experience it with complete equanimity, no aversion, no suffering: bad.

So I seem to think that the unpleasantness is what matters, not the sensory component and not the subject's desires or aversions. But I'm not sure that's the only way to read my verdicts.

Now here's the new case that's got me stuck. There's a patient with a rare neurological condition where the affective component of pain is present but somehow "detached" from the rest of the person's mental life. The patient says: "I can tell the unpleasantness is there—it's vivid—but it doesn't feel like it's mine exactly. It's like I'm observing it from the outside. It doesn't reach me." The sensory pathways and the affective pathways are both active, but the usual connectivity between the affective system and the self-related and motivational networks is disrupted.

What should I say about this case if I want to be consistent with my previous verdicts? What principle or principles fit my track record, do they agree on this new case, and if not, which further case would settle which principle I'm really running? And I guess I'd also want to know: if I go one way rather than the other on this new case, what would I be committing to about the nature of pain's badness?
