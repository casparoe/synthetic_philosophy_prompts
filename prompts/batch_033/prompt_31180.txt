I am a novelist. In the course of researching a novel about the restoration of ancient sculpture, I have fallen into a problem about material objects, and I have arrived at an argument whose steps each seem inescapable and whose conclusion I cannot accept. I should like to know which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

(1) On Monday a sculptor possesses a lump of clay; call it Lump. Lump exists on Monday.
(2) On Tuesday she shapes the clay into a statue; call it Statue. Statue does not exist on Monday, since no statue is there until the clay is worked into the right form.
(3) So Lump and Statue differ: Lump existed on Monday, Statue did not.
(4) If Lump and Statue were identical, they would share all their properties; hence they are not identical.
(5) Yet on Tuesday Lump and Statue are composed of exactly the same clay, occupy exactly the same place, and are indistinguishable by any measurement; in short, they wholly coincide.
(6) If two distinct material objects may wholly coincide, it is hard to see why the same clay does not also constitute indefinitely many further objects — a portion of matter, an aggregate of molecules, a mereological sum of its parts — each distinct from the rest and all occupying one place at one time.
(7) That cannot be right: there is one object on the workbench, not indefinitely many, and the principle that two material objects cannot occupy the same place at the same time would be false.

Which premise or step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am most reluctant to give up the indiscernibility of identicals in (4), but I cannot accept (7), and I do not know which of the remaining steps is the weak point.
