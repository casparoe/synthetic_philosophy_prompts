I'm a postdoc in logic, and in three weeks I'm running a single two-hour session to introduce intuitionistic logic to a dozen mathematics PhD students. They are fluent in classical logic, have never worked constructively, and most of them assume constructivism is either a historical curiosity or classical mathematics with arbitrary restrictions. Don't explain intuitionistic logic to me; plan how I should teach it.

By the end of the session they should be able to state the BHK interpretation in their own words, see why it does not license excluded middle or double-negation elimination, and distinguish proof of a negation from proof by contradiction. What has to be in place before the BHK clauses can do any work for them? Give me the sequence of moves and, for each, say what it depends on and what it buys; I want the dependency structure, not just a list.

What single example should carry the session, and why that one rather than the usual suspects (say, the classical proof that there exist irrationals a and b with a^b rational, or a standard failed appeal to excluded middle)? What must the example have, and what must it not have, if it is to carry the conceptual distinction between a construction and a mere guarantee of existence?

Which misconceptions will they arrive with or form partway through, and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off? I'd expect at least these: that intuitionists assert that excluded middle is false rather than declining to assert it; that "constructive" just means "no proof by contradiction," when proving a negation by assuming the proposition in question and deriving a contradiction is a constructive rule, and what is rejected is concluding a positive proposition from the contradiction derived from its negation; that intuitionistic mathematics is just classical mathematics with theorems deleted; and that the BHK interpretation is a psychological account of certainty rather than a specification of what counts as evidence for a proposition. Are there others I should plan for, and where in the sequence does each belong?

What should I explicitly leave out at this level (for example, Kripke semantics, realizability, or the embedding of intuitionistic logic into classical modal logic), and why would including it do more harm than good?

Finally, give me one question or small task for the last ten minutes whose answer would show me whether they have grasped the difference between having a construction and having a classical existence proof, rather than merely having learned the rules. I want a plan for the explanation, not a survey and not a reading list.
