I'm retired now, after a career in physical chemistry — kinetics, mostly — and Ive been reading philosophy properly for the first time. Lately it's Foucault's Discipline and Punish, and the thesis that's got its hooks in me goes roughly like this. From the late eighteenth century, the dominant form of power in western societies stops being sovereign command backed by spectacular public punishment and becomes discipline: continuous hierarchical observation, measuring people against norms and correcting deviations, and above all the examination, which fuses the two and turns each person into a documented case. Knowledge isn't separate from power on this story; the more you observe and sort people, the more you can manage them. Prisons aren't an aberration — they're the template for schools, hospitals, barracks, factories.

I'm not wedded to it, but it explains a lot and I want to know how exposed it actually is. So: what would count against it? Not just what the usual critics say — if this picture of modern power were false or badly overblown, where would it show up first? Which historical findings, institutional cases, or arguments should actually move me, and which would be a real problem for the thesis rather than just a complication? What would people working in that tradition admit causes trouble for it, and what would they dismiss as a misreading? And be straight with me: has the view been hedged — power is dispersed, it's an analytics rather than a theory, it describes rather than condemns, resistance is internal to power — to the point that it can't be wrong? If so, is that a flaw in the view, or am I making a category mistake by treating it like a hypothesis from my old line of work? I'd especially value defeaters a working scientist would respect: comparative institutional history, mechanisms, counterexamples — not just "he ignores X."
