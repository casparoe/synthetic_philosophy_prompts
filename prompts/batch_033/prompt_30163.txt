I've ended up defending a position I didn't expect too: that the handicap principle — the idea that signals are honest because they're costly, and that the cost is what keeps low-quality signalers from faking — is basically the right framework for animal communication, and that most of the alternatives are either special cases of it or describe something else (indices, cues, Fisherian runaway, sensory bias). I keep running into people who say the whole thing is unfalsifiable, that "cost" gets stretched to mean whatever it needs to mean, and that the cleanest cases of honest signaling are precisely the ones where cost seems to do no work at all (a frog's call pitch tracking it's body size, say). I want to know whether they have a point, or whether I should just bite the bullet.

So here's the ask. Treat the handicap principle as a live empirical and theoretical claim, not a slogan. What would count against it? I want the considerations ranked from fatal to merely awkward: specific empirical findings, formal or game-theoretic results, and conceptual distinctions that should move me. Be specific about which of these a committed defender of the handicap view would themselves concede as counting against it (not just what critics say), and which they'd dismiss as a misreading or a change of subject. Also be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — e.g., by defining "cost" so broadly that any honest signal can be redescribed as costly after the fact — and whether that's actually a problem here, or just how evolutionary explanations normally work.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the ranking you give, or where your reasoning in the answer is most likely to fail. I'd rather get a map of your uncertainty than a confident ranking that hides the load-bearing assumptions.
