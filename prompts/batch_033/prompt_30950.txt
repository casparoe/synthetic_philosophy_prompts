I have spent more time than I care to admit reading the usual debates about whether any amount of third-person information could settle a first-person question, and I have come to think that the most honest expression of what a field actually believes is what its practitioners do. Consider anesthesia. The specialty runs on the premise that a patient who meets certain behavioral and electroencephalographic criteria — no response to commands, no movement to surgical incision, a processed EEG index in the accepted range — is unconscious rather than merely paralyzed and amnestic. Depth-of-anesthesia monitors output a single number meant to track whether the patient is having experiences, and clinicians act on it: if the number is in range and the patient is unresponsive, they proceed on the assumption that there is no experiential state to worry about at that moment. In the intensive care unit, much the same logic applies: a patient's level of consciousness is scored on a behavioral scale, and the score determines whether staff speak to the patient, whether suffering is treated as possible, and in some jurisdictions whether life-sustaining treatment may be withdrawn.

The puzzle is that the same medical world has already admitted that behavioral criteria are fallible. Neuroimaging has shown that a small minority of patients diagnosed as being in a vegetative state can modulate their brain activity in response to commands, which many researchers take to indicate that some behaviorally unresponsive patients are in fact conscious. So clinical practice is quietly behaviorist in one setting and self-consciously unsure in another. My question is: what theory of consciousness does this practice actually presuppose? Does it commit medicine to a functionalist or behavioral account of consciousness, or is it compatible with the hard problem being real and with the possibility that unresponsiveness under anesthesia never guarantees the absence of experience? I would like a reconstruction of the implicit theory, a clear statement of what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely assumes for pragmatic reasons, and an account of what would have to change — in anesthesiology, in ICU scoring, in the law around withdrawal of treatment — if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which first-person facts are only loosely coupled to the markers clinicians can measure.
