I've been trying to get my head around something called the wrong kind of reasons problem. As I understand it: if a demon threatens to punish you unless you admire him, you have a reason to admire him, but it seems like the wrong kind of reason — it doesn't make him admirable. The standard move is to distinguish object-given reasons (features of the thing itself) from state-given reasons (benefits of being in the state of admiring it) and say only the former are the right kind.

I'd like a thought experiment built to a spec. Design a case where this object-given/state-given distinction misfires: it classifies something as a state-given reason that intuitively is the right kind of reason for the attitude, or vice versa. The case must involve deciding what to intend or what to prefer, not what to believe. Constraints: no demons, no eccentric billionaires, no sci-fi. The scenario should be an ordinary professional or technical decision — the sort of thing an engineer or a project lead might actually face. One paragraph.

After the scenario, walk through what the distinction says, why the verdict is wrong, and which structural feature of the case causes the misfire. Then tell me whether the distinction can be patched to handle this case without breaking its handling of the standard demon cases, or whether fixing it here opens a hole elsewhere.
