I was reading a popular book on rationality the other day and it summarized van Fraassen's Reflection principle like this: 'Reflection says a rational person should treat their future self as an expert. If you think that tomorrow you'll be 90% confident it will rain, then you should be 90% confident it will rain today. The principle is proven by a Dutch book argument, so violating it is a genuine incoherence, not just a stylistic flaw. It's also why a rational person can't expect their own opinions to drift: if you already know you'll change your mind without new evidence, then you're admitting your current opinion is wrong right now.' Ive seen people online say Reflection has known counterexamples involving forgetting or drugs, and that the whole 'future self as expert' thing is just a heuristic. But the book made it sound airtight. Can you go through that summary and tell me which parts a formal epistemologist would accept, which are overstatements or conflations, and whether the conclusion about not expecting your opinions to drift actually follows from the parts that are accurate?
