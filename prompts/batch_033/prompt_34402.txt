I run a small research team at a policy think tank, and we produce consensus forecasts — GDP growth, inflation, election probabilities, conflict risk, that kind of thing. Our rule is that when two analysts disagree on a number, they have to sit down and hash it out before we publish anything. The problem is we don't actually have a procedure for that conversation. It usually just ends with whoever argues louder or has more seniority, or they split the difference in a way that makes nobody happy and probably makes the number worse.

I've been reading about Aumann's agreement theorem, and it seems like it should tell us something about how to structure these conversations — the idea that two rational people with the same priors and common knowledge of each other's beliefs can't agree to disagree. I want to turn that into an actual protocol my analysts can follow. Not the math, not the proof — a step-by-step procedure for the conversation itself.

The constraints: it needs to be something two analysts can run in an hour or two without a facilitator. It should force them to surface the real source of the disagreement, not just trade conclusions. It should say what to do when they still disagree at the end — because that happens, and the theorem's assumptions sure aren't gonna hold perfectly in a conference room. And it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Write the procedure, with clear steps and decision points. Then walk it through two worked examples: one where two analysts disagree on a GDP growth forecast for next quarter, and one where they disagree on the probability that a specific piece of legislation passes. Flag the steps where the theorem's assumptions are clearly violated in practice and what you're supposed to do about it. Also tell me what the procedure *can't* handle — the kinds of disagreements where it'll just break.

Oh, and write the answer in American English.
