So I spent my career in visual neuroscience — retinal circuitry, mostly — and now that I'm retired I've been reading a lot of philosophy of perception. I keep coming back to Molyneux's problem because it's one of those rare questions where philosophy and empirical work actually talk to each other. For a discussion group I'm in (mixed bunch, some philosophers, some scientists), I want to write a short dialogue between two characters who disagree about whether a person born blind who suddenly gains sight could visually tell a cube from a sphere without touching them.

Here's what I want: one character defends the "no" answer, the other defends "yes." But neither should be a strawman — both need to be genuinely smart and well-informed. And crucially, I want them to engage with the modern empirical results, especially the Held et al. (2011) study with Project Prakash patients, where newly sighted kids couldnt immediately match seen to felt but picked it up fast. I dont want a dialogue that just recites Locke and Leibniz at each other.

Also, the dialogue should get at the deeper puzzle: even after the empirics are in, theres still a philosophical question about whether the spatial content of touch and vision are the same kind of thing, or whether "shape" means something different across modalities such that Molyneuxs question assumes a common currancy that might not exist.

Youre a professional philosopher who works on perception. Before writing the final dialogue, think through the strongest arguments each side can make when theyre fully informed about the modern science — what should the "yes" person say that isnt just "spatial concepts are innate," and what should the "no" person say that isnt just "look at the Held data"? Then write the dialogue as a real back-and-forth where each character responds to what the other actually said, and where the exchange pushes past the superficial disagreement into the harder conceptual issue.
