So two friends of mine got into it the other night about whether to donate to an AI safety organization. The argument went something like this:

(1) Mike said he'd rather give to a charity that distributes malaria bed nets, because you basically know what you're getting: a certain number of lives saved, pretty high confidence. He said the AI stuff people are into — trying to prevent a tiny risk of human extinction — is nuts, because once you say "this has a 0.001% chance of saving all future humans," the expected value is astronomical and you'd have to give everything to it, which is obviously crazy.

(2) Sarah said that's just the logic of expected value. If you really think there's even a tiny chance of preventing extinction, the math says that dominates everything else. She said if he's willing to use expected value reasoning for bed nets, he can't just drop it when the numbers get big.

(3) Mike came back with: but you can do this for anything. I could tell you that burying a statue of a ferret in my backyard has a 10^-30 chance of summoning a benevolent god who will grant everyone eternal bliss. The expected value is infinite, but you'd still think I was insane to skip a friend's wedding to bury ferret statues. So expected value calculations clearly have limits — we just ignore sufficiently tiny probabilities.

(4) Sarah said the difference is that the ferret statue thing isn't grounded in any actual evidence or model — it's a made-up number attached to a made-up story. When people work on AI risk, they're reasoning from things we actually know about technology trends, alignment difficulties, etc. So you can't lump them together.

(5) Mike said: sure, but the point is structural. Even with a real empirical basis, once your probabilities get small enough, your estimate is going to be dominated by model uncertainty, not the thing itself. At 10^-6 you're already mostly measuring your own error bars. So doing expected value math with those numbers is false precision.

(6) Sarah said that doesn't mean you should ignore the risk — it means you should be humble about the estimate. Treating a 10^-6 risk of extinction as literally zero is a bigger mistake than treating it as 10^-6, because zero is a precise number too, and it's almost certainly wrong.
