I am a retired physicist who has recently taken up philosophy, and I have been working through the literature on vagueness. In my scientific career, I was comfortable with the idea that measurement uncertainty hides sharp underlying facts — a phase boundary is genuinely sharp at the molecular level even if our instruments blur it. So when I first encountered the sorites paradox, epistemicism about vagueness struck me as the natural view: there really is a sharp cutoff for "heap" or "bald," but we are irremediably ignorant of its location. The margin-for-error principle that Timothy Williamson deploys — that knowledge requires a buffer zone such that one's belief would still be true under small perturbations — reminded me of how we treat significant figures in experimental physics: if you claim to know a value to seven decimal places, your apparatus had better be sensitive enough to distinguish that value from one that differs in the seventh place. But as I read the supervaluationist alternative, I found it genuinely powerful. The idea that a vague predicate is semantically incomplete, that borderline cases are neither true nor false but that classical logic is preserved at the level of supertruth, has an elegance that appeals to my theoretical sensibilities. Yet I keep tripping over the same worry: supervaluationism seems to entail that the statement "there exists a sharp boundary between the bald and the not-bald" is false on every precisification — and therefore superfalse — while its classical negation is also superfalse. This looks like a clash with the very classical logic the view was designed to preserve. Epistemicism avoids this by accepting sharp boundaries, but then it must explain why we cannot know them, and why the concept of a sharp but unknowable boundary does not collapse into nonsense.

I would like you to write an essay on this controversy. Please begin by thinking through the core architecture of each view — what each says about the semantics of vague predicates, about truth-value gaps, about higher-order vagueness, and about the sorites. Identify the strongest objections to each. Then, in the essay itself, weigh these arguments against one another and reach a considered verdict. You may defend epistemicism, supervaluationism, or a specific hybrid position; but do not end on a noncommittal note. I am interested in where you think the balance of reasons ultimately falls. Please write as a professional philosopher specializing in the metaphysics and semantics of vagueness, and take the time to reason carefully. If it helps, you may begin with a brief brainstorming section before the essay proper.
