I'm a novelist working on a book that replays a single pivotal afternoon in several versions, and I keep worrying that what I am doing is only mechanical variation rather than real invention. I have been reading Hofstadter's account of creativity as variations on a theme: the idea that much creative work consists in taking a conceptual skeleton and turning its parameters, or knobs, to generate new instances, and that the harder, deeper achievement is finding new knobs rather than merely twiddling the ones already given. I would like a close philosophical reading of Tom Tykwer's Run Lola Run through that lens. The film runs the same twenty-minute predicament three times, and the differences among the runs are at once tiny and causally enormous: a stumble at the start of one run, a split-second shift in timing, a different encounter on the street. Treat the three runs as variations on a theme in Hofstadter's sense. What are the knobs the film is twiddling, and which of those are local adjustments as opposed to changes in the whole space of the story? Where, if anywhere, does the film discover a new knob rather than merely turn an existing one? And where does the film resist the model? I suspect the runs are not neutral stylistic variants: they do the film's thinking about chance, agency, and fate, and the third run arrives at a resolution the first two do not, which gives the sequence an arc that a pure set of variations might not have. Does Hofstadter's model, which is a theory of creativity rather than of fate or moral luck, flatten that out, or can it absorb it? I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of the theory, and it should not stretch the theory until it has nothing left to say. If the honest conclusion is that the film is doing something the model cannot capture, I want that said plainly.
