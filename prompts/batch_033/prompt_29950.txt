Okay so I'm teaching this upper-level undergrad seminar on philosophy of perception — about 15 students, mostly philosophy majors but a few psych people, 75-minute sessions. Last night I got into this huge argument with my friend (who's in cog sci) about cognitive penetration, like whether what you believe or expect can literally change what you *see*, not just how you interpret it afterwards. He kept saying it's obviously real — he brought up the banana-looking-yellower thing and the hills-looking-steeper-when-you're-wearing-a-heavy-backpack studies — and I kept pushing back that all of that could just be judgment or response bias, not actual changes to perceptual experience itself. We went back and forth for like two hours and neither of us budged, and honestly by the end I was less sure of my own position than when we started.

It got me thinking that my students probably have the same confusion, and reading the papers only gets them so far — they can parrot the modularity arguments but they don't *feel* what's at stake. I want to design an in-class activity, something where they actually run into the phenomenon (or something that seems like it) in real time, and then we can use that experience to pull apart what the debate is really about. I don't want to just do the duck-rabbit or the dalmatian picture, that's too easy and they've seen those a million times. I want something more structured, where the setup itself forces them to confront the question of whether their cognition is changing their perception or just their post-perceptual reports. Ideally the activity takes about 30–40 minutes with a solid debrief after.

What I'm imagining is maybe a between-groups thing where different halves of the room get different priming or different expectations planted, and then we all look at the same ambiguous or noisy stimuli and compare reports. But I'm open to other formats — role-play, a game, whatever works. The key is that by the end they should feel the pull of *both* sides: they should see why someone would think perception is penetrated by cognition, but also why it's so damn hard to prove that's what's really going on and not just a post-perceptual effect. Also, be honest with me: what will go wrong when I run this? Like what will students say that derails the whole point, and how do I handle it in the moment? And which part of the philosophical debate does the activity just not capture no matter how well it goes?
