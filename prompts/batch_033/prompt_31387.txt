I direct a portfolio of software products at a mid-sized firm. Four times a year we run stage-gate reviews at which each active project is either continued, paused, or killed. The decisions are high-stakes (project budgets run to seven figures), they are made by an investment committee of non-specialists, and I have about half an hour per project to prepare a recommendation. I keep running into the same argument. One camp says the money and months already spent are sunk costs and should count for nothing; the other says that is naive, because in a dollar auction it can be rational to keep bidding once you have already bid heavily, since quitting locks in a loss whereas one more increment might win. I have read enough rationalist material to know that both sides are partly right: the correct default is to ignore sunk costs, but the dollar auction shows there are real cases where continuing is rational even though the project as a whole will never be worth what has been spent on it.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run at these reviews. It should be a checklist or ordered set of questions, with, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and what the answer implies for the decision. It should distinguish a genuine dollar-auction structure (a contest in which quitting concedes something to a rival and the next increment can change who wins) from ordinary unilateral escalation, and it should say how to handle the grey cases: recoverable value, reputational and learning payoffs, the difference between a bid that improves your position and a bid that merely postpones admitting a mistake, and whether a precommitment cap set before the contest began should bind the review. It should also say where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a project that is behind schedule and over budget with a competitor close to shipping a similar product; (2) a research prototype that has consumed its budget and needs more money to reach a demo, with no competitor in sight; and (3) a project that is technically healthy but whose business case has collapsed because a key customer left. Make sure at least one of these comes out with an answer my committee will dislike. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — the failure modes it is blind to. Please write in British English. Your final line should be a one-sentence summary of the procedure, beginning 'Summary:'.
