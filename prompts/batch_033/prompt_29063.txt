I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been reading about the inefficacy objection in the context of climate change. The argument, as I understand it, runs roughly like this: any individual action — driving less, giving up meat, forgoing a flight — makes no measurable difference to climate outcomes, because one person's emissions are a negligible fraction of the global total. So, the argument concludes, there is no moral obligation to take those actions; the burden falls entirely on systemic or governmental responses.

I've been wondering what John Stuart Mill would have made of this. He famously defended the harm principle — the idea that individual liberty can only be restricted to prevent harm to others — but he also wrote about cases where individually harmless actions aggregate into collectively serious damage. He could not have anticipated climate change as a specific problem, but the structure of the issue — individually negligible contributions to a collectively devastating outcome — seems to cut across central commitments in his work.

What would Mill's position on the inefficacy objection most likely be, given his published views? I'm particularly interested in whether the harm principle, taken strictly, would rule out moral obligations to reduce individual emissions, or whether there is something in his broader utilitarian commitments that would push the other way. If he would reject the inefficacy objection, on what grounds would he do so, and what would that imply for the kinds of individual actions he would consider obligatory?
