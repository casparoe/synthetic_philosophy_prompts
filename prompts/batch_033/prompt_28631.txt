I cover philosophy of mind and the cognitive sciences for a general-interest magazine, and over the past several years I have found myself convinced by disjunctivism about perceptual experience. In the form I accept: veridical perception is fundamentally a relation of acquaintance with mind-independent objects and their features; hallucination, however subjectively indiscriminable from the veridical case, is not the same kind of mental state; and the argument from illusion owes its force to the very assumption under dispute, namely that seeing and merely seeming to see must share a common experiential core.

I want a map of what would count against this position, not a restatement of the standard debate. Tell me what would have to be true, or what would have to be shown, for the view to be false or to collapse into a common-factor rival. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them a committed disjunctivist would concede as counting against the view rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding. For each candidate defeater, say whether it is live or merely logically possible. I would also like to know what the view predicts about introspection, about the bad case, and about the role of the perceived object in explaining the character of the experience, that a common-factor view does not predict, and which of those predictions are actually testable rather than merely illustrative.

Press hardest on the places where I suspect the position is most exposed. Does the positive account of hallucination — saying what the bad case is, if it is not the same kind of state as perception — leave the view hostage to a merely negative or epistemic characterisation that cannot bear the weight put on it? Does the subjective indistinguishability of the good and bad cases remain an unexplained coincidence on the view, and how much of a cost is that? How seriously should I take causal or neural commonalities between veridical perception and hallucination: if the same proximal neural state could be produced either by an object or by direct stimulation, would that vindicate the common-kind assumption, or merely show that different kinds of state can share a proximal cause? I would personally be most unsettled by a convincing demonstration that the view cannot say what hallucination is without quietly reintroducing a common factor, and least unsettled by bare appeals to neural overlap.

Finally, tell me whether the view, as it is usually defended, has been qualified in ways that insulate it from refutation — for example, by making the disjunctiveness purely epistemological, or by retreating from claims about the nature of experience to claims about reasons — and whether that insulation is a vice here or a sign that the position was never an empirical one. I would rather be told that my position is unfalsifiable than be left with the impression that it is safe.
