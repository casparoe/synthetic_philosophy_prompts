In our philosophy of mind course we have been reading Hofstadter's account of the self as a high-level pattern rather than a fixed substance, and his claim that something of a person can persist after their death in the minds of those who knew them, because those minds carry a rough copy of the pattern. In lecture the professor defended this with a software/hardware analogy: the self is like a program, the brain is like the machine it runs on, and just as a program is not destroyed when one computer is shut down and can be reinstalled elsewhere, your self can be partially reimplemented in other brains and in that sense survive. I would like an audit of how far this analogy holds. Please lay out the correspondences explicitly (program, hardware, data, runtime, copy, execution), then identify where the mapping breaks down, and in particular which features of software the analogy quietly imports — substrate independence, exact copying, functional equivalence, the absence of a first-person point of view — that a self may not have. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that an 'I' can be distributed across brains and persist in others survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the software analogy was carrying the argument; and if there is a better analogy, say what it would be.
