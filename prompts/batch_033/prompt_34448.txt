You are a professor of aesthetics and the philosophy of art. Please think carefully and work through the following issue step by step.

The paradox of fiction and the paradox of tragedy (or more broadly, the paradox of negative emotions in art) are usually treated as separate problems, but they put pressure on each other. The paradox of fiction asks: how can we have genuine emotional responses to characters and events we know don't exist? The paradox of negative emotions asks: why do we deliberately seek out artworks that make us feel fear, sadness, or pity — emotions we normally avoid in real life?

I want you to explain both paradoxes, and then focus on the interaction between them. In particular:

1. Start by laying out the paradox of fiction clearly: the three inconsistent claims, and the main families of solutions — the illusion theory, the pretend theory, the thought theory, and any others you think are important enough to include.

2. Then lay out the paradox of tragedy and horror: why it is puzzling that we seek out negative emotional experiences from fiction, and the main solutions — Aristotelian catharsis, the conversion or meta-response view, the rich-experience view, and so on.

3. Now the crux: for each major solution to the paradox of fiction, explain whether it can also handle the paradox of negative emotions, or whether it makes that second paradox harder to resolve. For example, if you adopt the pretend theory (we don't really feel fear at horror movies — we are engaged in make-believe), does that dissolve the paradox of negative emotions too easily, or does it fail to explain why we find the make-believe fear enjoyable? If you adopt the thought theory (emotions can be triggered by unasserted thoughts, not just beliefs), does it explain why fictional sadness is pleasant but real sadness isn't? Trace these interactions carefully.

4. Conclude with your assessment: is there a single unified solution that handles both paradoxes well, or do the two paradoxes pull in different directions, suggesting that our engagement with fiction is more fragmented than any one theory can capture?

I'm less interested in a survey of the literature than in a clear, argumentative analysis that traces the logical relationships between the positions. Where you think a view is incoherent or unstable, say so and explain why.
