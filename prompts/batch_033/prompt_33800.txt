I'm organizing a workshop on the nonidentity problem, and I want to produce a document that captures the state of the debate between two camps that have been talking past each other for decades. The two camps are:

**Camp A — The Harm-Based View.** An act can be wrong only if it makes some particular person worse off than they would otherwise have been. The nonidentity problem is not a decisive counterexample to this view: either the putatively wrong acts in nonidentity cases aren't actually wrong, or the harm can be identified after all once we get the counterfactual right (e.g., the child is harmed because we can compare their life to the one they would have had if the choice had been made differently).

**Camp B — The Impersonal View.** The nonidentity problem shows that the harm-based view is false. An act can be wrong even if it doesn't make any particular person worse off than they would otherwise have been, because wrongness sometimes depends on the quality of the outcome or the state of the world, not on comparative harm to identifiable individuals.

Draft the joint statement that a committed representative of Camp A and a committed representative of Camp B could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The statement should include:

1. **Agreed claims** — a numbered list of claims about the nonidentity problem, about person-affecting and impersonal principles, and about the relevant cases that both sides can endorse. These should be substantive, not just "we agree that Parfit discussed this." Find formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted toward one side.

2. **Disagreements table** — a table with columns: (a) the precise claim in dispute, phrased neutrally; (b) Camp A's position on it, phrased so that a Camp A theorist would accept the wording; (c) Camp B's position on it, phrased so that a Camp B theorist would accept the wording. Include at least four rows. The disputes should be the ones that remain after the agreed claims are set down, not the caricatured versions each side attributes to the other.

3. **Cruxes** — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side toward the other. For each crux, identify a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, and record what each side predicts about it in advance.

4. **Diagnosis** — if any part of the disagreement is merely verbal, identify which part and explain why. If the disagreement is genuinely substantive throughout, say so and explain what makes it substantive.

Throughout, be clear about what each side is committed to and what it is not, and avoid straw-manning either position. And please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
