I've been reading through the old debates about dutch book arguments for probabilism — you know, the idea that if your credences violate the probability axioms, there's a set of bets you'd individually accept that together guarantee you a loss. This argument has been around since Ramsey and de Finetti in the 1930s, and somehow the philosophical debate about what it actually shows is still going. I've been trying to figure out why it hasn't settled, and I keep going in circles.

The way I see it, there are roughly three camps at this point. Camp A says the Dutch book argument is a genuine justification for probabilism: it shows that violating the axioms makes you practically irrational, and since credences are guides to action, that's the right kind of irrationality to care about. Camp B says the Dutch book argument is just a dramatic illustration of an underlying coherence condition — it doesn't justify probabilism, it just makes vivid what happens when you're incoherent, and the real justification has to come from somewhere else, like representation theorems or accuracy arguments. Camp C says the whole framing is wrong and we should have moved on to purely epistemic arguments like Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument, which shows that incoherent credences are dominated for accuracy without any appeal to betting. But then Camp A and B both point out that the accuracy arguments have their own problems — they depend on choosing a proper scoring rule, and there's a whole literature on whether that choice can be justified non-arbitrarily. And around we go.

I've been in a reading group that's been on this for like two months and nobody has moved an inch. What I want to know is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive disagreement about what rationality requires, a verbal dispute about the word "justification," or are the three camps answering different questions — one about pragmatic rationality, one about epistemic rationality, one about formal representation — under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new theorem, a sharper distinction, or just admitting that different camps want different things from a "foundation for probability"? I have a suspicion that the whole thing turns on whether you think the point of credences is to guide action or to track truth, and that nobody's arguing about that background commitment directly, but I'd like to hear whether that's right or whether I'm missing something structural about the debate. If this whole way of carving things up is confused, say so — I'd rather get the framing right than get an answer to a badly posed question.
