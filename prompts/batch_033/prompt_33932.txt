I'm taking a philosophy of death course this term, and we've spent several weeks on the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies. The core moves are familiar by now: Epicurus argues that since all good and bad consist in sensation, and death is the cessation of sensation, death is nothing to us — "when death is, I am not." Lucretius adds the symmetry argument: the eternity of nonexistence before our birth doesn't trouble us, so the eternity after our death shouldn't either. The modern revival of the anti-Epicurean side, starting with Nagel, counters that death can be bad as a deprivation — it removes goods you would otherwise have had — and that this doesn't require the subject to experience the loss at the time it occurs. Subsequent work by Feldman, McMahan, and others refines the deprivation account and tries to pin down exactly what makes a death bad: the timing, the counterfactual goods, the structure of the life as a whole.

What puzzles me is that after fifty years of sustained exchange, the basic standoff hasn't budged. The Epicurean insists that nothing can be bad for a person unless there is a person for whom it is bad at the time it is bad, and that posthumous deprivation fails this condition. The deprivation theorist insists that this condition is simply not required — that we make counterfactual welfare judgments all the time (someone would have been better off had such-and-such happened), and that death is the limiting case. Each side seems to grant the other's central intuition as a datum and then deny that it settles the question. The literature has become extremely sophisticated, but the dialectical situation feels like a stalemate: both positions are internally consistent, both capture something we pre-theoretically want to say, and neither can force the other to accept its starting point.

I've begun to wonder whether the two sides are actually answering different questions under a single label. The Epicurean seems to be asking about whether death is an occurrent harm — something that happens to you — while the deprivation theorist seems to be asking about whether death makes a life worse considered as a whole. If that's right, the disagreement might be partly verbal, or at least partly about which notion of "bad for" is the one that matters. But I'm not confident in this diagnosis. It could instead be a substantive metaphysical disagreement about the nature of harm, or about whether nonexistence can enter into relations at all, or about the relevance of experiential constraints on welfare. Or it could be a clash of bedrock intuitions where neither side has non-question-begging leverage on the other.

What kind of disagreement is this, at bottom? I'd like a diagnosis that takes seriously what each side actually argues, not a summary of the positions. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a sharper distinction, a result from the metaphysics of time or harm, a reframing of the question — or is this a debate we should expect to remain at an impasse indefinitely?
