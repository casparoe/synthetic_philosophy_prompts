So I just got back from a long arguement with a friend about whether the Brexit referendum was even a legitimate excercise of democratic will, and the whole thing kept circling back to who actually got to vote. I know the basic facts well enough: the franchise followed the UK general election franchise, so British citizens (including overseas ones who'd been registered in the UK within the last 15 years), plus resident Irish and Commonwealth citizens, got a vote, and Gibraltar voted too; but most EU citizens living in the UK couldn't vote, and 16-17 year olds couldn't either even though they'd had the vote in the Scottish independence referendum. My friend's line was that the people most affected — EU nationals who'd built their lives in Britain — had no say, while a British retiree in Spain who hadn't lived in the UK for a decade could. I want to work through this properly with the boundary problem / all-affected interests stuff from democratic theory, not just vibes. Treat the Brexit referendum franchise as a case study: what would the all-affected interests principle actually have said about who should be in the demos for that decision, and what does it need 'affected' to mean to say that? Which features of the actual franchise does it explain, which does it get wrong or stay silent on, and what does the case end up showing about the principle — is it usable here, or does it fall apart into the everyone-affects-everyone problem as soon as you try to draw a line? And be honest about where it just has nothing to say about the 16-17 year old question rather than forcing it to speak.
