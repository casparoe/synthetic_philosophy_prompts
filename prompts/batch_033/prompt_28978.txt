ok here's a dialogue prompt. I've been reading old LessWrong threads about the unilateralist's curse and I want to see two smart people actually grind through the details, not just gesture at 'be careful about unilateral action.'

Write a dialogue between two people, call them A and B, who disagree about how much normative work the unilateralist's curse can do.

A thinks the curse is a robust, general argument for a strong default against taking risky unilateral action whenever you're in a group of people who might also act and who are at least roughly as competent as you. A keeps coming back to the toy-model result: if each of N people has an independent probability p of mistakenly thinking the action is a good idea, then the chance that at least one person acts wrongly is 1 - (1-p)^N, which goes to 1 as N grows for any fixed p > 0. So A thinks something like a principle of conformity—don't act unless you'd still act after conditioning on the fact that most others have declined to—should be the default.

B grants the math but thinks the curse only bites under a narrow set of assumptions, and that in most real cases those assumptions fail: errors aren't independent (people share sources and arguments), competence isn't identically distributed (some people genuinely have private information or relevant expertise), the set of 'people who might act' is usually vauge, and the conformity principle can itself lock in bad status quos or create information cascades. B thinks the curse is a warning about a specific kind of situation, not a general argument for deference.

Make both characters genuinely good. A should not just restate the convergence result; A should have real replies to B's points, for example about whether correlated errors actually undermine the argument or just move the problem up a level, and about how to distinguish genuine private information from the overconfidence the curse is about. B should grant the math and focus on exactly where the toy model stops mapping onto real cases. They should respond to each other's actual moves, and ideally get into what 'the group' even is, whether a conformity principle just relocates the error to a different stage of deliberation, and whether universal deference creates its own failure mode when the action would have been good. Don't have either side obviously win; end with the disagreement sharper than at the start.

Write it in a blog-comment-y register, like a particularly good LessWrong discussion, not academic prose.
