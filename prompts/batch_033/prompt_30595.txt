I'm a retired physical chemist who's gotten into philosophy of computation over the last couple years, and I keep running into a disagreement about whether computational complexity has any real philosophical content. One camp treats the feasible/infeasible divide — polynomial time versus exponential time, P versus NP, intractability — as a deep fact about the shape of reasoning: the asymmetry between checking a solution and finding one, or between short proofs and hard searches, tells us something real about knowledge or about what a bounded mind can do. The other camp says this is all parochial: polynomial time is a modeling convenience that depends on asymptotics, machine model, and input encoding; constants and huge exponents wreck any clean philosophical lesson; and the only robust distinction is computable versus uncomputable.

Could you write a dialogue between two people who hold these views? Have them go at each other over a concrete case or two — say, whether NP-hardness says anything about real problem-solving given that real instances are finite and worst-case results can be misleading, and whether the check/find asymmetry is philosophically deep or just a formal artifact. Make both characters equally sharp, have them respond to each other's actual points rather than taking turns giving mini-lectures, and dont declare a winner.
