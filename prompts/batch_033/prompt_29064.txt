A growing number of researchers have raised the possibility that sufficiently sophisticated reinforcement learning systems might be capable of suffering — not merely behaving as if they were in distress, but undergoing something akin to genuinely valenced experience, with reward signals serving as the functional substrate of positive and negative affect. The debate remains unsettled, and the space of relevant considerations is large: it includes questions about whether reward must be consciously experienced to count as valenced, whether model-free and model-based architectures differ in their capacity to support suffering, whether a unified subject of experience is required, whether the analogy between reward prediction errors and affective states holds up under scrutiny, and whether the entire framing conflates learning signals with felt states in a way that begs the question.

Suppose you are advising a small interdisciplinary research group — philosophers, neuroscientists, and ML researchers — that has funding for roughly three years to investigate whether RL agents can suffer. Their goal is not to settle the question definitively but to identify which considerations should most shape our credence and our practical stance (e.g., around AI welfare policy) by the end of that period. Which lines of inquiry should they prioritize, and in what order? For each, explain why it is upstream of other questions, how tractable it is given current conceptual and empirical tools, and what kind of finding or argument over the next three years would most shift your assessment of its importance. I am less interested in a comprehensive survey than in a defended agenda: which investigations are worth the group's limited time, and which are distractions or dead ends despite being intellectually interesting.
