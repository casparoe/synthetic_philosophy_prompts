I've been going down a rabbithole reading about Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I'm trying to work out what it actually means for the limits of human knowledge. I first came across it in a Scott Aaronson blog post years ago but I never really sat with the implications until now.

The way I understand it, Fitch showed that if you assume every truth is knowable, you can derive the conclusion that every truth is actually known — which is obviously false. So by modus tollens, not every truth is knowable. There are truths that are, in principle, beyond our capacity to ever discover. This seems like a huge result! It's basically a formal proof of epistemic limits — the kind of thing that gets mysterians like Colin McGinn excited about cognitive closure. It seems to licence the claim that there are facts about the world (about consciousness, about the fundamental nature of reality, whatever) that are just strucutrally inaccessible to inquiry.

But when I tried to explain this to a friend who does philosophy of logic, he sort of shrugged and said the paradox doesn't really show what I think it shows. He said the unknowable truths in question are "trivial" or "just logical artefacts" and that treating them as substantive limits on knowledge is a category error. He wouldn't elaborate, and now I'm left wondering whether I've been drawing the wrong conclusions.

I don't get it. If the proof is valid, then there are unknowable truths, full stop. What does it matter what form they take? An unknowable truth is an unknowable truth — it's not like the proposition "p and it is not known that p" is somehow less true than other propositions. If such truths exist for any domain, then that domain has unknowable truths. So why wouldn't this apply to, say, propositions about consciousness or fundamental physics?

I'm hoping you can help me untangle this. Please approach this as an expert on epistemic logic and philosophical methodology. Before you give me your answer, I'd like you to spend some time brainstorming different ways one might interpret what Fitch's paradox actually demonstrates — what are the different possible takeaways, and which ones are live options? Then tell me whether my friend is right that the paradox doesn't licence the kind of mysterian conclusions I'm drawing, and if so, what exactly I'm getting wrong. Also, if you think the standard interpretation of the paradox is correct and there really are substantive unknowable truths, I'd like to hear the strongest version of that case.
