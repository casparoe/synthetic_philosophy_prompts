In a decision-theory seminar we worked through the Ellsberg two-urn problem: one urn holds a known 50/50 mix of red and black balls, the other holds red and black in unknown proportions, and you win by drawing the color you bet on. Nearly everyone preferred to bet on the known urn when betting red, and also preferred the known urn when betting black — a pair of preferences that no single subjective probability assignment over the ambiguous urn can jointly rationalize. When I asked whether this pattern was rational or just a recurring mistake, a classmate shrugged and said, 'Better the devil you know than the devil you don't.'

That proverb is being asked to do real work there — it is offered as a reason to treat the ambiguous urn as worse — but it is a folk saying, not a precise thesis, and I suspect it condenses several very different claims. I want to push on it.

Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims 'better the devil you know than the devil you don't' could be expressing when deployed in this context, from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a merely descriptive generalization about how people actually choose, a defensible normative principle, or outright false; and say whether that reading would actually support treating the Ellsberg pattern as rational rather than merely explaining why people fall into it. In particular, separate a reading like 'the unknown option should be presumed worse in expectation' from a reading like 'the known option is more robust when the unknown could be adversarial' — they sound similar but do different work, and I want to see exactly where they come apart. Also say which reading my classmate probably needs for the proverb to justify the choice, and whether that reading can carry the weight.

Finally, if I am asking the wrong question — if the proverb is too loose to bear normative weight here, or the Ellsberg pattern itself is less settled than I am treating it as — say so plainly instead of humoring me.
