I'm taking a graduate seminar on origin essentialism — roughly, the view that a thing could not have had an origin very different from the one it actually had. Over the term our professor has given snap verdicts on a series of cases, and I've been keeping a running list:

- This wooden table could not have been made from a different block of wood.
- This wooden table could have been made from the same block of wood by a different carpenter.
- I could not have developed from a different sperm fertilizing the same egg.
- I could not have developed from the same sperm fertilizing a different egg.
- I could not have been born to different parents.
- A particular oak tree could not have grown from a different acorn.

Next week the seminar takes up a case we haven't discussed. Suppose the very same sperm and the very same egg that actually produced me had instead been frozen when they were produced, and combined fifteen years later than they actually combined, with the child born nine months after that. Could the child born fifteen years late have been me? A classmate says it obviously could, since it's the same sperm and egg; I'm not sure the professor will see it that way.

You are a professional metaphysician who works on essence, origin, and identity. Please think through this carefully rather than just giving a yes or no. Begin by brainstorming the candidate rules that would make the professor's recorded verdicts come out right — I can already see at least two, and I suspect there is a third — and say exactly what each rule predicts about the frozen-gametes case. Then give your best prediction of the professor's verdict, explain which rule you think the professor is actually running and why, and state the strongest consideration in favor of the runner-up rule. Finally, propose one further case (real or fanciful) that would cleanly separate the remaining candidate rules, and say what the professor would have to say about it under each rule.
