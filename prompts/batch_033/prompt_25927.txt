I'm teaching Slaughterhouse-Five in an intro lit class and I keep getting tangeled up trying to explain the free will stuff to my students. The Tralfamadorians say all moments exist at once and nothing can be changed — "so it goes" — and Billy Pilgrim seems to absorb this as a kind of comfort. But then there's the whole war narrative where Billy does make choices, or at least the novel treats him as doing things, even if passively.

I want to bring in the compatibilism vs. incompatibilism debate seriously — not just wave at it. Like, if the Tralfamadorian metaphysics is true, is that straightforwardly incompatibilist (no free will because the future is fixed), or could a compatibilist say Billy still acts freely because he acts on his desires without coercion, even if those desires were always going to be what they were? And does the novel itself take a position, or does it leave the tension unresolved?

I'm especially interested in whether Vonnegut is doing something the philosophical debate misses. The novel seems to suggest that believing in free will is somehow tied up with a kind of moralizing that the war renders absurd — like, the insistence on blame and responsibility is what the Tralfamadorian view is meant to cure. Is there a compatibilist position that can make sense of that, or does the novel push toward something more revisionary about our ordinary concept of free will?

Also, if you know the scene where Billy watches the war movie backwards and everything is healed — that seems crucial. What's the philosophical work that image is doing? I feel like it's not just a determinism point, it's something about the relationship between free will and the stance we take toward suffering, but I can't quite articulate it and I'd love a sharper reading.
