I direct a small foundation's grantmaking program, and every quarter I sit on renewal decisions for multi-year grants. I have read enough rationalist writing on sunk costs — the Concorde fallacy, the escalation-of-commitment literature, the standard one-line rule — to know both the standard answer and its limits. The one-line rule is that past spending should not influence the decision and only forward-looking costs and benefits count. That rule is clearly right about something, but as a standing policy for an institution that funds risky work it is also clearly incomplete. I want the sophisticated version turned into something my team can actually run.

Write a decision procedure for whether to continue funding a project that has missed its milestones. These decisions involve several years of funding, people's livelihoods, and our credibility with other funders. The procedure should be a sequence of questions, and for each question it should specify: what counts as a sufficient answer, which part of the underlying theory the question operationalizes, and the most likely way a non-specialist will misapply it. It should say where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the residual question is one of values rather than facts, and what the procedure cannot settle. It must be runnable by a program officer without formal training in decision theory in under an hour, and its output should be a memo that can go to our board.

The procedure needs to be honest about the following. Some things that look like sunk-cost reasoning are forward-looking in disguise: past spending can be evidence about project quality or about our own calibration; a half-built intervention can make the next phase cheaper than starting over; abandoning a project can destroy relationship-specific capacity, break promises to partners, or demoralize a field we want to keep working in; and a reputation for pulling funding at the first bad result can make future grantees unwilling to propose the very kind of risky work we exist to fund. Other things are genuinely the fallacy but still real as psychological and organizational costs, and I need to know how the procedure treats those — which it counts, which it discounts, and which it labels irrelevant. And the naive rule has its own failure mode: applied mechanically, it can amount to a policy of never updating on the fact that we chose badly, and it can be dynamically inconsistent with the commitment we would have wanted to make before the bad news arrived.

Run the procedure on three cases: (a) a project whose midpoint results are weak in absolute terms but exactly what the project's theory predicted, so the results are not bad news about the project; (b) a project that has built specialized infrastructure and staff capacity that would be lost if we walked away; and (c) a project we continue only because the board member who championed it does not want to admit failure. At least one case should come out the opposite of what the naive sunk-cost rule would say, and at least one should come out the opposite of what we would prefer to hear. Flag any case the procedure cannot settle and say what additional fact or value judgment would settle it.
