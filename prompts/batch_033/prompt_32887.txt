The thing that hooked me was Hofstadter's letter 'a' problem. You can write an 'a' in ten thousand fonts, in the handwriting of a hundred different people, at different sizes, slanted, partially occluded, and you still see an 'a'. But no single feature is present in all of them—serifs come and go, the bowl can be closed or open, the terminal can be a hook or a straight line or a little ball, the counter can be nearly any shape. There's no checklist. And yet nobody has any trouble. To a working scientist this feels like it should be a tight empirical question: what's the mental representation that lets us do this? But when I read the literature, I find the same positions have been trading the same arguments since the seventies—prototype theory, exemplar theory, the classical view, theory-theory—and nobody seems to have won. The experiments are clever, but somehow they never settle the thing. It reminds me of the situation in my own field before someone figures out the right assay: everyone's measuring something real but nobody's measuring the thing.

Diagnose the stalemate in the category debate (using the letterform case as your running example if it helps). What kind of disagreement is it, really? Is it about the empirical facts, or about what would count as an explanation, or about something else entirely? And what would it take to actually move it? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion—just the five points.
