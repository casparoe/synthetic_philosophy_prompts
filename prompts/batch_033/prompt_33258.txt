I've been going back and forth on this for a while, and I'd love a reading that takes both the philosophy and the story seriously.

The story is the old Appointment in Samarra parable — the one Somerset Maugham retold. Here's the text:

"There was a merchant in Baghdad who sent his servant to market to buy provisions and in a little while the servant came back, white and trembling, and said, Master, just now when I was in the marketplace I was jostled by a woman in the crowd and when I turned I saw it was Death that jostled me. She looked at me and made a threatening gesture; now, lend me your horse, and I will ride away from this city and avoid my fate. I will go to Samarra and there Death will not find me. The merchant lent him his horse, and the servant mounted it, and he dug his spurs in its flanks and as fast as the horse could gallop he went. Then the merchant went down to the marketplace and he saw me standing in the crowd and he came to me and said, Why did you make a threatening gesture to my servant when you saw him this morning? That was not a threatening gesture, I said, it was only a start of surprise. I was astonished to see him in Baghdad, for I had an appointment with him tonight in Samarra."

The standard reading is that it's a fable about the inescapability of fate: you can't outrun death, and your very attempt to flee is what delivers you to it. Fair enough.

But here's what's nagging at me. There's a puzzle in decision theory called Death in Damascus. In that puzzle, you meet Death, who tells you he's coming for you tomorrow. You can either stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo. The catch is that Death is a perfect predictor of your decision: if you're the kind of person who would flee to Aleppo, Death has arranged to meet you there; if you're the kind who would stay, he's arranged to meet you in Damascus. The puzzle is about what decision theory tells you to do — causal decision theory says you can't affect where Death is so just pick a city, but evidential decision theory says your choice is evidence about where Death will be and you should stay (or flee, depending on how you set it up). The point is that the puzzle isn't straightforward fatalism — it's a weird loop where your decision and the prediction are entangled, and the whole thing can send some decision theories into infinite regress.

Now the Appointment in Samarra parable looks structurally identical to the Death in Damascus puzzle: servant learns Death is looking for him, servant flees, servant's flight brings him to Death. But the parable seems to invite a fatalistic reading, while the decision-theoretic puzzle reveals that the scenario is actually much stranger than simple fatalism.

So here's my question. Read the Appointment in Samarra parable through the lens of the Death in Damascus decision-theoretic puzzle. Does the parable actually support fatalism, or does a careful reading reveal something closer to the decision-theoretic tangle? Is Death in the parable a perfect predictor, or is something else going on — and does the fact that Death is *surprised* to see the servant in Baghdad matter for how we should think about the prediction? What does the parable, as a work of fiction, show us about the puzzle that the decision-theoretic literature misses — or vice versa? I'm prepping a paper that uses the parable as a way into the decision theory, and I want to make sure I'm not flattenning the story into a mere illustration.
