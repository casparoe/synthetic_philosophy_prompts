I'm co-authoring a paper on the foundations of decision theory, and our progress has stalled because my co-author and I cannot converge on the absent-minded driver problem. We have spent three meetings on it, and I suspect the impasse persists because neither of us has fully unpacked what our disagreement rests on. The problem, in brief: a driver plans to take the second exit on a highway. He knows he is absent-minded — whenever he reaches an exit, he cannot remember whether he has already passed one. Exiting at the first exit yields a payoff of 0, exiting at the second yields 4, and missing both yields 1. Before setting out, he calculates that the ex ante optimal strategy is to exit with probability 1/3 at whichever exit he finds himself at, for an expected payoff of 4/3. The puzzle is whether, upon actually arriving at an exit and facing the choice under self-locating uncertainty, he should follow through on that plan, or whether action-stage reasoning licenses — or even requires — a different exit probability. Some argue that conditional on being at an exit, the expected payoff of exiting exceeds that of continuing, which would push toward exiting with probability greater than 1/3, perhaps as high as 1.

Please give me your credence that the ex ante optimal strategy (exit with p = 1/3) is the strategy a rational agent should actually execute at the action stage. By "should" I mean: the strategy that a correct theory of rational choice under imperfect recall would prescribe, where the live dispute is between those who treat the ex ante plan as binding on the agent's later selves and those who hold that action-stage deliberation can overturn it.

I want the number, but more importantly I want the decomposition. Break the question into the sub-questions your credence depends on, assign a rough probability to each, show how they combine into your overall credence, and identify which sub-question your answer is most sensitive to — that is, which one, if you changed your mind about it, would shift your overall credence the most. I am less interested in the bare number than in seeing the structure of the reasoning laid out, because I am trying to locate where my co-author and I actually diverge.
