I keep coming back to the Meno problem — the question why knowledge is better than mere true belief — and I'm struck by how little the contemporary debate has moved. Both sides grant that a lucky guess is worth less, epistemically, than knowing, yet after decades neither camp has shifted. Reliabilists analyze knowledge as true belief produced by a reliable process, but their critics reply that reliability is valuable only instrumentally: once the true belief is in hand, the fact that it was reliably produced seems to add no further value. Virtue epistemologists answer that knowledge is true belief from intellectual ability — an achievement — and that achievements are valuable in a way merely lucky success is not; their opponents reply that this relocates the question, since the value of achievement now needs explaining, and it is disputed whether all knowledge is an achievement in the relevant sense. My working suspicion is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, but I want that tested rather than assumed. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about what makes something valuable, a verbal disagreement about the word 'knowledge,' two camps answering different questions — one about the nature of knowledge, one about its value — or a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Engage with what each side actually says, and then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a class of cases, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
