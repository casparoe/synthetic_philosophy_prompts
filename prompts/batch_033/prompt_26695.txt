So here's a thing I've been chewing on. Lots of institutions — universities, cities, even whole countries — have been going through this process of renaming buildings, taking down statues, stripping honors from historical figures who, by our lights, did or believed awful things. Not people who were seen as monsters in their own time, but people who were pretty normal for their era: slaveholders when slavery was legal, eugenicists when eugenics was mainstream science, colonial administrators when empire was just what great nations did.

I'm not asking whether this practice is good or bad. What I want to know is: what picture of moral progress does this practice presuppose? Because it seems to me it's committed to a bunch of things that might not all hang together. For one, it seems to presuppose that moral standards really do get better over time — not just different, but better — and that we're in a position to know that ours are better than theirs. That sounds like some version of the expanding circle story, where moral concern keeps widening to include more kinds of people (and maybe animals). But it also seems to presuppose that the historical figures in question should have known better, or at least could have known better — otherwise why hold them responsible? And those two presuppositions might be in tension: if moral progress is this slow, cumulative expansion of the circle, and most people in a given era can't see beyond its boundaries, then in what sense were they culpable for not seeing?

Also, the practice seems to treat the circle's expansion as something like discovering what was always true, rather than just changing our minds — because if it's just a change in fashion, then taking down statues starts to look like mere aesthetic preference. But if it's discovery, then we need some story about why the moral truth was accessible to us and not to them, which sounds like it requires a pretty specific epistemology of moral knowledge.

So I guess my question is: reconstruct the implicit theory of moral progress that the statue-removal / renaming practice presupposes. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Tell me where it's underdetermined — where you could run the same practice on different philosophical engines. And then tell me: if we swapped out the presupposed view of moral progress for a different one (say, a purely constructivist one where morals just shift with power and circumstance, with no direction), what would the practice have to look like instead, if it survived at all?

Oh and — please seperate what's actually settled among people who work on moral progress from what's genuinely contested. If there's a live debate about whether moral progress is best understood as circle-expansion or something else, flag that rather than presenting one side as the obvious truth.
