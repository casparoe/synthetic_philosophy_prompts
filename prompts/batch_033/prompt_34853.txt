I have been teaching myself about mechanistic interpretability, mostly from blog posts and by training small models of my own, and I have run into an argument that troubles me because it seems to prove too much. I cannot locate the flaw, and I would like a definite diagnosis.

Here is the argument as carefully as I can put it.

(1) A trained neural network just is a fixed mathematical function from inputs to outputs: a long computation with all its weights frozen.

(2) The very same function can be produced by indefinitely many different sets of weights and internal arrangements. Permuting neurons, rotating activation spaces, or using an entirely different architecture can leave the input–output behaviour exactly unchanged.

(3) Therefore, for any internal feature an interpretability method claims to find — a curve detector, a truthfulness direction, a neuron said to mean a concept — there is some alternative description of a system with identical behaviour in which that feature is simply not present.

(4) If a feature is absent under an equally good description of the system, then the feature is not really in the system; it is an artefact of the description we happened to choose.

(5) So no feature found by interpretability is genuinely in the network. The representations are something we project onto the model, not something the model has.

(6) But the same line of thought applies to any physical system whose workings can be redescribed without changing its behaviour — which is to say, to every physical system, brains included. So if the argument works, nothing represents anything, and interpretability is not merely difficult but hopeless in principle.

I am fairly sure the conclusion is too strong, because it would empty the idea of representation altogether, and because it leaves unexplained why interpretability sometimes lets people predict or steer a model's behaviour in specific ways. But I cannot say which step is doing the damage.

What I would like from you: take a definite position. Tell me which premise or step you reject, why it looked plausible before it was challenged, and what your diagnosis costs — that is, what a defensible notion of representation has to look like if it is to survive the argument. If you think the argument is in fact sound, say so outright and defend the conclusion; but if you reject it, name the step and show exactly how the argument fails once that step is corrected.

Please keep your answer under 500 words, and put your bottom line in the first sentence.
