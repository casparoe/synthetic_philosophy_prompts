My friend walked me through this argument at a party and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. It seems to show that anyone who takes morality seriously can't actually love anyone, which is obviously insane, but I can't find the mistake.

(1) If you take morality seriously, you believe that for any action you perform, there's some answer to the question "was that the right thing to do?" that doesn't just reduce to the fact that you wanted to do it.

(2) If you believe that, then when you do something deeply personal — like rushing into a burning building to save your spouse — you must be able to answer, at least to yourself, why saving her was the right thing to do in terms that don't just say "because she's my spouse."

(3) But if you can answer why saving her was the right thing to do without appealing to the fact that she's your spouse, then in your practical reasoning, "she's my spouse" isn't doing the fundamental justificatory work. Something else is — something impartial, like "she's a person in danger" or "I had a duty of care."

(4) If "she's my spouse" isn't doing the fundamental justificatory work in your practical reasoning, then you don't really treat the relationship as a basic reason. You treat it as a marker that helps you identify where impartial reasons happen to apply.

(5) If you treat your relationship as merely a marker for where impartial reasons happen to apply, then you don't love your spouse in the way we ordinarily understand love — you're using the relationship instrumentally in your moral reasoning.

(6) So taking morality seriously means you can't really love anyone.

(7) That's absurd. Obviously morally serious people can love.

(8) So either (1) is false — there's nothing wrong with "I did it because she's my spouse" as a complete moral justification — or something else is wrong with the argument.

My friend says this is basically Williams's one-thought-too-many objection tightened into a deductive form, and he thinks the right response is to bite the bullet and accept that (1) is false — that morality doesn't actually require you to be able to justify your actions in impartial terms. But that feels like giving up on morality. I think the problem is somewhere in (3) or (4), maybe a slide between "the justification doesn't mention the relationship" and "the relationship isn't doing any work," but I can't make it precise. Where does this argument actually go wrong, and what's the cost of fixing it?
