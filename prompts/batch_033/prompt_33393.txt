I have repeatedly noticed a pattern in the way people with very different moral views sometimes cooperate, and I would like to know whether it has an established name and, if so, how it is properly delimited.

Here is a representative instance. One person's overriding moral concern is preventing early death from easily treatable diseases; another's is abolishing factory farming. Neither shares the other's priority, and neither expects to convert the other. Nevertheless they reach an arrangement: the first spends a weekend volunteering at an animal sanctuary, something the first regards as comparatively minor but the second regards as highly valuable; in return, the second donates a substantial sum to a malaria-prevention charity, something the second regards as comparatively minor but the first regards as highly valuable. Neither has changed their mind about what ultimately matters, and neither pretends otherwise; yet each judges that, by their own lights, the world is now better than it would have been had they simply pursued their own priorities separately.

I have seen the same structure in smaller cases. Two members of a committee with opposed views about what the committee should prioritise agree that each will support the other's proposal, because each finds their own proposal considerably more important than the other's, and finds the cost of supporting the other's proposal one they are willing to bear. Again, no one concedes that their own principles were mistaken, and the arrangement is not explained by personal advantage: what each party receives is measured in their own moral currency, not in their own welfare.

The nearest concepts I can find are not quite right. It is not ordinary compromise, because neither side gives ground on what they believe or accepts less of what they wanted for the sake of agreement; in a sense, each gets more of what they wanted. It is not political logrolling, which trades votes or favours on the basis of self-interest or differing intensities of policy preference rather than the pursuit of distinct moral ideals. It is not an ordinary economic exchange, because there is no common currency of self-interest in which both parties calculate the benefit. And it is not moral offsetting, because that is a single agent doing one good thing to balance out another thing they regard as bad; here the exchange runs between two people with different standards of goodness.

What I want to know is whether this exact configuration has an established name. I am less interested in the label itself than in the carving: what precisely the phenomenon is, what conditions are necessary for it (for instance, must each party regard the other's action as having at least some value, must the exchange be explicit and reciprocal, and must each be doing something they would not otherwise have done), how it differs from each of the neighbouring concepts above, and where its edges lie. If no established term exists, I would like you to say so and to propose one with a clear definition and boundary cases. Please answer in British English.
