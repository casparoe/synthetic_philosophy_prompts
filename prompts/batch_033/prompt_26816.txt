I'm teaching a philosophy of probability module to final-year undergraduates who have completed at least one course in formal epistemology. Next week I need to introduce Bertrand's paradox and its implications for the principle of indifference in a single 75-minute lecture. I want a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

The students have seen the principle of indifference applied to discrete cases (dice, coins, decks of cards) and are comfortable with it there. The challenge is getting them to see why the continuous case raises a qualitatively different problem, not just a technical complication. The plan should cover: the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why (what depends on what); the one worked example that carries the whole lecture; the misconceptions students predictably arrive with or form during the lecture and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off; what to leave out at this level and say so; and a check for understanding at the end that would show whether they grasp the philosophical problem rather than just the mathematical curiosity. The plan must also address the interpretive crux of the lecture: is the paradox best understood as exposing an inconsistency in the principle of indifference, or as showing that the principle is merely incomplete without a privileged way of parameterizing the space of possibilities? The teaching plan should be no more than 700 words.
