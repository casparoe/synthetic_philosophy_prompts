I'm a retired physicist and I've been reading moral philosophy for a couple years now, mostly around this problem of whether individuals make a difference in big collective harm cases. I've ended up with a set of views and I want to know if they hang together. Here's roughly where I am:

(1) I think buying factory-farmed chicken is wrong, even though my single purchase doesn't change the number of chickens raised—the expected harm over possible threshold effects is non-zero and non-trivial.

(2) I think there's no obligation to vote in a national election. The chance of being decisive is so tiny that even multiplied by the stakes it rounds to zero.

(3) I think the difference between (1) and (2) is that the supply chain for meat is "lumpy" in a way that elections aren't—there are actual thresholds where an extra N purchases triggers another batch of production—so expected utility works in one case and not the other.

(4) I also think there's something to the idea that buying the chicken makes you complicit in a practice in a way that not-voting doesn't, and this matters independently of the causal consequences.

(5) I'm convinced by Parfit's point that imperceptible individual contributions can add up to a real harm, and that "if I don't do it someone else will" is generally a bad argument.

I keep going back and forth on wether (3) is doing all the work and whether (4) is just a rationalization because I find voting annoying and chicken tasty. Can you go through these and tell me which ones are actually in tension, which tensions are only apparent, and what I'd have to give up to make the set consistent? I'd especially like to know if (3) and (4) are pulling in opposite directions without me noticing.
