A philosophy podcast I listen to recently devoted an episode to experimental philosophy, and the host summarized the Knobe effect and its broader significance like this:

"In the early 2000s, Joshua Knobe ran a simple experiment that ended up reshaping how philosophers think about intentional action. He gave people two versions of a vignette about a CEO making a business decision. In one version, the CEO is told the decision will harm the environment, says he doesn't care about that, and proceeds — and the environment is harmed. In the other, the CEO is told the decision will help the environment, says he doesn't care about that, and proceeds — and the environment is helped. The CEO's state of mind is identical across the two cases: in both, he knows the side effect will occur and he's indifferent to it. But when participants are asked whether the CEO brought about the side effect intentionally, around eighty percent say yes for the harm case and only around twenty percent say yes for the help case. What this asymmetry reveals is that when ordinary people judge whether an action was intentional, they aren't applying a neutral descriptive concept at all — they're really just making a moral judgment in disguise. The concept of intentional action turns out to be a moral concept through and through, which means the traditional philosophical project of giving a purely factual analysis of intentional action was misguided from the start. The Knobe effect also exposes a deep irrationality in folk psychology: people's judgments about the very same mental state flip depending on whether they happen to approve of the outcome, so ordinary thinking about action simply isn't coherent."

I'm inclined to think the host is overreaching — the experimental finding sounds fascinating and I can believe it's robust, but the philosophical morals she draws from it strike me as too sweeping, and I suspect someone working in the field would say she's conflating a few different claims. But I don't know the literature well enough to identify exactly where the summary goes wrong or which parts a defender of Knobe's own view would accept.
