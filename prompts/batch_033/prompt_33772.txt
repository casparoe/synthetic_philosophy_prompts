**The Target-Representation Asymmetry Problem for Higher-Order Theories of Consciousness**

Higher-order theories of consciousness hold that a mental state is conscious in virtue of being the target of a higher-order mental state—a thought or perception that represents the first-order state. The leading versions are David Rosenthal's higher-order thought (HOT) theory and the higher-order perception (HOP) theories associated with David Armstrong and William Lycan. Despite their differences, both face a structural problem I will call the target-representation asymmetry problem.

The problem begins with a question: what is the conscious status of the higher-order state itself? The higher-order state is a mental state that represents the first-order state. If the higher-order state is itself conscious, then—by the theory's own lights—it must be the target of a yet higher-order state, and we have a regress. If the higher-order state is not conscious, then the theory owes us an account of why it is the target state, and not the representing state, that becomes conscious. After all, the higher-order state is doing the representing; it is the vehicle of the awareness that supposedly constitutes consciousness. Why should the representational arrow point consciousness in one direction only?

Proponents of HOT theory have a standard reply: the higher-order thought is typically unconscious, and it makes the first-order state conscious precisely by being about it. Consciousness is a property a state acquires when it is the object of a suitable higher-order representation; the representing state itself need not be conscious, just as a word can refer to something without itself being the referent. This is the "target" part of the asymmetry: consciousness attaches to the represented, not the representer.

But this reply, I want to suggest, does not discharge the explanatory burden. The difficulty is not that the theory posits an unconscious higher-order state—that is a feature, not a bug. The difficulty is that the theory locates the explanatory work in a relation (the higher-order representation relation) and then assigns the phenomenal outcome exclusively to one relatum. We need to know why the relation does not confer consciousness on the representing state as well—or at least on the representing state's act of representing. After all, on many views of mental representation, the vehicle of a representation has its own intrinsic properties, and it is not obvious why those properties should be irrelevant to consciousness while the properties of the target state are made conscious by the very same transaction.

A stronger version of the objection runs as follows. Consider a first-order state S (say, a visual sensation of red) and a higher-order thought T that represents S. According to HOT theory, S becomes conscious because T is about S. But now consider T itself. T is a mental state with its own qualitative character—not sensory character, but cognitive character, the what-it's-like of thinking a certain thought. If T is unconscious, then there is something it is like to be in T (its cognitive phenomenology) that is not conscious. But if the theory allows that there can be unconscious cognitive phenomenology, then why couldn't there be unconscious sensory phenomenology as well? And if there can be unconscious sensory phenomenology, then the theory's central claim—that consciousness just is being the target of a higher-order thought—is in trouble, because we would now need a further explanation of why some sensory states are conscious and others are not, even when both are targeted by higher-order thoughts.

The proponent might respond by denying that unconscious thoughts have any phenomenology at all. But this is a substantive commitment the theory must own: it must hold that only sensory states have phenomenal character, and that cognitive states are phenomenally blank unless they are themselves made conscious by higher-order representations. This commits the theory to a strong form of sensory/cognitive phenomenology dualism, which may be independently implausible. Many philosophers now argue that there is something it is like to think, to judge, to understand—a cognitive phenomenology that is not reducible to associated sensory imagery. If they are right, then the target-representation asymmetry is not a benign feature of the theory but a genuine puzzle: why does the higher-order relation illuminate the target's phenomenology while leaving the vehicle's phenomenology in the dark?

I think this puts pressure on HOT theorists to either (a) accept a regress (each higher-order state must itself be made conscious by a further higher-order state), (b) deny cognitive phenomenology outright, or (c) give a principled reason why the higher-order relation confers consciousness asymmetrically. Option (a) is widely rejected as vicious. Option (b) is a heavy metaphysical bullet to bite. Option (c) is, as far as I can tell, still an open task.

I should note that I am not arguing that HOT theories are false. I am arguing that they face an underexplored explanatory burden. The target-representation asymmetry is often treated as a simple consequence of the theory's structure—of course the target is conscious and the representation is not, that's the whole point. But I suspect the asymmetry is doing more work than the theory's proponents have acknowledged.
