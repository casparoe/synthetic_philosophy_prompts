I've transcribed the passage below. I'd like you to act as a professional historian of early modern philosophy specializing in Berkeley and eighteenth-century debates about money, and to give me a careful, sentence-by-sentence accuracy audit of the columnist's summary. Take your time and think through the distinctions before deciding which parts are fair.

The passage: "Berkeley's views on money are a natural extension of his idealism. If everything is just an idea, then money is just a collective idea too — a social fiction we all agree to treat as valuable. In The Querist, he argued that gold and silver have no intrinsic value, that money is really only a kind of ticket recording who has a claim on the labor of others, and that because money is a pure convention, governments should freely issue paper currency to stimulate industry. That is why Berkeley pushed Ireland to create a national bank, and why he is often credited with anticipating modern fiat money by two centuries."

My own hunch is that the middle sentences are close to right and that the trouble lies in the framing and the conclusion: I don't know whether Berkeley himself would have accepted the phrase "social fiction," whether his monetary views actually rest on his metaphysical idealism, or whether he thought the conventional character of money by itself licensed the free issuance of paper currency. Please separate what is securely Berkeley's from what is the columnist's gloss, and assess whether that gloss is a fair extension of his position or a subtle distortion. In particular, address (a) whether Berkeley's account of money depends on his idealism or is independent of it, (b) whether he advocated the free issuance of paper currency without qualification, and (c) whether crediting him with anticipating fiat money survives the corrections you make.
