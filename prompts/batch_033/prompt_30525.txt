I've been teaching the Sleeping Beauty problem in my graduate seminar for years and have always presented it as a fascinating but manageable puzzle — two camps, each with respectable arguments, and no clear consensus. But a colleague recently showed me that the problem may be deeper than a mere dispute about credences. On his telling, it reveals an inconsistency in how we apply the principle of indifference, and the inconsistency does not stay neatly contained within the thought experiment. I have been unable to locate the flaw in his argument, and I am starting to worry that the standard framing of the problem papers over a genuine crisis for Bayesian epistemology. I would like you to tell me which step you reject and what rejecting it costs — and, since I am asking you to commit, what argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about your diagnosis.

Here is the argument:

(1) On Sunday, before the coin is flipped, Beauty's credence that the coin will land heads should be 1/2. The coin is stipulated to be fair, and Beauty has no information favoring either outcome. This is a straightforward application of the principle of indifference to the two possible outcomes of a fair toss.

(2) Upon being awakened during the experiment, Beauty knows that her current awakening is one of three possible centered possibilities: it is Monday and the coin landed heads (call this H-Mon), it is Monday and the coin landed tails (T-Mon), or it is Tuesday and the coin landed tails (T-Tue). From the inside, these three possibilities are subjectively indistinguishable — she has no memory of any prior awakening, no access to a calendar, and no sensory cue that discriminates among them.

(3) By the principle of indifference, when an agent has no information favoring any member of a set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities over any other, she should assign equal credence to each. Applied to the three centered possibilities in (2), Beauty's credence should be 1/3 for each.

(4) The centered possibility H-Mon is equivalent, in terms of what the coin did, to the uncentered proposition that the coin landed heads — if the coin landed heads, the only awakening is H-Mon, and if the coin landed tails, H-Mon does not occur. So Beauty's credence in heads upon awakening should be 1/3.

(5) Now we have a problem. The principle of indifference was applied in (1) to the partition {heads, tails}, yielding credence 1/2 in heads. It was applied in (3) to the partition {H-Mon, T-Mon, T-Tue}, yielding credence 1/3 in heads. Both applications seem legitimate on their own terms, yet they yield contradictory results for the very same proposition.

(6) One might reply that the partitions in (1) and (3) are different kinds of things — uncentered possible worlds versus centered possible worlds — and that the principle of indifference should not be applied across these categories simultaneously. But this reply concedes the central point: if we need to restrict the principle of indifference to avoid contradiction, then we need a principled rule that tells us when it applies and when it does not.

(7) And no such principled rule exists. Any attempt to formulate one — for instance, "always apply indifference to the most fine-grained partition" — either begs the question (why is the centered partition more fine-grained than the uncentered one, rather than the reverse?) or leads to further paradoxes when we consider variants of the problem (suppose the number of awakenings depends on the coin outcome in a more complex pattern; which partition is most fine-grained then?).

(8) We are left with a dilemma: either there is no consistent way to apply the principle of indifference in cases involving self-locating uncertainty, or there is a principled restriction we have not yet found. The first horn is unacceptable — we routinely reason under self-locating uncertainty (waking up disoriented, forgetting what day it is, finding ourselves in unfamiliar surroundings), and any adequate epistemology must account for this. The second horn is, at present, merely promissory.

(9) Therefore, Bayesian epistemology — which relies on the principle of indifference, or some surrogate like maximum entropy, to set priors in situations of ignorance — cannot handle self-locating belief. This conclusion is far more radical than anything the Sleeping Beauty literature typically claims; it amounts to saying that a foundational piece of probabilistic reasoning breaks down exactly where we need it to handle a ubiquitous kind of uncertainty.

I am not asking you to resolve the Sleeping Beauty problem in its entirety, nor to adjudicate between halfers and thirders. I am asking you to identify where the argument above goes wrong. Which step do you reject, and why did that step look plausible? What do you give up by rejecting it, and what do the alternative diagnoses give up? And finally: what argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about your diagnosis? Please answer in American English.
