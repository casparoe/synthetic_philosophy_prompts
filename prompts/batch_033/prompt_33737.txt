I'm an editor at a magazine that covers science, ideas, and culture—my own beat is mostly the philosophy of science and tech, but I oversee the arts section too. Here's the situation. One of our staff writers, a guy I've trusted for years, just handed in a review of the new Almodóvar film. The piece is terrific on its face: sharp prose, smart observations about the color palette, a genuinely interesting argument about how the narrative structure mirrors the protagonist's fractured memory. The problem? I found out through a stray Slack message that he hasn't actually seen the movie. He pieced the whole thing together from the trailer, four or five other reviews, and a couple of interviews with the cast. When I called him on it, he was completely unbothered. His argument: every factual claim in the review is accurate because he cross-checked it against multiple sources, and the critical analysis stands or falls on its own logic. "If the argument is sound, what epistemological difference does it make whether I sat through the thing myself?" I told him I wasn't running the piece, but I'll be honest—I had more instinct then argument behind that decision. And for someone who covers ideas for a living, that bothers me. I keep going in circles. Give me exactly five distinct reasons, each a sentence or two, why first-hand aesthetic experience is epistemically necessary for a critic—no introduction, no conclusion, just the numbered list.
