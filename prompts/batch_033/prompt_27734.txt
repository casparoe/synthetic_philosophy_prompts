I write literary fiction and I keep noticing a psychological pattern in people — both in real life and in characters I'm trying to build — that I don't have a good name for. It goes like this: someone wants something badly, fails to get it, and then, over time, doesn't just console themselves but actually comes to believe the thing they wanted is contemptible, and that they're *better off* without it — not in a transparently coping way, but in a way that feels genuinely principled and morally serious. The original desire gets rewritten into a moral conviction.

I had a character once who couldn't get his novel published. Five years later he'd developed an elaborate theory about how commercial publishing is a sign of artistic compromise, and he genuinely looked down on published authors — not bitterly, but with what felt to him like clear-eyed moral clarity. He wasn't performing. Another example: someone in my MFA program came from money, lost most of it through a combination of bad luck and bad decisions, and within a few years had become the most vocal anti-wealth person I knew — but she really believed it. The conviction seemed to grow in direct proprtion to the impossibility of going back. I've also seen it in relationships: a friend who kept getting rejected by ambitious, career-driven women, and who now holds forth about how ambition is a kind of spiritual sickness and he only dates "grounded" people. Again, he's sincere. You can feel it.

I know this is somewhere near sour grapes, rationalization, cognitive dissonance, maybe Nietzsche's ressentiment. But what I'm trying to isolate feels more specific than any of those. Sour grapes is a one-off dismissal — "the grapes were probably sour anyway" — and it has a whiff of conscious coping. This is a slow, identity-level transformation where the inablity to have X gradually produces a sincere moral philosophy in which X is not merely unavailable but *wrong*. And it's not straightforward self-deception either, because self-deception implies there's a hidden true belief somewhere underneath — but in the cases I'm describing, the original desire seems to have been genuinely extinguished and replaced by the conviction. The person isn't secretly pining for wealth while preaching against it; they've actually stopped wanting it, and the stopping and the preaching seem to be the same process.

Is there an established name for this exact thing? If there is, what distinguishes it from the nearby concepts — where exactly is the border between this and plain rationalization, or between this and a genuine change of heart that happens to be convenient? And if there isn't a name, can you help me carve it out: what are its necessary features, what are its boundary cases, and what would you call it? I'm less interested in the label than in getting the phenomenon itself in focus, because I want to write it accurately.
