I'm a junior fellow in legal philosophy, and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper at a faculty workshop at a law school where the prevailing orientation is broadly natural-law sympathetic — not Thomistic in any strict sense, but the senior faculty tend to think of law as a purposive normative practice that cannot be adequately analyzed without reference to moral ends. There are also a couple of Dworkinians in the room who will want to frame every issue in terms of interpretation and fit. Maybe one or two lean positivist, but they'll keep quiet. My paper defends a version of inclusive legal positivism that takes Fuller's eight desiderata seriously without conceding that they constitute a morality. The argument runs roughly as follows: Fuller was right that a legal system that systematically fails his desiderata — promulgation, clarity, non-contradiction, non-retroactivity, constancy, and so on — ceases to function as law, but wrong that this functional failure is itself a moral failure. The failure is better understood as a kind of conceptual breakdown: the system loses its capacity to guide conduct through rules, and that loss is normatively significant without being moral. I try to show that Fuller's "internal morality" is really an internal logic of rule-governance, analogous to the way a language must meet certain structural conditions to be a language at all — conditions that are normative for linguistic practice but not moral. The payoff is that positivism can absorb what is genuinely valuable in Fuller's critique without collapsing into natural law theory, and that the remaining disagreement between positivism and natural law is narrower than the standard narrative suggests.
