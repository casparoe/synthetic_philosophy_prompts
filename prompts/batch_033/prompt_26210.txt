I'm teaching a grad seminar on value theory this term, and we keep getting tangled up over wether all-things-considered betterness is transitive. You know the sort of spectrum argument I mean: start with a life containing two years of excruciating pain, end with a life containing a very long stretch of barely-noticeable discomfort, and fill in a chain of cases so that each step changes only slightly in intensity or duration. At each adjacent step it seems plausible that the next life is worse, yet by the end many people don't want to say the barely-uncomfortable long life is worse than the two years of agony. I'd like you to explain something fairly precise about how this argument is supposed to work, because I think my students are running together two different diagnosis.

Please distinguish the claim that the argument establishes genuine intransitivity—A better than B, B better than C, but A not better than C—from the claim that it merely shows vagueness or parity/incommensurability at the margins, so that the chain of determinate better-than judgments never really gets going. One natural transitivity-friendly move is to say the whole thing is just a Sorites paradox: the lesson is that we shouldn't accept the induction across adjacent cases, not that betterness fails transitivity. What does a defender of transitivity need to do to make that move stick? And what extra premise or assumption does the intransitivity reading need in order to resist being deflated into a Sorites? I want the dialectic laid out precisely: where the two readings first come apart, and what kind of consideration would tell in favor of one rather than the other. Don't worry about a full formal treatment—conceptual clarity is the point.
