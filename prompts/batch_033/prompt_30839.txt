I have been thinking on my own about blame, praise, and whether anyone ever deserves either, and I have ended up with an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, but I cannot find the step that fails. Here it is.

(1) Whenever I act — even when I deliberate carefully — I act from the way I am: my character, motives, beliefs, and temperament.
(2) I did not create the way I am. I did not choose my starting point, and any attempt to change myself would itself come from the self I already was. Tracing this back only leads to earlier features of myself that I also did not choose.
(3) So I am not ultimately responsible for the way I am.
(4) To be truly morally responsible for what I do, I would have to be ultimately responsible for the way I am, since the action flows from it.
(5) Therefore I am not truly morally responsible for anything I do.
(6) The same reasoning applies to everyone. So no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything.
(7) But that seems to prove too much. It would mean that resentment, gratitude, punishment, and praise are never deserved, even in the clearest cases.

I cannot tell whether the trouble is a false premise, an invalid step, or a conclusion that follows but forces me to choose between things I wanted to keep. Name the precise point where the argument fails, explain why that step looked right, and say what the main ways of rejecting it commit you to. I would most like to keep (1) and (2), because they just seem to be facts about how a person comes to be the way they are, but I want to know what I am paying if I keep them and reject something later. Please separate what is settled about this argument from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
