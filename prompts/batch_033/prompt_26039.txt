So I'm a novelist, not a philosopher — but I've got this residency application due for a cross-disciplinary institute that funds artists doing serious research in another field for six months. The hook: I'm writing a novel set in a city-state whose entire legal code is procedurally self-amending, kind of like Peter Suber's game Nomic, where every rule — including the rules for changing rules — can be changed by following the current rules. The society has been running this way for centures and it's produced some genuinely strange legal artifacts: paradoxes that got patched, amendments that turned out to be logically inert, moments where the whole system nearly ate itself. The novel follows a junior archivist who discovers that one of the foundational amendment procedures was itself adopted through a procedure that, by the rules in force at the time, probably wasn't valid — and the question of whether the whole legal order is therefore built on a mistake becomes a political crisis.

Here's what I need: a two-page research proposal I can submit with my application. The proposal should identify a genuine, tractable philosophical question that comes out of Nomic-style self-amending rule systems — something about the relationship between procedural self-reference and legal stability, or about whether there's a principled distinction between "normal" rule changes and paradoxical ones, or about whether a fully self-amending system can ever ground its own validity without an external stopping point. Whatever angle you think is richest and most under-explored. I need it framed as philosophy, not as a creative writing project — the institute wants to see that I can actually do the conceptual work, not just mine it for plot. Include: a crisp statement of the question, why existing work on legal self-reference (Hart, Ross, Suber, whatever's out there) hasn't settled it, the approach I'd take over the six months, what success looks like versus what failure looks like, and what deliverables make sense for someone whose end product is a novel rather than a journal article. I'll fill in the citations later, so don't bother with a bibliography — just refer to positions and arguments by name where you need to.

And listen, treat this seriously: think of yourself as a philosopher of law who's also comfortable with weirder formal systems and paradoxes. Start by brainstorming the possible questions you could pursue before settling on one — I want to see that you considered the landscape. And make sure the question you pick is actually open, not something that got definitively resolved in some paper I just haven't read.
