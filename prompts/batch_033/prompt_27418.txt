I was arguing with my roommate about Moore's paradox last night and I think I've tied myself in knots. The classic example is "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." The weird thing is that this sentence isn't logically contradictory — it's not like "it's raining and it's not raining." The world could easily be such that it's raining while I, for whatever reason, fail to believe it's raining. So far so good.

But here's where I get stuck. If I try to actually *assert* that sentence — like, stand up in front of someone and sincerely say "it's raining but I don't believe it's raining" — I can't even conceive of doing it successfully. The act of assertion itself seems to require belief. And if assertion requires belief, then doesn't that mean the proposition "I assert that p and I don't believe that p" is genuinely contradictory? Not just pragmatically weird, but actually impossible in the same way a round square is impossible? My roommate keeps saying I'm confusing the proposition with the act of asserting it, but I don't see how you can seperate them cleanly here — the content of what's asserted includes a claim about the asserter's own mental state, so the act and the content get all tangled up. Is there a way of thinking about this that actually keeps them apart, or is Moore's paradox deeper than the standard story admits?
