I'm taking a philosophy of economics course this semester and the prof threw a theorem at us that I genuinely cannot stop turning over in my head. It's one of those results where the reasoning feels airtight but the conclusion is so at odds with reality that you know something has to be wrong—you just can't figure out what. I've been arguing with my study group about it for two weeks and we're going in circles.

Here's the argument, cleaned up as best I can:

(1) If two agents are rational Bayesians, share the same prior probabilities, and each knows the other's posterior beliefs, then those posterior beliefs must be identical. (Aumann's agreement theorem—the prof says this is basically uncontroversial as a piece of math.)

(2) Any voluntary trade implies that the buyer and seller disagree about the value of the asset relative to the price. If they assigned the same expected value to it, at least one of them would be making a strictly losing move by trading.

(3) In a financial market, when a trader posts a bid or ask, that action is publicly observable. Other participants can see what prices people are willing to trade at. So something about each trader's valuation—or at least the direction of their private information—is effectively common knowledge among market participants.

(4) Therefore, by (1), if all traders are rational and share common priors, their posterior beliefs about asset values must converge and be identical.

(5) But if their beliefs are identical, then by (2), no one has a reason to trade.

(6) Yet financial markets see enormous trading volumes every single day.

(7) So either market participants are systematically irrational, or they don't share common priors, or the trades aren't genuinely voluntary, or something in steps (1)–(5) doesn't hold in the real world.

The guy in my group who's most into this stuff says the obvious move is to reject common priors—people just have fundamentally different starting assumptions about the world, and that's what drives trade. But I've heard that if you give up common priors, a lot of other stuff in economics and epistemology starts to unravel—like the idea that two people looking at the same evidence should eventually converge. Another friend thinks the problem is in step (3): just because you can see someone's order doesn't mean you know their *beliefs*—they could be trading for liquidity reasons, or hedging, or rebalancing a portfolio. But I'm not sure that really dodges the theorem, because the theorem doesn't seem to require that we know the *content* of their private information, only that we know they have some—and their willingness to trade at a given price signals that.

I'm hoping you can go through this step by step, as someone who actually knows the no-trade literature and the philosophy behind it. Which premise is the weakest, and what's the cost of rejecting it? Please think carefully and don't just grab the first objection—I've probably already chewed on it. And please separate what's basically settled among people who work on this from what's still genuinely contested. I don't want a consensus story that papers over the real disagreements.
