I've been following the Pascal's mugging debate in the rationalsphere for years and I'm starting to think we're not making progress, just repackaging the same moves in slightly different notation. The basic setup everyone knows: a mugger claims that giving him your wallet will prevent an astronomically vast amount of suffering—3↑↑↑3 people tortured, or whatever—and the tiny credence you assign to this being true, times the stakes, swamps every ordinary consideration. Expected utility theory says hand over your wallet. Everyone agrees that's an absurd result, but nobody agrees on *why*.

The standard moves, as I see them:

(1) Bounded utility functions. Just cap the utility scale so nothing can dominate everything else. But this runs into problems with small-stakes dominance and means you end up preferring a certainty of one person being tortured to a 0.0001% chance of 3↑↑↑3 people being tortured, which feels like its own reductio.

(2) Complexity priors / algorithmic information theory. Penalize the mugger's hypothesis for being Kolmogorov-complex, so the probability you assign is way lower than your naive "well I guess it could be simulating me" reasoning suggests. But is this a principled response or just gerrymandering your epistemology to avoid a bullet you don't want to bite? The mugger could in principle make his threat less complex—and anyway, why should the prior over hypotheses care about description length if reality doesn't?

(3) The whole thing is a reductio of expected utility theory itself, and we need a different normative framework. But which one, and does it reproduce EU's successes in ordinary cases? This camp tends to gesture at things but doesn't have a worked-out alternative.

(4) In practice we just ignore the mugger and that's fine, so maybe decision theory doesn't need to handle Pascal cases. This is the "move along, nothing to see here" response, which feels less like a solution and more like deciding the problem's not worth solving.

What frustrates me is that everyone in the debate knows all of these moves, and people seem to sort into camps based on what they were willing to give up *going in*. Nobody gets moved across camps. It's been years.

My hypothesis, which I'd like tested: the disagreement isnt really about Pascal's mugging at all. It's about what decision theory is supposed to be a theory *of*. If you think decision theory is a theory of ideal rationality—what a logically omniscient agent with unlimited computation should do—then the mugger is a genuine problem and you need a principled answer (probably bounding utility or some formal prior). If you think decision theory is a guide for bounded agents embedded in the world they're reasoning about, then you can hand the problem off to resource constraints and get on with your life—the mugger isn't a counterexample to anything because bounded agents don't face infinite-option-space problems in practice. And if you think decision theory is fundamentally about systematizing our intuitive judgments about choice, then the absurdity of handing over the wallet *is* the data and the Pascalian calculation is the thing to be explained away.

Is this right, or is there a deeper disagreement underneath—maybe about whether "ideal" rationality is even a coherent notion, or about the relationship between normative and descriptive theory? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, is there anything—a formal result, a new thought experiment, a distinction someone hasn't drawn clearly yet—that could actually move the debate? Or is this one of those things where we should just agree to label our starting assumptions and stop expecting convergence?
