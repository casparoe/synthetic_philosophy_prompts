I chair my university's academic policy committee, and we've been watching something unfold that I can only describe as a slow-motion race to the bottom. Over the past decade, departments have been quietly reducing their major requirements — dropping prerequisites, cutting the number of required courses, making capstones optional, inflating grades — because students shop for majors partly based on how heavy the workload looks, and departments are under enrollment pressure from the provosts office. Each individual decision to lower a requirement makes perfect sense given the incentives. But the aggregate effect is that our degrees are getting hollowed out, and everyone on the committee knows it, including the people who voted for their own department's reductions last year.

We're meeting next week to vote on whether to impose a college-wide floor on major requirements: minimum credit hours, a required capstone, and a cap on the percentage of A's a department can award. The opposition falls roughly into two camps: some think this is a real problem but that a central mandate is the wrong fix (departments should control their own curricula, and if students want lighter majors maybe that's legitimate information about what they value), and others think we're overstating the problem entirely.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete decision, but more importantly it should frame the problem clearly enough that people can't just vote their department's short-term interest. Engage seriously with the autonomy objection — that departments know their own disciplines best and a one-size-fits-all floor is a crude instrument. And if you think the multipolar trap framing is right, explain why voluntary coordination won't solve it, because that's the argument I keep hearing: "can't we just agree to stop, without a rule?"
