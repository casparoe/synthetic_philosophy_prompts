I'm a novelist, and I'm deep into a manuscript set in the 2050s where climate displacement has created these enormous stateless populations — people living in cities that are still functional, working jobs, sending kids to school, paying sales tax, but with no political rights whatsoever. The central plot follows a movement trying to get them the vote, and I've got three characters who keep arguing past each other about "no taxation without representation."

One of them — a kind of civic-republican type — uses it straight: you're taxed, you get represented, that's the deal. Another character, more cynical, says it's just a revolutionary slogan the American colonists cooked up to justify a war they already wanted to fight, and it was never meant to be a rigorous principle of democratic inclusion; it's basically a bumper sticker. And a third character says the principle is actually dangerous if you take it literally, because it would mean every tourist who pays VAT on a sandwhich has a claim to vote, which is obviously nuts. So they're all using the same five words to mean completely different things, and I realize I don't actually know what the principle *is*.

So: could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "no taxation without representation" might be making? Like, is it a claim about consent, about reciprocity, about all-affected-interests, about the legitimacy of coercion, about something else? For each reading, tell me whether it's defensible as a principle of democratic inclusion, and whether it gives my stateless characters a strong case without also handing the vote to every short-term visitor. I want to write these arguments faithfully so none of my characters are just straw men — and honestly I'd also like to know which of them is actually right, or at least less wrong, because the book is starting to make me unsure of my own politics.
