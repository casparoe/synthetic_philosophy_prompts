I have followed the dispute between Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness and its phenomenal-realist critics since I was a graduate student, and after three decades it has the feel of a trench war: each side can state the other's position, each side can predict the reply before it comes, and neither has moved. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, not a verdict on who is right.

Here is how I understand the two positions. Dennett holds that there is no place in the brain where experience comes together for a single audience, and no moment at which a content becomes conscious by entering that place. What we report as a unified stream of consciousness is, on his account, a retrospectively composed story produced by many parallel processes, none of which is the privileged vehicle of experience, competing to leave traces in memory and control. The picture of an inner theater with an inner spectator is, for him, a bad theory we mistake for an observation. Against this, philosophers such as Searle, Chalmers, and Nagel reply that the theory changes the subject. The thing to be explained, they insist, is that there is something it is like to be a subject — a first-person point of view that cannot be redescribed as a narrative, a set of dispositions, or a pattern of reports without losing the phenomenon. Chalmers's zombie argument is the sharpest version of this: a creature functionally and behaviorally identical to us but lacking experience seems conceivable, and if it is conceivable, experience is something over and above the functional story Dennett tells. Dennett's side replies that the zombie intuition is an artifact of the very theater picture under dispute, and that insisting on a leftover phenomenal residue after the functional story is told simply begs the question.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical. Neither side expects a brain scan or a lesion study to settle it, and both could in principle accept the same neuroscience. Nor is it merely verbal, since each side can formulate the other's view and still object to it. I suspect it is a disagreement about what an explanation of consciousness must do: Dennett demands an account of how a physical system produces the reports, behaviors, and self-models it does, including why it seems to itself to have a unified stream; his critics demand an account that preserves the first-person datum as a datum rather than dissolving it. If that is right, the debate is stalled because the sides differ about standards of explanation, about which data count, and ultimately about starting intuitions neither can argue for.

Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden factual or formal question doing the work — for instance about what functional identity can include, or about whether the contested notions can be made precise enough to test? Is one side actually making a claim that could in principle be shown false, and if so, which claim and by what kind of evidence? Is the question ill-posed rather than merely hard? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why? Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
