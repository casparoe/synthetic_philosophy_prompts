Im a novelist, and I've been reading Augustine's On Lying because one of my characters is a fanatical truth-teller and I wanted to understand the position from the inside. I keep getting stuck on this passage from the old translation I have in front of me:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

Heres what I need help with. He seems to be saying that lying is entirely about the state of your mind when you speak, not about whether what you say is actually true. I can sort of see the false-but-not-lying case (you're just mistaken), but the true-and-yet-lying case keeps throwing me. Is he really saying you can tell the literal truth and still lie? What exactly is the "double thought" doing in that example, and why does the "sense of his own mind" get to decide the matter instead of the facts?

And a selfish question: by this definition, is a novelist lying when she writes a scene she knows never happened? I know Augustine sets aside jokes earlier because the tone shows no intent to deceive, so maybe fiction is like that — but this passage, read on its own, seems to make my whole job a kind of lying. I want to know whether the passage really has that implication or whether I'm reading it too flat.

When you explain, don't just give me the safe paraphrase. Tell me which part of your reading you're least confident about and what would change your mind — what in the text or in the surrounding argument would make you read it differently.
