I've been reading G. E. M. Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet Mr. Truman's Degree, and I keep getting stuck on two sentences near the end. She writes:

"For men to choose to kill the innocent as a means to their ends is always murder..."

Then a little later she says:

"...killing the innocent, even if you know as a matter of statistical certainty that the things you do involve it, is not necessarily murder... On the other hand, unscrupulousness in considering the possibilities turns it into murder."

I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've been trying to work through this on my own, and I can tell this distinction is doing the real work in her argument against giving Truman an honorary degree. What I can't make precise is how the two cases come apart. If you know your action will involve killing innocent people, how is that not already choosing to kill them as a means to your end? Both seem deliberate in some ordinary sense. And what does she mean when she says that unscrupulousness in considering the possibilities turns the second kind of case into murder? I'd like an explanation of the underlying reasoning here, not just a summary of the essay or a definition of terms. Keep your answer to 350 words at most.
