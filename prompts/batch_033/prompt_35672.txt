I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, a woman in her early thirties blinded shortly after birth by a congenital cataract, undergoes a surgical procedure that restores her vision partway through the narrative. I want the phenomenological texture of her first weeks of sight to carry real philosophical weight rather than reading as mere speculation, and so I have been reading the primary sources on Molyneux's problem — Locke, Berkeley, Leibniz, Diderot, and some of the modern empirical literature from Project Prakash. I find myself returning again and again to the passage in Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8) where he first introduces the problem, and there is something in it that I cannot get into focus. Here is the passage:

> I shall here insert a problem of that very ingenious and studious promoter of real knowledge, the learned and worthy Mr. Molineux, which he was pleased to send me in a letter some months since; and it is this:—"Suppose a man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and the other, which is the cube, which the sphere. Suppose then the cube and sphere placed on a table, and the blind man be made to see: quaere, whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube?" To which the acute and judicious proposer answers, "Not. For, though he has obtained the experience of how a globe, how a cube affects his touch, yet he has not yet obtained the experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; or that a protuberant angle in the cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye as it does in the cube."—I agree with this thinking gentleman, whom I am proud to call my friend, in his answer to this problem; and am of opinion that the blind man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them; though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their figures felt.

What troubles me is the word "must" in Molyneux's reasoning: "he has not yet obtained the experience, that what affects his touch so or so, *must* affect his sight so or so." On one reading, this is straightforwardly empirical — the newly sighted man simply has not yet learned the contingent correlations between tactile and visual sensations, correlations that any sighted person acquires gradually in infancy. But the word "must" seems to gesture at something stronger, as though the connection between a tactile angle and a visible angle is not merely a contingent regularity to be discovered but a kind of necessity whose status is philosophically puzzling. If the connection is merely contingent, then the man's inability is just a matter of insufficient data, and the problem collapses into a question about learning speed. If it is something stronger, then Molyneux and Locke seem to be making a claim about the very structure of sensory ideas — that ideas from different sensory modalities are, in some deep sense, heterogeneous, and that no amount of tactile experience can by itself furnish the mind with the visual idea of a cube. I am trying to understand which of these readings is closer to what Locke actually means, and what hangs on the difference, because it will shape how I write the character's first visual encounters. If the heterogeneity reading is right, then her first sight of a cube ought to be genuinely unintelligible as a cube — not merely uncertain but cognitively opaque. If the correlation-learning reading is right, then the scene is one of rapid inference under uncertainty. These are very different fictional moments. Can you help me work through what Locke is committed to here, and whether the text supports one reading over the other?
