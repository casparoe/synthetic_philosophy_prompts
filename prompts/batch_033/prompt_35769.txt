I've been preparing a unit on aggregation in ethics for my upper-division seminar, and I recently came across a characterization of John Taurek's argument that gave me pause. It appears in a widely used teaching guide for ethics instructors — not a scholarly monograph, but the sort of resource one reaches for when assembling lecture notes. The passage reads as follows:

"Taurek argues that when you face a choice between saving one person and saving five, you have no moral obligation to save the greater number. His central claim is that harms to different people cannot be added together — there is no 'sum of suffering' that makes five deaths worse than one death, because there is no single subject who experiences that sum. Each person's loss is a loss to that person alone, and the fact that there are five of them doesn't create a larger loss anywhere in the world. From this, Taurek concludes that the only fair procedure is to flip a coin: this gives each of the six people an equal chance of being saved, which respects their equal standing. On his view, it would actually be wrong to simply save the five without giving the lone individual a fair shot, because doing so would treat that person as less important than the others."

I've read Taurek's paper myself, but it's been some years, and I found myself uncertain whether this summary captures his view faithfully or whether it subtly distorts it in ways that would matter to how one teaches the argument. I'd be grateful for a careful assessment. Which parts of this summary would Taurek accept, which would he push back on, and does the overall picture it conveys — particularly the claim about what he thinks is "wrong" and what is "the only fair procedure" — align with what he actually argued? I'm less interested in whether the summary amounts to a good objection to Taurek than in whether it is a fair rendering of his position, since I want to know whether I can responsibly assign it to my students as a primer before they tackle the original paper.
