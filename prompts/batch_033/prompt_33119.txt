I'm a backend engineer who has spent the last few years building event-sourced systems, and I've come to think that the choice between mutable state and immutable event logs runs deeper than engineering. In my world, you have two fundamentally different ways to model data over time. One way: you keep only the current state and overwrite it on every update — the row in the users table has one email column, and when the user changes their email you run an UPDATE and the old value is gone forever. The other way: you never overwrite; you append events to an immutable log (UserEmailChanged at timestamp T), and the current state is just a projection you compute by replaying the log up to now — a materialized view that is convenient but derivative. Colleagues and I have argued about which is the right default, and I've noticed that the arguments track something deeper than tradeoffs about storage or latency. People who prefer current-state-only talk as though the log is a luxury — the past is dead, why keep it around? — while event-sourcing people talk as though the log is the ground truth and the current state is a convenient fiction computed from the real data.

I recently stumbled into the philosophy of time and learned that philosophers have been having a structurally similar argument for centuries under the names presentism and eternalism. Presentism says only the present exists; the past is gone and the future is not yet. Eternalism says past, present, and future all exist equally — the universe is a four-dimensional block, and "now" is just an indexical like "here," picking out a perspective rather than an ontologically privileged moment. There is also the growing-block view: the past and present exist, but the future does not; the block grows as new moments come into being, which sounds a lot like an append-only log where the tail is still being written.
