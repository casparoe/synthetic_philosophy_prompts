I'm writing a paper on predictive processing and the free energy principle, and I'd like an audit of whether my core commitments can all be held together. Here they are:

1. The brain implements hierarchical predictive processing: it continuously generates top-down predictions that are matched against bottom-up sensory signals, with mismatches (prediction errors) propagated upward to revise higher-level representations.
2. The free energy principle (FEP) unifies perception, action, and learning as all minimizing variational free energy, which upper-bounds the surprisal of sensory states given an organism's model.
3. Active inference is the correct account of action: rather than computing optimal policies, the brain minimizes free energy by issuing proprioceptive predictions that are fulfilled by classical reflex arcs, thereby changing sensory inputs to match predictions.
4. Phenomenal consciousness is a function of the counterfactual richness of the generative model — roughly, a system is conscious to the extent that its internal model encodes a temporally deep space of "what would happen if" possibilities about its own sensorimotor interactions.
5. The FEP applies to all self-organizing systems that maintain a nonequilibrium steady state, not just to creatures with nervous systems. It is a principle about life and adaptive systems tout court.
6. The Bayesian brain hypothesis is a claim at Marr's computational level (what the brain computes and why), not a claim about algorithmic processes or neural implementation.
7. Precision-weighting of prediction errors gives the correct account of attention: attention is the process of optimizing expected precision by modulating the gain on prediction error units.

Please go through these and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions are superficial, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, explain what would have to give. I'd also like your response in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write a letter.
