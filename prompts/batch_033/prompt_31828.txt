I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas — I've written a fair bit about decision theory and the philosophy of choice, and a few years ago I did a long piece on L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience that honestly kind of rattled me. The core idea, for anyone who hasn't spent time with it: there are experiences that change you so fundamentally — what you value, who you are — that you can't know in advance whether you'll be glad you had them. You can't just ask someone who's been through it, because they're evaluating it with values you don't yet have. Having a child is the central example, but college itself, career changes, religious conversion — they all fit the pattern.

Anyway, my alma mater, a small liberal arts college, has asked me to give the commencement address this spring, and I said yes before really thinking through what I'd say. The standard commencement script is basically "this experience has transformed you, go forth and be transformed further" — but I can't in good conscience stand up there and sell that line, not after spending years thinking about Paul's argument. At the same time, these kids just finished four years and their families are weeping with pride and I'm not going to use the podium to undermine the whole thing.

So here's what I need: a commencement speech, maybe 8–10 minutes, that takes the epistemic challenge of transformative experience seriously — that doesn't pretend I know what they'll value on the other side of their next big choices — while still being genuinely appropriate for the occasion. It should land as honest and warm, not as a philosophy lecture or a exercise in undermining. The audience is graduates, parents, faculty; maybe a tenth of the faculty will know the Paul reference but nobody else will, and I don't want to name-check her or use the phrase "transformative experience" — the idea should do structural work but stay invisible.

Think carefully about what a speech like this can and can't say given the constraint. What kinds of encouragement survive the epistemic problem? What does it mean to wish someone well when you can't honestly claim to know what "well" will mean for them on the other side? I suspect the speech probably needs to find something — some kind of advice or stance — that's compatible with genuine uncertainty about the value of the transformative choices ahead. The final output should start with the speech itself, no preamble, no commentary. Take the role of a commencement speaker who's thought hard about this and wants to get it right.
