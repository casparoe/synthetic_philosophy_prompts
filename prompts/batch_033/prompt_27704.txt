For a feature I'm writing, I've been trying to understand why a particular debate in AI safety has remained so stubbornly unresolved, and I'd like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on who is right.

The debate is over whether we can define "low-impact" AI — an AI that avoids disruptive side effects — without first having a full specification of human values. One side says we can. Their thought is that we don't need to know exactly what humans value in order to know that a competent AI should not drastically reshape the world as a byproduct of whatever task it is doing. They propose formal impact measures that penalize an agent for making large or irreversible changes: for example, penalizing it for reducing the "attainable utility" of a wide range of possible goals (so the agent can't quietly close off options that someone might care about), or penalizing changes in which states remain reachable from the present one. The underlying intuition is that most plausible human values are compatible with many possible futures, so an agent that keeps the world "close" to where it was is a reasonable default even in the absence of a precise value function.

The other side says this is a mirage. Their argument is that "impact" and "side effect" are defined relative to a goal: a side effect is simply an effect that isn't the intended one, and you cannot distinguish side effects from main effects without knowing what the agent is supposed to be doing. Any impact measure therefore needs a baseline — "the world as it would have been without the AI" — and the choice of that baseline is itself value-laden, because there is no value-neutral way to carve the world into the AI's effects and everything else. They also point out that every concrete proposal smuggles in a normative standard: penalizing loss of attainable utility privileges flexibility and optionality; penalizing changes in reachable states privileges a control-oriented picture of what matters; and a bias against large or irreversible changes is, quite literally, a bias in favor of the status quo. So the demand for a value-free impact measure, on this view, is asking for a contradiction.

What puzzles me, after interviewing people in both camps, is that each side seems to accept the other's formal observations and yet draws the opposite lesson. The measure-designers will grant that no impact measure is perfectly value-free, and will add that an approximate, value-lite measure is still better than nothing. The critics will reply: approximate according to whose standard of approximation? — at which point the conversation loops. Nobody disputes the mathematics; the disagreement is about what the mathematics shows.

My working theory is that the two camps are disagreeing about what an impact measure is supposed to be: a practical engineering heuristic for making deployed systems more careful, or a principled solution to the value-specification problem. But I'm not confident in that, and I'd rather you tell me where I'm wrong than confirm it.

So I'm not asking whether low-impact AI is a good idea or who wins the debate. I'm asking for a diagnosis of why it has stalled. Is this a substantive disagreement about what can be formalized, a verbal disagreement about what "value-free" could mean, or a clash about what would count as an acceptable safety guarantee — about standards of success rather than facts? Are the two sides answering different questions under the same heading? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work (say, about whether real-world agents tend to behave well under value-lite constraints), or is the impasse a sign that the question itself is ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress: a new formal result, a conceptual distinction, an engineering demonstration, or a change of question? If you think no progress should be expected, say why.

Finally, I'd like you to flag what would change your own mind about your diagnosis — which argument or consideration would lead you to a different reading of the impasse, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
