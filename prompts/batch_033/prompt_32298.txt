Hey, I'm teaching a seminar on the hard problem of consciousness next term and I want to make a handout that's a decision tree — you know, one of those flowcharts where you answer a question at each node and it leads you to a named position. The idea is that students come in with pre-theoretic intuitions and the tree forces them to see what those intuitions actually commit them to, without them just picking a label they like the sound of.

I want the tree to start from one question: whether there's a genuine explanatory gap between physical facts and phenomenal consciousness. You know, the Mary's room / what-it's-like intuition — no matter how much you know about the brain, does it still feel like something's left unexplained about why there's subjective experience at all? From there, branch out.

The leaves I want to end up with are: Type-A physicalism (illusionism/eliminativism), Type-B physicalism (the gap is epistemic not ontological), Type-C physicalism (the gap is closable with new concepts), interactionist dualism, epiphenomenalism, panpsychism (including Russellian monism, at least for the tree's purposes), and mysterianism (we're cognitively closed). I know that's a lot. If the tree naturally generates other positions that are logically possible but nobody holds them, note that and say why.

Here's the thing: each node question has to be one a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. So no "do you accept the phenomenal concept strategy?" or whatever. The questions should be about the phenomena themselves — about the gap, about fundamentality, about causal powers, about whether the problem is soluble in principle. And the tree has to be built from the logical space first, not from a list of names. The questions define the space, and then we locate the actual positions in it.

Also, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't separate (I suspect panpsychism and idealism might end up at the same leaf, which is embarassing), and any positions that just don't fit anywhere (I'm looking at you, Searle). And note any unoccupied branches — combinations of answers that are logically possible but nobody defends, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Oh, and if you can think of a better starting question than the explanatory gap one, go for it — but I think that's the right place to start since it's what the whole debate turns on.

Write it casual, it's for undergrads, not a journal submission. A typo or two is fine, I'll clean it up later.
