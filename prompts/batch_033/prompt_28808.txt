A visiting speaker in our philosophy department recently gave a talk arguing that self-deception is strictly impossible, and I have been unable to shake the argument. He presented it roughly as follows:

(1) To deceive someone is to intentionally cause them to believe something you know to be false.
(2) Self-deception, if it exists, is deception in which the deceiver and the deceived are the same person.
(3) Therefore, a self-deceiver must intentionally cause themselves to believe something they know to be false.
(4) But it is impossible to intentionally cause yourself to believe p while knowing that not-p. The project demands that you simultaneously occupy the role of deceiver (which requires knowing that not-p) and deceived (which requires believing that p), and these two states — knowing not-p and believing p — cannot coherently coexist in a single mind at a single time.
(5) Therefore, self-deception is impossible.

He was careful to add that people plainly engage in wishful thinking, motivated reasoning, selective attention to evidence, and other forms of bias, but he argued these are not self-deception proper — they are distinct phenomena we lazily lump together under a single label.

I find the conclusion hard to accept, but I cannot locate the error. My instinct is that premise (1) builds too much into the definition of deception, or that premise (4) assumes an overly unified picture of the mind. But I do not trust my instincts here, and I want to know what a defender of the possibility of self-deception should say. Which premise or step in this argument would you reject, what does rejecting it cost, and what account of self-deception survives the rejection?
