You are a professional philosopher of logic and formal epistemology. I want you to produce a carefully constructed dialogue, and I want you to think through the arguments before you write.

**The topic:** The problem of logical omniscience for possible-worlds semantics in epistemic logic.

**The setup:** Two philosophers are sitting over coffee after a long day at a workshop on formal epistemology. The problem of logical omniscience came up during the Q&A of the final talk, and the exchange was cut short. Now they are continuing the argument on their own.

**The characters:**

- **Dr. Chen** holds that logical omniscience is a decisive objection to the standard possible-worlds framework. She argues that a semantics that ascribes knowledge of every logical truth, and closure under known entailment, to every agent cannot possibly model the epistemic states of real, cognitively bounded beings. It cannot distinguish the mathematician who has proved a theorem from the one who has merely grasped the axioms. She favors enriching the semantics—for instance, with impossible worlds, awareness structures, or a combination of the two—to capture the difference between what an agent knows in principle and what they have actually derived. She is willing to acknowledge that her preferred frameworks introduce technical complications, but she regards this as the price of descriptive adequacy.

- **Dr. Torres** holds that logical omniscience is a legitimate and indeed necessary idealization. He argues that the possible-worlds framework is not in the business of modeling the psychology of reasoning—it models the logic of knowledge attributions, and knowledge, unlike belief, is factive. If you genuinely know the axioms of Peano arithmetic, then in a perfectly ordinary sense you do know Goldbach's conjecture if it follows from them, even if you have not realized it yet. He is willing to concede that the standard framework is not suitable for every purpose—modeling resource-bounded agents, for instance, calls for different tools—but he insists that for its intended purposes, the idealization is harmless and indeed theoretically fruitful.

Neither character is a strawman. Both command real arguments, and both should make at least one genuine concession that strengthens rather than weakens their overall position.

**Instructions:**

1. **Plan first.** Before writing the dialogue, briefly sketch the core argumentative moves each character will make, how they will respond to each other, and where each might concede ground. This sketch is for your own planning and will not appear in the final output.

2. **Write the dialogue.** It should run roughly 500–700 words. The characters must engage with each other's actual points—no talking past each other. Each should press the other on the hardest case for their view. Include at least one moment where a character says something like "That's fair, but…" or "I grant you that, yet…"—a genuine concession that leads to a sharper formulation of their own position.

3. **End sharply.** The dialogue should end not with a resolution but with the disagreement clarified—each character should have a more precise sense of what the other is committed to and why the gap between them persists.
