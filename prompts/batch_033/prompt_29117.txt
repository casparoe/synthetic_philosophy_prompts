I've been toying with an idea for a short story and would like you to write it out for me — I'm more interested in seeing how the philosophical content gets handled than in claiming authorship myself. The premise is this: a team of physicists at a large research university has built a device (call it a "modal aperture," though you should feel free to rename it) that was intended to probe the quantum wavefunction in a novel way but instead yielded an utterly unexpected result. It opens a sensory window onto what appear to be other possible worlds. Crucially, these are not just branching Everettian worlds that share a causal past with ours — they are worlds with entirely different histories, different laws, different initial conditions. Worlds where sentient life never evolved. Worlds where the gravitational constant is slightly different and stars never ignited. Worlds where the Nazis won. Worlds, yes, with talking donkeys. In short, the device appears to confirm something close to David Lewis's modal realism: all possible worlds exist concretely, and "actuality" is merely an indexical matter of which world you happen to inhabit.

The story should follow one of the researchers — perhaps a postdoc or a junior faculty member — as she grapples with what this discovery means. In particular, I want the story to engage seriously with what I take to be the most disturbing upshot of modal realism: if every possible world is as real as this one, then every possible instance of suffering is genuinely occurring somewhere. There is a world in which a counterpart of you is being tortured right now, and that torture is just as real as the coffee you are drinking. The protagonist should struggle with this — not as an abstract puzzle but as something the device forces her to witness, or at least to know is out there, inescapably. Does she bear obligations toward the suffering counterparts she can now observe? Is it possible to go on living a normal life while knowing that the plenitude of worlds contains unspeakable horrors alongside unspeakable beauties? What does it do to a person's conviction that her own choices matter, if every choice she might have made is realized by a counterpart somewhere, and the total moral ledger of the pluriverse is fixed from the start? I would also be interested in how other characters — her advisor, her partner, a university administrator, a journalist — react to the discovery in ways that foreground different philosophical pressures (the epistemology of modal knowledge, the metaphysics of personal identity across worlds, the apparent tension between modal plenitude and the meaningfulness of deliberation).

The tone should be literary and character-driven rather than didactic; the philosophical ideas should emerge through the protagonist's experience and through dialogue and scene rather than through exposition. That said, a reader who has never encountered modal realism should come away with a clear sense of what the view claims and why someone might find it both intellectually compelling and existentially vertiginous. The story can end wherever feels right — there is no need to resolve everything, and in fact an unsettled ending might be truer to the material.
