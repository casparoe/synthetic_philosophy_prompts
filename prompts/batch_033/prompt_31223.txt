I've come to accept, or at least I'm strongly inclined toward, the all-affected interests principle as an answer to the boundary problem in democratic theory: the people who ought to have a say in a collective decision are, in its strong form, all and only those whose interests the decision actually affects. Its appeal, as I understand it, is that it defines the demos by the reach of the decision itself rather than by a preexisting jurisdiction — which is exactly the question the boundary problem says we're not allowed to beg.

But I want to know how much this view actually risks, and I suspect it has been quietly adjusted to survive every counterexample thrown at it. Tell me what would count against the all-affected interests principle. I want the objections sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one I want to know what a committed defender would concede counts against the view and what they would dismiss as a demand the principle was never meant to meet. In particular: (a) Can it handle the worry that everyone is affected by everything, through indefinitely long causal chains, so the demos swells to the whole world for nearly every decision? (b) Can it handle the constitutive regress — someone has to determine who counts as affected before the affected can decide, and that prior determination looks undemocratic? (c) What does it say about future people, whose interests a decision affects but who cannot be included in any actual procedure? (d) Is it a criterion of membership at all, or has it been quietly demoted to a critical or regulative ideal that no actual institution is expected to instantiate? (e) Does it have a real answer to the rival view that what matters is being subject to the coercive enforcement of a decision rather than merely being affected by it, and is that rival a different answer to the same question or a rejection of the question? Then give me your honest verdict on whether the principle, as it is usually defended now, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a principle of this kind, which might be doing normative or critical work rather than institutional design work. I would find it most persuasive if you could identify the observation, case, or institutional result that would actually lower a current defender's confidence in the principle, rather than something they would re-describe as already covered.
