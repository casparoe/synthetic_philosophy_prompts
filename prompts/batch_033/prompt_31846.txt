I have been working through the social-choice impossibility result usually called Arrow's theorem, and I have reconstructed the argument in a form that seems to me to prove too much: its conclusion is that no acceptable procedure for aggregating individual rankings into a collective ranking exists. Assume throughout that there are at least three options and at least two voters. I want to know where the argument goes wrong, or, if it does not, which premise is least costly to abandon and what abandoning it costs.

(1) Collective rationality. A procedure for aggregating individual rankings should take any profile of complete, transitive individual rankings of the options and return a complete, transitive collective ranking. Refusing to rank the options, or returning a ranking that cycles, is a failure of collective rationality.

(2) Unrestricted domain. The procedure must deliver such a ranking for every possible profile of rational individual rankings. It may not declare some preferences inadmissible and refuse to process them.

(3) Unanimity (Pareto). If every individual ranks x above y, then the collective ranking must rank x above y.

(4) Independence of irrelevant alternatives. The collective ranking of x against y should be fixed entirely by the individuals' rankings of x against y. How individuals rank x or y against some third option z should make no difference to the collective ranking of x and y.

(5) Non-dictatorship. There should be no individual whose strict ranking of any pair of options always becomes the collective strict ranking of that pair, whatever everyone else thinks.

(6) Now suppose a procedure satisfies (1) through (4). A standard theorem shows that it must violate (5). The proof runs roughly as follows. Call a coalition decisive for x over y when, in any profile in which every member of the coalition ranks x above y and every non-member ranks y above x, the procedure ranks x above y. By (3), the whole electorate is decisive for every pair. Using the independence condition and unanimity, together with the transitivity demanded in (1), one can show that decisiveness spreads across alternatives and descends through coalitions: a coalition decisive for one pair is decisive for every pair, and a decisive coalition with more than one member can be split into two non-empty parts, one of which is itself decisive for some pair. Iterating the descent, one reaches a decisive coalition with a single member; by the spread, that member is decisive for every pair, which is exactly the dictatorship ruled out by (5). Therefore no procedure satisfies all of (1) through (5).

(7) That conclusion seems to me to prove too much. Hiring committees, legislatures, multi-candidate elections, and journal referees do rank options, and we standardly regard some of their procedures as fair, or at least as not dictatorial. A result that says every such procedure must violate one of these minimal-looking conditions appears to indict collective decision-making as such.

Which step should be rejected, and what exactly is lost by rejecting it? Explain why the rejected step looked so plausible, and what the main positions that reject it each have to give up. I would most like to keep unanimity and non-dictatorship; I am less attached to the independence condition than to the others, but I do not see how to give it up without exposing collective rankings to manipulation through the introduction of irrelevant alternatives. Is there a stable position here, or does the argument force a choice between different ways of abandoning something that looked non-negotiable?
