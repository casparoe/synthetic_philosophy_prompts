In my upper-level course on cultural evolution, the textbook motivates the field with Dawkins's analogy between memes and genes. On this picture, a meme is a unit of cultural transmission — an idea, a tune, a technique — and culture is said to be Darwinian because memes, like genes, are copied with variation, compete for limited attention and memory, and are differentially retained. The textbook then uses the analogy to support a strong conclusion: the spread and persistence of beliefs and practices can be explained by selection working on the memes themselves, without appeal to the intentions or reasons of the people who hold them. I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Please provide the explicit correspondence table — what corresponds to the gene, the replicator, the vehicle or phenotype, the environment, mutation, and fitness — and then identify where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion rather than being merely superficial: for instance, fidelity of copying, blending rather than particulate inheritance, horizontal transmission, the absence of a clear genotype–phenotype distinction, and whether cultural variants are selected by a stable environment or actively reshaped by their hosts. I would also like to know what features of gene-based selection the analogy quietly imports into the cultural case that may not be there. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy than the gene one.
