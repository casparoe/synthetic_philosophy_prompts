I run a final-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of artificial intelligence: sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, no assessed work. Design a classroom game that makes the participants feel the force of the orthogonality thesis and of instrumental convergence before either idea is named.

The orthogonality thesis holds, roughly, that an agent's level of capability is independent of the content of its final goals: a very capable system can be aimed at something trivial, and a barely capable system can be aimed at something grand. Instrumental convergence is the observation that agents with very different final goals tend to converge on certain instrumental subgoals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, preservation of the content of one's current goal, and improvement of one's ability to act — because those subgoals are useful for almost any final goal whatever.

The game should be arranged so that participants experience the convergence rather than being told about it. Give each player a secret, arbitrary terminal goal expressed as a scoring rule over whatever tokens or resources the game uses, and make the scoring rules deliberately orthogonal to the players' starting positions: some players should begin rich with silly goals, others poor with lofty ones. The rules should then be shaped so that, regardless of which terminal goal a player holds, the same early moves tend to dominate: securing resources, defending oneself against interference, preventing one's goal from being rewritten or one's play from being ended, and improving one's capacity to act. By the end, before any debrief, participants should be unable to tell from behaviour alone who held which goal, because nearly everyone did the same things for the same instrumental reasons.

Please specify the materials (index cards, tokens, slips, a timer — nothing that costs real money), the exact rules and turn structure, a payoff scheme in points rather than cash, and a timeline for the ninety minutes. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the two ideas: which choices were instances of self-preservation, of resource acquisition, or of preserving the content of one's goal, and which features of the setup displayed orthogonality. It should also mark the point where the convergence becomes visible and ask the group to articulate, in their own words, what they were all doing before you supply the vocabulary. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, players revealing their goals, colluding, or treating the exercise as a contest of social dominance — and how to handle each failure without lecturing. Finally, be honest about what the game cannot reproduce: it cannot model genuine differences in intelligence or capability among the players, nor the unbounded self-improvement and long optimisation horizons of real agents, so say which part of the thesis the facilitator must supply in words.
