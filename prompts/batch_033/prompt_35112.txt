I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've been reading Hofstadter to write a character: an AI that insists it has a self and that the other characters ought to treat as if it does. The strange-loop picture seemed perfect for this — the 'I' isn't some extra ingredient in the machinery, it's a self-referential symbol, a loop where a system's symbols model the very system doing the modeling, nothing more. But then I built a little argument out of it for my notebook, and now it seems to prove to much.

(1) The self just is a strange loop, a self-referential symbolic structure. (2) If the self just is a strange loop, then any system that realizes a strange loop realizes a self — if water just is H2O, any H2O is water. (3) Lots of things realize self-referential symbolic loops that I can't bring myself to call selves: a program that prints its own source code, a formal system that encodes claims about its own provability, a sentence that says of itself that it is false. (4) So those things have selves. (5) But a quine program has no inner life, cares about nothing, can't be lonely or afraid — and those are exactly the qualities I need my AI character to have. (6) So the strange-loop picture must be leaving something out. But the entire appeal of the picture was that there is nothing else to leave in.

I want to keep (1), because it's the only thing that lets me write the book, but I can't see how to reject any of the other steps without special pleading. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does, and what do I have to give up to fix it? And I'd like to know wether the fix makes my character's self a matter of degree or of kind, because that changes how I write the scene where she tries to convince the skeptical engineer.
