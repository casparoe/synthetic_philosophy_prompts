I spent most of my career in experimental physics, where the gap between what a model says should happen and what actually unfolds in the lab is something you learn to treat as data rather than as an embarrassment. Since retiring I have been reading around in philosophy, economics, and decision theory, and I keep returning to a puzzle that sits squarely in that gap: the dollar auction. The setup is simple — an auctioneer offers a one-dollar bill to the highest bidder, with the twist that the second-highest bidder must also pay their bid and receives nothing. Bidding starts at a penny. What happens, of course, is that two people get locked in and end up paying far more than a dollar for a dollar, each reasoning that having already bid ninety cents it is better to bid a dollar and break even than to stop and lose ninety cents outright — except the other person then faces the identical logic at a dollar and five cents, and so on upward without any natural stopping point.

The standard game-theoretic analysis says a rational agent should never enter the auction in the first place, because once you are in, the logic of sunk costs makes each successive step look locally rational even as the sequence leads to a globally ruinous outcome. But this raises a deeper question: is the escalation itself genuinely irrational, or does the standard analysis miss something important? One position holds that the real failure is not the escalation but the initial decision to enter, and that once a player is committed, continuing to bid can be perfectly rational given that the alternative is to forfeit everything already sunk — the mistake was upstream. Others counter that this merely pushes the problem back a step: if you cannot trust yourself to exit later, then entering is irrational, but that seems to demand a kind of self-prediction and self-binding that ordinary agents lack. Still others argue that in real-world analogues — military interventions, failing projects, toxic relationships — the escalation is not really about sunk costs at all, but about reputation, or about not wanting to admit a mistake to oneself, or about genuinely uncertain prospects that might still turn around, and that these considerations can make continued investment rationally defensible in ways the stripped-down auction obscures.

I would like an essay that weighs these positions and reaches a conclusion. Specifically: is escalation of commitment best understood as a failure of individual rationality, or as an unfortunate but intelligible response to a genuinely difficult structural situation — one where even a clear-eyed agent might reasonably find themselves trapped? The essay should engage with the dollar auction as a formal model but also say something about what it teaches us regarding real-world escalation, and it should defend a verdict rather than ending on a note of "both sides have a point."
