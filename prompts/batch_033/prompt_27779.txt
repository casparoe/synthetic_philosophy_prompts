I am drafting a paper defending the existence of what I will call free-floating rationales: normative reasons that are not the reasons of any reasoner. Before I circulate the draft, I want an audit of whether the commitments I bring to it actually hang together. Here is the set I hold.

1. There are at least some free-floating rationales: considerations that count in favor of actions or attitudes even though no actual agent has them. I am also inclined to think there is no requirement that every such rationale be graspable or actionable by some possible agent.

2. These rationales are stance-independent. What makes them reasons is not any agent's desires, plans, or evaluative attitudes — not ours, not God's, not those of an ideal observer. They are not grounded in any commander's will.

3. Reasons are facts, not mental states. The reason to leave a burning building is the fact that the building is on fire, not anyone's belief or desire about that fact.

4. The guidance constraint: a consideration can be a reason only if it is the kind of thing that could, in principle, figure in an agent's practical deliberation and bear on what the agent does. Reasons are for reasoning.

5. Our considered normative intuitions, when they survive wide reflective equilibrium, are generally reliable guides to which of these rationales obtain. I see no better method for finding out what the reasons are.

6. I accept that our normative intuitions have a causal history of the kind evolutionary and social psychology describe, and I do not think this undermines their reliability. I also think the whole picture can be made compatible with a naturalistic worldview, without non-natural properties or a special faculty of normative perception.

Please go through this set and tell me which commitments or combinations are in tension, and how badly. I want you to distinguish real tensions from ones that only look like tensions and dissolve on inspection, and to explain the difference. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to restore coherence. I would rather have the problems ordered by severity than get a flat list of worries.

If the request itself rests on a confused premise, or if the task as posed cannot be done well, say so plainly instead of performing the audit anyway.
