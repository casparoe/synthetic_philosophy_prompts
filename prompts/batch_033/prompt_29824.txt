I'm a machine-learning engineer at a company that builds a triage system for hospital emergency departments. The system takes a patient's chart, vitals, and history and outputs a priority score that the department uses to decide who is seen first when it is overcrowded. We did not encode an explicit rule that certain complaints outrank others. The model was trained on several hundred thousand past visits to predict, from data available at intake, which patients would eventually need intensive care. None of us can say in advance exactly how it weighs, say, a seventy-year-old with mild symptoms against a forty-year-old with a borderline ECG; the weighting is the output of gradient descent over the training data, and it can be sensitive to correlations that no clinician would endorse. Hospitals are contractually told that the score is only a decision aid, that clinicians should always make the final call, and that they can override it. In practice, when the department is overwhelmed, the score effectively is the triage decision: nurses and physicians defer to it, and the override is rarely used.

Last month, at one of our client hospitals, a man in his sixties arrived with symptoms that looked routine. The system assigned him a low priority score, he waited for hours, and he suffered a cardiac arrest in the waiting room and died. A later review found that the model had latched onto a feature of his chart that in the training data was correlated with low acuity but was misleading in his case, and that several staff members each assumed someone else would second-guess the number. Since then, everyone has had a story about why they are not at fault. The hospital says it followed our guidance about clinician oversight. The clinicians say they were working beyond capacity and that the score is the only reason the department functions at all. My company says the model performed exactly as it was designed to perform, that it correctly predicted the outcome class it was trained to predict, and that we repeatedly warned clients not to treat it as an autonomous decision-maker. My manager says we should focus on improving the model and auditing the features rather than looking for a villain. Some colleagues say the system itself is responsible, in the loose way an organization can be; others say responsibility requires an individual who knew what was happening and could have done otherwise, and here no single person satisfies that.

I cannot shake the feeling that there is a hole in this story: after all the explanations, nobody is actually responsible for a death that our system was deeply involved in bringing about. But I cannot articulate exactly what the hole is, and I do not know whether the answer is to redesign the system, to change how hospitals use it, to hold the company responsible as a whole, or something else. Is my unease justified? Who, if anyone, is morally responsible here, and under what conditions would that be so? And is there a way to design or deploy a system like this so that the hole does not open up in the first place, or are these cases ones where the ordinary machinery of blame simply runs out?
