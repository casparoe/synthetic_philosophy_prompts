I've been trying to make sense of the art market's treatment of forgeries, and I keep running into what looks like a deep inconsistency that I suspect hides an interesting philosophical position.

Here is the practice that puzzles me. When a painting is discovered to be a forgery — say, a work long attributed to Vermeer turns out to have been painted by Han van Meegeren in the 1930s — its market price collapses essentially to zero, or to a modest curiosity-value. This happens even though the physical object has not changed, and even though, before the discovery, curators, critics, and collectors were genuinely moved by it. But the art world does not treat all non-originals the same way. A later workshop copy of a famous painting, honestly labeled as such from the start, is worth something — sometimes a great deal, if it is a good copy from the right period. A high-quality photographic reproduction is worth almost nothing. And a digital scan displayed on a screen is worth nothing at all, even if it is perceptually richer in some respects than the original viewed through dirty varnish in a crowded gallery. So it is not simply that copies are worthless; the hierarchy seems to track something about the causal chain connecting the object to the artist's hand, and about whether anyone was deceived along the way, and perhaps about scarcity. Meanwhile, in other domains we seem perfectly happy to separate experience from origin: nobody cares whether the MP3 they are listening to was ripped from the original master tape or from a CD pressed in 2003, as long as it is bit-identical. And in science, a result does not become less true if we learn it was discovered by someone unethical — the knowledge stands independent of its provenance. So the art case seems to be doing something different.

I am trying to reconstruct the implicit theory of aesthetic value that would make these practices coherent. Does the art market presuppose that the value of an artwork resides in its history rather than in its perceptible properties? If so, why does that history stop mattering once it is honestly disclosed — the labeled workshop copy still has a causal history, just a different one? Or does the practice presuppose something about the artist's achievement — that what we value is a certain kind of creative act, and the forgery is parasitic on that act in a way that an honest copy is not? And if that is the view, what exactly is the difference between the two kinds of parasitism, given that neither involves the artist's actual labor? I am also curious whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a hedonist about aesthetic value — someone who thinks the value of art is entirely in the experience it produces — consistently participate in this market, or does participation require a commitment to origin-dependent value? And if the latter, what would have to change — in pricing, in museum display, in conservation decisions, in the way we teach art history — if we adopted a purely experiential theory of aesthetic value instead?
