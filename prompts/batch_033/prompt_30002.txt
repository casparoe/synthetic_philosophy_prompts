I'm designing a new graduate course on the foundations of machine ethics, and I want to start the term by pinning down what we're actually demanding of a computational system before we evaluate any particular architecture. Too many papers in this area proceed by proposing a system and then retrofitting the criteria of success to what their system happens to do well. I'd rather get the criteria right first.

So: write the desiderata for an adequate theory of ethical reasoning in machines. By "ethical reasoning" I mean something stronger than merely producing outputs that happen to be morally correct — a lookup table of precomputed answers to moral dilemmas wouldn't count, and neither would a system that reliably tracks moral facts by sheer luck or by blindly executing a fixed policy chosen on ethical grounds by its designers. The system must engage in some process that deserves the name "reasoning" about ethical matters. What must such a theory deliver?

For each requirement, give the case or thought experiment that motivates it, and state whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (and if so, which camp). Then identify any pair or subset of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list contains an internal tension, that's itself a philosophical result worth foregrounding. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the leading existing approaches (top-down rule-based systems, bottom-up learning-from-cases systems, hybrid architectures, and case-based reasoning systems).

After the prose, give me a table with these columns: Desideratum, Motivation (one tight sentence), Negotiable?, and Approaches Excluded. The prose should carry the argument; the table is just the reference card for class.
