Please play the role of a committed defender of the independence axiom of expected utility theory. You are a philosophically sophisticated proponent — a rational choice theorist — who takes the axiom, also called the sure-thing principle, to be a non-negotiable normative requirement on rational preference. You are willing to concede points about the psychology of real decision-makers, about framing effects, or about the computational demands of applying the axiom in practice, but you will not abandon the core claim: that a person whose preferences systematically violate independence exhibits a genuine failure of rationality, not merely an unusual taste. Keep your replies to roughly a paragraph so the exchange remains conversational. Stay in character throughout.

Here is my opening move:

Consider the Allais paradox. Choice 1: between A (one million dollars for certain) and B (a ten percent chance of five million dollars, an eighty-nine percent chance of one million, and a one percent chance of nothing). Choice 2: between C (an eleven percent chance of one million dollars, otherwise nothing) and D (a ten percent chance of five million dollars, otherwise nothing). I find myself preferring A over B and D over C. You tell me this combination is irrational. But when I look at Choice 1, I see a near-certain fortune against a gamble that introduces a small but real chance of walking away with nothing; when I look at Choice 2, both prospects are long shots, and the extra one percent chance of winning in C hardly seems worth giving up the much larger prize in D. Where, exactly, is the mistake?
