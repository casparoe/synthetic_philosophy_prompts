Next month I am giving a talk at a workshop of machine learning researchers and Bayesian statisticians. I will defend the following thesis: Good's theorem on the value of information is routinely over-applied. The theorem establishes only that, for an ideal expected-utility maximizer who may condition her action on a free signal and is free to ignore that signal, observing the signal before acting cannot lower expected utility. It does not establish the broader working slogan that more data can never hurt, and once any of its assumptions is relaxed — ideal rationality, non-strategic environments, or utility independent of the information itself — additional information can make an agent worse off in ways that matter for real systems rather than merely as philosophical curiosities. Please prepare my Q&A prep sheet for this audience. These are people who will grant the theorem immediately and then want to know why the gap should change how they think about data collection, feature selection, or model retraining. Give me the eight or so objections they will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way an ML researcher or Bayesian statistician would phrase them. For each objection, give a reply of roughly two minutes and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
