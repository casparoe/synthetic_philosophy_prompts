I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I am preparing a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on the ethics of markets. I would like you to build a sorting quiz: Which position on repugnant markets do you actually hold? Please take on the role of a professional moral and political philosopher who works on commodification and the limits of markets. Think carefully, and do not go straight to the labels: begin by brainstorming the fault lines on which positions actually come apart, construct the logical space those fault lines generate, locate the recognized positions within it, and only then write the quiz and its scoring key.

The deliverable should be a classification instrument rather than a prose essay, and it should include the following parts.

First, eight to ten diagnostic questions. Each question should present one concrete transaction or proposed market, specified well enough that a student is answering about the case rather than about a slogan. Use at least these three cases: a living donor selling a kidney to a wealthy recipient; a hardware store raising the price of bottled water tenfold after a hurricane; and a citizen selling a vote to a campaign for a fixed sum. You may add cases such as paid surrogacy with strong legal protections, paying blood plasma donors, an amusement park selling queue-jump passes, consensual dwarf-tossing for entertainment, or carbon offsets. For each case, stipulate that all parties consent and specify the background conditions; then offer three or four answer options that track a distinct justificatory commitment rather than an overall label. The options should be ones a student can choose without knowing which position they are voting for. Vary across questions whether the case isolates wrongness in the exchange itself, background injustice, coercion, incommensurability, effects on equal standing, or bare repugnance, and tailor the wording of the options to the case instead of reusing a template.

Second, a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto six to eight named positions. Build the positions from the logical space, not from a list of labels. I expect the space to be generated by crossing at least these fault lines: whether the exchange is wrong in itself even under fair background conditions, or wrong only because of coercion, poverty, inequality, or asymmetric information; whether the wrong, when there is one, lies in the corruption of the norms or meaning of the good, in the treatment of incommensurable values as priceable, in damage to equal standing, or in the consequences; and whether felt repugnance is itself a reason or merely a heuristic that tracks one of the other wrongs. Positions that should appear as leaves include at least a corruption-of-the-good view, an incommensurability view, a background-injustice view, a coercion-or-exploitation-only view, an equal-standing view, a market-optimist view holding that no consensual exchange is wrong once externalities and information asymmetries are fixed, and a view on which felt repugnance itself carries independent justificatory weight. Use standard labels where they are unambiguous, and describe any position whose label would be contested rather than assigning it a name.

Third, a short section on the logical space itself. State which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and diagnose each empty cell: is it empty because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive enough that no one defends it, or because it is merely unexplored? Also flag any pair of positions your quiz cannot separate with the questions you chose, and say exactly which additional question or case would separate them.

Fourth, for each leaf, one or two sentences on the position's most awkward commitment: the case or implication that a smart defender of a rival position would press hardest.

Please write in a formal, seminar-ready register, but keep the instrument usable as a handout: questions vivid and concrete, and the scoring key simple enough that a student can apply it by counting letters. Do not include a bibliography or reading list, and do not rely on verbatim quotation from any text.
