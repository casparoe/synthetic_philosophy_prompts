The principle says, roughly, that your current credence in a proposition should equal your expected future credence in it — if you're pretty sure that next week you'll be 0.6 confident that the accused is guilty, then you should be 0.6 confident now. Then the principle seems to demand that you defer to a version of yourself you have good reason to distrust, which feels less like rationality and more like a hostage situation.

I'm trying to get clear on where the weight of the arguments actually falls, and I'd rather not just hear "its controversial." How much on whether the counterexamples — forgetting, drugs, cognitive decline — are best understood as genuine violations or as cases where the principle was misapplied because your future self isn't really "you" in the epistemically relevant sense? How much on deeper questions about whether diachronic epistemic norms even exist, as opposed to there just being synchronic norms at each time slice?
