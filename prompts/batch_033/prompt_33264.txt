I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Coordination, Cooperation, and Collapse" — 16 students, one 75-minute session. We've been working through the literature on multipolar traps and the Moloch dynamic: situations where every actor, pursuing locally optimal strategies, drives the system toward an outcome that leaves everyone worse off, and no one can unilaterally exit without being punished. The students nod along with the standard examples — arms races, environmental degradation, credential inflation, the Soviet collapse — but I can tell they experience these as something that happens to other people, or to large institutions, or to actors who simply lack the foresight to coordinate. I want to design an in-class simulation that makes them feel the trap from the inside. I want them to experience the moment where they know exactly what is happening, want to stop it, and still find themselves contributing to it because the incentives won't let them do otherwise.

The game should involve real but small stakes — points toward a participation grade, or candy, or just publicly visible scores that they'll care about in the moment. It should be playable in about 40 minutes, leaving 35 for debrief. The rules should be simple enough to explain in under five minutes, and the destructive dynamic should be visible to them as it unfolds: they should see the collectively bad outcome approaching and still feel pulled toward the individually rational choice each round. Ideally, the game should include a stage where they are allowed to communicate — but discover that cheap talk doesn't solve the problem, and may even make things worse by revealing intentions they can't credibly commit to. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the formal structure of multipolar traps — the payoff matrix, the inability to make binding commitments, the role of transparency and iteration, the difference between knowing the problem and solving it — and then connect it to the Moloch metaphor as a vivid name for the force that seems to take over despite everyone's better judgment.

Please give me the rules, the materials, the payoff structure, the timeline, and a detailed debrief script. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — participants colluding, someone deliberately tanking, the trap not being tight enough, a group discovering an unintended coordination strategy — and how to adjust the parameters to fix each problem. And be honest about what the game cannot capture: what feature of real-world multipolar traps does a 40-minute classroom game with a small group of peers inevitably distort or omit, and how should I address that gap in the debrief so students don't walk away with a false sense of how easy the real thing is to escape?
