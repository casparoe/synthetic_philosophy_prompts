I'm a TA for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and I'm grading a stack of essays on higher-order theories of consciousness. I want a second opinion on one answer — I've seen too many graders let subtle confusions slide because the prose sounds confident and the broad strokes are right, and I'd rather have someone who knows the literature cold flag the real problems.

Below is the exam question and one student's answer. Please read both carefully, assign a letter grade on the standard scale (A through F, with +/- allowed), and then give me exactly five specific, point-by-point comments justifying the grade. Each comment should be one or two sentences identifying something the answer gets right or wrong, with enough precision that I could write it in the margin. No introduction, no conclusion — just the grade followed by the five numbered comments. I want a committed call: don't hedge between two grades or tell me it depends on how I weight things. Pick a grade and defend it.

Before you grade, take a moment to think through the philosophical terrain: what are the real structural differences between HOT and HOP theories, what exactly does the targetless-representation objection show, and what would a top answer need to get right about the dialectic between them.

EXAM QUESTION:

"In the debate over higher-order theories of consciousness, both higher-order thought (HOT) theory and higher-order perception (HOP) theory claim that a mental state is conscious in virtue of being represented by a higher-order state. Explain the central difference between these two versions of the higher-order approach, and then critically evaluate whether the 'targetless higher-order representation' problem — the possibility that a higher-order state could occur without any first-order target — gives us reason to favor one version over the other. Be sure to consider how a defender of each theory would respond to the objection."

STUDENT ANSWER:

"Higher-order theories of consciousness all share the claim that a mental state M is conscious just in case there is a higher-order state H that represents M. The main divide is between higher-order thought (HOT) theory, defended by David Rosenthal, and higher-order perception (HOP) theory, defended by William Lycan.

On HOT theory, the higher-order state is a thought — a cognitive, assertoric state with conceptual content. It doesn't require any dedicated inner sense organ; it's just the same kind of cognitive capacity we use for ordinary thinking, directed inward. On HOP theory, the higher-order state is a quasi-perceptual state, produced by an 'inner sense' that scans first-order mental states. The key difference is that HOTs are cognitive and conceptual, while HOPs are sensory-like and may be non-conceptual.

The targetless higher-order representation objection says that if a higher-order state can occur in the absence of any first-order target — that is, if the higher-order system can misrepresent — then the theory predicts we could be conscious of a state that doesn't exist. This seems absurd.

This objection is much more damaging to HOP theory than to HOT theory. Perception can misrepresent — we can have hallucinations — so inner perception should be able to misrepresent too. If inner sense can fire without a target, HOP theory predicts we could have a conscious experience of, say, a red sensation when no such sensation exists. That seems like a serious problem.

HOT theory handles this better. Rosenthal argues that a HOT simply asserts that one is in a certain mental state. If the HOT occurs without a target, what happens is that it seems to the subject that they are in that state. But this isn't absurd — it's just a case of being mistaken about one's own mental life, which happens all the time. People think they're in pain when they're not, or think they're seeing something when they're not. So the targetless HOT isn't a problem; it just describes a familiar kind of introspective error.

However, there's a deeper issue. If HOT theory allows targetless HOTs, then it seems to allow consciousness without any first-order state at all — the HOT alone suffices for consciousness. But then HOT theory collapses into a weird kind of eliminativism about first-order states: all that matters for consciousness is the HOT. This seems to abandon the core higher-order insight that consciousness is a relation between two states. So while HOT theory fares better than HOP theory on the targetless representation objection, it may do so at the cost of undermining its own motivation."
