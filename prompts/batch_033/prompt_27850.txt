So I'm working on a piece about Goodhart's law and AI, and I keep running into what feels like an obvious objection that nobody addresses. The law, as usually quoted, is "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." But the classic examples — the boat-racing RL agent that spins in circles collecting bonus points, the recommendation algorithm that radicalizes people because engagement is the metric — these all involve really crude proxies. The fix seems straightforward: just build a richer objective. Instead of "maximize engagement," optimize for something like long-term user satisfaction as measured by a batter of surveys, retention patterns, and third-party wellbeing indices. If Goodhart's law were really a law, it would say that even this richer metric would fail. But that seems like a completely unfalsifiable claim — every time a metric works, you just say "well, the optimization pressure wasn't strong enough yet." Isn't Goodhart's law just unfalsifiable pessimism dressed up as insight?

I'd especially like to know what would change your mind on this — what argument or evidence would make you say "actually, Goodhart's law is overblown and specification gaming is just an engineering problem." And if you could wrap up with a one-sentence summary as the last line, that'd be great. American English please.
