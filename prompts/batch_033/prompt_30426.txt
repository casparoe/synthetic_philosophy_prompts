I am a professor of philosophy working on intergenerational justice, and I am developing a paper that uses a recent legal episode to test the reach and limits of our theoretical frameworks. I would like you to analyze Juliana v. United States — the constitutional climate lawsuit brought in 2015 by twenty-one young plaintiffs against the U.S. government — through the lens of intergenerational justice theory, and then reflect on what the litigation reveals about the theory in return.

Let me sketch the argument I am considering. In the version I find most plausible, the Juliana plaintiffs' case implicitly depends on something close to a Rawlsian just savings principle: the idea that each generation owes a duty to its successors to maintain institutions and environmental background conditions that make a decent life achievable. The complaint alleged that the federal government, with full knowledge of the consequences, affirmatively promoted fossil fuel extraction and combustion over decades, thereby loading catastrophic risks onto the young and the unborn. That looks like a paradigm violation of a cross-generational duty. But the case also exposes fault lines in the philosophical landscape. Parfit's non-identity problem threatens to dissolve the notion that particular future individuals have been wronged, since the very policies that generate climate disruption also determine which individuals come into existence. And there is the further difficulty, central to the discounting literature, of justifying the relative weighting of present and future interests when the future parties cannot participate in present decisions and their identities remain indeterminate.

What I am requesting from you is a disciplined analysis that moves in both directions — from theory onto the case, and from the case back onto the theory. Specifically, please address the following:

1. Map the Juliana litigation onto the central concepts of intergenerational justice. Who counts as the relevant parties in the theoretical model, and how well does the litigation capture the structure of intergenerational wronging as the theory characterizes it? Pay attention to where the legal framing — with its reliance on constitutional rights, the public trust doctrine, and the law of standing — aligns with philosophical analysis and where it forces divergences.

2. Evaluate whether Parfit's non-identity problem genuinely undercuts the plaintiffs' moral claim. On one reading, the fact that the plaintiffs are already-existing young people, rather than purely future persons, dissolves the difficulty. On another, it merely relocates it, since their injury claims depend on counterfactual comparisons to a policy regime that would have altered the conditions of their birth and upbringing. Which reading is stronger, and why?

3. Identify places where the case exposes weaknesses in intergenerational justice theory itself. Does the theory have adequate resources to explain why climate inaction constitutes a direct wrong to the specific young plaintiffs, as opposed to a diffuse wrong against humanity at large? Does it capture the distinctively institutional character of the wrong — the allegation that a particular government knowingly fostered a harmful energy system over many decades — or does the theory remain too abstract to register what makes this a wrong by identifiable actors rather than a tragedy of collective inaction?

4. Deliver a verdict: does Juliana v. United States ultimately strengthen or weaken the case for intergenerational justice as a framework for climate ethics? If it strengthens it, what is the most powerful theoretical lesson the case teaches? If it weakens it, what is the most serious theoretical problem the case exposes, and is that problem repairable within the framework?

Please write your analysis in American English, with the formality appropriate to a scholarly exchange among colleagues.
