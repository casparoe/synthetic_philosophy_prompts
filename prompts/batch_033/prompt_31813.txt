For a piece I'm working on about how online speech crosses the line from words into action, I've been reading the speech act literature and have settled into a set of views. Before I structure my argument around them, I'd like an audit of whether they hang together. Here they are:

(1) The constative/performative distinction is real but gradable — utterances sit on a spectrum rather than in two bins, and the interesting question is where a given utterance falls, not which category it belongs to.

(2) All utterances are actions, so the constative/performative distinction is ultimately pragmatic, not semantic: it tracks which aspect of an utterance we foreground, not what kind of sentence it is.

(3) Explicit performatives like "I promise to pay" are not a semantically special class. They have ordinary truth-conditional content, and the fact that uttering them constitutes the act described follows from what the words mean in context — not from a sui generis performative mechanism.

(4) Illocutionary force is grounded in the speaker's reflexive communicative intention — roughly, the intention to produce an effect in the hearer by means of the hearer's recognition of that intention — rather than in social convention.

(5) Some speech acts — marrying, sentencing, naming a ship — are institutionally constituted: their success conditions include the speaker's holding a designated role, and this requirement is not reducible to the speaker's communicative intentions.

(6) An insincere promise is still a promise. It succeeds as an illocutionary act, albeit defectively, rather than failing to be performed at all.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be in tension and why, and rank the tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. I'm not asking which view is right — I'm asking whether this particular combination can be held as stated.
