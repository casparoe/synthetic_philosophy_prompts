I've been puzzling over something that came up while I was trying to explain to a friend what makes a sentence more than just a list of words. Take "the cat is on the mat." It's not the same as the bare collection {the cat, the mat, the relation of being-on}. The sentence asserts that the relation actually holds between the cat and the mat — it presents them as unified in a particular way. So it seems we need some kind of glue: a metaphysical component corresponding to "instantiation" or "exemplification" that binds the cat, the relation, and the mat into a single fact or proposition. But then I have four things — the cat, the relation, the mat, and the instantiation tie — and these four don't yet form a unity either. To bind the instantiation tie to the original three, I seem to need a second-order instantiation tie. And then a third-order one to bind that one, and so on without end. No matter how many layers of binding I add, I always have a plurality of components that need further binding to become a single unified whole.

I've tried describing this to a few people, and the reactions are split between "that's obviously a deep problem" and "you're just confused about how relations work." I'd like to understand the landscape better. Does this regress have a standard name in metaphysics? What are the main families of response — do people deny that propositions have constituent structure, or deny that instantiation is a further constituent, or accept the regress but argue it's harmless? I'm less interested in a bare label and more interested in what the regress is supposed to demonstrate: which assumptions about propositions, relations, and unity generate it, and which assumption most philosophers think we should abandon.

Please end with \answer{...} containing the standard name for this regress, or \answer{no established name} if there isn't one.
