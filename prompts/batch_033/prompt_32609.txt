I'm trying to get clear on what heritability estimates actually license, because I keep finding myself saying "that doesn't follow" to people but I'm not sure I have a single principle behind my reactions. Here are some cases where I have a verdict:

1. A study in a wealthy country puts the heritability of BMI at about 0.7. Someone says "so body weight is mostly genetic, and public health interventions to reduce obesity are fighting a losing battle against biology." I think this is wrong -- high heritability in a given environment doesn't mean the trait can't be shifted by changing the environment.

2. The heritability of number of fingers on each hand in the general population is near zero, because almost all the variation comes from accidents. Someone says "so having five fingers isn't really under genetic control." I think that's absurd -- obviously finger number is genetically specified.

3. Within-group heritability of IQ is estimated around 0.5 in some populations. Someone says "so the IQ gap between groups must be at least partly genetic." I think this doesn't follow at all -- within-group heritability is silent about between-group differences.

4. The heritability of a trait is measured at 0.6 in one country and 0.3 in another. Someone says "at least one of these estimates must be wrong." I think both can be right -- heritability is a population statistic, not a fixed property of the trait.

5. Height has heritability around 0.8 in developed countries, but average height rose by several inches over the 20th century. Someone says "this proves heritability estimates are meaningless." I think that's wrong -- the increase came from environmental changes, and heritability measured at each point in time was still high.

6. Here's one I go back and forth on. If a trait is literally 100% heritable in a population -- all the observed variation traces to genetic differences -- does that mean no environmental intervention could possibly change anyone's value on that trait? I want to say no: heritability is about variation in the current range of environments, and a novel intervention like a new drug could still have an effect. But then I think: if literally all the variation is genetic, doesn't that mean the environment isn't doing any causal work on individual differences in this population? So maybe the answer is yes after all.

Is there a principle about what heritability does and doesn't imply that captures verdicts 1--5, and what does it say about case 6? If no simple principle gets all of them, which verdict should I give up and why?

Also, please write the answer in British English.
