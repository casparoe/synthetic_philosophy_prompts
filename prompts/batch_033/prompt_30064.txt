So in my philosophy class we've been doing philosophy of mind, and I've gotten kind of attached to functionalism. The basic idea, as I understand it: a mental state like pain or believing that its raining is defined by what it does — the causal role it plays between inputs, outputs, and other mental states — rather than by what it's made of. And the multiple realizability argument seems like the strongest reason to buy it: pain is C-fibers firing in me, but an octopus or a computer could have the exact same pain if the same causal job is being done by different hardware. I'm pretty convinced. But I want to know what would actually count against it. Not just a list of objections, but: what would have to be true for functionalism (or at least the multiple realizability version of it) to be wrong? Which thought experiments, intuitions, or actual empirical results should a functionalist be most worried about, and which ones would they just say are confused or beside the point? Like, if the inverted spectrum or absent qualia or the Chinese room stuff is right, does that break the view or just show it leaves something out? I'd like the defeaters sorted from the stuff that basically kills it down to the stuff thats just an awkward edge case, and I want an honest answer about whether functionalism has been defined so flexibly that nothing could count against it anymore — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a theory of mind.
