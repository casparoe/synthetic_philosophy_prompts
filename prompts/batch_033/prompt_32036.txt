**Question (from the exam):**

State Adams's Thesis for indicative conditionals, being careful about what kind of claim it is (a claim about truth conditions, about rational credence, about assertability, or something else). Then explain the structure and the upshot of Lewis's triviality results. What exactly do they show about the prospects of assigning truth conditions to indicative conditionals? Finally, describe one standard response to the results and assess it briefly.

---

**Student's answer:**

Adams's Thesis says that the probability of an indicative conditional "If A then C" equals the conditional probability of C given A, P(C|A), whenever P(A) > 0. Adams intended this as a constraint on rational credence and assertability, not as a truth-conditional semantics; on his view conditionals may lack truth conditions altogether. Lewis showed that this position is unstable. His first triviality result proves that any probability function satisfying the Thesis must assign every proposition probability 0 or 1. The proof expands the probability of the conditional by cases on C: P(A → C) = P(A → C | C) P(C) + P(A → C | ¬C) P(¬C). Applying the Thesis to the conditionalized probabilities yields P(A → C | C) = P(C | A ∧ C) = 1 and P(A → C | ¬C) = P(C | A ∧ ¬C) = 0, so P(C | A) = P(C). Since this holds for every A and C, the probability function is trivial. The upshot is that indicative conditionals cannot have truth conditions at all, and Adams's Thesis is false; the only remaining option is to adopt the material conditional, whose probability matches P(C | A) in the special case where A and C are independent. Adams's own response was to deny that conditionals express propositions, but this is merely a retreat and does not explain why the Thesis seemed right.
