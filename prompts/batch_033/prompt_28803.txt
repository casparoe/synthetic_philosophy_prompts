Consider the lottery paradox and the preface paradox as a pair of constraints on any theory of rational belief or acceptance. Both put pressure on the principle that rational acceptance is closed under conjunction, but they do so from different directions: the lottery paradox through high-probability evidence, and the preface paradox through a reflective judgment of one's own fallibility.

Below are five broad strategies that have been proposed for handling one or both paradoxes. Rank them as unified treatments of the two paradoxes taken together — that is, by how well each strategy offers one principled diagnosis that handles both cases without excessive damage to ordinary epistemic practice. Present the result as a tier list (top, middle, bottom; ties allowed). For each strategy, give one sentence of justification for its placement, and where two strategies are close, say in a clause what tips the balance. Do not spend time summarizing the paradoxes beyond what your ranking needs.

Strategies:

A. Reject conjunctive closure for rational acceptance or belief. The rational acceptability of each member of a set does not ensure the rational acceptability of their conjunction. Applied to the lottery, one may accept of each ticket that it loses without accepting that every ticket loses; applied to the preface, an author may accept each claim in her book without accepting the book's overall error-free conjunction.

B. Strengthen the threshold for acceptance or belief. High probability is not sufficient; acceptance requires knowledge, certainty, or satisfaction of a contextually set epistemic standard. This blocks the lottery paradox by denying that one should accept that one's ticket loses. To handle the preface paradox, the strategy must then either deny that the author properly accepts each individual claim in the relevant closure-governed sense, or deny that she properly accepts the preface claim itself; the difficulty is doing this without special pleading.

C. Eliminate or deflate binary acceptance in favor of graded credence. The paradoxes are treated as artifacts of an all-or-nothing attitude that is not theoretically fundamental; once the agent is modeled as having only degrees of belief, there is no closure principle of the offending kind left to violate. The cost is that ordinary talk of belief, acceptance, and assertion must be explained away or reconstructed pragmatically.

D. Permit rational but inconsistent belief sets. The correct response to the two paradoxes is to allow that a rational agent may believe each of p1,...,pn and also believe that at least one of them is false; consistency is not a requirement of rationality. The burden is to explain which inconsistencies are benign and why the usual ideal of consistency should be abandoned.

E. Distinguish two or more epistemic attitudes. For example, separate low-stakes acceptance from outright belief or assertion, or separate categorical belief from high credence plus a pragmatic policy. The paradoxes are diagnosed as conflating attitudes with different evidential thresholds and different closure properties. The risk is that the distinctions look gerrymandered unless they are independently motivated.
