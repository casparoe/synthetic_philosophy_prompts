I am a doctoral student in political theory, and last week I sat in on an argument between two colleagues, Clara and Ben, that has stayed with me. Our department had just awarded its annual research prize to a student whose dissertation chapter is genuinely original but came together quickly and with little visible strain; another student, who had worked far harder but produced a less striking chapter, was passed over. I was not part of the argument, but I took notes. (1) Clara said the committee got it right: the prize is for the quality of the contribution, and the person who produces the better work deserves the award; rewarding effort instead would turn a merit prize into a consolation prize. (2) Ben replied that this is exactly backwards as a claim about desert. Nobody earns their native ability, so the effortlessly brilliant student cannot be said to deserve anything that flows from it; effort is the only input a person can plausibly claim as their own, so if the prize is about desert it should track effort, not raw talent. (3) Clara answered that effort is no more earned than ability — it depends on temperament, upbringing, and constitution — so the only-reward-what-is-within-a-person's-control test does not rescue effort-based desert; it destroys desert altogether, and in any case rewarding effort regardless of output would encourage wheel-spinning. (4) Ben conceded that effort-based desert is unstable, but said this cuts both ways: once desert talk is shown to be shaky all the way down, Clara can no longer say the brilliant student deserved the prize. What the committee can say is that allocating the prize to the stronger work is instrumentally useful, since it directs resources toward whoever is likely to produce the most; but that is an incentive justification, not a desert justification. (5) Clara replied that the disagreement had collapsed: meritocracy as a selection principle just is the practice of allocating by predicted contribution, and it never needed a metaphysics of desert; since Ben now grants that the prize was rightly awarded on incentive grounds, he has conceded everything that was actually at stake.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher working on distributive justice and moral responsibility, and to adjudicate this exchange carefully. Begin by separating the different things "desert" and "merit" might mean here, then go through the five moves in order and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Say who is right about which specific point, and — most useful to me — what the two of them are jointly missing, if anything.
