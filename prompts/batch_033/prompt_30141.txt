I've been in a reading group on normative ethics for about a year now, and we spent the last three sessions on the demandingness objection to consequentialism, and honestly I'm frustrated — not because I think either side is obviously right, but because the debate seems completely frozen. The structure is always the same. Someone points out that act-consequentialism, taken seriously, would require a comfortably-off person in a rich country to give away nearly everything they have to the global poor, to never take a vacation, to work as many hours as possible in the highest-paying job and donate the surplus, basically to treat their own projects and relationships as a rounding error. The critic says this is absurd — no plausible moral theory can demand that. The consequentialist then picks one of maybe four responses: bite the bullet and say yes, morality really is that demanding; retreat to some indirect form like rule-consequentialism; go scalar and say actions aren't right or wrong, just better or worse, so there's no sharp "demand"; or introduce agent-centered prerogatives that let you give some weight to your own interests, which to me always looks like deontology in a consequentialist trenchcoat. And then the critic says the bullet-biting is inhuman, the rule-consequentialism collapses back into act-consequentialism or is ad hoc, the scalar view changes the subject, and the prerogatives give away the game. Nobody moves. Rinse and repeat since the 70s.

What bugs me is that both sides seem to agree on basically all the non-normative facts. They agree about how much good a dollar can do, about the scale of global poverty, about human psychology and motivational limits. So what kind of disagreement is this? My hunch is that it's a disagreement about what the job description of a moral theory is — the consequentialist thinks a theory tells you what the ideal agent does, full stop, and the fact that you can't bring yourself to do it is neither here nor there; the critic thinks a moral theory has to be action-guiding for the kind of creatures we actually are, and a theory that issues demands you predictably won't meet has failed on it's own terms. If that's right, they're not really disagreeing about morality, they're disagreeing about what it means to give an account of morality. But maybe I'm wrong — maybe there's a hidden empirical disagreement about what level of sacrifice people can actually sustain long-term, or maybe it's just a bedrock clash of intuitions about whether the point of morality is to make the world as good as possible or to specify terms of reasonable interaction among persons. Diagnose this. What kind of disagreement is it, really? And given your diagnosis, what — if anything — would count as progress? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
