I'm a retired chemist who's been reading moral philosophy for a couple years, and there's a pattern I keep noticing that I don't have a word for. It goes like this. A friend of mine cheats on his taxes, then turns around and blames his brother-in-law for doing the same thing. Everything he says about the brother-in-law is true — it really was wrong, the guy really did it — and yet something feels off. Not off in the sense that the criticism is false, because it isn't. It's more that my friend seems to lack the... I don't know, the place? the authority? the right? to be the one saying it. Another instance: a colleague who never does his share of lab chores criticizes a new postdoc for not doing hers. Again the content is right, but I want to say he's in no position to make the complaint. I've seen the terms 'tu quoque' and 'ad hominem' used for this kind of situation, but those seem to be about deflecting an argument by pointing at the speaker; here the argument is fine and the speaker is the problem.

I don't just want a label. I want to know whether there's an established concept for the thing you lose when your own hands are dirty, how it differs from plain hypocrisy as a character flaw and from tu quoque, and where its edges are. Does the defect attach to the blame itself, or only to the act of voicing it? Does it apply to all moral criticism or only to blame? Do you lose it forever, or can you get it back by confessing? You're a professional philosopher working on moral responsibility and the ethics of blame. Please think carefully, and if it helps, start by brainstorming the nearest concepts before you settle on an answer.
