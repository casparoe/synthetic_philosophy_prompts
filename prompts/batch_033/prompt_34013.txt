I'm on a working group for our county's pretrial services office, and we're revising the policy on how risk-assessment scores get presented to judges at bail hearings. The group has gotten stuck on one concrete case, and I've been asked to draft the decision memo we'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. The defendant is a 34-year-old man charged with felony theft and one prior failure to appear. The tool spits out a single number, but the underlying data tells a messier story: across all defendants in the county last year the failure-to-appear rate was about 21%; among men aged 30 to 39 with a prior FTA it was about 43%; and among the small slice matching his full profile (age, charge type, zip code, prior record) it was 76% — but that cell only has 17 people in it. Some members think the narrowest class is obviously the right one, since its the most similar group to the defendant. Others say a cell of 17 is just noise and the intermediate class is the best balance. A third faction says the whole question is badly posed: there is no fact about this defendant's real probability until you fix a reference class, and the data by itself doesn't fix one (one member keeps muttering about the reference class problem, which has not settled anything).

Draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete policy for how scores get reported to judges — a single number, a range across reference classes, or not at all — how judges should be instructed to use whatever we give them, and a timeline for putting it in place. It should take the strongest version of the opposing views seriously, especially the view that the most specific matching class is the only defensible one, rather than just restating the legal standard for bail.
