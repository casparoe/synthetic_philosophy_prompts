Question:
Explain the disagreement between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness. Cover at least: (i) whether they accept bivalence, (ii) whether they accept excluded middle, and (iii) what they say about whether there is a sharp cutoff in a sorites series.

Student's answer:
Epistemicism says vagueness is a matter of ignorance, not a matter of the world or language being fuzzy. For any sentence, it is either true or false, so bivalence holds. That means in a sorites series from heap to non-heap, there is a sharp cutoff point, even though nobody can know where it is. Supervaluationism disagrees: it says borderline sentences are neither true nor false, because they are true on some admissible precisifications and false on others. But it still wants to keep classical logic. It does this by saying a sentence is true simpliciter iff it is true on every precisification. Since on any one precisification the sentence "either this is a heap or it isn't" comes out true, that sentence is true on every precisification. So supervaluationism keeps bivalence as well—every instance of excluded middle is true. The real difference is just about the cutoff: the epistemicist says there is a cutoff we can't know, while the supervaluationist says there is no cutoff at all, because no precisification is singled out as the correct one.
