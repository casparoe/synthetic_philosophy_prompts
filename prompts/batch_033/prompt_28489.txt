I'm on an interpretability team and have to write a short where-I-stand doc before a joint workshop with some philosophers of science, and I'd rather get my own view sorted before I present it. I mostly work with sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and activation steering, and I've ended up committed to the following five claims. They each feel right, but I have a nagging sense they don't all hang together, and I can't locate the problem myself.

1. I'm a realist about the features our SAEs find: the big, steerable ones are the network's actual joints, the units in terms of which the computation is really organized, not just a convenient redescription of the weights.
2. The only evidence that counts for calling something a representation is causal. If you can't intervene on the candidate and produce the downstream changes its content predicts, you don't have a representation. Correlational or similiarity-based evidence is suggestive at best.
3. I don't think there is a unique correct decomposition of a network into features. Different dictionaries, trained differently, can find different features that each pass the causal tests, and I don't think there's a fact of the matter about which dictionary is the right one.
4. What a feature is about is fixed by its functional role — what downstream behavior it supports — not by any intrinsic resemblance between the activation pattern and the thing represented.
5. When I actually choose between interpretability methods, I'm an instrumentalist: the best method is whichever lets me predict and control the model best, full stop. I don't think I have any other grip on better method than that.

Could you audit this set? I want the pairs that are in genuine tension, ordered by severity, with what would have to give in each case and what the revision would cost. Also tell me which pairs only look like they're in tension and dissolve on inspection, because I don't want to go to the workshop flagging false alarms. Be blunt about which commitment is doing the least work.
