I am a self-taught control-systems programmer with no academic background. For years I have been turning over the old story of the digger wasp Sphex: it drags a paralysed cricket to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect, comes back out, and only then drags the cricket in. If someone moves the cricket a few centimetres while the wasp is inside, the wasp does not simply grab the cricket where it now lies; it repeats the whole routine — drag the cricket back, leave it at the entrance, go inside, come out, drag it in — over and over. The behaviour looks intelligent at first and mechanical at second glance. The word sometimes used for this is sphexishness, and the philosophical worry I keep coming back to is this: if apparently thoughtful behaviour can be a rigid program, then perhaps our own flexibility is just the same thing with more states, and the difference between a free action and a reflex is a difference in the size of the program rather than in kind.

I think about this in the vocabulary I actually use at work: finite state machines, states and transitions, guard conditions, sensor polling versus interrupts, reactive versus model-based control, behaviour trees, error recovery routines, and goal states. I am fluent in that vocabulary and only an amateur in philosophy.
