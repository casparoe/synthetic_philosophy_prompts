So I was at a backyard thing last weekend and two friends got completely derailed arguing about baldness, of all things. It started when someone pointed out a guy across the yard whose hairline was receding and Carlos said "he's not bald yet" and Mina shot back "okay, so how many hairs does he have to lose before he is?" Carlos said that's ridiculous, there's no exact number, that's not how words like "bald" work. Mina said fine, but walk it through: you start with a guy with a full head of hair, pluck one hair, obviously not bald; pluck another, still not bald. If no single hair ever makes the difference, then you can go all the way down to zero and still say "not bald," which is crazy. So there has to be a step where it flips. Carlos said that's literally the sorites paradox and it doesn't prove there's a sharp cutoff, it proves our concepts are vague and classical logic gets the wrong result when you chain conditionals like that. Mina said calling it "vague" is just a label for "I refuse to admit there's a boundary because I can't find it." She said if you really think there's no fact of the matter about whether someone with exactly X hairs is bald, then for borderline cases the statement "he is bald" is neither true nor false — and you've just given up bivalence, which is a way bigger deal than admitting there's a cutoff you cant pinpoint. Carlos said bivalence was never a good fit for natural language in the first place, and if she's so confident there's a precise boundary, what kind of fact is it? Is it stored in our brains? In the dictionary? In some ideal physics of scalps? Mina said it's just the semantic value of the word, which is set by our usage, but our usage can determine a boundary we don't consciously know — lots of things we do determine facts we can't introspect. Carlos said that's spooky and unfalsifiable. Mina said his view has worse problems, because if "bald" has borderline cases where the sentence is neither true nor false, then "it is borderline whether he is bald" is itself either true or false, and you get higher-order vagueness that never bottoms out. At that point someone opened another bottle of wine and I lost the thread. I've been turning it over since and I genuinely can't tell who's on stronger ground.
