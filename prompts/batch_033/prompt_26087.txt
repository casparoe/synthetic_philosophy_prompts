I was arguing with my roommate about effective altruism the other night — he's been getting into it and keeps saying stuff like "if you're not donating half your income to the most cost-effective charities you're basically letting people die." I pushed back that a life lived that way sounds miserable and narrow, and he shot back that morality doesn't care whether its demands are comfortable. Which, fine. But I feel like there's an argument lurking here that if it works proves way too much, and if it fails it might pull down the very idea that morality is overriding. I tried to write it out clean and now I'm stuck in it:

(1) Morality makes claims on us that override other kinds of reasons — if I morally ought to do X, the fact that X is boring or hard or conflicts with my hobbies doesn't get me off the hook.
(2) If morality's reasons override everything else, then the rational response is to be as morally good as possible — to maximize moral value in your life.
(3) Being as morally good as possible looks an awful lot like being a moral saint: someone whose time, energy, and resources are overwhelmingly devoted to moral projects — relieving suffering, promoting justice, that sort of thing.
(4) But a moral saint can't really cultivate the kinds of non-moral goods that make a life rich — developing a subtle aesthetic sensibility, becoming a really good cook, deep friendships that aren't instrumentally aimed at doing good, athletic excellence, all of that. These things take enormous time and attention, and from the saint's perspective that time is better spent saving lives or preventing harm.
(5) A life stripped of those goods isn't one we'd wish for the people we love. If my kid told me they wanted to be a moral saint — give away everything, no hobbies, choose friends solely by who they could help most — I'd be devastated, not proud.
(6) So morality demands of us a life we can't wholeheartedly endorse as an ideal.
(7) But if that's what morality demands, and we're right to recoil from it, then morality doesn't actually have the authority we thought — it's just one source of reasons among others, and sometimes we should tell it to take a back seat.
(8) That can't be right either — it feels like giving up on something central to what morality is.

So the argument seems to show that morality either demands too much or doesn't demand at all. Where does it actually go wrong? If I have to pick a premise to give up, I'd sacrifice (2) before (1) or (7), but I'm not sure I can do that cleanly. What are my options, and what am I paying for each?
