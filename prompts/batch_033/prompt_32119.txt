I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so I may be missing something basic. I've been reading about Jain epistemology on the side, and I'm stuck on what looks like a null-pointer exception in the doctrine of anekāntavāda.

As I understand it, anekāntavāda says that reality is irreducibly complex, and any claim about it captures only a partial truth — it's true in some respects, false in others, and the full picture requires holding multiple perspectives at once. This is formalized in syādvāda, the logic of conditional predication, where statements are prefixed with "syād" (roughly "in some respect" or "maybe").

Here's my problem. Take the statement S: "All claims about reality are only partially true; no claim is unconditionally or absolutely true." If S is itself a claim about reality, then by its own criterion S is only partially true, which means there's some respect in which S is false — i.e., some respect in which there ARE absolutely true claims. But if S is not a claim about reality (if it's meant to be a meta-level principle that doesn't apply to itself), then the view has a special exemption that looks ad hoc, and I don't see what principled reason a Jain could give for why S doesn't fall under its own scope. Either way, anekāntavāda seems to self-destruct.

I realize Jains have been at this for a couple thousand years and presumably have an answer. I just can't figure out what it is from the introductory material I've read. What am I getting wrong? And please tell me what argument or consideration would be most likely to change your mind about your interpretation of how anekāntavāda handles this — where your analysis is most vulnerable.
