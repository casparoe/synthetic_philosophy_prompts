A friend of mine has been pushing this argument for weeks and I can't find the exit, but the conclusion feels impossible to accept. It runs like this.

(1) If A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C.
(2) Consider a long sequence of possible worlds, P0 through P1000. P0 contains one billion people, all living extraordinarily good lives. P1 contains two billion people, each living a life only a tiny bit less good than the people in P0. P2 contains four billion, each a tiny bit less good again. Keep going: each step doubles the population and lowers the quality of each life by an amount so small that, comparing any two neighboring worlds, the next one seems clearly better — twice as many people, and each life only a hair worse.
(3) By transitivity, then, P1000 is better than P0.
(4) But P1000 contains an astronomically large number of people whose lives are only barely worth living — mild contentment, no real achievement, nothing you'd call flourishing. Surely P0 is better than P1000.
(5) So P1000 is both better than P0 and not better than P0.

He says the only ways out are to deny transitivity, accept that a vast number of barely-worth-living lives really is better than a billion flourishing ones, or find some principled place to break the chain. I want to keep transitivity and I also don't want to accept his conclusion, but every place I try to break the chain looks arbitrary. Wich step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Is the argument sneakier than it looks, or is it valid and just forcing me into a bad trade?
