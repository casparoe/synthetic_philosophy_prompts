I'm a novelist, and I've been stuck on a scene for weeks. The book is set in the early 1980s. One of my characters is a retired strategist who spent his career at a defense think tank building game-theoretic models of escalation and brinkmanship, and in this scene he's explaining to his daughter—a philosophy graduate student—why he walked away from the whole enterprise. I want her to be a real match for him, not a sounding board, but I need to get the argument right before I can write her reply.

His argument runs like this:

(1) An action is rational only if, at the moment you perform it, it does better than the alternatives at promoting what you care about then and there.

(2) Suppose your country has just absorbed a massive nuclear strike. Ordering retaliation doesn't bring back the dead, doesn't protect the survivors—it probably invites whatever remains of the enemy arsenal to finish the job—and it makes the world worse for everyone still alive. So retaliation fails the test in (1).

(3) A threat to do something it would be irrational to do when the moment comes is not a threat a rational adversary will take seriously; the adversary can see that you would be better off not carrying it out.

(4) A threat of nuclear retaliation deters only if the adversary takes it seriously enough to change course.

(5) Therefore a rational adversary cannot be deterred by a threat of massive retaliation.

(6) But the whole doctrine of mutual assured destruction assumes that rational adversaries are deterred by precisely that threat.

(7) So either deterrence theory has rationality wrong, or deterrence works only because the threatener stops being rational in sense (1)—precommits, automates the response, or cultivates a reputation for following through even when following through is against his interests.

(8) And the same reasoning applies to every costly threat: a company vowing to sue a small competitor into bankruptcy, a parent promising to take away the car even though she hates chaufering. If the reasoning were sound, no one could ever make a credible threat to do something they'd rather not do. But such threats do work. So the argument proves too much, and something in the premises has to give.

My instinct is that the daughter should accept as much of this as she can, and then locate the exact step where it breaks—or, if it's valid, say which premise she should give up and what she's paying for it. She'd like to hang onto (1) and (2) if possible; she thinks being rational is choosing well at the moment, and she thinks the retaliatory strike really is pointless once the missiles have flown. But she also refuses to believe that all threats are bluffs. So: which step should she reject, and what does rejecting it cost her? And why did that step look so plausible in the first place?
