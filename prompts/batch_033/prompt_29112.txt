**Draft Essay: The Discursive Dilemma and the Inescapability of Group Agency**

The discursive dilemma, introduced by Philip Pettit and Christian List, is standardly presented as a technical problem in social choice theory: when a group votes on logically interconnected propositions, majority voting on each proposition can yield an inconsistent set of group judgments, even when every individual member holds perfectly consistent views. A three-judge panel illustrates the point. Each judge must rule on whether a valid contract existed and whether it was breached; liability follows only if both conditions obtain. Judge A finds a contract but no breach, Judge B finds a breach but no contract, and Judge C finds both. Each judge's individual judgments are consistent: A and B conclude no liability, C concludes liability. Yet if the panel votes on each premise separately, the majority finds a contract (A and C) and a breach (B and C), which entails liability — a conclusion that a majority of judges individually rejects.

The standard response treats this as a procedural choice: should the group use premise-based voting (vote on each premise, then deduce the conclusion) or conclusion-based voting (vote directly on the conclusion, ignoring the premises)? Much of the literature frames this as a trade-off between collective rationality and responsiveness to individual views. Premise-based voting ensures that the group's judgments are internally consistent and driven by reasons, but it can override the majority's view on the conclusion. Conclusion-based voting respects the majority on the bottom-line question but severs the connection between the group's conclusion and the reasons that are supposed to support it.

I want to argue that this framing obscures a deeper point. The dilemma is not merely about which voting procedure to adopt; it reveals that any group that aspires to be more than a mere aggregate of individual preferences must take on some of the attributes of a group agent. Specifically, the dilemma shows that collective rationality and individual responsiveness cannot be fully reconciled through any mechanical aggregation rule — a point that List and Pettit themselves have developed in their work on impossibility results in judgment aggregation. But the implication I want to draw is stronger: the very attempt to construct a reasoned group position pushes a group toward agency, because resolving the tension between premises and conclusions requires something like a group-level deliberative capacity that cannot be reduced to individual votes.

Why does this matter? Because much of deliberative democratic theory operates with an implicit picture in which groups can be both fully responsive to individual citizens and fully rational in their collective outputs. The discursive dilemma, on my reading, shows that this picture is incoherent. If a group is to maintain propositional consistency across its judgments — if it is to function as a rational interlocutor in public discourse — it must sometimes form judgments that are not simple functions of individual votes on each proposition. This means the group must have a kind of "mind of its own," however minimal. Deliberative democrats who are squeamish about group agency must therefore either accept that democratic bodies will sometimes be collectively irrational (in the sense of holding inconsistent sets of propositions) or give up the ideal that every collective judgment should track individual judgments in a straightforward, proposition-by-proposition way.

I conclude that the discursive dilemma is not a bug in democratic procedure but a feature that reveals the genuine stakes: democracy requires us to take group agency seriously, with all the normative baggage that entails — including questions about whether group agents can bear responsibility, have intentions, or possess epistemic virtues that differ from those of their members.
