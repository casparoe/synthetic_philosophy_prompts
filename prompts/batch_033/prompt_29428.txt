ok so im taking this ethics course and we've been doing theories of well-being — hedonism, desire-satisfaction, objective list, all that. and i thought i had a clear view but then i started writing down my reactions to different cases and now im totally confused. id love to know what theory of well-being actually fits my verdicts, or which verdicts i need to abandon.

here are the cases and what i think:

1. the experience machine (nozick's original). i wouldnt plug in. a real life with real achievements and real relationships is better than a simulated one, even if from the inside you cant tell the difference. so hedonism seems wrong.

2. the deceived spouse. someone's husband is secretly cheating and despises her, but he acts perfectly loving, and she feels loved and happy. i think her life is going worse than she thinks it is, and worse than an identical life where the husband really does love her. so well-being isnt just about your mental states.

3. the happy sadist. someone genuinely enjoys torturing people, feels fulfilled by it, never gets caught, and dies satisfied. i dont think their life is going well. getting what you want isnt enough if what you want is monstrous.

4. the grass counter (rawls). someone whose only desire is to count blades of grass, and they do it, and theyre content. honestly i think their life is going fine. if thats what they want and theyre not hurting anyone, who am i to say otherwise. but this feels like it conflicts with case 3 — why is the sadist's life not going well but the grass counter's is? is it just that harming others is the line? or am i being inconsistent?

5. the lonely genius. makes a massive scientific breakthrough that saves millions of lives, but is personally miserable, has no friends, and dies thinking they wasted their life. did their life go well *for them*? i want to say it was a valuable life but not a good life for the person living it. but then i dont know how to separate those two things.

6. the contented delusion. someone falsely believes theyre a beloved celebrity, and this makes them happy. but actually theyre just a normal person living a quiet life, and their belief is totally disconnected from reality. i think their life is not going well — the happiness is built on a lie. but then why did i say the grass counter's life IS going well? is it because the grass counter isnt deluded about what hes doing, or because his desire is actually satisfied (he really is counting grass) whereas the deluded person's desires arent actually satisfied (theyre not actually a celebrity, they just think they are)?

7. posthumous ruin. someone lives a great life, acheives a lot, dies happy. then immediately after they die, everything they built is destroyed and their reputation is permanently trashed. did their life go worse? i lean no — you cant be harmed after you die. but i also feel like something bad happened *to their life*, and i dont know what to do with that feeling.

so yeah. hedonism is out (cases 1 and 2). simple desire-satisfaction is out (case 3, and maybe 6). objective list theories seem to handle 3 and 5 but i dont know what they say about 4. and i have no idea what any theory says about 7.

what theory of well-being best captures these verdicts? which ones do i have to give up? and what does that theory say about someone who voluntarily plugs into the experience machine fully aware of what it is, because they genuinely value pleasure above everything else — is their life going well, or are they making a mistake about what's good for them?
