I've been working in computational neuroscience for a few years now, and over time I've found myself increasingly convinced by the predictive processing framework — roughly, the idea that the brain is fundamentally a hierarchical prediction engine that minimizes prediction error either by updating its internal generative models or by acting to bring sensory inputs in line with its predictions. What drew me in wasn't just the elegance of the free energy principle as a mathematical formulation, but the way the framework seems to absorb so many otherwise disparate phenomena under a single organizing idea: binocular rivalry, the rubber hand illusion, the way placebos work, motor control recast as active inference, even certain forms of psychopathology understood as failures of precision-weighting. It feels like one of those rare cases where a theory earns its grandiosity.

But I've also noticed a pattern that makes me uneasy, and I want to take that unease seriously rather than dismiss it. A colleague of mine — sympathetic but skeptical — recently remarked that predictive processing has become "the sort of theory that explains everything and therefore risks explaining nothing." That stung, because I've had the same thought late at night. So here's what I'd like: I hold the view that predictive processing, grounded in the free energy principle, is the best account we currently have of perception, action, and cortical organization. Tell me what would count against it. I want the evidence, arguments, and conceptual challenges that should genuinely move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be — from results that would be fatal to results that would merely require modest revision. I'm particularly interested in three things. First, empirical findings that the framework struggles to accommodate or that its rivals handle more naturally — are there perceptual phenomena, neural signatures, or behavioral patterns that look like genuine anomalies for the predictive story? Second, conceptual or mathematical critiques suggesting the free energy principle is too thin to do real explanatory work — that it's more of a tautological organizing scheme than a falsifiable theory. And third, whether the framework has been qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a theory of this kind or just the price of doing business at this level of generality. I'd like an honest map of the position's exposure, not a prosecutor's brief.
