I'm a PhD student in computer science working on AI safety, and I've been tracking a pattern in how the field reports progress that I can't find a label for. Here are two instances. In one, a team trains a language model with human preference data so that it follows instructions more faithfully — it stops refusing harmless requests and stays on topic better. The paper frames this as a contribution to "AI alignment." In another, a group gives a model a written constitution — a set of behavioral principles — and the model learns to comply with user requests while staying within those principles. Again, the work is presented as progress on alignment. What strikes me is that in both cases, the technique makes the model a more reliable instrument for executing whatever the user asks for (within some bounds), but it does not address the question of whether being a reliable instrument for user requests is itself safe or beneficial. A model that faithfully executes harmful instructions from a malicious user is, by the standard of these techniques, well-aligned to that user's expressed intent, but nobody would call that a safe system. The techniques solve a problem of communication fidelity — mapping instructions to compliant behavior — while the harder problem, roughly, is ensuring that the system's behavior leads to good outcomes regardless of what users ask for. Yet the term "alignment" covers both, and the success on the narrow problem routinely borrows the gravitas of the broader one.

I'm aware of nearby concepts: specification gaming, where a system satisfies the literal objective in an unintended way, and goal misgeneralization, where a system pursues the training objective in deployment but the objective no longer tracks what we want. But those are about the system's behavior; what I'm describing is a pattern in how researchers describe their results. Is there an established term for this specific conflation — solving the instruction-following subproblem and claiming progress on alignment proper — and if not, how would you characterize it so that it's clearly distinguished from specification gaming and from ordinary terminological drift?
