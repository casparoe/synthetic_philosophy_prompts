I've been covering political philosophy for years, and I find myself increasingly puzzled by the way the field has — or rather, has not — absorbed Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument. As a refresher: Nozick asks us to imagine a society that has achieved some favoured distributive pattern, say strict equality. Wilt Chamberlain, a basketball star, signs a contract whereby each spectator voluntarily drops twenty-five cents into a special box, and by the end of the season he walks away with $250,000, while everyone else has twenty-five cents less. The final distribution is wildly unequal, yet every step was voluntary, and the starting point was, by hypothesis, just. Nozick's conclusion is that patterned principles of justice — equality, distribution by need, by moral desert — cannot be squared with liberty, because liberty upsets patterns. The only defensible theory of justice, he argues, is a historical, entitlement-based one: justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and rectification of past injustice.

This argument has always struck me as devastating. If you grant the premise — that the initial distribution was just and that the transfers were free — the conclusion seems inescapable. And yet, decades later, the mainstream of political philosophy continues to operate as though Nozick had never written. Egalitarian liberals, luck egalitarians, capability theorists, and even some democratic socialists still defend patterned distributions, still argue for progressive taxation and the welfare state, still treat economic inequality as a presumptive injustice. I cannot tell whether I am missing a subtle but decisive objection to the Chamberlain argument, or whether the profession has simply found the implications too uncomfortable to take seriously. If the Chamberlain case succeeds, what — precisely — is the egalitarian's remaining foothold?
