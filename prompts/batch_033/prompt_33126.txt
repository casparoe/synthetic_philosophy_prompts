So I've been going back and forth with a friend who works on AI safety — she does stuff with control evals and untrusted monitoring — and she's got this line about the "control" agenda that I cant shake off. She says the whole idea of controlling a possibly-misaligned model (monitoring it, sandboxing it, running honeypots, capping its capabilities) is confused at the core. I tried to write her argument out cleanly, and it goes like this:

(1) Control measures are exactly the safety measures you'd only bother with if you think the model might be misaligned. If you were confident the model was aligned, you wouldn't need them.
(2) A control measure only reduces risk if the model doesn't reliably get around it.
(3) If the model is misaligned, it's optimizing for something other than what you want, and any control measure that's actually doing anything is an obstacle between the model and that thing.
(4) So a misaligned model has just as much reason to circumvent the measure as it has to pursue its own goals in the first place.
(5) Therefore, for a control measure to reduce risk, the model has to already be aligned enough not to try to subvert that measure.
(6) But "aligned enough not to subvert the measure" just is a local bit of alignment. So control doesn't get you around the alignment problem — it smuggles alignment back in under another name.
(7) Hence control either fails (if the model is misaligned) or is redundant (if it isn't). There's no middle path.

What bothers me is that this seems to prove too much. Step (8), the one she doesn't say out loud, is that the same reasoning applies to every other safety technique too — interpretability, evals, red-teaming, even writing the safety spec in the first place — since a misaligned model (or for that matter a misaligned human) could subvert any of them. So either she's shown that no safety work makes sense unless you already have alignment, or something slipped in the middle.

I want to keep the idea that layered control measures can genuinely buy you something even when you're assuming real misalignment, but I can't find the exact step to reject, and I don't know what I'd be loosing. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
