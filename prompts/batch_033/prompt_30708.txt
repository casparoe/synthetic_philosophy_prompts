Evolutionary psychologists commonly explain a present-day human trait — sexual jealousy, a language-acquisition instinct, certain mate preferences — by proposing that it is an adaptation shaped by selection in ancestral environments, and they defend the proposal with a mix of cross-cultural data, experiments on modern subjects, and reverse-engineering from the trait's presumed contribution to fitness. Reconstruct the theory of evidence and explanation this method presupposes. In particular: what does it assume about the legitimacy of inferring unobserved ancestral causes from present-day behavioral data; what evidential role does a merely plausible adaptive story play, as distinct from its heuristic role in generating hypotheses; and is the method committed to strong adaptationism, the claim that every trait is an adaptation, or only to the weaker claim that adaptationist hypotheses deserve to be pursued? Then assess the just-so story objection: is it best understood as charging that the practice violates its own implicit standards of evidence, or as rejecting those standards themselves — and what would a practice that took the objection seriously have to do differently?
