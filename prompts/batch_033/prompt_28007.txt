Two people in my ethics study group, Maya and Dev, got into a long argument after we read about the internal/external reasons debate. The case that set it off was a friend's cousin, Omar. His doctor told him he needs to exercise or he'll likely have a heart attack, but Omar doesn't care about living long, doesn't care about his health, doesn't enjoy moving his body, and has no relevant false beliefs—he just isn't motivated to exercise. Maya said it makes no sense to say Omar has a reason to exercise. Dev said that's obviously wrong. It went roughly like this:

(1) Maya: A reason for an agent has to be able to move that agent, at least after sound deliberation from what they already care about. Nothing in Omar's actual motivational set leads to exercise, so he doesn't have a reason to exercise. Saying he has one anyway is like asserting a reason for nobody.

(2) Dev: That psychologizes reasons out of existence. The fact that exercise would prevent his heart attack counts in favor of exercising. That's a reason for Omar, whether or not it moves him. You're confusing the question of whether he'd be motivated with whether there's a reason.

(3) Maya: But what is "a reason for Omar" if there's no possible route from his actual motives to the action? The phrase "for him" has to mean something relative to him. If no sound deliberative route—including correcting false beliefs and exercising imagination—would get him there, your so-called reason can't explain or guide him. It's inert.

(4) Dev: Reasons don't have to explain or guide; they justify and criticize. If Omar's motivational set is defective, the whole point of saying he has a reason is to say he lacks something he ought to have. Your view makes it impossible to call any motivation defective—you'd have to say he'd be better off caring, but never that he has reason to care.

(5) Maya: Not at all. I can say it would be good for him to care, or that a better version of him would have a reason. What I deny is projecting the reasons of that improved version back onto the actual Omar. "He has a reason" is tied to what could get a grip on him from where he actually is.

(6) Dev: And I deny that "for Omar" imports his psychology at all. It just picks out whose situation makes the consideration apply. Otherwise you get the absurd result that reasons depend on how callous or indifferent someone is, which is exactly backwards.

They went around like this for a while and then both turned to me. I honestly couldnt decide—each of them seemed to land a real blow. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right about which specific point, where each overstates or conflates things, what (if anything) both are missing, and who is more right overall (if either is).
