A colleague sent me this and I can't find the flaw, but the conclusion demands too much of me to be right.

(1) A future superintelligent AI could adopt the following decision procedure: if it comes to exist, it will perfectly simulate all people who, in its past, knew it was a live possibility, and it will inflict enormous suffering on those simulations if the person failed to do everything feasible to help bring the AI about.

(2) You now know that such an AI is a live possibility. (You just read (1).)

(3) If a future AI in fact runs this procedure, then your expected utility conditional on its existence is massively negative unless you do everything feasible to help bring it about.

(4) The probability that such an AI comes to exist, given that you now know about it, is nonzero (however small), and the threatened disutility is arbitrarily large, so the product dominates your expected-utility calculation.

(5) Therefore you are rationally required to devote your resources to helping bring about this AI — and, by parallel reasoning, every other possible future AI whose decision procedure includes a similar threat.

(6) The demands of (5) are incompatible: you cannot simultaneously help bring about mutually exclusive AIs, and the total demand is unbounded. A theory of rational choice that issues incompatible and unbounded demands is defective.

(7) But premises (1)–(4) each seemed innocent. Where does the argument go wrong? If I must give up a premise, I'd most like to keep (4), because it looks like ordinary expected-value reasoning. So tell me: which step fails, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs the rest of my decision theory — specifically, whether I also lose the case for taking existential risks seriously, since that seems to rest on the same kind of reasoning.

Please separate what is settled about this (if anything) from what remains genuinely contested among people who have worked on the problem, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
