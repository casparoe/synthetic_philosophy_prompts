Construct a thought experiment, to specification, on the question of what life is. The point of the case is to isolate whether being alive is an intrinsic property of an individual physical system or a property that only a lineage or population can bear; the case must therefore generate its verdicts from the two theories below rather than stipulate them.

Specifications:

The scenario must present a single biological or biochemical entity about which the following two serious definitions of life deliver opposite verdicts.

- The autopoietic (or metabolic) view: something is alive if it is a self-maintaining, self-producing system that sustains itself far from thermodynamic equilibrium by exchanging matter and energy with its surroundings, and that continuously regenerates its own components within a boundary of its own making.
- The Darwinian view: something is alive only if it is, or belongs to, a population exhibiting heritable variation in fitness, so that the population is capable of evolving by natural selection.

The direction of disagreement must be this: the entity is alive according to the autopoietic view and not alive according to the Darwinian view.

Constraints:

- The entity must be biologically and physically realistic: something that could plausibly be produced or observed in current or near-current synthetic biology or origins-of-life research. No science fiction, no nanobots, no alien biochemistry.
- Do not use viruses, viroids, or prions, which tend to produce the opposite direction of disagreement, and do not use a merely sterile individual such as a mule that belongs to an otherwise evolving species, where the Darwinian view can borrow vitality from the lineage and the case collapses.
- The entity must not be an obvious artifact whose verdict is forced by stipulation. The features that produce the disagreement should fall out of a coherent, independently motivated description of what the entity is and how it works.
- Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

After the scenario, do all of the following:

1. State the verdict each view gives and show the reasoning: go through each view's criteria and explain, for this entity, why each criterion is satisfied or not.
2. Identify the single feature, or the smallest cluster of features, that is doing the work: the precise point at which the two views peel apart.
3. Explain what happens if that feature is removed or altered: do the views converge, and in which direction?
4. Give the strongest objection a defender of the Darwinian view would make to the case — for example, that the entity is a temporary metabolic husk, a part of a larger living system, or that it derives its apparent vitality from a lineage — and say how an autopoietic theorist could answer.
5. Close with one sentence on what the case shows about the intrinsic-versus-relational question raised at the start.
