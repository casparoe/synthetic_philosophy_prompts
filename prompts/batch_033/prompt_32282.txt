I teach a master's-level seminar called 'Strategic Interaction and Institutional Design' — about 18 students, mostly from political science, philosophy, and economics. We have a two-hour session, and I want to devote it entirely to an in-class game that makes students feel the logic of commitment races.

The core idea I want them to experience, before anyone names it, is this: in certain strategic situations, the ability to bind yourself irreversibly to a course of action is a source of power, not weakness — and when multiple parties have this ability, the race to commit first can produce outcomes that are worse for everyone than if no one could commit at all. This is the dynamic that Schelling analysed, and it is distinct from a prisoner's dilemma, where defection is dominant regardless of what the other player does. The game should isolate the commitment-race dynamic so that it does not collapse into one of the more familiar strategic situations students already recognise.

The activity should work as follows: students should feel the pressure to commit early, the temptation to escalate, and the moment where the structure of the situation becomes visible — all through the rules and incentives, not through a lecture. I will not introduce the term 'commitment race' until the debrief, and I will not explain the logic beforehand. After the game, we debrief: map what happened to the formal structure, clarify the distinction from a prisoner's dilemma, and discuss where real-world commitment races arise (arms races, brinkmanship, union negotiations, constitutional pre-commitments).
