Bit of background: I'm a retired physical chemist, and for the last year or so I've been sitting in on a philosophy of social science reading group at the local community college. There's usually 10 to 14 of us, ages maybe 25 to 75, and we get about 90 minutes. We've been reading about judgment aggregation, and I want a session that makes the discursive dilemma actually happen to us rather than just be an example on a page. I can follow the three-judge paradox when someone writes it out, but I don't think anyone in the group has really felt how a bunch of individually consistent people can end up with a majority-supported set of views that's collectively inconsistent, and how hard it is to say what the 'group' should do about it. So: design a structured classroom activity or small game for that setting that gets participants to produce the dilemma themselves before anyone names it. I'd like it to be a committee or review panel of some kind — maybe a grant panel, since that fits the room — where the group has to answer two or three logically connected sub-questions and then a conclusion that follows from them. The rules should be set up so the group's majority answers on the sub-questions push them toward a conclusion the majority would reject, or the other way around. I want them to notice the inconsistency on there own, not have the facilitator announce it.

Please include: the exact rules and any materials (ballot slips, a whiteboard layout, role assignments if any); a timeline for the 90 minutes; what the facilitator should and shouldn't say before the reveal; a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal distinction between premise-wise majority voting and conclusion-wise majority voting (and why neither is obviously 'the group's view'); what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to recover; and an honest note on which part of the philosophical problem the activity can't reproduce and has to be supplied afterwards. Also, I don't want a lecture up front, and the name 'discursive dilemma' shouldn't be said until the debrief.
