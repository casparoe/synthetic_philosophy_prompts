I'm presenting a paper at a conference on early modern philosophy and science next month. My thesis is that Hobbes's notorious geometrical work — his attempts to square the circle, his acrimonious disputes with Wallis, his unorthodox definitions and his rejection of algebraic methods — was not a detour from his philosophical system or a case of a philosopher simply overestimating his mathematical competence, but a coherent extension of his materialist conception of science. Specifically, I argue that Hobbes's insistence that geometry must proceed by the generation of figures from motions of bodies, and his consequent rejection of abstract, non-constructive, or purely symbolic methods, follows directly from his view that all genuine knowledge is knowledge of causes and that causes are, in the end, nothing but motions. If that is right, then the standard intellectual-biographical narrative that cordons off Hobbes's serious political philosophy from his embarrassing mathematical crankery is unsustainable: his geometry and his civil philosophy share the same methodological and metaphysical commitments, and the geometry is where those commitments are worked out most explicitly and most rigorously.

The audience will be about two-thirds historians of early modern philosophy — Hobbes specialists, but also people working on Descartes, Spinoza, Gassendi, and Leibniz — and one-third historians of mathematics who work on the seventeenth century. The philosophy people are broadly sympathetic to contextualist approaches and to the idea that metaphysical commitments shape scientific practice, but they tend to regard Hobbes's geometry as philosophically thin and biographically interesting at most. The historians of mathematics know the Wallis–Hobbes correspondence in detail, think Hobbes's constructions were simply wrong, and are likely to see my paper as an attempt to elevate philosophical interpretation over mathematical correctness — a move they have seen before and do not trust.
