Chalmers (in Reality+) argues that virtual objects are real digital objects. A virtual sword isn't a physical lump of metal, but it's still a real object because it's constituted by computational processes with genuine causal powers. The sword can cause damage to enemies, it can be traded between players, and its properties persist over time independently of what any one player thinks about it. So it meets the standard criteria for being real: it has causal powers, it persists through time, and it's mind-independent — the sword exists on the server whether or not I believe it does.

But Chalmers' account has a problem: he treats virtual objects as if they're on par with physical objects, and they clearly aren't. A virtual sword can't cut actual flesh. Its causal powers are quarantined inside the virtual environment. This suggests we should think of virtual objects as what Amie Thomasson calls artifactual objects — they're created by human intentions and depend on collective human practices for their continued existence, like money or marriage. But unlike money, virtual objects are also fictional, because they exist within a representational framework that everyone knows isn't meant to depict reality.

I think the best view is a kind of pluralism: virtual objects are real qua digital objects (following Chalmers) and also fictional qua representational entities. The mistake is to think we have to pick one category. A virtual sword is both a real computational structure and a fictional sword, just like a painting of a pipe is both a real canvas and a fictional pipe. This resolves the tension: when someone says "that sword isn't real," they're right about the fictional status; when they say "I really got that sword," they're right about the digital reality.

One objection is that this pluralism multiplies entities unnecessarily. Why not just say virtual objects are fictional and be done with it? The answer is that calling them merely fictional misses the genuine difference between a virtual sword and a sword in a novel. The virtual sword can be interacted with, its properties can be changed by player actions, and it plays a role in a shared social practice — none of which is true of a sword in a novel. So we need the category "real digital object" to capture what's distinctive about virtual objects.
