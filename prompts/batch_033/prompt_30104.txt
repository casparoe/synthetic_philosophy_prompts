I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar with about sixteen students and one seventy-five-minute session. Please design a structured in-class activity that lets the students feel the force of the peer-disagreement problem — specifically the tension between conciliationism and steadfastness — before either position is named. They should come away seeing three things: why discovering that an apparent epistemic peer disagrees with you can seem to require a large revision in your confidence; what it would take to rationally hold your ground; and what each stance costs. The pull of the problem should come from the rules, incentives, or roles, not from anything the facilitator says up front. There should be no opening lecture, and the terms conciliationism and steadfastness should not be used until the debrief.

The activity must be runnable in one seventy-five-minute session, with no real money and no web access. I would like the disagreement to be genuine rather than merely role-played: give the students a single, shared body of evidence that reasonable people evaluate differently, and pair students whose initial judgments in fact diverge. At some point the facilitator should stipulate explicitly that the members of each pair are to treat one another as epistemic peers — same evidence, same track record on this kind of task — and the design should acknowledge that this stipulation is doing real work. There should be a joint phase in which each pair must submit a single answer and a joint justification, followed by a private revision phase before the answer is revealed, so that individual movement toward or away from a peer can be separated from social negotiation. The payoff scheme should reward accuracy rather than agreement, so that consensus is not incentivized for its own sake. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the equal weight view, the steadfast alternative, higher-order evidence, the bootstrapping worry, and the charge that conciliationism courts spinelessness or skepticism. It should also be honest about which feature of real peer disagreement the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.
