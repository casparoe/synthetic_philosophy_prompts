My reading group has been going round in circles on the common prior assumption for months now, and I'm starting to suspect its not the debate everyone thinks it is. Everyone accepts Aumann's agreement theorem as a piece of maths; the fight is about what it shows. One camp treats the common prior as a coherence condition on rational belief: roughly, rational agents should have the same priors conditional on the same background information, so any persistent disagreement between two Bayesians has to trace back to private information or a mistake somewhere. Another camp says that's exactly backwards — priors are subjective, there's no fact of the matter about the 'right' prior before the evidence, and the theorem only says what happens if you already assume the controversial thing. Two perfectly rational people can start with different priors, update on the same evidence, and still disagree, and that's fine. A third camp, mostly the game-theory people, treats the common prior as a modelling convention for incomplete-information games: not a claim about real agents, just a way of making differences in belief representable as differences in information. I've read enough LessWrong threads to know that when a disagreement survives this long with everyone agreeing on the formal results, it's often because the two sides are using the same word for different things or answering different questions. My working theory is that 'prior' is doing three different jobs — psychological starting point, normative constraint, modelling device — and the question 'is the common prior assumption true?' is ill-posed until you say which job you mean. But I keep failing to convince anyone, and I can see a possibility that there's a genuine normative disagreement underneath, about whether you can ever be criticised for your priors. I want a proper diagnosis, not a survey of positions.
