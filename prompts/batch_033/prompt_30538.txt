I've been going down a rabbit hole after seeing the Chinese Room come up in a LessWrong comments section for the thousandth time, and I'm struck by how the argument has barely moved since Searle first posed it in 1980. The setup is common knowledge: a monolingual English speaker sits in a room with a rulebook that lets him take Chinese characters in and produce appropriate Chinese characters out, without him understanding a word of Chinese. Searle's intuition is that there's no understanding anywhere in the room, so running the right program can't be sufficient for understanding. The systems reply says the whole room-plus-book-plus-man is the system, and that's what understands. Then there's the robot reply, the brain-simulator reply, and the common-sense point that the man is just the CPU and CPUs don't understand individually either. But after forty-odd years both sides are mostly re-asserting there starting intuitions about where the understanding is supposed to be located, if anywhere.

My working theory is that it's a verbal dispute over the word 'understand' plus a hidden question about whether simulation is duplication, but I can't tell if that's the whole story. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal, a hidden empirical claim about what a physical system would need to do for its outputs to be genuinely about the world, a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether observer-relative ascriptions like 'understands' are real, or something else. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Please answer in British English.
