So I've been working on this piece about how behavioral economics has basically rediscovered stuff philosophers argued about centuries ago, and the akrasia/hyperbolic discounting connection keeps coming up. You know the story: Aristotle had this whole nuanced account of weakness of will where the akratic person knows what's best but fails to act on it because there knowledge isn't fully "active" in the moment — it's like they're asleep or drunk or reciting verses without really grasping them. Fast forward to the 1990s and George Ainslie and others are modeling this exact phenomenon with hyperbolic discount curves, where your preferences reverse as the smaller-sooner reward gets closer in time. The present self wants to quit smoking, the future self lights up anyway.

Here's what I keep wondering and can't quite settle: if Aristotle were dropped into a modern behavioral econ seminar and got the full download on hyperbolic discounting, quasi-hyperbolic models, the beta-delta framework, all of it — what would he make of it? Would he see it as a vindication of his analysis, a formalization of what he was gesturing at with the practical syllogism breaking down? Or would he push back hard and say the mathematicians are missing the normative dimension entirely — that akrasia isn't just a discount-rate problem but a failure of the rational part of the soul to govern the appetitive part, and no amount of beta-delta tweaking captures that? And more specifically: Aristotle distinguished between weakness proper (akrasia) and impetuosity (propeteia), where the impetuous person acts on passion without deliberating at all while the weak person deliberates but gets overpowered. Would hyperbolic discounting map onto one of these better than the other? I'm trying to figure out whether Aristotle would see the behavioral economists as allies who finally gave his theory mathematical legs, or as people who fundamentally changed the subject. What's your read?
