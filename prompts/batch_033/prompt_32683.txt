I'm taking a final-year epistemology course, and we have spent the last several weeks on Fitch's paradox of knowability. I find myself persuaded that the paradox succeeds as a refutation of the knowability principle — the claim that, for any true proposition, it is possible that someone knows it. The argument, as I understand it, runs as follows: assume the knowability principle (∀p(p → ◇Kp)) and assume there exists at least one truth that is not actually known (∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp)). Take any such unknown truth, and form the proposition that it is true and unknown. By the knowability principle, it must be possible to know this conjunctive proposition. But knowledge distributes over conjunction, so knowing it would require knowing each conjunct; and knowing the second conjunct — that the proposition is unknown — would, given the factivity of knowledge, entail that it is unknown. Yet the knower would themselves know the first conjunct, and therefore the proposition would be known after all. Contradiction. So the only consistent option is to deny that there is any unknown truth, which is absurd. The knowability principle therefore collapses into the claim that all truths are actually known, and must be rejected.

I have come to the view that this argument is decisive — that unrestricted verificationism is untenable and that the Fitch derivation shows why. But I am aware that the literature contains a range of responses, and I want to know what would genuinely count against my position rather than what those responses merely claim.

What I am after is a map of the defeaters. What would have to be true for the Fitch argument to fail as a refutation of the knowability principle? Which escape routes are live options, and which are merely logically possible but carry costs no one should accept? I would like them sorted by how decisive they would be if they succeeded: from defeaters that would overturn the argument entirely, through those that would force a substantial qualification, down to those that would leave the core point intact but require a clarification. For each, I want to know what a defender of the Fitch argument would have to concede, and whether the concession damages the argument or merely sharpens it.

I am also interested in the question of insulation. It seems to me that the Fitch argument has provoked a series of reformulations of the knowability principle — restricted to non-epistemic propositions, reformulated in terms of different modal operators, recast in intuitionistic logic, and so on. If the argument's defenders have responded to these by saying that the principle they meant to refute was only the original unrestricted version, has the target shrunk to the point where the argument no longer refutes anything of philosophical interest? If so, is that a problem for the argument, or is it a legitimate narrowing of its dialectical target? I want an honest assessment of whether the Fitch argument, as it is standardly deployed, still risks anything — and whether it should.

Please indicate how confident you are in each part of your assessment, and single out the point you are least sure of. I am not looking for a summary of the literature — I want a critical map of the argument's exposure.
