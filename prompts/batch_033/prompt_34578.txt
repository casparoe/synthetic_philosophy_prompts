**Exam Question:**

In his 1963 paper, Edmund Gettier presented counterexamples to the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief. Explain the structure of Gettier's counterexamples and assess what his argument definitively establishes, as opposed to what it leaves open or what later philosophers have read into it.

---

**Student's Answer:**

The traditional analysis of knowledge, going back at least to Plato's Theaetetus, holds that knowledge is justified true belief (JTB). On this view, S knows that P if and only if (i) P is true, (ii) S believes that P, and (iii) S is justified in believing that P. Gettier's 1963 paper challenged this analysis by presenting two counterexamples that satisfy all three conditions yet intuitively do not count as knowledge.

The structure of a Gettier case is as follows: a subject has strong evidence for some proposition that is in fact false, the subject validly deduces a further proposition from this false belief, and the deduced proposition turns out to be true — but only by luck, not because of the subject's evidence. In Gettier's first case, Smith has good evidence that Jones will get the job and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith deduces that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. Unbeknownst to Smith, he himself will get the job, and he also happens to have ten coins in his pocket. Smith's belief is therefore true and justified, but he does not know it. In the second case, Smith has good evidence that Jones owns a Ford, and deduces the disjunction "Either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona." Jones does not own a Ford after all, but Brown does happen to be in Barcelona, making the disjunction true. Again, Smith has a justified true belief without knowledge.

What Gettier definitively establishes is that the JTB analysis is insufficient — the three conditions can be met without knowledge. But his argument also shows something more specific: it shows that knowledge requires that one's justification not depend on any false proposition. This is the "no false lemmas" condition. In both cases, the subject's true belief is inferred from a false intermediate step, and this is what prevents the belief from being knowledge. Thus, Gettier established that the correct analysis of knowledge is JTB plus the requirement that the chain of justification contains no falsehoods.

What Gettier leaves open is whether this no-false-lemmas condition is sufficient on its own, or whether further conditions — such as a causal connection between the belief and the fact, or a sensitivity condition — are also needed. His paper also does not address whether the JTB analysis can be salvaged by strengthening the justification condition rather than adding a fourth condition, a possibility some externalists have pursued.
