I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long piece on global workspace theory and the running dispute over access versus phenomenal consciousness. In several interviews, researchers have reached for the same illustration: the brain as a theatre in which most processing occurs backstage in the dark, a 'spotlight' of attention picks out a small portion of the stage, and whatever falls within that spotlight is broadcast to a diffuse audience of downstream consumer systems. The picture is then made to do real argumentative work. A representation that never reaches the lit stage can still influence behaviour — masked priming is the stock example — so, the reasoning goes, the difference between the conscious and the unconscious is simply the difference between the globally broadcast and the merely active, and phenomenal consciousness either is that broadcast or is not a separate scientific target at all.

What I need from you is an audit of the analogy, not another summary of the theory. Lay out the correspondences explicitly: what stands for the stage, the spotlight, the audience, the darkness, and whether anything answers to a director or a script. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that are harmless expository conveniences from those that quietly carry the conclusion. I especially want to know what the theatre picture imports without licence: a unified spatial stage, a sharp boundary between lit and unlit, a single vantage point from which the illuminated contents are seen, and the implication that being seen by an audience is enough for there to be something it is like. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that global availability exhausts consciousness survives once those imports are removed, and whether any other analogy would be more honest about what the theory actually claims.
