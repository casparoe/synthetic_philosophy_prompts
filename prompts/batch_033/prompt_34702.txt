I'm slowly drafting a paper on whether individuals in rich countries are obligated to cut meat and flights, and every time I bring it up I get the same three local arguments: one fewer burger doesn't change how many animals are raised, one vote never decides an election so voting is a waste, and one person's emissions are a drop in the bucket. Before I go further I want the full spec, not another defense of one of those moves. Write out the desiderata that any adequate theory of individual obligation in collective-harm cases has to meet. I want the cases it has to get right — threshold/trigger cases, firing-squad overdetermination, imperceptible-effects and drops-of-water cases, one-off acts (voting) versus ongoing practices (diet, driving, flying) — and the distinctions it can't collapse. In particular: "my act makes no difference" versus "my act is not wrong"; ex ante expected difference versus ex post actual difference; and a duty that falls on "us" versus a duty that falls on each of us. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste (the consequentialist's demand that everything cash out in expected consequences, the Kantian's demand that the maxim be universalizable, the purist's demand that a genuinely obligating reason must track actual difference). I especially want the map of where the list strains: which pairs of these desiderata cant be jointly satisfied? My hunch is that taking tiny probabilities seriously and not making every charitable act obligatory pull against each other, and that the firing-squad case and the "only actual difference obligates" requirement are in tension, but I want the full list of pressure points, not just those two. End by saying which single requirement does the most work against the standard positions and why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble, don't restate the question.
