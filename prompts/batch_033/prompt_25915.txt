I've been trying to work out what I actually think about the problem of dirty hands in politics — whether a political leader can ever be justified in doing something morally wrong for the sake of a greater good, and if so, under what conditions. I've gone through a series of cases and recorded my gut verdicts, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or whether I need to revise some of my judgments.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A head of state authorizes a drone strike on a high-level terrorist commander. Intelligence places the commander at a compound, but there are also civilians present — perhaps a dozen, including children. The strike kills the commander and eight civilians. I think this is justified, though I also think the leader should carry the moral weight of those deaths and not treat them as merely a cost of doing business.

2. A finance minister learns that a major bank is about to collapse. If the news breaks before the government has a rescue plan, there will be a run on the banks and a severe recession. The minister tells a press conference, falsely, that the bank's balance sheet is sound and there is no cause for concern. The lie buys 48 hours, the rescue is arranged, and the economy is saved. I think this is justified.

3. A mayor of a struggling city accepts a large campaign contribution from a real estate developer. Later, the mayor votes to approve the developer's zoning variance. The mayor tells herself that the variance is good for the city anyway and that she would have voted for it regardless. I think this is not justified — it's ordinary corruption, not a dirty-hands case at all.

4. A defense minister is told by intelligence officers that a captured terrorist has information about an imminent attack that will kill hundreds. The minister authorizes harsh interrogation methods that cross the line into torture. The information is obtained and the attack is prevented. I find myself thinking this is not justified, even though the outcome has the same structure as the drone case — innocent people spared at the cost of violating a moral prohibition. But I can't easily say what the difference is.

5. A president discovers that a foreign head of state is planning a surprise invasion of an ally. Diplomatic channels have failed and there is no time for sanctions. The president orders the assassination of the foreign leader. The invasion is called off, and a war that would have killed tens of thousands is averted. I think this is justified, but barely, and I'm less confident about this one than about the drone case.

6. A governor is trying to pass healthcare reform that will provide coverage to two million uninsured people. A powerful state senator whose vote is the decisive one will only support the bill if the governor commutes the prison sentence of the senator's nephew, who was convicted of embezzlement. The governor knows the nephew is guilty and deserving of his sentence. She commutes it, the bill passes, and two million people gain coverage. I find this case genuinely agonizing. Something about it feels worse than the drone case or the finance minister's lie, but I struggle to say why — after all, the harm (one guilty person going free early) seems smaller than the harm in the drone case (eight innocent people dead). Yet my gut says this is not justified, or at least that it's a different and more troubling kind of wrong.

7. A prime minister's coalition partner is threatening to bring down the government over a policy dispute. If the government falls, a fragile peace process in a neighboring country — which the prime minister has been brokering for two years — will collapse, likely reigniting a civil war. The prime minister learns that the leader of the coalition partner is having an affair. She quietly arranges for evidence of the affair to be leaked to a friendly journalist, knowing the scandal will weaken the coalition partner's bargaining position and keep the government intact. The peace process survives. I think this is justified, though it leaves a bad taste.

I keep going back and forth on whether there's a single principle that captures all of these. It seems like I accept dirty hands when the action is directly aimed at preventing a catastrophe and the wrong is a side effect (the drone case), but I also accept the finance minister's lie, which is a deliberate deception, not a side effect. I reject the mayor's case as mere corruption, but the governor's commutation case feels corrupt-adjacent even though the stakes are enormous and the governor gains nothing personally. And I can't figure out why I reject torture but accept assassination — both involve deliberately harming a person to prevent greater harm, and both violate norms I otherwise treat as absolute.

Is there a principle — or a small set of principles — that makes sense of these verdicts? What does it say about the cases I'm unsure about, and which of my verdicts would it force me to abandon? I'm less interested in a label for my view than in a precise statement of what the view would have to be, and an honest accounting of where it breaks against my intuitions.
