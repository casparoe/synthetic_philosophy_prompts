Question:

Explain Russell's theory of definite descriptions. Your answer should (a) state the analysis Russell assigns to a sentence of the form "The F is G"; (b) explain how the theory evaluates "The present King of France is bald," and how this differs from Strawson's treatment; (c) explain how Russell handles the ambiguity of "The present King of France is not bald"; and (d) explain the distinction between primary and secondary occurrences of a definite description and why the distinction matters.

Student's answer:

"Russell's central idea is that grammatical form can be a misleading guide to logical form. A definite description such as 'the F' looks as though it functions as a singular term referring to some object, but Russell holds that it is an incomplete symbol: it has no meaning in isolation, and it disappears when the sentence in which it occurs is fully analyzed. For a sentence of the form 'The F is G,' the analysis is that there is exactly one F, and that thing is G. In quantifier notation: ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y=x) ∧ Gx). The analysis contains an existence component (there is at least one F), a uniqueness component (there is at most one F), and a predication component (the unique F is G). Because the description is eliminated in favor of quantifiers, the theory avoids Meinongian commitment to objects such as the round square, and it avoids Frege's apparatus of sense, which would assign a sense to an empty description even though there is no reference.

This yields Russell's treatment of 'The present King of France is bald.' Since France has no king, the existence component fails, and the whole quantified analysis is false. The sentence is therefore false, not meaningless. This contrasts with Strawson, who holds that the use of the sentence presupposes the existence of a unique present King of France; since the presupposition fails, the sentence is neither true nor false.

For 'The present King of France is not bald,' Russell holds that the sentence is ambiguous. On one reading the description has primary occurrence, and the negation takes wide scope: the sentence says that it is not the case that there is a unique present King of France who is bald. This reading is true, since there is no such king. On the other reading the description has secondary occurrence, and the negation takes narrow scope: the sentence says that there is a unique present King of France and he is not bald. This reading is false, because the existence component fails. Russell needs the primary/secondary distinction because descriptions interact with scope-taking expressions such as negation and propositional-attitude verbs. In 'George IV wished to know whether Scott was the author of Waverley,' for example, the description can be read as falling inside or outside the scope of 'wished to know,' and the two readings attribute different cognitive states to George IV."
