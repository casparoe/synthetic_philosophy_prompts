I teach an upper-level aesthetics seminar, and I want to open the unit on the paradox of tragedy with a poem that makes the problem felt before we turn to the standard positions. Please write a pantoum of seven quatrains, in the voice of someone leaving a performance of a tragedy—not a scholar or critic—trying to understand why she is disturbed by what she saw and yet unwilling to have missed it, and why the scenes she most dreaded are the ones she keeps rehearsing. The pantoum's machinery should do philosophical work: the forced return of identical lines in new company should embody the way the very same represented suffering is at once aversive and valued, so that a repeated line means differently on its second occurrence than it did on its first. Do not resolve the paradox into admiration of the playwright's skill or relief that it is not us; if those responses appear, the poem should refuse them or show their limits. A specialist in the paradox of tragedy should recognize the candidate positions in play—the conversion of painful feeling, the pleasure of controlling an emotion one is free to leave, the thought that intense feeling is itself something we want—without any theory being named, and an ordinary reader should find it a genuine poem rather than a versified abstract. The poem should end with the tension still open, since the position I want the class to occupy is one of unresolved wonder, not premature solution.

After the poem, add one titled section, Commentary on the choices made, of about 300 words. In it, identify the lines or pairs of lines that carry the philosophical content and explain precisely how the pantoum's recursive form models the paradox rather than merely describing it. Indicate where the poem touches each main candidate solution and where it deliberately declines to settle among them.
