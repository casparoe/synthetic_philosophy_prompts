I keep running into the same move when I teach formal epistemology. A grad student, or sometimes the author of one of the textbooks I use, will say that the principle of indifference is just the obvious thought behind a fair lottery: you've got n possibilities, no reason to favor any of them, so you give each 1/n — same as a fair die. And from this they conclude that for any parameter we're ignorant about, their's a unique rational credence; just spread the probability evenly. Then I bring up Bertrand's paradox and the room goes quiet, and by next week they're back to leaning on the lottery analogy. I want a careful audit of how far that analogy actually holds. Set up the correspondence table first: what in the fair-die case maps onto what in the epistemic case — the faces, the fairness, the physical symmetry, the number of outcomes, the act of throwing. Then walk through Bertrand's paradox and show exactly wich feature of the die is quietly doing the work that the target lacks. And tell me whether the conclusion — that ignorance by itself picks out a unique distribution — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the lottery analogy was smuggling in a natural partition the whole time. If there's a better analogy that wouldn't mislead students the same way, I'd like that too.
