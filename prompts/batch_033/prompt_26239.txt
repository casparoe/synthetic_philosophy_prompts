I've been reading Tolstoy's *What is Art?* for a reading group and I'm trying to get a handle on how his theory actually cashes out. The book is full of verdicts — he's constantly saying this is real art, that is counterfeit — and I've been compiling them to see if there's a consistent principle underneath. Honestly, some of them seems to pull in different directions and I'd love a second pair of eyes.

Here's what I've got so far for things Tolstoy unambiguously praised as true art:
- The peasant songs and folk tales he encountered on his estate — he says they transmit genuine feeling and are understandable to everyone, regardless of education.
- *Uncle Tom's Cabin* and the novels of Dickens and Hugo — he says they express the Christian spirit of human brotherhood and are accessible to any peasant.
- The story of Joseph from Genesis — he calls it universally intelligible, sincere, and morally serious.
- Some of Schiller's plays — he praises them as morally earnest and infectious across class lines.

And here's what he condemned as counterfeit:
- His own novels, including *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina* — he says they were made for the amusement of the idle rich, meet none of his criteria, and he regrets having written them.
- Beethoven's Ninth Symphony and the late string quartets — he says they're incoherent, fail to transmit a definite feeling, and only a tiny coterie pretends to understand them.
- Wagner's operas — same complaint, plus he thinks they're grandiose, insincere, and a kind of hypnotic manipulation rather than genuine art.
- Baudelaire, Verlaine, Mallarmé, and the Symbolist poets generally — obscure, elitist, no genuine feeling to transmit, written for a decadent few.
- Much of Renaissance painting — technically skilled but emotionally empty, made to flatter wealthy patrons and display virtuosity.

The new case I want to test him on: a song like 'We Shall Overcome.' It's simple enough that anyone can sing it — you don't need training or a programme note. It's been used in labour movements and the civil rights movement, so it's drenched in moral feeling and the spirit of brotherhood. It's not the work of a single named artist; it evolved out of gospel and folk traditions, and no one knows exactly who wrote which bits. And it genuinely moves people — you can see it on film, crowds singing it and weeping. By the standards of infectiousness and universal accessibility, it seems to pass with flying colours.

But here's the rub. It's a political song, and Tolstoy was deeply suspicious of political movements — he thought they replaced the kingdom of God with earthly programmes and stirred up partisan feeling rather than the universal religious emotion he thought art should transmit. And the fact that it has no individual author might trouble him, since he talks at length about the artist's sincerity as the source of infectiousness, and you can't exactly locate the sincerity of a tradition or a crowd.

What would Tolstoy's verdict have been? Which of his earlier judgments is the closest analogue? And if I've mischaracterised his principle — if there's a better formulation of what unifies his praise and blame across these cases — what is it, and which further case would settle the matter?
