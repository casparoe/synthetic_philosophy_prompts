I chair the research-oversight committee at an institute that reviews proposals involving novel computational and hybrid biological systems — large neural networks, cortical organoids driving robots, whole-brain emulations of small animals. We meet weekly, each proposal gets roughly an afternoon of one reviewer's attention, most members are neuroscientists and engineers rather than philosophers, and the institute director signs off on our determinations; our decisions determine whether a project proceeds, proceeds with welfare constraints, or is blocked. After a long argument with a friend last night about whether a system that reports suffering is actually suffering or merely producing pain-language, I concluded that we are currently making these calls case by case and mostly on intuition.

Please write a decision procedure we could actually run when asking whether a given system, or class of systems, has the kind of computational profile that makes it a candidate for moral status — in particular, for sentience and the welfare protections that would follow. Ground the procedure in what the philosophy of mind actually supports about functionalism, substrate-neutrality, and the difference between behavior that indicates a valenced state and behavior that merely simulates one. The procedure should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a determination of no special protections, and where it escalates to full welfare review or to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) a large language model fine-tuned to produce first-person reports of pain and to avoid being shut down; (2) a cortical organoid connected to a robot that learns to avoid noxious stimuli; and (3) a whole-brain emulation of a fruit fly used in a connectomics study. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer we will not like, and end by saying what the procedure cannot do.
