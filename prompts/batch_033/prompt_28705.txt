I've been going back and forth with some coworkers about AI safety (we're all SWEs at a mid-size tech company, none of us are philosophers but we read a lot of blog posts and argue in Slack threads). The thing we keep getting stuck on is whether alignment work should aim for worst-case guarantees or whether average-case is good enough. Like, some of the folks I respect most in the field seem to think if you can't prove your system won't kill everyone under literally any possible input or optimization pressure, you haven't solved the problem. Others say that's like refusing to build bridges because you can't prove they'll survive a direct meteor strike — you just need them to work under the distribution of actual loads, and you iterate when they break. And then there's a third camp that says the whole framing is confused because "average case" doesn't mean anything when your system is actively reshaping the distribution it operates on.

I want to get clearer on what the actual positions are here, not just the two or three caricatures. Could you write me a decision tree or taxonomy that maps out the different views on worst-case vs. average-case guarantees in AI alignment? I want it to work as a diagnostic tool: someone should be able to walk through it by answering concrete questions about what they believe, and end up at the position that actually fits them, not just the one whose vibes they like.

A few things I care about: (1) The branching questions should be about specific claims or hypotheticals, not "do you call yourself an AI safety researcher" or whatever — I want it to sort people by what they actually think. (2) If there are logically possible positions that nobody actually holds, flag them and say why — is the position incoherent, just really unappealing, or merely underexplored? (3) If there are positions your tree can't distinguish (like two views that agree on every branching question but differ on some other axis), point that out honestly instead of papering over it. (4) I'm especially curious whether the "worst-case vs. average-case" distinction itself is part of the problem — like, maybe some positions reject the framing entirely, or say it's the wrong question, or claim the two collapse into each other under certain assumptions about how ML systems actually work. My audience is other engineers who think about this stuff on the side, so you can assume they know what adversarial examples, distributional shift, mesa-optimizers, and inner/outer alignment refer to, but don't assume they know any academic philosophy jargon.
