I've spent the last few weeks in a forecasting group arguing about whether two forecasters who have exchanged all their evidence are rationally required to converge, and we keep hitting the same impasse: one side cites Aumann's agreement theorem as though it settles the matter, and the other side waves at different priors or bounded rationality and changes the subject. I've been around LessWrong-style discussions long enough to recognize all the standard moves, but I want the specification pinned down before I commit to any theory of rational disagreement.

Write me the desiderata for an adequate account of when rational agents may disagree. The account must take Aumann's theorem as a fixed point in its exact setting — common prior, common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities, and the rest — while still making sense of the fact that well-informed, apparently sincere people disagree persistently about politics, religion, and forecasting questions. For each requirement, give the case or formal result that motivates it. I want you to be explicit about which requirements are non-negotiable for any account that respects the theorem and which are optional commitments of particular camps (for example: that rational disagreement must trace entirely to differing information; that differing priors can themselves be rational; that bounded computation is essential; that common priors are a regulative ideal rather than a description of real agents). Distinguish carefully the things an account must not collapse: differing information versus differing priors versus differing processing versus differing values; disagreement about facts versus disagreement about how to weigh shared facts; and merely disagreeing now versus agreeing to disagree under full common knowledge of each other's views. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — as it looks like it might be once you demand common priors as a norm, the agreement theorem as a limit result, and rational persistent disagreement among well-informed peers — say so outright; that tension is the part I most want pinned down. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard positions.

Finish with a short scorecard of the usual camps — the "Aumann shows rational disagreement is impossible" camp, the "priors are just different and that's fine" camp, and the bounded-rationality camp — against the list, no more than a few sentences each, with the weight kept on the criteria rather than the scorecard. And wherever you come down, tell me what would change your mind: which theorem, case, or argument would force you to revise the list, and which criterion you are least confident belongs on it at all.
