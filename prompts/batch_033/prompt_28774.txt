I was talking with a friend who's a public defender, and she mentioned something that's been stuck in my head. She said one of the most important things she does for clients before sentencing isn't legal at all — it's coaching them on how to show remorse. Apparently judges really do give noticeably lighter sentences when the defendant seems genuinely sorry, and harsher ones when they don't. And this isn't just some informal judicial instinct: in a lot of jurisdictions, "acceptance of responsibility" is literally written into the sentencing guidelines as an explicit mitigating factor.

What I can't figure out is what picture of punishment this whole practice presupposes. If you think punishment is about deterrence, then remorse only matters insofar as it predicts lower recidivism — but you could be totally unremorseful and still unlikely to reoffend (maybe you're a first-time offender in a crime of passion, and the situation will literally never arise again). If you think punishment is about retribution, about giving people the suffering they deserve, then it's weird that feeling bad afterward changes the moral calculus at all. The wrong was already done; the suffering you deserve should be fixed at that point, no? And if punishment is about rehabilitation, then remorse might be a good sign but it's not the same thing as rehabilitation — you could be remorseful and still dangerous, or unremorseful and already reformed.

There's also a deeper tension here that bothers me. If someone is psychologically incapable of the kind of remorse the court is looking for — say, a defendant with severe psychopathy, or someone on the autism spectrum who doesn't express emotion in neurotypical ways, or someone on medications that blunt their affect — then we're effectively punishing them extra for a condition they didn't choose. That starts to look like punishing someone for who they are rather than what they did.

My friend floated this idea that maybe punishment is fundamentally about the community's resentment, and that when someone shows remorse they're acknowledging the community's standing to resent them, which somehow dissolves the grounds for the punishment. She mentioned some philosopher — Strawson, I think? — who wrote about "reactive attitudes" like resentment and indignation being the real foundation of moral responsibility. But I haven't read any of that stuff.

So I guess my question is: reconstruct the theory of moral responsibility that the remorse-as-mitigating-factor practice implicitly relies on. Is it internally coherent? Does the practice strictly require that theory, or would it also make good sense under rival theories of punishment? And if we rejected that theory entirely — say, for a bare consequentialism where only future outcomes matter — what would actually have to change in how sentencing works?
