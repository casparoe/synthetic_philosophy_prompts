I've been working through the combination problem for panpsychism and I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove too much. I want to know where it goes wrong.

Here is the argument:

(1) The combination problem for panpsychism is the challenge of explaining how the conscious experiences of micro-level entities (micro-subjects) compose the unified conscious experience of a macro-level entity like a human being (a macro-subject). If panpsychism cannot give an intelligible account of this composition, it cannot explain the existence of macro-subjects, and panpsychism is false.

(2) Any theory of consciousness must explain how the features of micro-level constituents compose the features of macro-level entities, including conscious macro-subjects. Call this the general composition requirement.

(3) The physicalist's explanatory task — explaining how non-conscious micro-entities compose a conscious macro-entity — is just the general composition requirement applied to the physicalist's starting materials. If the physicalist cannot satisfy the general composition requirement, physicalism is false.

(4) The panpsychist's explanatory task — explaining how micro-subjects compose a macro-subject — is also just the general composition requirement applied to the panpsychist's starting materials.

(5) The hard problem of consciousness is the physicalist's admission that they cannot currently satisfy the general composition requirement. But we do not treat this as a refutation of physicalism; we treat the hard problem as an open explanatory challenge, not a decisive objection.

(6) By parity of reasoning, the combination problem should be treated as the panpsychist's version of an open explanatory challenge, not as a refutation of panpsychism.

(7) Therefore, the combination problem does not refute panpsychism.

I find (7) unacceptable. I think the combination problem really is a deeper objection to panpsychism than the hard problem is to physicalism — the two problems feel different in kind, not just in degree of difficulty. But the argument seems to force me to treat them as equivalent explanatory debts, differing only in which starting materials get composed. I cannot tell whether the argument is valid and I must give up my conviction that the combination problem is special, or whether some step is false.

I want a committed answer: pick the step you reject, explain why it fails, and say why it looked plausible enough to get this far. If you reject (4) — the claim that the panpsychist's task is just the same requirement applied to different starting materials — then tell me exactly what the panpsychist faces that the physicalist does not, and what a physicalist who accepts your diagnosis must give up about how they understand their own explanatory burden. Do not survey possibilities; defend a specific diagnosis.
