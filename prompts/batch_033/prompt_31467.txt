My friend Jen and I have been arguing for like six months about something that started with a stupid argument about life insurance. She works in actuarial science and I'm a software engineer who reads a lot of stats blogs. The argument goes like this: when someone says "the probability that you'll develop condition X is Y%," what group are they comparing you to? Every person belongs to like a million different reference classes. I'm a 34-year-old, male, non-smoker, runner, but I also have a family history of heart disease. Do you put me in "34-year-old males" or "non-smoking runners" or "people with my specific genetic markers"? Each gives a totally different number, and there's no obvious stopping point — you could always narrow the class further until it's just me, at which point the probability is either 0 or 1. Jen says there IS an objectively correct answer: you should use the narrowest reference class that still has enough data to give a reliable frequency, and that's just what good statistical practice demands. I think that's circular — "reliable" is doing all the work and it's ultimately a judgment call about what you care about. There's no fact of the matter about whether the "right" probability of me getting heart disease is 12% or 7% or 23%; it depends on which reference class you pick, and that depends on your purposes.

We've been going back and forth and honestly we're both tired of it, so we decided to try an adversarial collaboration — write a joint document we can both sign, laying out what we agree on, exactly where we disagree, and what would change our minds. We've identified what we think are the main cruxes: (1) whether there's a principled stopping point for narrowing reference classes, (2) whether the choice of reference class is truth-apt or purely pragmatic, and (3) whether the fact that different reference classes give different probabilities means there's no single "correct" probability for a particular individual. But we're open to the idea that we've framed these wrong. Can you draft that joint statement? We want a numbered list of agreed claims first, then a table of disagreements with one row per crux. For each crux, we want to write down what kind of evidence or argument we'd both accept as bearing on it, and what each of us predicts.
