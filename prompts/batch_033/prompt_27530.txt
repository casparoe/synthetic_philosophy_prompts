I'm a third-year PhD student and I've somehow been asked to give a paper at a law-and-political-theory workshop next month, and I'm already dreading the Q&A. The paper defends a chastened version of the Ulysses contract picture of constitutional precommitment: the idea that a constitutional settlement is usefully understood as a polity binding itself against its own predictable future failures of nerve, shortsightedness, or factional passion. I'm not claiming the people is literally a single person. My line is that the analogy still earns it's keep because we routinely attribute temporally extended agency to groups (a university, a firm, a union), and because the Ulysses framing explains something a bare cost-benefit story about amendment thresholds doesn't: why some commitments get entrenched as matters of a polity's standing plans rather than just made expensive to reverse. I also argue the dead-hand worry is overblown once you notice that amendment procedures are themselves part of the binding, since they specify the conditions under which the later collective self gets to speak again.

The audience is the problem. It's mostly democratic theorists and constitutional lawyers who think the Ulysses analogy is not just imperfect but ideologically loaded — they'll reach for the dead-hand objection, the changing-membership objection, and the worry that precommitment talk is just a fancy way of letting past majorities veto present ones. There are also two or three public-choice-flavored people who are broadly sympathetic to precommitment but will press me on whether the analogy does any real work beyond a story about raising decision costs, and one senior person who I know will ask about central bank independence and whether my view is secretly just a defense of depoliticizing everything.

I don't need a reading list or a restatement of my argument. I need a Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this specific audience, not the objections I'd get from a room of rational-choice theorists or constitutional originalists. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each one the way one of these people would actually put it in the moment, and give me a two-minute reply plus a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. At the very end, on its own line, put \answer{...} containing a short label for the single objection I should spend the most time preparing for.
