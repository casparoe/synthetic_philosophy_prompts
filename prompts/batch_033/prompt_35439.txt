ok so ive been reading a bunch of history of science stuff (not for school, just got interested) and theres this pattern i keep seeing that i dont have a word for and its driving me nuts.

like take the shift from phlogiston theory to oxygen. when you actually read what the phlogiston people were saying, they werent stupid. they had this whole system where phlogiston explained why metals were similar to each other, why combustion stopped in closed containers, why calcination increased weight (phlogiston had negative weight or something — weird but internally consistent). and when lavoisier came along with oxygen, it wasnt like he just showed them a decisive experiment and they went "oh right our bad." the two sides literally couldnt agree on what would count as a good experiment because they had different ideas about what properties matter and even what the words referred to.

same thing with pre-darwinian biology. people like cuvier werent just "wrong about evolution" — they were operating in a framework where "species" meant an eternal fixed type and the whole point of comparative anatomy was to discern the divine plan. you cant just say "but look at the fossil record" because what counts as evidence for transmutation vs evidence for successive creations depends on background assumptions that arent shared.

and the aristotelian-to-galilean shift too. aristotelian physics isnt just galilean physics with mistakes — its a totally different way of carving up the world where "motion" includes growth and change, where objects have natural places, where vacuum is conceptually impossible. you cant translate aristotles claims into newtonian language without them coming out sounding like category errors.

so the pattern im trying to name is this: two theoretical frameworks that are so different that (1) you cant fully translate claims from one into the other without distorting them, (2) they dont share enough standards of evidence to resolve their dispute by just "checking the data," and (3) neither side is being irrational — from inside each framework the other side looks like its systematically missing the point.

i know kuhn wrote about paradigm shifts and i think he had a term for this but ive only read summaries and wikipedia, and i cant tell if what im describing is exactly his concept or a specific sub-part of it or something that other people have refined since. if there are multiple terms that are close (like from kuhn, feyerabend, etc) can you sort out which one fits my description best and where the boundaries are between them? and if my description actually mixes up two different things, id want to know that too.
