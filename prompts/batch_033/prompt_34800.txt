I've been trying to get my head around Nozick's entitlement theory — the three principles of justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and rectification of injustice — and the Wilt Chamberlain argument that liberty upsets patterns. I keep thinking there must be a real case that tests this framework properly, and I've settled on the restitution of Nazi-looted art as my candidate.

Here's what I want you to do. Walk me through the Nazi-looted-art problem using Nozick's three principles as the lens. Start with the thefts: what does Nozick's framework say about the justice of the holdings before the Nazis took them, and what does it say about the transfers — outright theft, forced sales at gunpoint, sales under duress? Then follow the chain: the art passes through dealers, auction houses, private collectors, museums — some of whom bought in good faith, some not. How does Nozick's principle of justice in transfer handle each link? Which transfers are valid and which aren't, and does the framework have a prinicpled way to tell them apart?

Then the hard bit: rectification. Nozick says we need to rectify past injustices, but he's famously sketchy about how — he gestures at the difference principle as a rough rule of thumb. Apply this to the art case. What does rectification actually require? Return to the original owners or their heirs? What if the art has been in a public museum for seventy years and the public has come to rely on access to it? What if the current possessor bought it in good faith? Does Nozick's framework have the resources to answer these, or does it just say "something should be done" without telling us what?

Then run it the other way: what does the case study reveal about Nozick's theory? The Wilt Chamberlain argument is all about how free transfers can disrupt an equal pattern without injustice — but here we have the opposite: a pattern (current museum holdings) that was built on a chain of injustice. Does the theory give us clear guidance, or does it buckle? And does the case reveal that the distinction between justice in transfer and rectification is less clean than Nozick pretends?

I'm not looking for a bibliography or a summary of the Washington Principles — I know the history well enough. What I want is the conceptual analysis. And if you think I've misread Nozick, or that the Nazi-looted-art case is a bad test for reasons I haven't considered, say so plainly. I'd rather be corrected than get a forced mapping. Also, when you're speculating or stretching the theory beyond what it explicitly says, flag it — I want to know which bits of the analysis are solid and which are interpretive.
