I'm on a small team at a large hospital system that reviews legacy internal policies — the rules nobody can remember the reason for. Every month we get handed a dozen or so rules that a department wants deleted or loosened: double-signature requirements, mandatory checklists, sign-off chains, rules about which job class can do what. Our job is to recommend keep, modify, or remove to a COO who signs off, and the stakes run from patient harm to lawsuits to staff just quietly working around the rules. Most of the people who wrote these rules are retired or dead, and the people living under them mostly experience them as friction. I've been reading Burke and Chesterton's fence — the idea that you shouldn't tear a fence down until you know why it was put up, and Burke's point that inherited institutions can carry accumulated practical wisdom that no single person in the present can reconstruct — and I want to actually operationalize that, rather than using it as a slogan to block every change or to wave away every complaint.

Write me a proceedure my team could run on a proposal to remove a legacy rule. It should be a checklist or flowchart-in-words we can work through in under an hour per rule, without access to the original rule-writers. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes-and-remove, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. I especially need the hard cases handled: rules whose original reason we can't recover, rules whose original reason no longer applies but that have picked up a new function, rules that look pointless but are cheap to keep, and rules that are costly but might be load-bearing in a way nobody can articulate. Then run the procedure on three of ours: (1) a rule that two nurses must both read back a high-risk medication order before it's given, which the nurses say is redundant; (2) a rule that controlled-substance waste still has to be logged on paper with two signatures even though our electronic system records it; (3) a rule that a chaplain must be offered a consult before a patient on a psychiatric hold can be moved to lower-restraint status, which some staff want removed as religiously inappropriate. Make sure one of these comes out as an answer my team won't like, and show that case honestly. Lead with a single-sentence version of the procedure's core rule in your first sentence, then give the procedure and the worked cases, with no preamble and no restating of the question. At the end, say what the procedure cannot do, tell me how confident you are in the translation from Burke and Chesterton to a usable checklist, and single out the point you're least sure about. American English, please, and keep the procedure itself clean even if the examples are a little rough.
