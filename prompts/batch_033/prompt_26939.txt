You are an expert metaphysician with a sideline in the methodology of philosophy—someone who thinks carefully about what makes philosophical debates tick and what would count as resolving them. I'd like you to diagnose a debate that seems to me to have been spinning its wheels for well over a century. Please think through the structure of the disagreement step by step before offering your analysis; it may help to begin by mapping out, as precisely as you can, what each side needs the other to grant and why neither side grants it.

The debate concerns what is sometimes called the unity of the proposition. Suppose a proposition—say, the proposition that Socrates is wise—has constituents: Socrates and wisdom. What makes this a proposition, something capable of truth or falsity, rather than a mere list or aggregate of two items? One family of answers says that a relation, often called instantiation or exemplification, holds between Socrates and wisdom, and that this relation's holding is what unifies the constituents into a proposition. But then a regress threatens: if instantiation is itself a constituent that needs to be unified with Socrates and wisdom, we seem to need a further relation to do that unifying, which in turn needs unifying, and so on. This is Bradley's regress.

Now, this debate has been running since at least the late nineteenth century, and it was central to the early work of Russell and Wittgenstein. Yet as far as I can tell, the same moves recur in the contemporary literature—the view that there is a non-relational tie that binds constituents without itself being a further constituent, the view that the regress is benign rather than vicious, the view that propositions are primitive unities not built from constituents at all, the view that the problem only arises if we mistakenly model propositions on objects arrayed in space. No one seems to have moved anyone else.

What I want is not a survey of these positions but a diagnosis of the impasse. Is this a substantive metaphysical disagreement about how the world is, or is it a disagreement about what counts as an acceptable explanation—about when we are permitted to stop asking "and what unifies that?"? Are the two sides answering different questions (one about the metaphysics of propositions, one about the semantics of predication) under a single heading? Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about the logic of relations, or about type theory—that, if settled, would break the deadlock? Or is the regress simply the wrong way to frame the problem, such that the debate is ill-posed from the start?

I have a working hypothesis—that the real disagreement is about whether explanation requires a bottom level of facts that are not themselves further explained, and that the two sides differ on whether a brute unity is an acceptable stopping point—but I want to know whether this diagnosis holds up under scrutiny and what follows from it. In particular: given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? A new formal result? A conceptual distinction that has not been drawn clearly enough in the literature? Or should we expect the debate to remain permanently stalemated, and if so, what does that tell us about the question itself?
