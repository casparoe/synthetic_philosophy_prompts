You wrote in The Incoherence of the Philosophers that when fire touches cotton, God creates the burning directly — fire is an inanimate thing with no action of its own, and observation only shows burning happens with fire, not by fire. I want to push back on this as someone who studies nature, but using discoveries you wouldn't have known about. Stay in character: you're an 11th-century Ashʿarite theologian, you can draw on anything I tell you, and you're defending your view seriously, not as a strawman. Here's my opening.

I've found that if I remove an invisible substance from the air — call it oxygen — fire won't burn cotton, period, no matter how hot the flame. I've also found that the same set of equations predicts when cotton ignites, how planets move, a stone falling, the Sun's heat — all of it, with the same handful of unchanging numbers, everywhere in creation, without ever mentioning God. When the numbers look wrong, we predict something unseen, go look, and find it every time. You say the connection between fire and burning isn't necessary — it's just God's habit. But if the habit runs on a single closed bookkeeping system that never misses and lets us predict the invisible from the visible, doesn't that start to look less like mere habit and more like nature having its own causal powers?
