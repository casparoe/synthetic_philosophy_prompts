So my friend and I spent like two hours arguing about free will last night and he laid out this argument that I can't shake. It goes like this:

(1) The Sphex wasp does this thing where it drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to check the burrow, comes back out, and drags the cricket in. If you move the cricket a couple inches while it's inside, the wasp comes out, drags the cricket back to the exact same spot, goes inside to check AGAIN, and you can loop this forever. It never "gets" that it could just skip the check and drag the cricket straight in.

(2) What the wasp is doing is running a fixed subroutine: cricket-at-entrance triggers check-burrow, check-burrow-done triggers drag-cricket-in. It's not really "choosing" anything. It's just a mechanism.

(3) Now here's the move: humans are also running subroutines triggered by environmental cues. We just have way more of them, and they're nested and recursive and context-sensitive. But at bottom, every human action is the output of some subroutine or chain of subroutines that got triggered by some input.

(4) Adding more subroutines, or making them recursive, or having them modify each other — none of that changes the basic fact that you're still a stimulus-response machine. Complexity doesn't get you from "mechanism" to "free will." Quantity doesn't become quality.

(5) So if the wasp isn't free, neither are we. The only difference is we have more gears.

I told him this proves too much and therefore something has to be wrong, but I couldnt say where exactly. Is the problem in (3) — that human cognition isn't just subroutines? Or is it in (4) — that complexity actually CAN get you to free will? Or is it something about the whole setup, like the wasp IS free in some thin sense and we're just being snobs about it? I feel like I need to reject something but each option seems to cost me something I don't want to give up.
