I've been teaching a philosophy of physics seminar this semester, and we've spent the last three weeks on Maxwell's demon. The students have now read the major proposed resolutions — from Szilard's 1929 paper through Landauer and Bennett to more recent treatments that bring in algorithmic information theory and fluctuation theorems. I want to give them a structured way to think about which resolutions genuinely dissolve the conceptual puzzle rather than just showing that the Second Law survives on net. Too many popular accounts treat all resolutions as interchangeable once they make the entropy numbers add up, but I think there are real differences in how deeply each one engages with what Maxwell was actually getting at.

I'd like you to rank the following five approaches to resolving Maxwell's demon by how fundamentally they address the underlying conceptual tension — that is, not merely whether they balance the thermodynamic books, but whether they clarify what was wrong with Maxwell's original reasoning about an intelligent being who could sort molecules without expending work. Use a tier system: S-tier for resolutions that cut to the conceptual heart of the matter, A-tier for strong resolutions that leave a minor loose end, B-tier for resolutions that work technically but don't fully illuminate the puzzle, and C-tier for resolutions that are more distracting than helpful. Within each tier, order the approaches from strongest to weakest.

Here are the five approaches:

1. The Szilard-engine analysis: the act of measurement itself necessarily involves a thermodynamic cost that compensates for the entropy reduction, because acquiring one bit of information about the system requires a minimum entropy increase of k ln 2.
2. The Landauer–Bennett resolution: measurement can in principle be done reversibly with no entropy cost; it is the logically irreversible erasure of the demon's memory to reset for the next cycle that carries the irreducible entropy cost of kT ln 2 per bit.
3. The Brillouin negentropy resolution: the demon must use radiation to observe the molecules, and the scattering of photons needed to distinguish fast from slow molecules introduces sufficient entropy to save the Second Law.
4. The algorithmic-information approach (associated with Zurek and others): the demon's status as a finite-state machine means it cannot store an arbitrarily long measurement record without its own algorithmic randomness imposing limits — the cost is tied to the demon's computational boundedness.
5. The fluctuation-theorem resolution: Maxwell's demon is best understood as a statistical fluctuation, and modern fluctuation theorems show that apparent Second Law violations are exponentially suppressed over long timescales without needing to invoke information-theoretic costs at all.

Please structure your response in three sections:

- "The Tier List" (roughly 150 words): Present the ranking in tier order, with a one-sentence rationale for each placement.
- "Tier Boundaries" (roughly 200 words): Explain what distinguishes S-tier from A-tier, A-tier from B-tier, and B-tier from C-tier in this specific context — what criterion are you using to draw the lines, and why does it matter for understanding Maxwell's original challenge?
- "Deepest Disagreement" (roughly 150 words): Identify the single most substantive disagreement among these approaches — the point where two of them genuinely conflict in their diagnosis rather than just emphasizing different stages of the demon's operation — and say which side you find more convincing and why.
