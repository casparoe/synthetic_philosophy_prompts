I've been working through the literature on Wittgenstein's On Certainty and I keep hitting the same wall. Everyone seems to agree that Wittgenstein identified a class of things — people call them "hinges" or hinge certainties — that stand fast for us, that ordinary doubt and justification presuppose, and that can't be supported by evidence in the usual way: that I have hands, that the earth has existed for more than a few minutes, that sort of thing. But the moment anyone tries to say what a hinge actually is, the literature splinters. One camp treats hinges as ordinary propositions that we aren't justified in believing but are nonetheless entitled to trust. Another says they aren't propositional at all, but something more like rules of action or animal certainties that show up in what we do rather than in what we assert. A third says they are propositional and can even be known, just not on the basis of evidence. And a fourth says the whole category is a grab-bag, and that "hinge" covers several different phenomena that got lumped together. What's frustrating is that these camps mostly cite the same handful of passages and don't seem to move eachother.

My working theory is that this is largely a verbal dispute about how to use "proposition", "belief", and "knowledge" — and that the real issue is whether the phenomenon Wittgenstein is pointing at requires a genuinely new epistemological category, or whether it can be handled with existing tools plus a few ceteris paribus clauses. But I could be wrong; maybe underneath the terminology there's a substantive disagreement about the nature of certainty, about what knowledge requires, or about what Wittgenstein was actually doing. I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse rather than a verdict on who's right. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a clearer taxonomy of the different hinge-like things, an argument that one side is just misreading the text, or an honest admission that the question is ill-posed?
