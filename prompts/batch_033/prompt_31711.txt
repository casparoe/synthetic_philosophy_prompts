A friend and I spent last night arguing about actual infinities, and I came away unable to state her position precisely. I know how she responded to a series of cases, but I cannot tell whether her responses express one rule or a compromise between two. Here is her record.

When I gave her the original Hilbert's hotel case, in which a single new guest arrives at a full hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3 and so on, she said the guest can be accommodated: move the occupant of each room n to room n+1, and give the new guest room 1. When I added countably many new guests, she said the hotel can still accommodate them: move the occupant of each room n to room 2n, and give the new guests the odd-numbered rooms. When I asked about infinitely many coaches, each carrying infinitely many guests, she said those guests could be accommodated too, by pairing each guest's coach number and seat number with a unique room number. She also said, without hesitation, that there are not more natural numbers than even numbers, because the two collections can be paired off exactly.

Yet when I described Thomson's lamp, which is switched on and off at times one half, three quarters, seven eighths and so on, the switches alternating without end before one minute has elapsed, she refused to say whether the lamp is on or off at the one-minute mark. She called the question ill-posed and said the story simply fails to determine a final state.

We never got to the following case. A machine stands before an endless tape, blank at noon. It writes the natural numbers, one per cell, at an accelerating pace: it writes 1 at 12:30, 2 at 12:45, 3 at 12:52:30, and so on, so that the entire infinite sequence is completed by one o'clock. What would she say is on the tape at one o'clock?

I am less interested in the bare answer than in the rule behind it. What rules are consistent with her record, what does each rule predict about the tape, which rule do you think she is actually running and why, and what further case would most cleanly settle the matter?
