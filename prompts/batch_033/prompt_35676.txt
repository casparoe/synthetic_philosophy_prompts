I serve on the disciplinary committee at a private university. We're considering a proposal to expand our code of conduct to cover off-campus, private behavior. The catalyst was a group of students who run a subscription-based website where they post videos of themselves engaging in consensual activities that are fully legal but that a large number of alumni, parents, and current students find morally repugnant. I'm going to leave the nature of the videos vague: the details don't matter for the principle, and I don't want the discussion to get bogged down in whether this particular activity is actually harmful.

The proposal would allow the university to sanction students for any off-campus conduct that "seriously violates the moral standards of the university community," even when the conduct is legal and causes no demonstrable harm to any identifiable person. The proponents make two main arguments:

1. A university is not just a credentialing service; it is a moral community. If we don't enforce shared moral standards, we cease to be a genuine community and become a mere transaction. The university has a right to protect its moral character, just as it has a right to set academic standards.

2. Even if no individual is directly harmed, conduct that flouts the community's core moral values erodes the bonds of shared commitment that make the institution possible. A community that tolerates what it regards as serious vice will eventually disintegrate — not through any single act, but through the gradual loss of the shared moral consensus that holds it together.

I recognize the structure of these arguments from somewhere, and I suspect they're restatements of a position that has been thoroughly debated in legal philosophy. I don't know the literature well enough to trace the genealogy myself, but I want to think through the principles at stake rather than just follow my gut.

Here's what I'd like from you:

**The Genealogy** (~300 words): Identify the philosophical position these arguments are drawing on, the debate it emerged from, and the main counterarguments. I don't need citations — just the intellectual architecture. If I'm right that there's a famous debate here, lay out the key moves on both sides.

**Applied to the University** (~400 words): Take the arguments from that debate and apply them to this specific case — a private university regulating off-campus student conduct. What translates cleanly from the legal context to the university context? What doesn't? Are there features of a university that make the case for moral enforcement stronger or weaker than it is for the state?

**My Verdict** (~300 words): Tell me how you think I should vote, and why. Be explicit about which principle you're applying, and whether you'd apply it differently if this were a municipal government rather than a university.

**The Decisive Hypothetical** (~200 words): Give me a hypothetical case that would test whether I'm really committed to the principle I'm leaning on. If I vote to sanction, give me a case where the same principle would require me to sanction something I probably don't want to sanction. If I vote against, give me a case where the same principle would force me to tolerate something I probably don't want to tolerate. I want to know what I'm signing up for.
