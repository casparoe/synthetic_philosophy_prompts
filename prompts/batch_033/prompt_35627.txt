I'm on the ethics committee at a large fertility clinic. We're rolling out expanded preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) and we need a decision framework for how our genetic counselors should talk to patients about embryo selection. The principle of procreative beneficence — roughly, that you should select the embryo expected to have the best life — keeps coming up in the bioethics literature, and some of our clinicians are sympathetic to it. But we need something operational that won't make our counselors feel like they're running a eugenics program.

Please write a decision procedure our counselors can follow when a patient asks "which embryo should we choose?" It should be grounded in procreative beneficence and the liberal eugenics tradition (the kind that insists on individual choice and state neutrality, not the coercive kind), but it also needs to be honest about where the principle runs into trouble — where it's silent, where it gives answers that are clearly wrong, and where the line between "selecting for the best life" and "selecting for socially valued traits" gets blurry enough that the procedure has to punt to human judgment.

Constraints: the procedure has to be runnable in a 30-minute counseling session, the patient may have limited statistical literacy, and the decision has to be documented in a way that would survive an ethics audit.

Apply it to two cases: (1) a couple selecting among five embryos where one carries a mutation for a serious early-onset disease, and (2) a couple selecting among three embryos with no disease markers, but PGT-P scores are available for height, IQ polygenic risk, and educational attainment. Show where the procedure gives different recommendations and why.

And please include at least one case where the procedure tells the counselor to say something the patient doesn't want to hear — or tells the counselor something they don't want to hear about the limits of their own role.

You're a bioethicist who actually understands the arguments for and against procreative beneficence, not just the caricatures. Start by brainstorming the key tensions the procedure has to navigate, then build the procedure. Be honest about the hard cases — I don't want something that looks clean on paper and falls apart in the room.
