I'm writing a novel where the protagonist, a retired statistician, has talked himself into thinking the Principal Principle refutes itself. I want the scene to be strong enough that a smart reader can't just laugh him off, but I also need to know where the argument actually goes wrong, because I suspect it does. Here's his argument as I've drafted it:

(1) The Principal Principle says that, setting aside inadmissible evidence, if the objective chance of A is x, then a rational credence in A should be x.
(2) For any proposition about the past, the objective chance of that proposition right now is either 0 or 1, because the past is settled.
(3) I know (2) for arbitrary past events: I know the chance is 0 or 1, even when I don't know which.
(4) So by the Principal Principle, for any past event, my credence should be either 0 or 1 — certain it happened or certain it didn't.
(5) But I'm not certain whether it rained in Paris on the day I was born.
(6) So the Principal Principle proves too much and has to go.

I'd rather keep the Principle than give it up, and (2) feels undeniable, so I'd like the problem to be in (4). But maybe thats wishful thinking. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If (4) doesn't follow, I need the precise reason, and whether the Principle needs to be restated to block it.
