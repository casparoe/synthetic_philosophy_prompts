I'm preparing a discussion for a philosophy-and-pop-culture group I help run, and I'd like your help as a professional philosopher working in ethics. The topic is the inefficacy objection to individual contributions to collective harms: the idea that a single person's action — buying factory-farmed chicken, taking a flight, casting a vote — makes no perceptible difference to the outcome, so there is no point in doing the supposedly right thing. This has become personally urgent for me lately. I went vegetarian a few months ago after years of guilty hesitation, and I keep running into the thought that my one choice doesn't change how many animals are raised and slaughtered, so why am I doing this?

I want to use Michael Schur's *The Good Place* as the centerpiece for the discussion. The show dramatizes the inefficacy objection directly in its first-season twist: no human has entered the Good Place in five hundred years because every action in the modern world carries unintended harmful consequences — buying a tomato supports exploitative labor, giving flowers to your grandmother funds toxic pesticides, and so on. The system has grown so complex that individual virtue is impossible. The later seasons offer a response: the protagonists do not simply try to be individually good; they work to redesign the entire points system.

I would like you to read *The Good Place* through the lens of the philosophical literature on the inefficacy objection. To start, please brainstorm the different versions of the objection the show captures. The tomato example is about causal inefficacy through complex supply chains. The fact that Doug Forcett — who lives an absurdly scrupulous life — is still doomed suggests something stronger: that even perfect individual action cannot solve collective problems. And the show's central dramatic question is whether individual moral effort matters at all.

Then give me a structured reading that addresses the following:

1. Which philosophical responses to the inefficacy objection does the show dramatize, and where? Does it lean on threshold or triggering arguments, where individual actions have some chance of being the decisive one? Does it appeal to integrity or expressive reasons for acting, independent of consequences? Does it suggest that the real problem is coordination failure rather than inefficacy as such?

2. Where does the show get the philosophy right, and where does its need for a satisfying television narrative lead it to simplify or distort the real arguments? Be honest: a show resolved by a reformed afterlife bureaucracy is not a philosophical treatise, and the points system is a metaphor with limits.

3. What does the show add to the philosophical debate that standard journal articles do not capture? I am thinking of the way it makes the psychological weight of the inefficacy objection vivid — the existential despair of realizing your best efforts might be pointless — which is hard to convey in a premise-and-response format.

4. If you were leading this discussion, which specific scenes or episodes would you point the group to, and what questions would you pose to connect the fiction to their own lives? Keep in mind that this has become personal for me and for many of the group members: we are not just analyzing a show, we are trying to figure out whether our individual choices matter.

The goal is not to reduce the show to a philosophical illustration, nor to treat the philosophy as a mere plot device. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions — one that takes the comedy seriously as a work of moral imagination and takes the philosophical problem seriously as a genuine difficulty.
