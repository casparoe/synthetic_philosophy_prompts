I'm in a reading group that's working through a popular philosophy of science book — the kind you'd find in an airport bookstore, not a proper academic text. The author has a chapter on scientific reasoning and gives this summary of inference to the best explanation:

"Scientists don't just collect data and see what patterns emerge; they reason backward from the evidence to the hypothesis that would best explain it. This is called inference to the best explanation, or IBE. The idea is simple: when you have a set of competing hypotheses, you pick the one that explains the data most fully, most simply, and with the fewest extra assumptions. This is really just Bayesian reasoning under the hood — the hypothesis that explains the data best is the one with the highest posterior probability, and once you formalize IBE you see it's just a heuristic for what Bayes' theorem already tells you to do. The only real debate is about what makes one explanation better than another, but there's broad agreement on the core criteria: simplicity, scope, and fit with background knowledge."

I'm suspicious of this but I don't know enough to say exactly where it goes wrong. I've heard that the relationship between IBE and Bayesianism is actually pretty contested — some philosophers think they're rivals, not allies — and I vaguely remember something called the "bad lot" objection that seems like it'd be a bigger deal than the paragraph lets on. But the book is well-reviewed and the author has a PhD, so maybe I'm just picking up on something from an unrepresentative blog post. Can you go through this summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher of science would accept, what they'd say is oversimplified, and what's just wrong? And please, where there's genuine disagreement in the field, flag it as such — don't present one side of a live debate as if it's the consensus.
