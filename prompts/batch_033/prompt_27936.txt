So I was at this philosophy conference last month — I cover the science and ideas beat for a magazine, so I end up at these things — and after one of the panels I wound up at dinner with two moral philosophers who got into it about abortion. Not the political back-and-forth you hear on cable news, but the actual arguments. Specifically Thomson's violinist and Marquis's future-like-ours. I took notes on my phone because it was genuinely the most interesting conversation I'd had all weekend, and I've been turning it over ever since.

Here's roughly how it went.

Claire (I'm changing names) said she thinks Thomson basically settled the personhood-independent case for abortion rights back in the 70s. Her line was: even if we grant the fetus is a person with a right to life, the right to life doesn't include the right to use someone else's body without their consent. The violinist analogy makes that vivid — you wake up hooked to a famous violinist who needs your kidneys for nine months, and nobody would say you're obligated to stay connected even though unplugging kills him. So abortion is permissible even on the most conservative assumptions about fetal personhood.

David pushed back. He said the violinist case only maps cleanly onto pregnancy from rape. In the vast majority of cases, the pregnant person engaged in voluntary intercourse, and that changes the moral calculus. You're not a random bystander who got kidnapped; you took an action that foreseeably created a dependent being, and that generates special obligations.

Claire came back with: so if I walk through a bad neighborhood at night and get mugged, did I consent to being mugged because I took a foreseeable risk? Voluntary action that raises probabilities isn't consent, and it doesn't waive your rights.

David said that's a bad analogy because pregnancy isn't an external harm imposed by a third party — it's a biological process your own body initiates in response to an act whose whole evolutionary point is reproduction. More importantly, he said, the fetus isn't just some stranger; it's a being you and your partner caused to exist in a state of total dependency. That's not like the violinist at all.

At this point Claire said he was smuggling in Marquis's argument — that what makes killing wrong is depriving a being of its future of value — and that Thomson already showed the right to life doesn't do the work anti-abortion arguments need it to do, even granting personhood.

David said no, he wasn't making Marquis's argument. He was making an argument about special obligations that arise when you cause someone to be dependent on you. The violinist analogy works for rape but not for voluntary pregnancy, and Thomson herself acknowledged her argument was strongest in the rape case.

Claire said that's a concession, not a defeat — if Thomson's argument works for the hardest case (where there's no hint of assumed responsibility), and the only remaining dispute is about edge cases of responsibility, then the core principle stands.

David said those aren't edge cases, they're the overwelming majority of abortions, so calling them edge cases begs the question.

They went back and forth a bit more but that's the spine of it. I'm not a philosopher and I couldn't tell who had the better of each exchange. Can you go through this step by step and adjudicate? For each move — Claire's initial claim, David's responsibility objection, Claire's bad-neighborhood reply, David's dependency counter, Claire's claim he's smuggling in Marquis, David's clarification, Claire's edge-case move, David's majority-case rebuttal — tell me who's right, who's wrong, and what both of them are missing if anything. I want a committed verdict on each point, not a "on the one hand, on the other hand" survey. And overall, who's got the stronger position? American English please — I might quote you in the piece I'm writing.
