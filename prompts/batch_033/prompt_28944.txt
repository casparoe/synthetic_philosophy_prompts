I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of human enhancement, and I plan to use the film Gattaca as a case study. I'd like a careful philosophical reading of the film through the principle of procreative beneficence — the view, associated especially with Julian Savulescu, that prospective parents have at least a strong reason, and on his version an obligation, to select among the possible children they could have the one expected to have the best life — and through the liberal-eugenics position, which tries to separate voluntary parental genetic selection from coercive, state-directed eugenics.

I don't just want the familiar warning-against-genetic-selection reading. I want a reading that treats both the film and the philosophy with care, and that is willing to say where the two come apart. In particular:

- Where does procreative beneficence actually illuminate the film, and where does the film attack a different target? Much of the film's moral outrage concerns how Vincent is treated as an existing in-valid, but selection before conception concerns which possible person comes to exist, not how existing people should be treated. Does the film run these together, and does that weaken or strengthen its critique?

- What does the figure of Jerome Morrow show about the limits of the principle? He is, in the film's terms, genetically elite, yet his life goes badly. Is that an objection to a principle framed in terms of expected well-being rather than guarantees, or does it point to a different worry about the expectations and identities that selection might create?

- The ending seems to celebrate Vincent's exceeding his genetic predictions, while the film also shows that his genes really do constrain him — his heart, his eyesight, his constant risk of exposure. What view of the relation between genotype and a life's going well does the film actually endorse, and could a defender of procreative beneficence accept part of it?

- Finally, does the film's dystopia indict liberal eugenics specifically, or does it depend on features a liberal-eugenic position could consistently reject — a rigid genetic caste system, employment discrimination, and the treatment of genetic prediction as destiny? Where should a careful defender of procreative beneficence concede that the film has found a genuine problem rather than dismissing it as a straw man?

Please write this as a reading of the film rather than a summary of positions, and be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration, and don't distort the philosophy to fit the film. Also, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on enhancement. For example, I'd like to know where points like the distinction between ex ante selection and ex post discrimination, or the expected-value structure of the principle, are relatively uncontroversial, and where the field is divided — such as whether selection is obligatory or merely permissible, and whether it is best understood as beneficence toward the child or as an impersonal choice. Don't present a contested view as the consensus.
