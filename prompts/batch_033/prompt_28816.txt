so i'm in a philosophy of language course and we just spent a couple weeks on vagueness, mostly on epistemicism vs supervaluationism. epistemicism says vague words have sharp boundaries, we just can't know where they are, so every borderline sentence is determinately true or false. supervaluationism says borderline sentences are neither true nor false — something counts as true only if it comes out true on every acceptable sharpening of the vague term.

the thing i can't stop thinking about is how actual courts deal with vague legal language, because it seems inconsistent. on one hand, a court will strike down a statute for being void for vagueness if ordinary people can't tell what it forbids — the law is too indeterminate to give fair notice. on the other hand, the same courts decide cases all the time that turn on vague words like "reasonable", "substantial", "vehicle", or "undue", and the opinions are written as if there's a determinate right answer to whether this particular thing counts. a judge doesn't say "it's borderline, so there's no fact of the matter"; they rule, and the ruling treats one side as correct. and its not obvious those two practices fit together: if the boundary is real but unknowable, how does that square with striking laws down for not giving fair notice? if borderline cases are gappy instead, how can a court announce that one side is simply right?

so what theory of vagueness does this practice presuppose? does deciding borderline cases commit the law to epistemicism, or can supervaluationism make sense of it by saying the court is just picking a precisification for institutional reasons while the law itself stays gappy? is the void-for-vagueness doctrine consistent with whatever the ordinary cases presuppose? reconstruct the implicit theory, say what the practice strictly presupposes vs what it merely fits with, and then take a stand on which view actually best explains what courts are doing — and what would have to change in legal practice if the other view were true.
