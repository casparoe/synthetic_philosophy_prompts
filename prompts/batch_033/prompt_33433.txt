I've been turning over the heterogeneity problem for hedonism and I think I've built an argument that accidentally proves value theory is impossible, and I can't figure out which step I'm supposed to reject. Hoping someone here can spot the move I'm missing.

Here's the chain.

(1) The heterogeneity problem says pleasures are too diverse to form a natural kind. The pleasure of a backrub, the pleasure of solving a proof, the pleasure of schadenfreude, the pleasure of a runner's high, the pleasure of aesthetic appreciation — what do these actually share? If hedonism can't answer, it's relying on a concept it can't analyze, and an unanalyzable primitive at the foundation of your theory is a problem, not a feature.

(2) The standard hedonist replies try to go phenomenological: all pleasures share a distinctive felt quality, or they're all experiences you want to continue, or they all involve a pro-attitude toward the experience. But each of these either smuggles in a non-hedonic notion (like wanting) or flattens genuine differences in how pleasures feel. The heterogeneity runs deep enough that the phenomenological approach keeps missing.

(3) So far so standard. But here's the part that got me stuck. The heterogeneity problem is often what pushes people toward desire-satisfaction or preference-based theories. The move is: don't ask what pleasures share; ask what people want, and that's what's good for them. The diversity of pleasures stops being a problem because you've changed the subject.

(4) But desire-satisfaction theories face the exact same problem, structurally. The desire for a sandwich, the desire to finish a novel, the desire that your children flourish after you're dead, the desire to be well-regarded by people you'll never meet, the desire that some mathematical conjecture be true — what do these share besides being things people happen to want? If you say "they're all propositional attitudes," the hedonist can say "pleasures are all phenomenal states." If you say "they're all states whose satisfaction constitutes well-being," that's just restating the theory, not solving the heterogeneity problem. So the problem doesn't actually favor desire theories over hedonism; it's symmetric.

(5) If the heterogeneity problem is fatal to hedonism, it's fatal to desire theories too, for the same reasons.

(6) Now objective list theories. They avoid the problem by being pluralist — they don't need to unify everything under one concept. But objective list theories face the "why these items?" problem. If your list is pleasure, knowledge, achievement, friendship, autonomy, you need a principle for why those things and not others make the list. And whatever principle you give is going to be a unifying concept — a thing that all and only the list items satisfy — which brings you right back to the heterogeneity problem, just one level up. Either the principle is contentful (and then it faces heterogeneity among the things it picks out) or it's not (and then the list is ad hoc).

(7) So we seem stuck. Monistic theories face the heterogeneity problem directly. Pluralist theories face it indirectly when they try to justify their list. Either way, any theory of well-being needs a unifying concept that can handle radical diversity, and maybe no such concept exists.

(8) The conclusion seems to be that value theory as a whole is in principle impossible — that the very idea of a theory of well-being is incoherent. But this conclusion is absurd. Lives go better or worse, we reason about this all the time, and some of that reasoning is genuinely normative rather than just instrumental. So something has gone wrong somewhere.

I'm tempted to reject (4) by saying that desire-satisfaction theories have an easier time because "being desired" is a functional property rather than a felt one, and functional properties are allowed to be heterogeneous in a way that phenomenal properties aren't. But I'm not sure that actually works — it feels like I'm just declaring that the problem doesn't apply to the theory I happen to prefer. And it doesn't help with (6) anyway.

So where's the mistake? If you had to give up one premise, which one and what does giving it up cost? Please write your answer in continuous prose, no headings or bullet points, just an email-style response. And if you think the whole setup is confused at a deeper level than any individual premise, I want to hear that too.
