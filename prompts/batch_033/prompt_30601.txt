I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in normative ethics, and I am writing the final examination. I would like one essay question on the doing/allowing distinction that a student cannot answer adequately by restating the standard contrast between killing as an action and letting die as an omission, or by rehearsing the familiar idea that negative duties not to harm are stricter than positive duties to aid. The question should instead turn on the following crux: the widely accepted position that withdrawing life-sustaining treatment (for example, disconnecting a ventilator at a competent patient's request) is classified as letting die rather than killing, even though withdrawing is plainly an action. I want the question to make students explain why this classification puts pressure on any attempt to ground the doing/allowing distinction in the bare difference between acting and refraining or in bare causal involvement, and then assess whether the distinction can be revised—for example, by distinguishing between initiating a lethal sequence and declining to continue aid that is already in place, or by appealing to what a patient has a right to demand—without the revision becoming ad hoc or simply consequentialism in disguise.

Please write three things:

(1) The exam question itself, suitable for a 90-minute in-class essay, worded so that a strong answer must do more than summarize the distinction.

(2) A model answer of roughly 700 words.

(3) A marking scheme worth 20 marks that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain, and that identifies at least three distinct, realistic mistakes—each with a one-line explanation of the misunderstanding it reveals—that should cost marks.

Please write everything in American English, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
