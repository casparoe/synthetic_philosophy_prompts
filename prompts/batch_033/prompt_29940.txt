I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist is a composer in the grip of a long creative block. In reading around the psychology of creativity, I have encountered Hofstadter's claim that the crux of creativity lies in "variations on a theme" — the image of twiddling knobs on an existing conceptual structure until something genuinely new falls out. I need to understand this model well enough that my character can think with it, argue about it with a rival, and have it shape the way she approaches her own composing. Could you walk me through the model in some depth? In particular: what, precisely, is a "knob" in this picture — are we talking about parameters of a mental representation, and if so, where do those representations come from? What distinguishes knob-twiddling that yields mere pastiche from knob-twiddling that yields something we recognize as original? And how does the model account for the phenomenology of creativity — the sense that an idea arrives unbidden, rather than being arrived at by deliberate variation — without simply dismissing that experience as an illusion?
