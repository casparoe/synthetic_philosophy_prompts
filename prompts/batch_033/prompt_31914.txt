I've been working through an argument about material constitution that seems to prove too much, and I need help finding the flaw — or accepting the conclusion.

(1) Consider a clay statue, Goliath, and the lump of clay, Lumpl, that constitutes it. Goliath cannot survive being squashed into a ball; Lumpl can. By Leibniz's Law, they are distinct objects.

(2) Goliath and Lumpl share all their physical parts, all their categorical properties, and all their causal powers at every moment they both exist. Squash them and the same physical forces are applied to the same matter; the difference is only in what we say afterwards.

(3) If two objects are distinct, their distinctness must be grounded in some actual difference between them — a difference in what they are like, not merely in how we describe or sort them.

(4) The only differences between Goliath and Lumpl are modal (what each could survive) and sortal (one is a statue, the other a lump). Neither is an actual, categorical difference.

(5) So their distinctness is ungrounded. But ungrounded distinctness is metaphysically unacceptable — it cannot be a brute fact that there are two objects here rather than one.

(6) Therefore Goliath and Lumpl are identical. But then Goliath can survive squashing (since Lumpl can), which contradicts the starting datum.

(7) The argument generalizes: for any ordinary object, we can define a coinciding object with different modal properties, and the same reasoning forces their identity — which collapses all modal distinctions among coinciding objects. That seems to eliminate the very idea of constitution.

Where does this go wrong? Which premise is the weakest, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I'm especially interested in whether premise (3) is question-begging and whether there is a way to reject (5) without abandoning the idea that identity facts need grounding.
