My mate and I just spent three hours going back and forth on this and I'm still worked up about it. Here's the situation.

Last year my gran died and left me this small landscape painting she'd always told me was an original by a reasonably well-known local painter from the 1920s — not like a national name or anything, but someone whose work sells at auction for maybe five or ten thousand pounds. I had it hung in my flat, I'd look at it and think about my gran and her stories about how she bought it as a young woman, and it genuinely meant something to me.

Anyway, I finally got it appraised last month and the appraiser came back and said it's not an original at all — it's a copy, probably done a couple decades later by an unknown amateur. It's a very good copy, mind you. The brushwork and the colours are nearly identical. The appraiser said that even when he first looked at it he wasn't sure. But the canvas is wrong for the period and under UV there are pigments that weren't available yet.

So here's where the argument with my friend comes in. He says I'm being irrational to feel differently about the painting now. His line is: nothing about the object itself has changed. You enjoyed looking at it before you knew, and the visual experience it gives you is exactly the same. If you were willing to pay to have it cleaned or framed before, the economics of that decision haven't changed either. He says the only reason I'm upset is snobbery — I valued the story, not the painting, and the story was never actually part of the painting. He thinks that if I'm honest with myself, nothing of value has been lost except a false belief, and false beliefs aren't valuable to lose, they're just false.

I told him that's absurd. I'm not saying the painting looks different — it doesn't. But it *is* different. The fact that my gran's hands are the hands that picked out that *particular* painting, that the painter himself stood in front of that canvas and made those marks — that was part of what I valued. The painting had a kind of historical connection, a direct line to people and a time that mattered to me, and now that line is broken. It feels like the difference between holding a letter your grandad actually wrote and holding a photocopy of it, even if the photocopy is perfect and you never touch the original anyway.

My friend says that's just sentimentality dressed up, and that there's no rationally defensible difference between the two situations if the visual and tactile properties are identical. He says if I can't detect the difference without an expert and a UV lamp, then the difference doesn't count as a real difference *in the object* — it only counts as a difference in my head. And differences in my head are my problem to get over, not features of the world that justify feeling like I've lost something.

We went round and round and got nowhere. I feel like he's missing something fundamental, but I couldn't nail down what it is. So I guess my question is: is there a philosophically respectable way of saying that the painting I have now is genuinely less valuable — not just to me subjectively, but in some defensible sense — than the painting I thought I had? Or is my friend right that I'm just being precious about provenance?
