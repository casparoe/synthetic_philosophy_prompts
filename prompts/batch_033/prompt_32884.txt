I'm a grad student in philosophy and I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about self-deception. 1. Linda's husband starts coming home late smelling like someone else's perfume, and she finds a receipt for a restaurant she's never been to. She tells herself he's just working late on a big project, the perfume is from a huggy colleague, and the receipt must be from a client dinner. She ends up genuinely believing this. Verdict: textbook self-deception.

2. Mark suspects he has a serious medical issue but cancels his doctor's appointment and never reschedules. He doesn't form any explicit belief — he just stops thinking about it. If you ask him, he says "I dunno, probably fine." Verdict: not self-deception. This is avoidance, a different thing.

3. Sarah knows her boyfriend is cheating. She tells herself "he's faithful" out loud every night, like a mantra, and eventually she comes to beleive it. Verdict: self-deception, but it feels different from Linda's case — more deliberate, almost like a project she undertook.

4. Tom is a mediocre guitarist who badly wants to be talented. He watches videos of great guitarists, but when he compares himself, he unconsciously focuses on their mistakes and ignores their strengths, while doing the reverse for his own playing. He ends up genuinely believing he's nearly as good as they are. There's no moment where he knew the truth and chose to believe otherwise — his desire just shaped his evidence-processing without him noticing. Verdict: I want to say this is self-deception, but I'm not sure. It feels less like deception and more like bias.

5. A CEO whose company dumps toxic waste tells herself the environmental impact is trivial, the regulations are excessive, and she's providing jobs for the community. She genuinely believes she's acting ethically. But if you showed her damning evidence, she'd find a way to dismiss it. Verdict: self-deception, sustained by a standing disposition to reject counterevidence rather than a one-time act.

6. A man is in denial about his wife's terminal diagnosis. He plans vacations for next year, talks about "when she gets better," and seems to genuinely believe she'll recover. Yet he also quietly updates his will, makes funeral arrangements, and cries in his sleep. Verdict: this is the one that keeps me up. It seems like he both believes and doesn't believe she's dying. That shouldn't be possible, but it feels like the most paradigmatic case of self-deception there is.

7. A student convinces himself he failed an exam because the professor is unfair, not because he didn't study. He knows, somewhere, that he should have studied, but he's suppressed that thought. When a friend points out that other students who studied did fine, he says "they just got lucky with the questions." Verdict: self-deception, and probably the most common everyday kind.

My problem: I can't find a principle that gets all of these right. Cases 1, 3, 5, and 7 feel like self-deception. Case 2 doesn't. Case 4 I'm torn on. Case 6 seems to involve believing P and not-P at the same time, which looks like a straight contradiction. And the standard philosophical puzzle — if you intentionally deceive yourself, you must know the truth to aim the deception, but then you can't really be deceived — seems to threaten cases 1 and 3 but not case 4.
