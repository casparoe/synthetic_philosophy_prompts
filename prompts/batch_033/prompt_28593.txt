I'm a novelist — literary fiction, three books out, working on the fourth — and I've been reading some philosophy lately because a friend shoved L.A. Paul's *Transformative Experience* at me and said it would mess with my head. It has, but not in the way she expected. I'm trying to figure out whether my views about writing and experience actually cohere, and I'd like someone to audit them rather than just tell me I'm overthinking it.

Here's where I've landed, more or less:

(1) Paul is right about the core thing: for genuinely transformative experiences — becoming a parent, fighting in a war, undergoing a religious conversion — you cannot know what the experience is like before you have it. The epistemology is closed until you're on the other side. I buy the argument about the limits of imaginative projection when you lack the relevant phenomenology.

(2) But I write novels, and my novels include characters who go through experiences I have never had. My second book is narrated by a woman who becomes a mother; I have no children. My third has a long section from the perspective of a soldier; I've never served. And I don't think these sections are failures — I think they're the best work I've done.

(3) Readers, including readers who *have* had those experiences, tell me the depictions ring true. A veteran wrote to me after the third book and said I'd captured something about the boredom of combat that he'd never seen in fiction before. I take this as evidence that I got *something* right, not just that I got lucky.

(4) And yet — and this is where it gets messy — I also believe that if one of my readers later undergoes the experience I depicted, they will discover that they didn't really understand it from the novel. They might say "I thought I knew, but I didn't." The novel gave them something, but not the thing itself.

(5) I think the imagination, when it's disciplined by close observation of other people, wide reading, and a kind of empathetic attention I've spent twenty years cultivating, can bridge the gap between not-having-had and having-had *enough* to make art that isn't fraudulent. It can't bridge it for decision theory — I wouldn't tell someone to decide whether to have children by reading my novel — but it can bridge it for something else. Im not sure what to call that something else.

(6) Finally, I think there's a difference between knowing-what-it's-like and whatever it is that a good novel delivers. But I'm not convinced that difference lets me off the hook. If Paul is right that you can't know what it's like, and my novel doesn't deliver knowledge of what it's like, then what *does* it deliver, and is that thing worth as much as I think it is?

So that's the set. I can feel tensions in there but I can't sort out which are real problems and which are just me tying myself in knots. Go through these and tell me: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest way to make it all consistent — or whether I have to give something up. Rank the tensions by severity rather than just listing everything that rubs against everything else. And be blunt: if my whole view of what fiction does is incompatible with Paul's epistemology, I'd rather know that than paper over it.
