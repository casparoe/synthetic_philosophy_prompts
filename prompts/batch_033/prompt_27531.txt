Essay notes: "Real Patterns and the Asymmetry of Stances"

Dennett's "Real Patterns" (1991) argues that the beliefs and desires posited by folk psychology are real because they pick out genuine patterns in behavior — patterns that permit compression and prediction. This is meant to chart a course between eliminativism (intentional states are nothing at all) and full-blown realism about propositional attitudes (they are robustly real in the manner of tables and chairs). The key move: patterns can be real without being perfectly sharp, and the intentional stance detects such patterns.

But there is a tension I want to explore. The physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance differ not merely in the vocabulary they deploy but in how much idealization they impose on the system being described. The physical stance, at least in principle, aims to describe the system as it is — its actual physical states and trajectories — without normative or teleological assumptions. The design stance introduces teleology: it describes the system in terms of what it was designed (by evolution or by engineers) to do. The intentional stance goes further: it assumes the system is rational — that it holds mostly true beliefs and will act to satisfy its desires in light of those beliefs.

Now, if a pattern is only detectable when we assume rationality, in what sense is that pattern in the system rather than partly imposed by the stance? Dennett's answer, I take it, is that the pattern is real because it corresponds to a compressible regularity in the system's actual behavior; the rationality assumption is merely a heuristic for locating it. But this seems to require that the system actually behaves approximately rationally. And many systems to which we apply the intentional stance — humans, nonhuman animals, perhaps future AI systems — are only imperfectly rational. So the pattern we detect is a pattern in the system's behavior as seen through an idealizing lens. The lens does not merely magnify; it smooths, fills gaps, and corrects errors.

This seems different in kind from the physical stance. When we take the physical stance toward a rock, we do not assume it is an ideal rock; we simply describe its actual physical properties. The patterns we find — its mass, its trajectory — are there regardless of any idealizing assumptions we bring. The design stance occupies an intermediate position: to describe a heart as a pump is to idealize (some hearts malfunction), but the idealization is grounded in a historical fact about selection, not in a normative assumption about rationality. Malfunction is defined against a design specification that was actually shaped by evolutionary history; irrationality is defined against a normative standard that the system may never have been shaped to meet.

So here is the worry: if the intentional stance necessarily involves normative idealization (rationality), then the patterns it detects are partly constituted by the stance itself. This pushes toward a more constructivist or instrumentalist reading of intentional-state patterns than Dennett's "real patterns" language comfortably admits. Dennett wants to say that beliefs are real because they are real patterns. But if the pattern only emerges when we idealize — when we treat the system as more rational than it actually is — the reality of the pattern may be thinner than he suggests. It begins to look less like detecting a pattern and more like projecting one.

A possible resolution: perhaps the design stance is the more fundamental one for grounding real patterns, and the intentional stance is best understood as a special case of the design stance — applied specifically to systems whose design (by evolution or otherwise) equips them to pursue goals in environments of sufficient complexity that rational-agent modeling becomes the most compressed description available. On this view, the real patterns are the design-level ones — the functional architecture of the system — and intentional-state talk is a useful shorthand for complex, high-level design-level patterns. But this would demote the intentional stance relative to the role Dennett gives it, and it is not obvious that Dennett would accept the move.

I am not convinced this tension is fatal to Dennett's view, but I think it is underexplored. The core question is: can the intentional stance detect real patterns without those patterns being partly constituted by the stance's own idealizing assumptions? And if it cannot, how much realism should we really accord to the patterns it detects — and does the answer differ in an important way from the patterns detected by the physical and design stances?
