I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about Integrated Information Theory, and I've come away with a set of case-verdicts that I can't quite systematize. My friend is a committed IIT proponent — she keeps saying that Φ just is consciousness, that the theory elegantly explains why the cerebellum doesn't support consciousness (vast numbers of neurons, but a feedforward architecture with low integration) while the cerebral cortex does, and that the counterintuitive cases are a feature, not a bug. I find myself agreeing with her on some cases but not others, and I want to know whether there's a single principle that captures my pattern, or whether I'm holding inconsistent views.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them.

1. **Cerebellum vs. cortex.** I agree that the cerebellum, with its roughly 80 billion neurons arranged in a highly regular, grid-like feedforward architecture, is not conscious — or at least supports vastly less consciousness than the roughly 16 billion neurons of the cerebral cortex. This seems right to me.

2. **Thermostat and photodiode.** I don't think either is conscious. A thermostat has a two-state feedback loop, and a photodiode carries a single bit of information about ambient light, but there's nothing it's like to be either of them.

3. **The XOR grid.** Here's where I part ways with my friend. If I understand correctly, IIT implies that a large grid of XOR gates, wired to maximize recurrent integration, would have high Φ and therefore be conscious — possibly much more conscious than a human. I find this wildly implausible. I don't think an array of logic gates, no matter how cleverly wired, has any experience at all. It may be computing something, but there's nothing it feels like to be that computation.

4. **Brain simulation.** I think a perfect computer simulation of a human brain, running on a digital computer, would be conscious. If the simulation replicates every neuron, synapse, and causal pattern, I think it would have experiences — maybe not identical to ours, but genuine experiences. My friend says IIT assigns this zero or negligible Φ, because the real causal structure is the silicon hardware, not the simulated brain. I can't accept that.

5. **Anesthesia and dreamless sleep.** I agree that a person under deep anesthesia or in dreamless sleep has little or no consciousness. This seems uncontroversial, and IIT handles it fine.

6. **Split-brain patients.** I think that when the corpus callosum is severed, each hemisphere becomes a separate conscious subject — two minds, one skull. IIT seems to get this right too, and I count it in its favor.

7. **The nation of China.** I don't think a nation of 300 million people communicating with each other is a conscious entity. There's no what-it's-like to be China. IIT says no, and I agree.

8. **The octopus.** I think an octopus is conscious. Its nervous system is radically different from ours — distributed, with a majority of neurons in the arms — but I'm confident there's something it's like to be an octopus. I'm not sure what IIT says here, and I'd want any theory I endorse to get this right.

So my pattern seems to be: I agree with IIT on the cerebellum, the thermostat, the nation, and split brains — all cases where IIT says "no consciousness here" or "separate consciousnesses" and I nod along. But I disagree with IIT on the XOR grid (IIT says yes, I say no) and the brain simulation (IIT says no, I say yes). And I have an octopus-shaped loose end. It also strikes me, as I look at my own list, that my verdicts on the XOR grid and the brain simulation might be in tension with each other: if I think a simulation of a brain is conscious, why do I think a grid of gates with high integration isn't? What feature am I tracking that splits them apart?

What I want to know is: is there a principle of consciousness that gets all of my verdicts, or close to all of them? If I have to drop one or two to achieve consistency, which should go, and what does the resulting principle look like? In particular, I'd like to know whether my combination of verdicts pushes me toward some form of biological theory, or functionalism, or a hybrid I haven't heard of — and whether whatever principle best fits my verdicts can survive the cases that IIT handles so neatly. I'd also welcome a prediction from the candidate principle on a case I haven't listed: suppose we gradually replace every neuron in a human brain, one at a time, with a silicon artifact that replicates the neuron's input-output function exactly. At what point, if any, does consciousness disappear, and why?
