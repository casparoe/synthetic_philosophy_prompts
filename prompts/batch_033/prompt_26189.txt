I'm a systems designer at a mid-size game studio. My job involves designing mechanics and the systems that teach players how to use them — tutorials, difficulty curves, feedback loops, the whole onboarding pipeline. Over the years I've developed a working vocabulary for a distinction I don't have a proper name for. On one side, there's the knowledge you can put in a tooltip or a tutorial pop-up: "pressing L1 at the right moment parries the attack, leaving the enemy staggered for 1.2 seconds." A player can read that, understand it, and repeat it back. On the other side, there's the knowledge that only comes from doing: the feel of the parry window, the ability to read an enemy's wind-up animation and react in time, the judgment to know whether parrying or dodging is the right call in a chaotic fight. You can explain the first kind perfectly and the player still won't have the second. I've been trying to design a game where the onboarding is as smooth as possible, and I keep running into the same wall: some things simply will not transmit through text, diagrams, or video, no matter how clear you make the explanation.

A friend who studied philosophy told me that a philosopher named Gilbert Ryle wrote about exactly this in the 1940s. He called it the difference between "knowing how" and "knowing that," and he argued that knowing-how is not just a matter of having a stock of true propositions in your head that you then somehow execute. He had a regress argument: if every intelligent action required first consulting an internal proposition about how to act, you'd need a further skill to know how to apply that proposition to the situation at hand, and so on infinitely. That stopped me short, because it sounds like something I've observed in playtesting: you can give a player a perfect written breakdown of boss patterns and they'll still freeze up, because reading the breakdown and using it in real time are two different capacities, and the second one can't itself be supplied by another written breakdown.

I'd like you to take on the role of someone who is both a professional philosopher and fluent in game design thinking. Restate Ryle's knowing-how / knowing-that distinction — and his argument that the former cannot be reduced to the latter — entirely in the vocabulary I work with: explicit vs. implicit tutorials, declarative knowledge vs. procedural skill, knowledge checks vs. execution checks, skill floors and ceilings, the difference between consulting frame data and having the muscle memory to act on it, and anything else in that neighborhood you find useful. Be as precise and honest as you can. Then give me what I'll call the residue report: which parts of Ryle's position translate cleanly into that vocabulary, which parts you had to stretch or distort to make fit, and which parts seem to have no expression in game-design terms at all. Finally, tell me what you make of the residue: does it point to something game design is missing conceptually, or does it suggest that Ryle's argument is less airtight than it looks once you try to operationalize it in a concrete domain?
