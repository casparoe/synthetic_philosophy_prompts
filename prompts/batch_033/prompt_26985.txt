One of the things I keep coming back to in the philosophy of logic and language is that the Liar paradox has been with us since antiquity, and yet there is still no consensus about what it actually shows. The core data are not in dispute: a sentence like 'This sentence is false' is true if and only if it is false, given the T-schema and classical logic, and that is a contradiction. The disagreement is entirely about which ingredient to surrender. One family of responses gives up the idea that a language can contain its own truth predicate, and stratifies truth into a hierarchy of metalanguages. Another keeps self-reference and the T-schema but allows truth-value gaps, so the Liar sentence simply fails to be either true or false. Another keeps self-reference, bivalence, and the T-schema, and accepts that the Liar is both true and false, revising the underlying logic so that a contradiction does not trivialize the system. And a deflationist or contextualist response tries to dissolve the problem by arguing that the Liar is a defective or context-shifting use of 'true' rather than a genuine counterexample. Each of these positions handles the original Liar, and each has a well-known revenge problem or an awkward consequence, and yet the debate seems to reproduce itself indefinitely rather than converge.

I have been reading around this for a while — the usual moves get rehearsed in blog comment sections as well as in the literature — and my working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really a disagreement about logic or about the semantics of 'true' at all, but about what the project is: whether the goal is to model natural language as it actually is, to describe an idealized but coherent language that captures what is salvageable, or to solve a puzzle that arises only because we imported formal assumptions that natural language never actually obeyed. In other words, the sides may be answering different questions under one heading — some doing descriptive linguistics, some doing normative logic, some doing metaphysics of truth — and the appearance of a single dispute may be an artifact of a shared vocabulary. I want to know whether that diagnosis is right, and if so, what would actually count as progress. If it is not right, I want to know what kind of disagreement this really is: a factual one about natural language, a normative one about which consequences of a theory are acceptable, or a bedrock clash of intuitions about whether contradictions can ever be true. And given whichever diagnosis is correct, is there anything — a new formal result, a distinction, a different way of framing the question — that could actually move the debate, or should we expect the stalemate to be permanent?
