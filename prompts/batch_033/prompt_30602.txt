I have been going down something of a rabbit hole in the philosophy of pain, prompted by rereading some old rationalist-adjacent discussions of valence and suffering, and I keep coming back to the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia. As I understand it, patients with pain asymbolia can identify, localise, and rate the intensity of noxious stimuli—a pinprick, say—while denying that the sensation is unpleasant or that they mind it; the usual affective-motivational dimension of pain seems to be missing while the sensory-discriminative dimension remains intact. I would like you to write a short philosophical dialogue set in a seminar room, between two equally sophisticated philosophers who disagree about what this phenomenon shows about the badness of pain.

One character should defend the view that pain's badness—its hurtfulness or unpleasantness—is intrinsic to the experience itself, so that a state lacking that character either is not genuinely pain or is at best a degenerate or incomplete case of it. The other should defend the view that the sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational components of pain are dissociable, and that asymbolia reveals the badness of pain to be a further evaluation, response, or attitude rather than something given in the sensation itself. Both characters should be charitable and competent; neither should be a strawman. They should respond to each other's actual moves rather than talking past one another, and they should work over the same evidence—for example, an asymbolic patient who accurately reports the location, quality, and intensity of a pinprick while seeming untroubled by it—and disagree about what that report establishes. At some point, each character should either make a concession or refine their position in response to the other's argument.

The dialogue need not reach a resolution, but it should leave the reader with a clear sense of why the disagreement is difficult rather than merely verbal. Please write it in British English, with attention to the difference between the claim that pain feels bad and the claim that feeling bad is something we attribute to pain.
