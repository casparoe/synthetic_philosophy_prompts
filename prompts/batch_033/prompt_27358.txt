I keep running into a three-way standoff in arguments about the mind, and I would like to know whether it has an established name — or, if it does not, how it should be carved off from the things it gets confused with.

Take a perfectly ordinary action: I decide to raise my hand, and my hand goes up. Three claims all seem true at once. First, the decision is doing real causal work — my hand went up because I decided, not by coincidence. Second, there is a complete physical account of the hand's going up: some sequence of neural events is by itself sufficient to bring about the movement. Third, the decision is not simply the same thing as any of those neural events.

The difficulty is that these three claims together seem to commit me to saying the hand's rising had two separate sufficient causes, the decision and the neural event, running alongside each other — which would make every deliberate action a repeated coincidence of a kind we would not accept anywhere else. But each way out has a cost. Deny the first claim, and the mind becomes a passenger that never affects the body. Deny the second, and one gives up the completeness of physics. Deny the third, and one is identifying the mental with the physical outright.

So I have two questions. Is there an established name for this trilemma itself, as opposed to names for its various exits? I have seen people gesture at "overdetermination" and "epiphenomenalism," but those seem to name two of the exits rather than the trap. And is there a standard formulation of the principle doing the real work — something like "one effect, one sufficient cause" — and is that principle meant to be a metaphysical truth or only a methodological rule of thumb? If the trilemma has no standard name, what would you call it, and how exactly would you define it so that it does not collapse into overdetermination or epiphenomenalism?

Finally, please say how confident you are in the identification and in your account of the principle, and single out the point you are least sure about.
