I've been working through the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies, and I've ended up with an extended version that seems to prove far too much. I'm hoping you can help me locate the flaw — or tell me if there isn't one and I should simply accept the conclusion.

Here is the argument:

(1) For an event to be bad for a person, there must be a time at which it is bad for them, and at that time there must exist a subject to whom it is bad.
(2) The only candidate times for death's being bad are before death, at the moment of death, and after death.
(3) Before death, the person is alive and death has not yet occurred; it cannot be bad for them at that time without backward causation.
(4) At the moment of death, either the person still exists — in which case death has not fully occurred — or they do not, in which case there is no subject.
(5) After death, the person no longer exists, so there is no subject.
(6) Therefore there is no time at which death is bad for the person who dies. So death is not bad for the person who dies.

Now the extension:

(7) The same reasoning applies to any posthumous event: the violation of your will, the destruction of your life's work, harm to your children — none of these can be bad for you, since you do not exist when they occur.
(8) Worse, the reasoning applies to events during your life that you never experience: being betrayed by a spouse who keeps the secret until your death, being defamed while under general anesthesia, having your interests systematically undermined without your knowledge. In each case, there is no time at which an experiencing subject coincides with the harm.
(9) But it is absurd to say that a lifelong secret betrayal that never comes to light is not bad for the person betrayed.
(10) So premise (1) is false.
(11) But if (1) is false, the Epicurean argument against death's badness collapses with it.

I find myself both convinced by (9) and suspicious that something slipped past me between (7) and (8). The Epicurean might reply that secret betrayals during life are bad for you because they set back interests you actually have while alive, whereas after death you have no interests to set back. But then why can't the interest in continued life — which you certainly have while alive — make death itself bad for you when it frustrates that interest? That seems to hand the anti-Epicurean their conclusion directly, which makes me wonder whether the reply is available to the Epicurean at all.

So: where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If it doesn't, which premise is the least costly to give up, and what does giving it up commit you to? I would especially like to know whether the Epicurean can resist step (8) without abandoning their core claim about death. And if the whole setup rests on a confusion I haven't noticed — an equivocation on "bad for," a scope shift, a false dichotomy in the candidate times — please say so plainly rather than forcing a resolution that papers over it.
