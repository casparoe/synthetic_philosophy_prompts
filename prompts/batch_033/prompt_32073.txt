I've been assembling a short film-and-philosophy unit and keep returning to Frank Capra's *It's a Wonderful Life* as a way into the dispute between transworld identity and David Lewis's counterpart theory. The Pottersville sequence appears to presuppose transworld identity: the George-less world that Clarence shows George Bailey is presented as a way the actual world would have been without him, and the people he asks after or encounters there — Mary as a solitary librarian, his mother running a boarding house, Uncle Billy institutionalised — are presented as those very individuals, made worse by his absence. That, presumably, is what gives the sequence its moral force for George. But Lewis's counterpart theory denies that one individual exists in more than one possible world; on his account, a statement such as "Mary would have become a solitary librarian had George never been born" is true not because the actual Mary appears in a George-less world, but because she has a counterpart there, picked out by a context-sensitive relation of similarity rather than by identity. Kripke's Humphrey objection pushes the other way: when we ask whether Humphrey might have won the election, we are asking about Humphrey himself, not about some similar stand-in.

I would like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical debate seriously, rather than using one as a mere illustration of the other. Where does the emotional and dramatic logic of the Pottersville sequence genuinely require something like transworld identity, and where would counterpart theory actually accommodate the scenes better — for instance, in the way the Pottersville versions of familiar people are at once recognisable and disquietingly altered, so that the similarities doing the work shift from scene to scene? Is the film simply committed to a naive metaphysics of "what would have been", or does it dramatise something about the phenomenology of modal thought — about how people actually care about unrealised possibilities — that the Lewis–Kripke debate tends to pass over? I would also like you to say plainly where the film resists a counterpart-theoretic reading, and what the film adds to the philosophical argument that the argument alone does not deliver.
