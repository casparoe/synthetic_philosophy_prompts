I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and in three weeks I'm leading a two-hour session in an interdisciplinary reading group on consciousness. The group is mostly doctoral students in psychology, neuroscience, and computer science; they have read one short piece on the hard problem of consciousness and left with split reactions — some convinced it is the only question worth asking, others convinced it is a verbal muddle. My job is to introduce the meta-problem of consciousness: roughly, the problem of explaining why the hard problem seems so hard to us, why the explanatory gap feels unbridgeable, and why certain thought experiments strike us as compelling. Please don't explain the meta-problem to me; I need a plan for how to teach it. What has to be in place before the distinction between the hard problem and the meta-problem can even land, and in what order should the remaining pieces go? I want one carrying example or case that does the main work through the whole session — what features must it have, and should I avoid the zombie thought experiment or Mary's room, and why?

My current instinct is to open by asking them what still feels unexplained after all the functions are accounted for, but I worry that this smuggles in the very intuition the meta-problem is supposed to explain, and I'd like to know whether that worry is real and whether it changes where I should start. What misconceptions should I expect from this particular audience — I am anticipating at least the collapse of the meta-problem into the hard problem, and the deflationary reading on which the meta-problem is really a debunking of the hard problem, and perhaps a sociological reading on which it is only about why philosophers talk the way they do — and at which exact points in the sequence should I head each off? Finally, what should I openly refuse to settle at this level, and what single question can I pose at the end that would show whether they have understood the distinction itself rather than merely learned to use the phrase?
