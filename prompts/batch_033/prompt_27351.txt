I run a workshop on decision theory under uncertainty, and two of its most persistent participants have agreed to an adversarial collaboration. One is a committed subjective expected utility theorist: she holds that rational belief and preference are fully represented by a single precise probability measure, updated by Bayes' rule, and that residual ambiguity is either ordinary risk or confusion. The other is a defender of imprecise probabilities: he holds that rational agents may, and sometimes should, represent their uncertainty by a set of probability measures or by lower and upper previsions, and that some acts are rationally incomparable rather than merely hard to choose between. Write the joint statement they should both be willing to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, not a transcript of their argument and not a verdict on who is right.

The document should have three parts.

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. It should state, in language both can sign: what each framework can represent and what it cannot; what the standard Ellsberg choice pattern does and does not show; the mathematical facts about dilation and what they leave open; what the standard coherence and Dutch book arguments establish; and which of their long-running disagreements are merely verbal or representational, with an explanation of why.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give the precise claim in wording each side would accept for its own position, and in a formulation the other side would recognize as fair. Address at least these four questions: whether a rational agent must have a complete preference ordering over all acts; whether a set of probabilities is anything more than a compressed representation of a second-order probability distribution; whether dilation is a reductio of imprecise conditioning or a correct consequence of it; and which decision rules for sets of probabilities, if any, are rationally permissible. If any of these disagreements is partly verbal, say which part and why.

Third, a cruxes table. For each crux, give a concrete case, protocol, or argument that both sides agree bears on the question; record each side's prediction or verdict in advance; and state what each side has agreed in advance would count as a reason to revise. Suggested cruxes to refine or replace: the Ellsberg two-urn choice under transparent, incentive-compatible conditions; the dilation scenario as a test of the correct updating rule; a sequential betting protocol in which a naive maxmin expected utility rule appears to accept a Dutch book; and a case designed to test whether refusing to rank two acts is ever rationally required rather than merely permitted.

Throughout, separate what is settled among specialists from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. In particular, do not suggest that the empirical ambiguity-aversion literature settles the normative question, and do not suggest that the mathematical results by themselves decide which side is right. Since the document is for internal use, do not include citations or a bibliography; describe experimental findings as stylized facts both sides accept for the purpose of the collaboration.
