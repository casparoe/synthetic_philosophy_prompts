**Question:** Explain Parfit's hitchhiker thought experiment. What does it show about the relationship between rationality and promise-keeping? Does it show that it can be rational to make yourself irrational?

**Student's answer:**

Parfit's hitchhiker case is a thought experiment where you're stranded in the desert dying of thirst and a passing driver offers to take you to safety, but only if you promise to pay him a thousand dollars once you get there. The problem is that once you're safe, you have no reason to actually pay him — you already got the ride, and paying won't get you anything new. But the driver is really good at reading people, and he won't pick you up unless he believes you're the kind of person who would actually pay.

The case shows that it can be rational to be irrational. If you can somehow make yourself into someone who genuinely feels bound by promises no matter what — someone who doesn't just calculate what's in their interest each time — then the driver will pick you up and you'll survive. So being irrational about promise-keeping ends up serving your rational self-interest. This is a paradox, because it means rationality basically tells you to stop being rational in some areas of your life.

Parfit uses this to make a bigger point about self-defeating theories. Just like act utilitarianism can be self-defeating (if everyone tried to maximize utility in every single action, they might produce less total utility), individual rationality can be self-defeating too. The solution is the same in both cases: follow rules or cultivate dispositions instead of calculating case by case. This is basically why rule utilitarianism was invented, and the hitchhiker case shows the same logic applies to rationality.

One obvious objection is that you can't just decide to become irrational. If you're naturally a calculating person, you can't just flip a switch in the desert and suddenly become someone who cares about promises. But Parfit says this is just a practical problem, not a deep philosophical one. The conceptual point still stands: if you could become that kind of person, it would be rational to do so.

In conclusion, the hitchhiker case shows that pure act-by-act rationality can be self-defeating, and that it makes sense to cultivate dispositions like promise-keeping even if individual acts of promise-keeping look irrational when you examine them one at a time.
