I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether Bernard Williams's 'one thought too many' objection actually lands against utilitarianism. We kept going in circles on whether the man who rescues his wife and then justifies it to her is really a worse husband than the one who doesn't have the thought at all, and whether the utilitarian can reply that the right-making features don't need to be present in the agent's deliberation. I'd like to see the argument played out more carefully than either of us could manage. Please write a dialogue between a utilitarian and a defender of Williams's integrity objection. The utilitarian should grant that there is something genuinely alienating about requiring agents to justify their deepest commitments from an impartial standpoint, but should argue that this is a demand of moral seriousness, not a reductio. The Williamsian should press the point that the alienation is internal to the relationship itself — that the husband who thinks 'it's permissible because she's my wife and the impartial calculus happens to favor it' already has a defective marriage. Both should push each other to clarify what kind of justification is supposed to be at issue, and neither should concede too easily.
