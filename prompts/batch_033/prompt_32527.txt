I'm giving a departmental colloquium next month on the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. The paper makes three claims. First, that the obstacle — the fact that an agent reasoning in a system capable of encoding its own proof predicate cannot, by Löb's theorem, prove that its own reasoning is sound without collapsing into triviality — is not a formal curiosity but a genuine architectural constraint. Second, that the four most-discussed attempts to circumvent it (weakening the logic, switching to probabilistic reflection principles, restricting self-reference to a tractable fragment, and stratifying the agent into a hierarchy of ever-stronger reasoners) each either fail to deliver the self-trust property they were designed for or reintroduce the obstacle at the next level of reflection. Third, that this has a practical upshot for alignment: we should not expect a reflective agent to certify its own reliability in a way that would let it safely self-modify without external oversight, and architectures that rely on such self-certification carry a structural risk that is not merely an engineering problem.

The audience is a philosophy department with a strong logic group, plus a handful of visitors from computer science. Specifically: three logicians who do provability logic and ordinal analysis but have never thought about agents; two philosophers of mind who work on self-knowledge, epistemic akrasia, and Moore's paradox and may or may not know Löb's theorem; one AI researcher from CS who builds reinforcement learning systems and thinks formal obstacles are beside the point for anything that learns from data; one theoretical computer scientist who works on verification and typed self-reference; and a cluster of grad students who read the rationalist blogs and are sympathetic to the agent-foundations program but have not worked through the proofs themselves. No one else in the room works on reflective stability or decision theory.
