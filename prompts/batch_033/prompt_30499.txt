I am a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun reading the philosophical literature on intellectual property and the ownership of ideas. A working life spent around priority disputes, material transfer agreements, and questions of who may use whose method has left me with a supply of hard cases, but not with a clear statement of what a theory in this area is actually for. Before I read another proposal, I want the specification: a list of desiderata that any adequate account of the ownership of ideas must satisfy, where adequacy is judged relative to the following purpose—an account good enough to guide thinking about scientific priority, about the ethics of using another person's ideas, and about the general shape intellectual-property protection should take. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Mark which are non-negotiable (I have in mind, at least, the distinction between an idea, its expression, and its physical embodiment; the difference between independent invention and copying; and the need to explain why protection, if it exists, ever gives way to a public domain) and which are merely the preferences of particular camps (whether attribution is a property right or a separate moral claim; whether the underlying justification is labor, personality, or social incentive). Flag any pairs of desiderata that may resist joint satisfaction—for instance, enough control to reward the creator and enough freedom for later researchers to build on the idea—and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the standard contenders, scoring the Lockean, Hegelian, and utilitarian accounts against the list only briefly. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard should be the shortest part.
