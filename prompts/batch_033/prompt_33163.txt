I'm taking a philosophy elective at my high school, and for the past several weeks we've been working through a unit on practical rationality — specifically, the question of whether it can ever be rational to deliberately make yourself irrational. Our teacher has been presenting us with cases one by one and giving her verdicts, and next week she's going to hand us a new case and ask us to predict what she'll say, along with an account of the principle we think she's implicitly applying. I've kept careful notes, and I want to sort through the pattern before class.

Here are the cases she's ruled on so far. (1) Parfit's hitchhiker: you are stranded in a desert, and a stranger offers you a ride home on the condition that you sincerely promise to pay him a large sum once you are safe. You know you will have no incentive to pay after the ride. You can take a drug that temporarily makes you genuinely believe you will pay, so that your promise is sincere and the stranger detects that sincerity. She says: rational. (2) The anger pill: a businessperson takes a drug that induces genuine, detectable rage before a high-stakes negotiation, knowing the other party will make concessions to avoid dealing with someone so volatile. She says: rational. (3) The madman deterrent: a nuclear-armed state deliberately cultivates a leader who is genuinely irrational — someone who really would launch a suicidal retaliatory strike — so that adversaries are deterred from attacking. She says: rational. (4) The happy delusion: someone deliberately cultivates the false belief that their mediocre paintings are masterpieces, solely because holding this belief makes them happier; no one else is ever exposed to the belief or affected by it. She says: not rational. (5) The afterlife pill: a person takes a drug that makes them sincerely believe in an afterlife, purely to eliminate existential dread; the belief has no effect on anyone else. She says: not rational.

The new case she will give us is this: a student is considering applying to a highly competitive summer program. He knows that if he accurately calculates the acceptance rate, he will conclude it is not worth the effort and will not apply. The program would genuinely be life-changing if he got in, and his chances, while low, are not negligible. He considers taking a drug that temporarily impairs his ability to estimate probabilities, so that he will go ahead and submit the application. No one else will detect his altered state or his level of confidence. I can see at least two principles that fit her first five verdicts equally well. One is a detection principle: it is rational to induce irrationality in yourself when and only when another party will detect that irrationality and change their behavior toward you in a way that benefits you. The other is a commitment principle: it is rational to induce irrationality whenever it helps you bind yourself to a course of action you would otherwise fail to follow through on, regardless of whether anyone else detects it. The new case divides them: the detection principle says not rational, the commitment principle says rational. Which principle do you think she is actually running, what verdict should I predict, and what further case would settle which one she has in mind?
