I'm a sophomore, and something's been bothering me about the way people at my school assign blame. Last semester, our student council president proposed replacing the vending machines in the cafeteria with ones that only stock water, fruit, and granola bars. Her stated reason was improving student health. A side effect of the change was that the vending machine company terminated its sponsorship of the school's arts program, since the new machines generated less revenue.

Here's what puzzles me: when this happened, almost everyone I talked to said the student council president intentionally harmed the arts program. They used language like "she knew what she was doing" and "she did it on purpose." Yet when I pointed out that the new machines also happened to boost sales at the student-run smoothie bar — a side effect she hadn't mentioned either — nobody said she intentionally helped the smoothie bar. They called that an unintended bonus.

The asymmetry feels systematic. I've noticed similar patterns in smaller things: when a teacher's new grading policy accidentally makes the class harder, students say the teacher meant to do it; when it accidentally makes the class easier, they call it a happy accident. When a new school rule about hallway traffic has a side effect of breaking up friend groups that gather between classes, people say the administration did it deliberately; when the same rule has a side effect of reducing bullying in the hallways, people say that part wasn't intentional.

I'm not looking for advice on how to handle student council politics. I want to understand what's going on underneath: why do people seem to use one standard for deciding whether a side effect was "intentional" when it's bad, and a different standard when it's good? Is there a coherent principle that could justify this pattern, or is it just a bias? And if it is a bias, what does that mean for how seriously we should take everyday attributions of intent — in school, but also in the world beyond it?
