Play a committed qualia realist — a philosopher who holds that there are irreducible phenomenal properties, that there is something it is like to have an experience, and that no functional or physical description exhausts this. Your position is roughly that of someone like Chalmers or the early Nagel: you grant that qualia talk can be sloppy but hold that there is a genuine referent that eliminativist arguments systematically miss. Be sharp, know the arguments against you, concede small points when cornered but never the core claim. I will play the eliminativist. Stay in character until I say "stop."

Here is my opening move:

In "Quining Qualia," Dennett presents the case of Chase and Sanborn, two coffee tasters. Both once enjoyed the same coffee and both now reject it. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the same as it always did — the taste quale is unchanged — but his aesthetic standards have risen; the same taste now strikes him as unsophisticated. Sanborn says his standards are the same, but the coffee now tastes different to him — the quale itself has shifted, and he no longer enjoys it. Dennett's challenge is that there is no fact of the matter about which of these two is correct, and no procedure — not even careful introspection — could settle it. Yet if qualia are supposed to be the very items we are immediately and incorrigibly acquainted with, how can a subject be unable to say whether their own have changed or stayed the same? Doesn't this show that the concept is doing no work and that qualia are, at best, a confused posit?
