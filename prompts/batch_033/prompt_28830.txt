I recently retired from a career in physical chemistry and have since been sitting in on an undergraduate course in the philosophy of language, where the instructor has asked me to help with grading. The assignment concerned the liar paradox and theories of truth. Below are the question and one student's answer, reproduced as submitted.

Question: Explain the strengthened liar paradox, and assess whether a truth-value gap approach such as Kripke's can handle it. In your answer, distinguish the simple liar from the strengthened liar, and briefly compare Kripke's approach with Tarski's.

Student's answer:
The simple liar, "This sentence is false," leads to a contradiction under classical bivalence. If it is true, then what it says—that it is false—is the case, so it is false. If it is false, then what it says is the case, so it is true. Thus it is true if and only if it is false, which classical logic cannot accept.
The strengthened liar, "This sentence is not true," replaces "false" with "not true." This matters because a theory that rejects bivalence can classify the simple liar as neither true nor false, whereas the strengthened liar is supposed to resist that classification.
Kripke's theory is the standard gap approach. It begins with the non-semantic facts and builds up the extension and anti-extension of the truth predicate in stages. Sentences such as the liar and the truth-teller never enter either set, so they are ungrounded and, on the strong Kleene three-valued scheme, receive no truth value. The simple liar therefore comes out neither true nor false, and the contradiction is blocked.
The same treatment extends to the strengthened liar. Since "This sentence is not true" is equally ungrounded, it too is neither true nor false, and the paradox is dissolved. One might worry that a sentence that is neither true nor false is still not true, so the strengthened liar would be true after all; but this worry arises only if one forgets that, in Kripke's framework, negation over an ungrounded sentence yields no truth value, so the sentence does not assert anything true. The strengthened liar therefore poses no special problem.
Tarski's solution, by contrast, bans self-referential sentences from the language altogether. It is more drastic, but it is the only approach that fully avoids the strengthened liar, since a language that cannot talk about itself cannot formulate the paradox in the first place.
