I was at a dinner party last weekend and somehow we got onto whether science can tell us what we ought to value. Someone (a friend of a friend, very confident) kept saying "you can't get an ought from an is" as though it just ended the conversation — like, you say that and suddenly everyone has to concede that evolutionary psychology has nothing to say about ethics, that AI alignment is philosophically confused, that anyone trying to ground morality in human nature is making a category mistake. The thing is, I've heard this slogan a hundred times and I've never actually sat down and figured out what it really means. It gets thrown around in my philosophy of mind class too, when people talk about whether consciousness gives you normative reasons, and I've seen it in online arguments about whether the fact that something is "natural" makes it good. Every time, the person saying it acts like they've just played a trump card and the discussion is over.

So I want to get clear: what are the distinct, precise claims that "you can't get an ought from an is" might be making? I can feel there's a whole family of claims here — a narrow point about valid deduction, a semantic point about how normative words mean something different from descriptive ones, maybe a metaphysical point about normative facts being a fundamentally different kind of thing, maybe even an epistemic point about what you can learn from observation. Lay them out as a menu, from weakest to strongest, flag which ones are near-trivial (like, no one would dispute them once stated clearly), which are substantive but defensible, and which are probably false or at least way too strong. And the thing I most want: for each reading, tell me whether it actually supports the dinner-party move of dismissing empirical or scientific work as irrelevant to ethics. I'm starting to suspect the slogan does a lot less work than people think, and that the move from "there's a logical gap" to "science is irrelevant to values" might itself be sloppy. Bonus points if you can identify which reading Hume himself probably had in mind versus what the slogan has morphed into in internet argument culture.
