At a party last week two friends of mine got into it about how you're supposed to reason when a theory says the universe contains more or fewer observers, and I honestly couldn't referee it. Here's roughly how it went.

Sam said the self-indication assumption (SIA) can't be right, because of the presumptuous philosopher argument: take two cosmological theories that are identical in every observational prediction, except one says the universe contains a trillion times more observers. SIA would force you to be a trillion times more confident in the many-observer theory before you've collected any new data, which Sam said is just absurd.

Dana replied that your own existence is new data. The fact that you exist is exactly the kind of observation that discriminates between a one-observer universe and a trillion-observer universe, and refusing to count it is just refusing to use evidence because it's too close to home. She added that the self-sampling assumption (SSA) has its own embarrassment, namely the doomsday argument.

Sam came back that the doomsday argument doesn't force anything, since it depends on a reference class and a prior you have to supply, whereas SIA's trillion-to-one confidence is forced no matter what. He also said SIA is hostage to an arbitrary choice about what to count as an observer — count person-moments instead of people and the trillion turns into whatever number you like.

Dana said that was backwards: the reference-class problem is SSA's problem, because SSA tells you to sample from your reference class but never says which one, while SIA avoids it by sampling over all possible observers. And if a theory really predicts more observers on any way of counting them, she said, favoring it isn't arbitrary, it's ordinary likelihood reasoning.

After that it just went in circles. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
