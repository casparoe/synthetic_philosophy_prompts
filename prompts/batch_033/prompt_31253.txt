In a book on the history of religious ideas, I read a paragraph about Maimonides' view of the biblical animal sacrifices that has stayed with me, and I want to know how much of it to trust. The author writes, roughly:

'Maimonides held that the sacrificial cult was never God's real desire. In the Guide of the Perplexed he explains it as a concession to the idolatrous habits of the ancient Israelites: since people in that world could not conceive of worship without slaughtering animals, God tolerated a domesticated version of that worship — redirected from idols to himself — purely as a stepping stone toward the higher worship of prayer and knowledge of God. On this account the sacrificial laws have no intrinsic value; they were a temporary accommodation, and in an ideal or messianic future they would be left behind.'

The first part strikes me as plausible, but the last sentence seems to move faster than the evidence, especially since I vaguely recall that Maimonides codified the sacrificial laws in detail in the Mishneh Torah and wrote something about their restoration in the messianic age. I cannot tell whether the author is drawing a fair conclusion or quietly sliding from 'not the primary purpose of the commandments' to 'no value at all,' and from 'a concession to ancient conditions' to 'destined to be abolished.' Which parts of this summary would a careful scholar of Maimonides accept, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and does the conclusion that Maimonides regarded the sacrificial cult as intrinsically valueless and due to disappear actually follow from what he wrote?
