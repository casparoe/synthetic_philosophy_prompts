I run an intelligence analysis unit in a government agency. My analysts produce assessments on geopolitical and security questions, and every assessment goes through internal review before dissemination. A persistent problem: reviewers sometimes challenge an assessment not because the evidence is weak or the reasoning is flawed, but because the conclusion, if accepted, would have undesirable consequences — it might embolden an adversary, alarm allies, constrain policymakers, or be politically weaponised. I am told that philosophers have analysed exactly this kind of distinction under the label "the wrong kind of reasons problem": roughly, the difference between reasons that bear on whether an attitude (here, a belief) is correct, and reasons that bear on whether it would be good to have that attitude. I need a decision procedure my review team can apply to classify a given objection to an assessment as a reason of the right kind (bearing on the truth of the assessment or the quality of the evidence and reasoning supporting it) or a reason of the wrong kind (bearing on the desirability of accepting or disseminating the assessment). The procedure must be a checklist or sequence of tests that reviewers with no philosophy background can apply in under ten minutes. It should handle cases where the distinction blurs, and it should explicitly flag where the procedure cannot settle the matter and an editor or senior analyst must decide. Then apply the procedure to these three objections:

1. "If we assess that the rebel faction controls significant territory in the north, foreign governments will cite our assessment as a pretext for military intervention."
2. "The analyst's primary source for the claim about weapons stockpiles has recanted their earlier testimony and has a documented history of fabrication."
3. "The assessment uses the phrase 'cannot be ruled out' for a low-probability event; this wording will be read by non-expert readers as implying the event is likely, and the resulting public alarm will itself cause diplomatic damage."
