I recently read a feature in a serious magazine that summarized a technical result in value theory, and before I mention it in a seminar I want to know how much of the summary I can rely on. The article presents the argument this way:

"Take a spectrum of painful experiences. At one end is a brief episode of extremely intense pain; at the other is a very long episode of mild, barely noticeable discomfort. Between the two lies a long series of intermediate cases, each of which reduces the intensity of the pain by a tiny amount while increasing its duration substantially. At each step, the next experience seems worse than the one before, since the added duration matters more than the slight drop in intensity. We thus get a chain in which each item is better than its successor. And yet, looking at the spectrum as a whole, the years of mild discomfort seem better than the brief intense pain. The author concludes that 'better than' is intransitive, and then draws the further moral that there is no single scale on which the value of experiences can be measured: any attempt to rank such cases along one dimension of value distorts what is really going on."

I recognize this as a spectrum argument, and I am inclined to think the author's presentation is more careful in the setup than in the concluding moral. But I would like a patient audit before I repeat it. Please go through the summary claim by claim: which parts accurately reflect what the underlying argument establishes, which parts conflate distinct relations or overstate the result, and how much of the concluding moral actually survives a careful reading of the argument? I want to be able to tell my students where the argument's real force lies and where the magazine's presentation slides into something stronger than the argument supports. Please write your answer in American English.
