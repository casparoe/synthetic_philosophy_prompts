I'm a graduate student in epidemiology with a side interest in philosophy of science, and I've been working through the literature on causal inference — particularly the tension between counterfactual theories of causation (Lewis, and the Rubin potential-outcomes framework that dominates my field) and interventionist theories (Woodward, Pearl). Something about the way my discipline institutionalizes the randomized controlled trial as the gold standard for causal claims strikes me as philosophically undertheorized, and I'd like your help thinking it through.

Here's the practice I have in mind: evidence-based medicine ranks RCTs — and especially systematic reviews of RCTs — at the top of its hierarchy of evidence, well above observational studies, case-control studies, and expert opinion, no matter how sophisticated the statistical adjustments in the lower tiers. The canonical justification is that randomization breaks confounding by making treatment assignment independent of potential outcomes, which lets us estimate the average treatment effect without bias. But this justification is couched in the language of the potential-outcomes framework, which is explicitly counterfactual: we're comparing Y_i(1) to Y_i(0), what would have happened to the very same individual under treatment and under control. And yet the practice seems to trust only those counterfactual estimates that come from an actual intervention — randomization. So I'm trying to figure out: does the RCT hierarchy presuppose a counterfactual metaphysics married to an interventionist epistemology, and if so, is that marriage stable? If causation is fundamentally about counterfactual dependence, why should actual interventions be epistemically privileged in the way the hierarchy insists? And if causation is fundamentally about interventions — as Woodward and Pearl would have it, where X causes Y just in case there is a possible intervention on X that changes Y — why does the practice keep talking about individual-level counterfactuals that we can never observe both sides of? There's a further puzzle: Pearl's do-calculus gives the interventionist framework resources to extract causal claims from observational data under certain structural assumptions, but the institutional practice of evidence-based medicine largely doesn't trust those resources, except in narrow cases where randomization is genuinely impossible. So the hierarchy seems to be doing philosophical work that nobody in the department wants to acknowledge.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of science who specializes in theories of causation. Start by laying out the distinction between counterfactual and interventionist theories clearly enough that I can see where they genuinely disagree and where they might be compatible — don't just give me textbook definitions. Then reconstruct the philosophical commitments that the RCT hierarchy appears to presuppose, carefully distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with or has inherited from institutional history. Finally, tell me whether the practice is philosophically coherent on its own terms, and what would have to change about how medical evidence is evaluated if we committed consistently to one theory of causation or the other. Think carefully and work through the architecture underneath the standard story — I already know why randomization controls for confounding. I want to know what theory of what causation is makes that fact carry the weight it does.
