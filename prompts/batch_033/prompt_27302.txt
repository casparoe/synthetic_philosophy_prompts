I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about induction and Goodman's grue, and I came away with a clearer sense of where I stand, but also a nagging feeling that some of my commitments might not fit together. I would like an audit. Here is roughly the set of views I ended up defending:

(1) Hume was right that induction cannot be given a non-circular justification — any attempt to justify induction either begs the question or regresses — and I do not think anyone has genuinely answered him on that point.

(2) Nevertheless, I do not think this makes induction irrational. Rationality does not require the kind of foundational guarantee Hume was looking for; inductive practices can be rational in a more modest sense, perhaps because they are unavoidable or because they reliably track the world even if we cannot prove that they do.

(3) Goodman's grue paradox is a genuinely different problem from Hume's. Hume asked whether induction is justified; Goodman asked, given that we make inductions, why we project some predicates (like green) and not others (like grue — observed before some future time t and green, or not so observed and blue). The two problems are independent, and solving one does not solve the other.

(4) The right response to grue is that grue is gerrymandered in a way that green is not: it is defined disjunctively in terms of a particular temporal boundary, and we have a principled reason to prefer non-gerrymandered, non-disjunctive predicates when projecting from observed cases to unobserved ones.

(5) But I also worry that the notion of a gerrymandered or unnatural predicate is itself hostage to our inductive practices — we only think green is more natural than grue because we have already done a great deal of induction, and the very idea of naturalness may just be a projection of our inductive habits rather than an independent constraint on them.

(6) I think Bayesianism dissolves the grue problem in practice: we simply assign a much lower prior probability to the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue than to the hypothesis that they are all green, and once the priors are in place, conditionalization does the rest. The grue hypothesis is not ruled out; it is just swamped.

(7) The real lesson of grue, in my view, is that any formal theory of confirmation needs a substantive, non-formal constraint on which predicates are eligible for projection, and this constraint cannot itself be justified formally — it is a kind of bedrock, something we bring to inquiry rather than something inquiry can vindicate.

These feel right to me individually, but I suspect they cannot all be true together. I want to know which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. If I have to revise, I want to know what the cheapest fix is and what it costs — not a lecture on induction, just a diagnosis of my own position.
