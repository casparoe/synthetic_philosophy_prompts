I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about the analytic-synthetic distinction — whether some truths hold purely in virtue of meaning, or whether, as Quine argued, the whole idea of a sharp line between meaning and fact is untenable. Going by my gut on cases, here is where I land.

"All bachelors are unmarried": analytic if anything is. I don't see how you could deny this without changing the subject.

"All triangles have three sides": also analytic. This simply unpacks what a triangle is.

"Water is H₂O": not analytic. It took empirical inquiry to discover this, and someone could competently use the word "water" without knowing anything about its chemical composition.

"Nothing can be red all over and green all over at the same time": I want to say this is analytic, but I hesitate. Is it built into the meanings of "red" and "green," or is it a very deep fact about how color perception works — something we could discover to be false under unusual conditions? I lean analytic but I'm not fully settled.

"Force equals mass times acceleration": this is the one that genuinely bothers me. In some textbook presentations it looks like a definition of "force," which would make it analytic. But in others it looks like an empirical generalization that could in principle be revised — and indeed it was revised by relativity. I can't make up my mind.

"All cats are mammals": I think this is analytic. If we discovered that the creatures we call cats were actually sophisticated robots with no evolutionary history, I'd say we were wrong that they were cats, not that cats aren't mammals. The classification seems built into the concept.

"The morning star is identical to the evening star": clearly not analytic. This was an astronomical discovery.

"All events have causes": not analytic. It's a substantive metaphysical claim, and someone who denies it isn't misusing the word "event" or "cause."

"If A is taller than B and B is taller than C, then A is taller than C": analytic. This follows from the logic of comparative relations, which seems built into the meaning of "taller than."

"Gold is the element with atomic number 79": here I waver. On the one hand, it feels like water = H₂O — an empirical discovery. On the other hand, "gold" seems more tightly tied to its atomic number than "water" is to H₂O; if we found a substance that looked like gold, felt like gold, and had all the superficial properties of gold but had atomic number 78, would we call it gold? I think not. So maybe this one is analytic after all, or at least closer to the analytic side than the water case.

"All bachelors are unhappy": clearly not analytic, even if it were true.

"Every effect has a cause": I find myself pulled in two directions. It sounds like "all events have causes," which I called synthetic. But "effect" is defined in terms of being caused — so maybe "every effect has a cause" is analytic in a trivial way that "every event has a cause" is not. I'm not sure whether to treat these differently.

Here's what I'm after. Is there a single principle — or a small set of principles — that captures these verdicts and explains the ones I'm uncertain about? If no clean principle gets all of them, which verdicts should I reconsider? And what does your account say about a case I keep going back and forth on: "All knowledge is justified true belief"? Before Gettier, many would have called that analytic. After Gettier, it looks false. But if it's analytic, how could it be false? And if it's synthetic, what was the empirical discovery?
