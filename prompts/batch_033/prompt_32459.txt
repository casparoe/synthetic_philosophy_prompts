For a metaphilosophy seminar I am organizing, I need a decision tree that sorts participants into positions on the question "Does philosophy make progress?" The tree must be built from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of established positions: first carve the space with diagnostic questions, then locate actual defended positions within it, and flag any logical combinations that no one seems to occupy.

**Positions that must appear as leaves** (you may add others if the logical space demands them):

- Cumulative convergence: philosophy gradually converges on correct answers to its questions, in a way structurally similar to the sciences, even if much slower and less conspicuous.
- Eliminative progress: philosophy makes progress primarily by ruling out false options, narrowing the space of live possibilities, and sharpening the questions—without necessarily converging on a single correct answer.
- Dialectical progress: philosophy advances through cycles of position, critique, and synthesis, with each stage genuinely surpassing the last, but without any fixed endpoint.
- Problem dissolution: philosophy progresses by dissolving its problems—showing that they rest on conceptual confusions—rather than by solving them.
- Perennialism: the same fundamental questions recur across centuries; there is no net advancement, only variation in vocabulary and style.
- Pragmatic progress: the genuine measure of philosophical progress is downstream impact on law, science, politics, and everyday life, not internal resolution of philosophical questions.

**Diagnostic questions**: The first branch should separate participants based on whether they think philosophy exhibits any directional change worth calling progress at all. Subsequent branches should probe: whether convergence of expert opinion is the right metric; whether the continued relevance of ancient texts (Plato, Aristotle, Zhuangzi) is a sign of failure or a sign of depth; whether consensus is necessary for progress; and whether the object of progress is answers, questions, concepts, or practical outcomes.

Each node must be phrased as a concrete question that someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to—no jargon, no position labels embedded in the question wording. The audience is philosophy graduate students and faculty, so the questions can assume familiarity with the debate but must not presuppose allegiance to any side.

**Unoccupied regions**: Identify any logical combinations of answers that no one seems to defend (for example, what would it mean to hold that philosophy converges on answers but also that there is no fact of the matter about those answers?). For each such region, state briefly whether it is probably empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive on reflection, or because it is simply unexplored.

**Inseparable positions**: Flag any two positions the tree cannot cleanly separate with the diagnostic questions you have chosen, and explain what additional question would be needed to split them.
