I play a lot of two-player abstract games with a friend, and he recently showed me a line of reasoning he calls strategy stealing, which he says proves that the first player has a winning strategy in any finite game with perfect information, no chance, and no draws. The argument goes like this. First, in such a game, one of the two players must have a winning strategy. Second, suppose the second player had one. Third, the first player could make an arbitrary opening move, then from that point on pretend to be the second player and follow the second player's winning strategy. Fourth, if the stolen strategy ever calls for a move on the square the first player already occupies, she simply plays anywhere else, since that extra piece is a free move that can never hurt her. Fifth, the first player would therefore win, which contradicts the supposition that the second player had a winning strategy. Sixth, since the second player has no winning strategy, and by the first step one of the two players must have one, the first player does. He says this is why the first player wins at Hex. But then he applied the same reasoning to tic-tac-toe and concluded that the first player wins there too, even though tic-tac-toe is a draw with perfect play. That is where I got lost: the argument seems to prove too much, but each step looked fine to me. I can feel that something changes between the claim that the second player has no winning strategy and the claim that the first player has one, and I suspect the harmless-extra-piece assumption is doing more work than my friend admits, but I cannot pin down either point. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does the strategy-stealing assumption require before the argument can go through? If I give up the step you identify, what does that cost me in games like Hex, where the conclusion happens to be true?
