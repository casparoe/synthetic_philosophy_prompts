I recently joined a local civic-tech group that tries to get city agencies to treat their policies as revisable hypotheses rather than fixed commitments, and I have been reading Dewey's experimentalist account of democracy and inquiry to think through what we are actually doing. That has made me want to see how well the theory handles a real, large-scale case, rather than the small participatory settings where it usually gets invoked.

Take the New Deal, roughly 1933 to 1938, as the episode and Dewey's experimentalism as the lens. By Dewey's experimentalism I mean roughly this: inquiry begins from an indeterminate or problematic situation; ideas are instruments whose meaning lies in the observable consequences they produce when acted on; a claim is warranted to the extent that it survives testing in experience; ends and means are not fixed separately but are revised together as inquiry proceeds; and democracy is the method by which a public organizes itself to conduct such inquiry about the indirect consequences of associated life, rather than a mere mechanism of voting or preference aggregation. Roosevelt's administration improvised a series of large interventions: abandoning the gold standard, banking reform and deposit insurance, the National Industrial Recovery Act and the first Agricultural Adjustment Act, the Tennessee Valley Authority, public works and relief through the Works Progress Administration, the Wagner Act, and Social Security. Some of these were explicitly framed as trials to be watched and adjusted, others were pushed as principled commitments, and the Supreme Court struck down parts of the early program in 1935 and 1936, after which the administration regrouped.

Analyze the New Deal through this lens. For a handful of the programs I named, state the hypothesis each embodied, what would have counted as evidence for or against it, whether the administration actually observed outcomes and revised in light of that evidence, and what the program's fate shows about democratic experimentalism. Then run the analysis the other way: what does the episode show about Dewey's theory? Where does it confirm that experimental governance is workable, where does it reveal that the theory is silent or in tension with how a mass public actually inquires, and does the New Deal's heavy reliance on administrative expertise and executive energy undermine Dewey's claim that the public itself can be the inquirer? I want you to commit: tell me, on balance, whether the New Deal is better read as an instance of Deweyan experimentalism or as an episode that chiefly shows its limits, and defend that verdict rather than laying out both sides and stopping.
