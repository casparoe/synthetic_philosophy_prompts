I work as a legal officer for a small statutory regulator of letting agents and private landlords. Several times a month a disciplinary or registration case turns on the meaning of a single provision of our enabling statute or rules, and I have to advise a lay committee on how to read it before they vote. The decision can cost a licensee their livelihood or deprive a tenant of a remedy, and it must be defensible on judicial review; I usually have half a day, no budget for counsel, and a committee that is intelligent but legally untrained. I need a procedure I can reuse rather than starting from scratch each time.

Please write me a decision procedure for choosing between a textual and a purposive reading of a statutory provision, grounded in the textualism/purposivism debate. The committee should not need to know the theory, but I do: make clear which steps are genuinely dictated by the theory and which are pragmatic shortcuts or judgment calls, and where textualism and purposivism would give different instructions at the same step say so rather than blurring them into a general 'look at the context'. In particular, the procedure should keep distinct (i) resolving a genuine ambiguity in the language, (ii) declining to extend clear text merely because the result seems harsh, and (iii) the point at which an otherwise textualist approach recognises an absurdity or drafting error and departs from the ordinary meaning. Present it as an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer for each, where the procedure stops with a definite answer, and where it must hand the matter to the committee as a question of values rather than law.

Then run the procedure on these three cases and say explicitly what answer it gives: (1) a licensed agent is alleged to have 'entered a tenant's premises' without the required notice after flying a drone through an open window to photograph the interior; (2) an unlicensed person is alleged to have 'advertised a property as available to let' by telling tenants in a private messaging group that a flat would shortly be available; (3) a licensee with a diagnosed anxiety disorder asks to attend a disciplinary hearing by video link rather than 'in person' as the rules require. Ensure that at least one of the three produces an answer I will not like, and end by stating what the procedure cannot settle. Please write the answer in British English.
