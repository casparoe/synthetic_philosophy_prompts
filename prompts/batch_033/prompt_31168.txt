I've been reading through some of the recent literature on moral uncertainty — the problem of how to act when you're not sure which moral theory is correct — and I keep running into the same argumentative move. The move is to draw an analogy between moral uncertainty and ordinary empirical uncertainty and then conclude that the rational response is, in both cases, the same: maximize expected value. Here's roughly how it goes. When I'm uncertain about a factual matter — whether it will rain, whether a job offer will come through — I list the possible states of the world, assign probabilities to them, evaluate how good each outcome would be, and pick the action with the highest expected value. The analogy says: when I'm uncertain about morality — whether utilitarianism or Kantianism or contractualism is correct — I should do the same thing. I list the candidate moral theories, assign credences to them, use each theory's own evaluative standards to assess how good each action would be, and then pick the action that maximizes expected choiceworthiness, weighted across theories by my credence. The analogy has real practical work to do: it is used to argue that we should sometimes partially accommodate moral views we find unlikely rather than betting everything on our favorite theory — for instance, that even a committed utilitarian should give some weight to deontological constraints, or that someone who is ninety percent convinced that factory farming is permissible should still give substantial weight to the ten percent chance that it's a moral catastrophe.

My question is: how far does this analogy actually hold? I want a systematic audit. What maps onto what — credences over possible worlds map onto credences over moral theories, outcome utilities map onto theory-relative choiceworthiness, and so on — and where does each mapping start to break? I'm particularly interested in the disanalogies that threaten the conclusion rather than ones that are merely cosmetic. For example, I've heard it said that in the empirical case there's a single currency of value (utility) across all states, whereas in the moral case each theory supplies its own unit of evaluation and there's no guarantee of intertheoretic comparability. How serious is that objection, and are there others that are worse? Does the expected-value conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the analogy turn out to have been carrying more weight than it can bear? If there's a better analogy available — something that captures the structure of the problem without the misleading imports — I'd want to know that too.
