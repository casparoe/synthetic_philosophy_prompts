I've become convinced by the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies. The core reasoning, as I understand it, is this: for something to be bad for you, there must be a you for whom it is bad; but when you are dead, there is no you — no subject of experience, no bearer of interests — so there is nothing for death to be bad for. The state of being dead is not an experience one undergoes, and the deprivation of goods you would have enjoyed had you lived is not a harm that attaches to anyone, because the person who would have enjoyed those goods no longer exists. I don't deny that dying can be bad — the process can involve pain, fear, loss of dignity — and I don't deny that death can be bad for the survivors. But death itself, the state of nonexistence, cannot be bad for the one who dies.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not just the standard objections I've already encountered — that we can be harmed by posthumous events like the ruining of our reputation, or that we have an interest in our lives going well that death frustrates even if we're not around to experience the frustration — but the strongest possible case against the position, organized by how decisive each consideration would be if it succeeded. I want to know which arguments, if they were sound, would force me to abandon the view entirely, which would merely require me to qualify it, and which are often raised but don't actually threaten the core claim. I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the position, as I've stated it, has been insulated by qualifications that make it unfalsifiable — for instance, by restricting what counts as a harm to only experiential states — and if so, whether that kind of insulation is a problem for a view of this kind or is instead a reasonable feature of a conceptual claim about what harm consists in.
