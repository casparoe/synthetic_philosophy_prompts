I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I've been working through some of the recent literature on pragmatic encroachment — the idea that whether you know something, or what you're justified in believing, can depend on what's practically at stake. I find the view independently plausible, and I've been trying to identify cases where it makes a clear and uncontroversial difference to how we should reason.

Last week in my formal epistemology seminar, we discussed the Sleeping Beauty problem. For those unfamiliar: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, she is awakened on Monday only. If tails, she is awakened on Monday and Tuesday. In either case, she is given a drug that erases her memory of any previous awakenings, so each time she wakes up, the experience is subjectively identical. The question is: upon awakening, what credence should Beauty assign to the coin having landed heads?

The literature is split between halfers (who say 1/2) and thirders (who say 1/3). I read Elga's original paper and some of the back-and-forth, and I have to say I'm puzzled — not by the problem itself, which seems to have a clear answer, but by why anyone takes the halfer position seriously. Here's my reasoning: if Beauty is offered a bet on heads each time she wakes up, she should be willing to accept it only at odds that reflect a 1/3 credence; otherwise she'd be a money pump across repeated trials. This looks to me like a straightforward case where the practical stakes — the structure of possible awakenings — determine the rational credence. It seems like a perfect illustration of the kind of pragmatic encroachment I've been studying: the mere fact that Beauty might be awoken twice in the tails world changes what it's rational for her to believe upon awakening, not just what it's rational for her to do.

But when I raised this in class, my professor said I was conflating decision theory with epistemology and that the Sleeping Beauty problem isn't about pragmatic encroachment at all — it's about self-locating belief and the principle of conditionalization. I didn't really follow the distinction. If what you should believe is constitutively tied to what you should do — as pragmatic encroachment suggests — then why isn't the betting argument decisive? And if the problem really is about self-locating belief, why does almost everyone, including committed halfers, agree that Beauty should bet as a thirder would? Doesn't that concession already give away the game? What am I missing?
