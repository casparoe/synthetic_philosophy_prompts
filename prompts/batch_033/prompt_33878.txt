The Moloch essay and subsequent work on multipolar traps have shaped a recognizable intellectual camp: the view that many of the worst features of our world arise from situations where individually rational choices aggregate into outcomes that no one wants, and that recognizing this dynamic is essential to understanding — and perhaps escaping — artificial intelligence risk, institutional decay, and environmental degradation. But there is also a recognizable opposing camp: critics who think the multipolar-trap framing is overextended, that it conflates genuine coordination failures with ordinary conflicts of interest, that it often smuggles in contestable assumptions about what the "good" outcome would be, and that it underestimates the capacity of institutions to solve the problems it diagnoses. The two camps have been talking past each other for years.

I want you to draft the joint statement these two camps could produce after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should include:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. These should cover the core conceptual machinery (what a multipolar trap is, the formal structure, the difference between a trap and a prisoner's dilemma), the uncontroversial real-world examples both sides accept as genuine traps, and the empirical and institutional preconditions each side agrees are necessary for a trap to be a live concern. These claims should be substantive — not just definitions, but substantive points of agreement that narrow the disagreement.

2. A disagreements table. For each row, give: (a) the question in neutral wording; (b) the trap-theorist camp's answer, phrased so that its own proponents would accept it; (c) the skeptic camp's answer, phrased so that its own proponents would accept it; (d) why it matters — what further claim each side would draw if their answer were granted.

3. A section identifying the cruxes. For each crux, state: the sub-question that, if settled, would move at least one side; the empirical observation, historical case, or formal argument both sides agree bears on it; and each side's prediction about what that evidence would show if fully developed.

4. A section on verbal disputes. Identify any part of the disagreement that turns out, on closer examination, to be about words rather than substance — where the two camps use the same term differently, or where one camp's "trap" is the other camp's "trade-off." State the clarification that dissolves the verbal dispute and what residual disagreement, if any, remains.

5. Finally, a section on what each side would count as a serious concession. That is: what empirical discovery, theoretical result, or historical case study would each camp acknowledge as substantially weakening its own position? This should be written in the first-person plural voice of each camp respectively.

Do not write the document as a bland compromise. The agreement claims should be as strong as they can be while remaining genuinely acceptable to both sides. The disagreement formulations should capture the real intellectual stakes. Where the two camps share a value commitment, say so; where they differ on values, say so rather than pretending it is an empirical dispute. If you conclude that one side's view is harder to formulate precisely than the other's, note that asymmetry rather than papering over it.
