I'm in a philosophy of probability seminar and we've spent the last two weeks on the reference class problem: a single event (this tumor being malignant, this driver causing that crash, this coin landing heads) has no probability until you sort it into a class, but the same event belongs to indefinitely many classes that give different numbers, and nothing in the world seems to pick one of them as the right one. Every time I read a proposed solution it just picks a class and then argues about it, and I'm tired of watching people argue one case at a time.

Before I read any more of them I want the spec, not another theory. Write the desiderata: what does an adequate account of reference-class selection for single-case probabilities have to deliver? For each requirement, say what case or consideration forces it, and mark it non-negotiable versus a preference of one camp (frequentist, propensity, subjectivist, legal-evidence people, whatever). I'm especially after two things: wether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable (that's the part I'd bring to class), and which single requirement does the most to kill the popular fixes like always taking the narrowest reference class that still has reliable statistics. Also, I know some people think the right move is to deny that single-case probabilities are a thing at all — if that's a live option, say which requirements it satisfies and which it flouts. Give me exactly seven, as a numbered list, each item a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
