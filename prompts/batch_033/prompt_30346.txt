I've ended up in a long-running argument with a flatmate about decision theory, and we've built an argument that lands somewhere neither of us wants to be, but we can't agree where it breaks. I'd like you to tell us which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

Here's the argument.

(1) If your preferences over gambles violate the expected-utility axioms — say, you prefer A to B, B to C, but C to A — then there's a money pump: a sequence of exchanges, each of which you'd accept given your stated preferences, that leaves you strictly worse off than where you started.

(2) If there's a sequence of choices, each of which you'd accept on its own, that jointly guarantees you'll end up worse off, then your preferences are irrational.

(3) So the axioms are requirements of rationality.

(4) But the same form of argument can be run against anyone whose attitudes are imprecise or gappy. Someone who thinks the chance of rain tomorrow is somewhere between 0.3 and 0.7, or who has no settled ranking between two very different life plans, can supposedly be walked through a series of trades, each acceptable by their own lights, that they'd end up regretting.

(5) Yet it isn't irrational to have interval credences about a coin you've never tossed, or to decline to rank two incomparable careers.

(6) Therefore the coherence argument proves too much: if it's sound, rationality demands a sharpness and completeness that ordinary, sensible agents can't be blamed for lacking.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep (5), and I'm tempted to reject (2) — but if (2) goes, I worry the money-pump argument looses its force even against intransitive preferences, which seems like a big price. Is there a subtler fault somewhere between (3) and (5)? Please write your answer in British English.
