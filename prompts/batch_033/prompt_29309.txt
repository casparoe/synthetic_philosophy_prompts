I'm a journalist working on a piece about how game theory models hold up against actual historical messes, and I keep coming back to the dollar auction. You know it: two players bid for a dollar, highest bid wins the dollar, but the second-highest bidder also has to pay their bid and gets nothing. The trap is that once you're both in, the logic pushes you past the dollar — at 90 cents vs 95, you'd rather bid $1.00 and break even than lose your 90 cents, but then the other guy bids $1.05 and you're both losing money and still can't stop.

I want to use it to analyze the Eurozone's handling of the Greek debt crisis, roughly 2010–2015. Walk me through the mapping: who are the bidders here — Greece vs. the Troika? Germany vs. Greece? Or is there a domestic politics auction running in parallel inside each country? What's the "dollar" they're bidding for, and what were the sunk costs — not just the euros but the political capital, the credibility of the euro project itself?

Then the hard part: where does the model break? The dollar auction assumes the players are rational in the narrow game-theory sense, just responding to incentives. Were the actual decision-makers rational that way, or were they making different kinds of errors — wishful thinking, saving face, genuinely believing each bailout would be the last? And flip it around: what does the Greek crisis tell us about the dollar auction as a model? Does it show the model is too thin to explain real escalation, or does it reveal something about the model we should've seen from the start — like that in real life the auctioneer keeps changing the rules, or that theres never just two players?
