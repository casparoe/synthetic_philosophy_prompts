I'm a software engineer who works on compilers and programming languages. In popular science and in some philosophy of biology, I keep encountering the claim that DNA is a code in a strong sense—not just a loose metaphor, but that the genome literally encodes information the way a program encodes an algorithm, that genes are instructions, and that transcription and translation together constitute a decoding process. This claim is used to support the idea that genetic information has genuine semantic content (there is something it is about), and that we can think of mutations as bugs. I want you to audit this analogy. Give me a correspondence table: what in the molecular biology maps to what in the software domain—source code, the instruction set, the compiler, the execution environment, the build process, the running program. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, which features of software the analogy quietly imports that biology lacks (the intentionality of a programmer? a stable instruction set? a separate build phase?), and whether the conclusions people draw from the analogy—about semantic content, about "coding for" traits, about the genome as a program—survive once the disanalogies are on the table. I want to know whether the analogy earns its explanatory keep or whether it is a loose metaphor that has been asked to carry weight it cannot bear.
