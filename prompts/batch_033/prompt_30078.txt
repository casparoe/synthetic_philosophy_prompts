I work in AI alignment and I've been following the corrigibility / shutdown problem literature for a few years now, and it feels like the whole conversation has ground to a halt in a peculiar way. On one side you've got people who think corrigibility is basically the central challenge — if we can't build systems that let us turn them off or correct their goals, we're in serious trouble. On the other side you've got people who think the whole framing is confused, or that corrigibility falls out naturally if you get other things right (value learning, low-impact measures, etc.), or that it's not even a coherent thing to ask for because any agent smart enough to matter will resist shutdown by default, and any agent that doesn't resist shutdown just isn't optimising hard enough for its goals — so the only move is to not build agents with the wrong goals in the first place.

But here's the thing: the debate seems to have stalled not because anyone has been convinced, but because the two sides appear to be talking past one another about what would even count as a solution. One camp wants a formal guarantee; the other camp thinks the whole idea of a formal guarantee is misguided because corrigibility is inherently about the relationship between two agents with asymmetric power and knowledge, and you can't fully formalise that without baking in assumptions about the environment that make the result vacuous. And then there's a third voice, which I hear less often but which nags at me: that we're all arguing about corrigibility when the real problem is something else entirely, and the impasse is a symptom of that.
