Two friends of mine got into a surprisingly heated argument last weekend and I keep thinking about it. I'm a software engineer, so this is way outside my wheelhouse, but it's been bugging me. We were hiking and saw a deer with what looked like a pretty bad limp — maybe hit by a car or something.

My friend Dana said it was sad but that's just nature, and we shouldn't interfere. My other friend Amir said that's a cop-out: if we saw a dog limping we'd help it, and the only difference is we've decided wild animals are "supposed" to suffer. Dana said no, the difference is ecosystems — if you start intervening in every case of animal suffering in the wild, you'll mess up predator-prey dynamics, population balances, all of it. Amir said that's an empirical claim, not a moral one: maybe we should figure out how to reduce wild animal suffering without wrecking ecosystems, and the fact that it's hard doesn't mean it's not a moral problem.

Dana said there's something perverse about humans deciding we know better than millions of years of evolution. Amir said evolution doesn't optimize for happiness — it optimizes for reproduction, and a lot of animals live short brutal lives full of fear and pain as a result. He said if we have the capacity to reduce that suffering, we should, and the "let nature take its course" stance is just status quo bias dressed up as wisdom.

Dana came back with: but if we start managing wild animal welfare, where does it stop? Do we eliminate predators? Force-feed prey animals? It's a totalitarian project disguised as compassion. Amir said that's a slippery slope argument and we can draw lines — we already intervene in some cases (wildlife rehabilitation, vaccination programs) and nobody thinks it leads to totalitarianism.

At that point Dana said Amir was treating animals like they're just humans in fur coats, projecting our own values onto creatures that don't experience the world the way we do. Amir said that's the same move people used to make about other races — "they don't feel pain like we do" — and it's suspiciously convenient for whoever wants to avoid doing anything.

They asked me what I thought and I honestly didn't know what to say. Can you adjudicate this? Go through each move and say who's right about what. I'm especially curious whether Dana's ecosystem argument and Amir's response to it actually engage each other or whether they're talking past each other.
