I'm in a philosophy reading group, and last week we read a pair of papers on peer disagreement. The discussion afterward turned into a real argument between two members, Maya and Sam, and I've been turning it over ever since. I'd like someone to adjudicate their exchange move by move.

The setup: they both read the same paper arguing for a controversial thesis in metaphysics (something about composition as identity — though the topic itself doesn't matter much). Maya came away persuaded; Sam was unpersuaded. They agree they're epistemic peers on the topic: similar background knowledge, comparable intelligence, both read the paper carefully, neither was impaired or distracted. The question is what they should do in light of their disagreement.

Here's how their exchange went:

(1) Maya said: We should both substantially lower our confidence. If I think the paper is convincing and you don't, and we're peers, then the fact of our disagreement is itself evidence that at least one of us made a mistake. The rational thing is to move toward each other — maybe not all the way to 50/50, but significantly.

(2) Sam replied: That can't be right. I've already assessed the evidence. I read the paper and found the argument wanting. The fact that you reached a different conclusion doesn't give me new evidence about the paper — it gives me evidence about you. If I were to treat your disagreement as a reason to downgrade my confidence, I'd be double-counting: I already took the paper's arguments into account, and your judgment is just your own assessment of those same arguments.

(3) Maya came back: That's exactly the move the conciliationist literature warns against. You're using your own reasoning about the first-order evidence to dismiss my disagreement as higher-order evidence. The independence principle says that when evaluating the epistemic significance of a peer's disagreement, you can't rely on the very reasoning your peer disputes. Otherwise you just assume you're the one who got it right, which is question-begging.

(4) Sam answered: Then the independence principle is self-undermining. If I apply it consistently, I have to conciliate about everything — including the independence principle itself, since plenty of smart philosophers reject it. At that point I can't even state my epistemic norms with confidence. That's a reductio.

(5) Maya said: That's not a reductio — it's just the cost of taking disagreement seriously. The fact that consistent conciliationism is uncomfortable doesn't make it false. Maybe we should be far less confident about lots of things than we are.

(6) Sam said: But here's the deeper problem. You're asking me to treat your belief as evidence about the paper, independent of the paper itself. But your belief came from your assessment of the paper. So treating your belief as evidence is just treating your assessment as evidence — and I've already done my own assessment. To weigh your assessment against mine, I have to use my own assessment. There's no neutral standpoint. Conciliationism pretends I can step outside both of our perspectives and weigh them equally from nowhere, but I can't.

I couldn't tell who was gaining ground at any point. Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: for each of the six moves, say whether the speaker is on solid ground, making a mistake, or somewhere in between, and explain why. Then give an overall assessment: who is more right, and what — if anything — are both of them missing?
