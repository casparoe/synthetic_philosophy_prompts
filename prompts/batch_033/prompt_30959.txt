I'm on the science advisory committee for our state's fish and wildlife commission, and next month we've got to vote on whether to list a little spring-dwelling minnow — call it the glassy dace, since I shouldn't use the real name — under our endangered species act. Our statute lets the commission list species and subspecies, and there's a separate clause allowing distinct populations to be listed, but the commision has been skittish ever since an earlier listing got challenged in court because the taxonomy was a mess and the population turned out not to be a real species. So now they want us to tell them whether this fish is a species before they'll act.

The biology, briefly. The fish lives in two clusters of spring-fed creeks in the high desert. The northern and southern clusters have been mostly seperate for something like ten or twelve thousand years. They look nearly identical, but they differ in spawning temperature and salinity tolerance, and you can diagnose them with a couple of genetic markers. In the lab they interbreed and produce fully fertile offspring. In the wild there's a few miles where the two creek systems braid together during spring floods, and there's some gene flow each year — not much, but enough that the two populations aren't cleanly isolated. The genetics also say the southern population is more closely related to a widespread dace species two drainages over than it is to the northern population, which makes the southern population paraphyletic with respect to that other species. The southern population is the one in trouble: groundwater pumping has dropped the water table and two of its springs have gone dry in the last decade.

The committee is split. One side says this fails the biological species concept because of the ongoing gene flow, and fails the phylogenetic species concept because of the paraphyly, so it isn't a species — it's an ecotype, or at most an evolutionarily significant unit, and our law doesn't list those. The other side says species are partly a human sorting convention anyway, the biological species concept was never meant to settle cases where two populations meet only along a narrow hybrid zone and exchange genes at a trickle, and what matters is that this is a diagnosable, ecologically distinct lineage on its own trajectory, so we should list it as a distinct population and stop pretending species status is a yes/no fact about nature. A third view, from the chair, is that we should list it but only if we adopt an explicit framework where 'species' is defined relative to the question we're asking, and then say which question we're answering for legal purposes.

I need to circulate a short memo before the vote. It should recommend one concrete action — list, don't list, or list with conditions — with the reasoning, including an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. Also, since this exact fight will come back with every spring fish in the state, tell me whether the committee should adopt a general rule about how much gene flow, monophyly, or diagnosability matters for listing, and what that rule commits us to philosophically.
