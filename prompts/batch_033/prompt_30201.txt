I run the question-review team at a small real-money prediction platform. Alongside ordinary empirical questions, we list markets on open problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science — "Will the Riemann hypothesis be proved by 2040?", "Will a polynomial-time algorithm for 3-SAT be published by 2035?", and the like. We review roughly a dozen proposed questions a week and must decide whether to list each one, reject it, or treat it as a duplicate of an existing market. The recurring difficulty is that two questions can be the same underlying claim in different words, and we cannot tell whether we are looking at two markets or one. "Will Goldbach's conjecture be disproved by 2040?" and "Will someone exhibit an even integer greater than 2 that is not the sum of two primes by 2040?" are equivalent, but only because of what the conjecture means. By contrast, "Will P = NP be settled by 2040?" and "Will a polynomial-time algorithm for 3-SAT be published by 2040?" are not equivalent, though many of our users talk as if they were. We currently decide these cases by feel, and arbitrageurs have begun to notice.

I would like a decision procedure my team can actually run, grounded in what formal work on logical uncertainty — in particular logical induction — says about assigning probabilities to sentences whose logical relationships are unknown or too expensive to compute. The procedure should tell us, for any proposed question, whether to list it, reject it, or merge it with an existing market, and it should say how to handle the prices when we do not merge. Give us the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or mathematical judgment rather than procedure. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

The constraints are real. Our reviewers have an undergraduate-level background in mathematics or computer science but are not logicians, and we have no automated theorem prover — only a script that catches surface-level duplicates such as synonym substitution and double negation. A reviewer can spend about twenty minutes on a proposed question. The stakes are high for us because the markets are real-money and a mistaken merge or an inconsistent resolution rule can be exploited, but refusing to list too many questions kills the platform.

Then run the procedure on these three cases: (1) a proposed market on whether a polynomial-time algorithm for the Hamiltonian path problem will be published by 2035, alongside an existing market on whether a polynomial-time algorithm for 3-SAT will be published by 2035; (2) the two Goldbach phrasings above; and (3) a proposed question that is logically equivalent to an existing one under a nontrivial theorem that no one on the team can verify in the time allowed. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular, where logical non-omniscience makes the consistency I would prefer unattainable. Please write the answer in American English.
