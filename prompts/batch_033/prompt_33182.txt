A friend and I just spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether it can ever be rational to discount future wellbeing simply because it lies in the future, and he left me with an argument I cannot find a clean way out of. I will reconstruct it as he gave it.

(1) Pure time preference is the practice of counting a benefit or harm as less significant solely because it occurs later in time rather than sooner — holding everything else fixed: magnitude, certainty, who it accrues to, and so on.

(2) If pure time preference is rationally permissible, then temporal location must be a property capable of making a normative difference all by itself.

(3) Temporal location is not a property capable of making a normative difference all by itself. It is a mere indexical feature of one's perspective: the fact that a harm occurs on Tuesday rather than Monday, or in 2124 rather than 2024, is no more normatively weighty than the fact that it occurs in Jakarta rather than Chicago. From the point of view of the universe, all times are equally real; privileging the present moment is parochial in just the way we recognize that privileging one's own nation or species is parochial.

(4) Therefore, pure time preference is not rationally permissible. A rational agent must be temporally neutral: the wellbeing of someone living a century from now matters exactly as much as the wellbeing of someone alive today, other things equal.

(5) If temporal neutrality is rationally required, then we are rationally obligated to make very large sacrifices now for the sake of the far future. Future people vastly outnumber present people, and resources invested now can compound into enormous benefits over centuries. A temporally neutral agent would save at drastically higher rates, devote substantial effort to reducing existential risks, and generally treat the interests of distant generations as no less urgent than the interests of her own contemporaries.

(6) But we are plainly not rationally obligated to live this way. A theory of practical reason that demands we impoverish ourselves for the sake of the forty-first century is too demanding to be credible, and it clashes too violently with the way reasonable people actually deliberate and live.

(7) Therefore, at least one of (2), (3), or (5) must be false — or the inference from them to (6) must contain a mistake.

My friend thinks the weak point is (3), and he told me that some philosophers have tried to show that our relationship to our own future selves, or the structure of agency over time, gives temporal location a normative significance that spatial distance lacks. He finds those arguments unconvincing, which leaves him stuck with the conclusion that we are all systematically irrational about time — a conclusion he is oddly comfortable with. I find that conclusion preposterous, but I cannot tell where the argument goes wrong.

Please identify which premise or inferential step should be rejected, explain why it looked plausible in the first place, and say what rejecting it commits one to. If you think the problem is in the move from temporal neutrality to the demandingness conclusion in (5), be specific about where that move overreaches.
