I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of logic, and last week's session on the Liar paradox and dialetheism spilled over into a heated argument between two of my classmates that I've been replaying in my head ever since. We'd been working through Graham Priest's *In Contradiction* and the surrounding literature, and the debate crystallized around whether paraconsistent logic actually delivers on the dialetheist's core claim.

The exchange went something like this.

Marcus opened by defending Priest: "The Liar forces dialetheism. Hierarchical solutions can't express their own semantics without inconsistency. Gap theories — saying the Liar is neither true nor false — end up generating a strengthened Liar: 'This sentence is not true,' which is true if it's gappy, so the gap collapses. Priest's approach is the only one that takes the Liar at face value: the sentence is both true and false, full stop. And paraconsistent logic — specifically LP — gives us a formal framework where this doesn't trivialize everything, because explosion fails."

Elena wasn't having it: "But LP doesn't let you genuinely accept a contradiction. In LP, a sentence and its negation can both be designated — both 'true' in the logic's sense. But that just means the logic's negation isn't exclusionary. In any ordinary understanding, to negate something is to rule it out. If your 'not' doesn't rule out the thing it negates, you've simply changed what 'not' means. So dialetheism as advertised — 'there are true contradictions' — is trading on the ordinary, exclusionary sense of contradiction, while the formal machinery uses a non-exclusionary negation. That's bait and switch."

Marcus pushed back: "You're begging the question by identifying 'ordinary negation' with classical, Boolean negation. Natural language negation isn't univocal. In intuitionistic logic, not-P doesn't mean P is false in the classical sense — it means assuming P leads to a contradiction. In relevant logics, negation has still different properties. Paraconsistent negation is just another member of this family. The dialetheist's claim is that one legitimate sense of 'not' — the one formalized in LP — best models the negation actually at work in paradoxical self-reference. There's no equivocation; there's a proposal about which formal connective captures the real logic of the Liar."

Elena countered: "Then the dialetheist needs to tell me what the Liar's 'not' actually means, in terms I can understand independently of the formal semantics. If I utter 'this sentence is false' in English, I mean to deny the sentence — to assert its non-truth in a way that precludes its truth. If LP negation doesn't capture that preclusion, then LP hasn't modeled my utterance; it's modeled something else and called it the Liar. The burden is on the dialetheist to show that the ordinary concept of denial or falsity is itself incoherent in a way that LP negation makes coherent, rather than simply replacing an incoherent concept with a coherent but different one."

Rate each move on its merits, identify what each person is getting right and what they're missing, and say whether Elena's equivocation charge can be satisfactorily answered or whether it exposes a genuine instability in the dialetheist's position.
