I've been working through the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over whether anyone ever deserves punishment, and I keep noticing that the two of them seem to be grading rival positions against different, mostly unstated, standards of adequacy. Caruso writes as if a defensible practice of punishment would have to survive a deterministic, luck-pervasive picture of how people come to be who they are; Dennett writes as if that demand is a nonstarter, built on a conception of free will that no actual practice of holding people responsible needs. Before I can adjudicate the debate, I want the standard itself made explicit.

Write out the desiderata for a theory of just deserts and punishment, where the theory is meant to be adequate to the practice of criminal punishment in a liberal state. Do not summarize the two views; give me the full specification of what a theory in this area has to get right. It should cover: the cases it must handle (for instance, the offender with a frontal-lobe tumor, the recidivist whose childhood was a catalogue of abuse and neglect, the remorseless psychopath who is cognitively unimpaired, and the ordinarily decent person who commits a one-off offense under extreme pressure); the distinctions it cannot collapse (free will, moral responsibility, blameworthiness, desert, and aptness for punishment; excuse versus mitigation; backward-looking retributive warrant versus forward-looking social protection); and the established findings from neuroscience and psychology it must be consistent with rather than explain away. For each item, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which merely reflect one camp's commitments, and identify any subset that looks jointly unsatisfiable — that last part is what I most want to see. Close with a brief scorecard of Dennett's compatibilism and Caruso's free-will skepticism against the list, and say which single requirement does the most work in separating them.
