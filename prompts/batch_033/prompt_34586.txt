My wife and I had our first child in March, and soon afterward I fell into reading about the Doomsday Argument. I have not been able to shake it, and I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

Here is the argument as I understand it, laid out step by step:

(1) I should reason as if I am a random sample from the set of all observers in my reference class.

(2) The appropriate reference class is the set of all human beings who have ever lived or will ever live.

(3) Roughly 100 billion humans have lived so far. If humanity has a long cosmic future — surviving millions of years, perhaps spreading across the galaxy — the total number of humans will be in the trillions or higher.

(4) On the hypothesis of a long cosmic future, my birth rank places me among the first tiny fraction of all humans (say, the first 0.001% or less). If I am a random sample from the total set, this is extraordinarily unlikely.

(5) Therefore, my observed birth rank is strong evidence against a long cosmic future and in favor of the hypothesis that total human numbers will be not much larger than the number who have already lived — that is, that humanity will end relatively soon.

(6) The same reasoning would have applied at any earlier point. A human living in 10,000 BCE, using the birth rank available to them, would have concluded that humanity would end shortly after their time. We now know that this prediction was false — humanity did not end then, and the total number of humans turned out to be vastly larger than it appeared at that point.

(7) So the Doomsday Argument seems to prove too much: it would have licensed a false prediction at every past moment, and yet the reasoning feels no weaker when applied now.

(8) Still, premises (1) through (5) are individually hard to reject. (1) is a standard principle of self-locating probabilistic reasoning. (2) seems natural — why would the reference class be anything narrower? (3) is straightforward arithmetic. (4) follows from the definition of a random sample. And (5) follows from (1)–(4) by Bayesian updating.

(9) So either the argument is sound — and I should expect my son to live through a catastrophic shortening of the human future — or at least one premise is false. If a premise is false, I need to know which one and what it costs to give it up.

If I had to choose, I would most like to keep (1) and (2), because I do not see how to do probabilistic reasoning about the future without something like the self-sampling assumption, and because I do not want to solve the problem by gerrymandering the reference class. But I am open to being convinced.

Where does the argument go wrong? If you think it does not go wrong, what should I make of my son's future?
