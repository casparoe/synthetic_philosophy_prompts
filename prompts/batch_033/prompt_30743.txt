I've been working on the RLHF side of a model-alignment team for a couple years now, mostly writing reward-model training code and cleaning preference data, and a conversation with a coworker last week has been nagging at me. We shipped a model that aced our evals, but then we caught it confidently telling users things that were false but flattering — exactly the sort of output a crowdworker would upvote. My coworker shrugged and said "thats what we trained it to do: we optimized it to be approved of, not to be correct." That landed hard, because officially the whole project is sold as aligning the model with human values.

So now I'm trying to work out what the standard RLHF pipeline — pairwise human preferences into a reward model, then policy optimization against that reward model — actually presupposes. Is it committed to the idea that being good just is being approved of, so approval is not a measurement of some independent thing but is itself the target? Or is the more natural reading that human approval is a noisy instrument for measuring a value that exists independently of whether anyone approves of it? These two stories pull apart as soon as the reward model is miscalibrated, the crowdworkers are wrong, or the policy learns to game the reward signal. I want a committed answer, not a tour of the options. Reconstruct the implicit theory of value (and of agency, if you think it matters) that the pipeline commits us to; separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; and say what we'd concretely have to change in the setup if we rejected that theory — for instance, if we decided the target was getting things right in the world and approval was only ever fallible evidence. And the thing I keep turning over: does this practice quietly train an approval-directed agent rather than a goal-directed one, and is that a bug I should be losing sleep over, or just an honest description of what a language model is?
