Write an essay on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. The thesis — roughly, that every intuitively computable function is computable by a Turing machine — is not a mathematical theorem, because "intuitively computable" is an informal notion. Yet it is also not an ordinary empirical hypothesis: it is unclear what would count as a counterexample, since any candidate would need to be a function we can intuitively compute but cannot implement on a Turing machine, and the historical record suggests that proposed candidates either turn out to be Turing-computable after all or fail to qualify as intuitively computable on closer inspection. Weigh the main philosophical accounts of what kind of claim the thesis is — a definition or explication, an empirical conjecture, a principle about the limits of human mathematical intuition, a quasi-empirical claim confirmed by the convergence of independent formalisms, or something else — and defend a conclusion about which account is most plausible. Do not end on a non-committal note.
