I am a high school student and serve as the secretary of my school's student council. The council has five officers with equal authority, and under our current rules any one of us can act in the council's name: posting on our social media accounts, emailing the principal or another school, signing us up for joint events or campaigns, or making promises to other clubs. This has become a recurring problem. Several times this year, one officer committed the whole council to something that most of us would not have agreed to, and by the time the rest of us found out it was too late to back out without real embarrassment.

I recently read about an idea called the unilateralist's curse, and it seems to describe our situation almost exactly. As I understand it, the idea is this: when any member of a group can act on behalf of the whole group, and the members disagree about whether an action is a good idea, the group tends to take actions that most of its members would reject, because it takes only one member with an overly optimistic view to act; the larger and more divided the group, the more likely such a member is to exist.

I would like you to turn this idea into a reusable decision procedure that each officer can run before taking any unilateral action of this kind. It should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to the full council or to a particular officer because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be usable by high school students with no formal training, in under ten minutes, and sometimes with only a few hours before a deadline. Our full council meets once a week, so the procedure cannot simply say to wait for the meeting every time; it should say when waiting is required despite the deadline and when a quick text-message vote of the five officers is enough. It should also mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and which cases it cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. I want to sign the council up by the end of the day for a weekend fundraising event with a neighboring school. I think the event would be good for us, but the other four officers are doubtful: the student body is already overcommitted, and the event would require a lot of work. Backing out after signing up would damage our relationship with the other school.
2. An officer wants to post a routine, non-controversial reminder on our account that applications for next year's council close on Friday.
3. An officer wants to send the principal a sharply worded email in the council's name criticizing a new hall-pass policy. The officer believes the student body is furious; the rest of us are divided about whether this is the right way to raise the issue. The email cannot be unsent and would be read as the official position of the council.

Please make sure at least one of these is a case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do. Finally, tell me which argument or consideration would most change your answer if it turned out differently, and where in your reasoning you are least confident or most likely to be wrong.
