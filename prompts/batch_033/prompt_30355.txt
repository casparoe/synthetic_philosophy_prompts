Next month I'm presenting a paper at my department's epistemology workshop defending a level-splitting view of epistemic akrasia: it can be rational to believe p even while you rationally believe your total evidence doesn't support p, because the standards for first-order belief and the standards for your higher-order beliefs about your evidence aren't the same, and they don't have to cohere the way the anti-akrasia camp says they do. This stopped being abstract for me last spring, when I got a brutal referee report on a different paper and realized I was doing exactly this: I still believed the theorem was right, even though I'd come to think the report showed my evidence didn't support it, and I couldn't make myself see that combination as irrational. So now I'm comitted to the view, maybe more than is good for me.

The workshop audience is mostly epistemologists, not decision theorists or philosophers of mind. Two of the faculty have written on higher-order evidence and both think akrasia is always irrational — one through a structural requirement that your beliefs cohere with your beliefs about what your evidence supports, the other through the claim that higher-order evidence always defeats the first-order belief. There's a formal epistemologist who thinks in accuracy-first terms and worries about calibration and scoring rules, and an externalist who thinks this whole debate is a distraction from whether the belief-forming process is reliable. The grad students will have read the standard anti-akrasia papers but aren't already in either camp.

My positive argument, in one sentence, is that misleading higher-order evidence — like a generally reliable but in this case wrong psychologist telling you your belief was caused by bias — can make it rational to believe your first-order evidence doesn't support p without changing what your first-order evidence actually supports; since each level answers to its own evidence, the akratic pair can be the rational response at both levels. Give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a survey of the literature. I want the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way they'd actually phrase them. For each: how much damage it can do, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and be honest about whether I should weaken the thesis before the talk or go down with the ship. Commit to definite answers — don't lay out options and leave me to pick.
