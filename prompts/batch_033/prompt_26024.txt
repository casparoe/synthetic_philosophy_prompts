I've been turning over an argument from a metaphysics seminar and it's starting to freak me out because I can't see where it breaks. It goes like this:

(1) For a particular a to have a property F, a and F must stand in some relation — call it instantiation.
(2) But a relation only relates its relata if it is itself related to them. A relation doesnt just magically glue things together; the glue has to stick to the things.
(3) So for instantiation to relate a and F, there must be a further relation — call it instantiation₂ — that relates instantiation to a and to F.
(4) But then by the same reasoning, instantiation₂ needs instantiation₃ to relate it to its relata, and so on without end.
(5) Since the regress never bottoms out, the original fact — that a is F — is never actually constituted. There is no fact of the matter.
(6) This reasoning applies to any putative fact whatsoever, since any fact involves something's standing in some relation.
(7) So there are no facts. Nothing is ever anything.

Obviously (7) is insane. But I've been staring at this for a week and I can't tell whether the problem is with (2), or with treating instantiation as a relation in the first place, or with some assumption about what it takes for a fact to be "constituted." My prof mentioned that Bradley himself thought the regress was vicious and that it showed something deep about the inadequacy of relational thought, which makes me worry the argument might actually be sound and I just don't want to accept the conclusion.

Where does this go wrong? If I have to give up a premise, which one costs me the least, and what do I have to buy in its place?
