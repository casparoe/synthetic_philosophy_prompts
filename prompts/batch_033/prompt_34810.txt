I've been going back and forth with my roommate about this and I think I broke Scanlon's contractualism. We were arguing about triage protocols (he's a med student, I'm in philosophy) and I ended up convincing myself of something that can't be right.

Here's the argument:

(1) In Scanlon's contractualism, an act is wrong iff it's disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject.

(2) The reasonable rejection test is individualistic: you can't aggregate. One person's serious complaint isn't outweighed by piling up a bunch of smaller complaints from other people. That's the whole thing that separates it from utilitarianism.

(3) So: five people will die without organ transplants. One healthy guy walks in. Killing him to harvest his organs is wrong because the principle "don't carve up innocent visitors" is not reasonably rejectable — his complaint is massive, and the five patients' complaints, however genuine, can't be added together to outweigh it. So far so good.

(4) Now the burning building: you can save one person on the left or five on the right. If you go right, the one dies. If you go left, the five die.

(5) Consider the principle P: "when you can save either a smaller group or a larger group, and all else is equal, save the larger group." Can the one person on the left reasonably reject P? His complaint is: P tells you to let me die. That's a complaint about losing his life — the same complaint any of the five would have.

(6) Since we can't aggregate the five complaints, the one's complaint stands on its own and is not outweighed. So the one can reasonably reject P.

(7) But consider Q: "save the smaller group." Each of the five can reasonably reject Q, for the same reason.

(8) So no principle permitting either choice passes the test. Both choices are wrong.

(9) But you have to choose, and obviously you should save the five. And if the reasoning in (5)–(8) holds, it holds for any numbers: 1000 vs 1, a million vs 1. Contractualism seems to imply we can never justifiably save the many over the few.

I know Scanlon has stuff about this in What We Owe to Each Other — the tie-breaking argument, something about the value of life giving each person a reason to want a principle that saves more lives. But I can't see how that doesn't just smuggle aggregation back in through the back door. What step am I messing up, or is this actually a genuine problem he never fully solved?
