I retired a few years ago from a career in the physical sciences and have been spending my retirement working through the philosophy of science, especially Bayesian confirmation theory. A puzzle has come to bother me enough that I want to devote a year to it. In my own field, the observations that most convinced us of a new theory were often not new at all: they were facts that had been known for decades but had resisted explanation, and when a theory finally accounted for them, we treated that as powerful confirmation. The Bayesian formalism I am now studying, however, seems unable to represent this. If a known fact already has probability one, conditionalizing on it cannot change the probability of anything, so the cases scientists find most persuasive are invisible to the theory of confirmation. I gather this is called the problem of old evidence, and I have a half-formed suspicion that the standard responses misdiagnose it. The responses I have encountered either re-evaluate the old evidence against a reconstructed historical prior in which it was not yet known, or conditionalize on the fact that the new hypothesis entails the evidence rather than on the evidence itself. My suspicion is that both moves dodge a deeper question about what it is for something already known to become evidence: the difficulty may lie not in the evidence's age but in how a rational inquirer's total evidence is individuated, and in whether conditionalization is the right model of that transition at all.
