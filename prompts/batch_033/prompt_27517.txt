I am a novelist, and in my current book two characters — a field ornithologist and a former logician — spend an evening disputing the raven paradox. The ornithologist puts her credence that observing a white shoe confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black at 0.05; the logician puts his at 0.8. I would like your own credence in that proposition, where "confirms" means that a rational observer ought to raise their credence in the hypothesis at least slightly upon learning of the white shoe. Please decompose the reasoning into at least these terms: (a) the probability that a positive instance of a universal generalization confirms it, so that a non-black non-raven, as a positive instance of the generalization that all non-black things are non-ravens, confirms that generalization independently of background knowledge; (b) the probability that this generalization is confirmationally equivalent to the hypothesis that all ravens are black, so that evidence for one is evidence for the other; (c) the probability that, once realistic background knowledge is admitted — that non-black things vastly outnumber ravens and that the shoe is already known not to be a raven — the observation still raises the probability of the raven hypothesis; and (d) the probability that "confirms" has a single determinate, theory-independent meaning here. Assign each term a number and a one-line justification, show how the terms combine into your final credence, identify the term to which your credence is most sensitive, and then say which of my two characters you are nearer and on which term their disagreement most likely lives.
