I'm trying to work out what I actually think about honoring sunk costs, and I'd like help extracting the principle, if there is one, from my verdicts on cases. I'll just report my gut reactions.

1. I bought a non-refundable $90 ticket to a concert. By the day of the show I have a bad cold and I've also realized I no longer enjoy the artist's music. I go anyway, and when I'm honest the only reason is that I paid. I think going is a mistake: the $90 is gone either way.

2. I'm 600 pages into an 850-page novel that I find boring. I keep reading, telling myself I've come too far to stop. I think this is irrational, but I also can't bring myself to stop, which makes me suspect I don't fully believe my own verdict.

3. A friend has been unhappy in a relationship for years. She says she stays because they've been together nine years and she can't throw that away. I think staying for that reason alone is a mistake. The nine years are not an asset she loses by leaving; they're just the past.

4. A company has spent $40 million developing a product that is now clearly inferior to a competitor's. It decides to spend another $10 million to finish and launch it, on the reasoning that otherwise the $40 million is wasted. I think the extra $10 million is a fresh mistake, unless finishing actually buys the company something going forward.

5. I keep repairing an old car, year after year, because I've already put so much into it. Each new repair costs about what the car is worth. I think this is irrational: the past repairs don't make the next repair a better purchase.

6. I'm halfway through a jigsaw puzzle I no longer enjoy. I keep going because I've done half. I don't think this is irrational — it's a harmless way to spend an evening — but I'd be suspicious of someone who said the completed half was itself a reason to continue.

7. A gambler has lost $500 at roulette and doubles his bets because he is due to recover his losses. I think this is obviously irrational.

8. I'm ten miles into a long-planned hike I've trained for. The last four miles will be painful and somewhat risky in the weather. I decide to finish because I've come this far. This feels not just permissible but almost admirable to me — even though "I've come this far" sounds uncomfortably close to the reasoning I reject in the other cases.

9. I told my team I'd deliver a project by Friday. By Wednesday I've concluded the project isn't worth finishing. I finish anyway, partly because I've already put in two days and partly because they're counting on me. I think finishing is right, but I don't think the two days are doing the work — the promise is.

Is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that gets all of these, or nearly all of them, and that isn't just the platitude "past costs don't count unless something forward-looking does"? Please state it precisely enough that I can apply it, and show how it handles each case. In particular, I want to know how it separates the hike, and maybe the puzzle, from the concert and the gambler, or whether that separation can't be drawn and one of those verdicts has to go.

Then apply whatever principle you end up with to the case I keep going back and forth on: I've completed 70% of an online course I paid $300 for and now find it useless and unpleasant. Should I finish, and does the answer change if the reason I'm inclined to finish is only that I paid and that I've already done most of the work? Finally, tell me which of my nine verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to abandon, and whether a simpler principle that overturns one or two of my verdicts is more defensible than a complicated one that saves all of them.
