So I'm a novelist, and last month at this literary festival afterparty I ended up trapped between two people who went at each other about evolutionary psychology for like an hour. I was just trying to drink my wine. But I've been turning it over ever since because I'm working on a novel that deals with some of these ideas about why people are the way they are, and I genuinely don't know who was right.

Here's roughly how it went, as best I can reconstruct it:

Mira (she's a historian, I think) kicked it off by saying evolutionary psychology is basically the astrology of the sciences. You pick a trait, you spin a story about why it would've helped our ancestors reproduce, and you call it an explanation. There's no way to check if it's true. It's just-so stories dressed up in jargon.

Dan (some kind of biologist) said that's the laziest version of the critique. Plenty of evo psych hypotheses make testable predictions. He brought up the Trivers–Willard hypothesis about parental investment — it predicts sex-ratio shifts based on maternal condition, and he said those predictions have been tested and confirmed across species and across human populations. That's not storytelling, he said, that's normal science.

Mira came back with: sure, but for every Trivers–Willard there are fifty papers saying something like "men prefer younger women because fertility declines with age, and this explains age gaps in marriage across all cultures." But that doesn't explain anything new — people already knew men prefer younger women, and "fertility" is just a fancier word for the same observation. The explanation didn't earn its keep.

Dan said no, it ties a specific cultural pattern to a specific reproductive constraint in a way that generates new predictions. It predicts, for instance, that the age gap should narrow when men marry later or remarry — which it does. It also predicts cross-cultural variation in ways purely cultural explanations don't.

Mira leaned in at this point and said: here's my real problem. The stories always seem to land exactly where the researcher's own cultural assumptions already pointed. Victorian evo psych found that women were naturally coy and monogamous. 1990s evo psych found that men were naturally promiscuous spreaders of seed. It's suspicously convenient.

Dan admitted that's a real issue, but said it's a sociological critique, not a methodological one. Every field has scientists imposing their biases. The fix isn't to throw out the whole enterprise — it's to do better science, and he said modern evo psych has gotten more rigorous about cross-cultural data and preregistration.

Mira said she just doesn't see what "better science" would even look like for a field where you can't run the experiment. You can't rewind human history and tweak the ancestral environment. So you're stuck reverse-engineering from correlational data and a single outcome, and reverse-engineering from one endpoint is always underdetermined. You can always tell multiple adaptive stories that fit the same facts.

Dan replied that this is true of half the sciences — geology, cosmology, evolutionary biology itself all deal with historical, one-off events you can't rerun. The question is whether you can make novel predictions, and evo psych can and does.

At that point someone dragged them off for more drinks and I never heard the conclusion. But as someone who basically tells stories about people for a living, this debate gets under my skin — because it's partly about whether certain kinds of stories count as knowledge. Could you go through their back-and-forth and adjudicate it move by move? Who had the better of each exchange, and is there something important neither of them brought up?
