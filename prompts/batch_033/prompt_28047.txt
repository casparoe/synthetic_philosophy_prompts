I have come to accept epistemicism about vagueness: the view that vague predicates such as 'heap' and 'red' have sharp, unknowable boundaries, and that vagueness is a matter of ignorance rather than semantic indecision, truth-value gaps, or context-sensitivity. Tell me what would count against this view. I do not want the first-pass complaints about the boundary being arbitrary or about ordinary speakers failing to locate it; I want the defeaters that should actually move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest account of which ones defenders of epistemicism would themselves concede as counting against the position. In particular: what would higher-order vagueness have to look like to be a genuine problem rather than a restatement of the first-order view? What would a counterexample to the relevant margin-for-error principles for knowledge have to show, and how much of the view would fail with them? Which commitments in the philosophy of language and logic does the epistemicist have to accept as costs — concerning bivalence, the T-scheme for vague sentences, or the semantics of comparatives — and are any of those costs more than merely awkward? Are there formal or empirical results that would embarrass the view rather than merely fail to confirm it? End with an assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Then, as the final line, write 'Verdict:' followed by a one-sentence statement of how exposed epistemicism is.
