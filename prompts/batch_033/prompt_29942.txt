I'm a PhD student in econometrics, mostly panel data and treatment effects, and I've ended up going down a rabbit hole of alignment forum posts about Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK). From what I can tell, the problem is roughly this: a model has learned a lot about the world in the course of being trained to predict, and you want to build a reporter that answers questions out of that knowledge, but your only training signal is human judgement about the answers, and that judgement can be systematically wrong or gameable. I can't help feeling it's, at least formally, a problem about latent variables and mismeasured proxies: there's some unobserved ground truth, you get a noisy or biased label correlated with it, and your trying to recover the truth rather than the label. But the ELK people seem to think something special is going on beyond 'measurement error with a weird correlation structure'.

I'd like you to restate the ELK problem, and the main claims floating around about why it's hard and what a solution has to satisfy, in the vocabulary I actually use: latent variables, measurement error, proxies, identification, validation data, instruments, partial identification, that sort of thing. Be as faithful as you can, and flag any place where you're stretching a term to fit. Then give me a residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no econometric counterpart at all. In particular I want to know whether my intuition that ELK is 'just' non-classical measurement error with a strategic, optimisation-aware reporter is right, or whether the alignment people have identified something my vocabulary genuinly can't express. If something doesn't survive the translation, say whether that's a limitation of my framework or a sign that that part of ELK was never precise to begin with.
