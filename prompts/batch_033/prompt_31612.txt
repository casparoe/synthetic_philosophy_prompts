I'm living with chronic pain, and the pain psychologist I've been seeing keeps returning to the phrase "pain is just a signal." His point is that pain is the body's alarm system, that it need not track ongoing tissue damage, and that I should stop treating every flare-up as an emergency. I take the clinical point seriously, but I am not sure what exactly the slogan is supposed to amount to.

I have also been sitting in on a philosophy of mind seminar in which we discussed pain asymbolia: patients described in the neurological literature who can identify a stimulus as painful, locate it, and rate its intensity, yet say it does not actually hurt them and show no urge to withdraw from it. The case is usually treated as evidence that pain's sensory-informational aspect and its affective-motivational aspect can come apart. That makes me worry that "pain is just a signal" might be smuggling in a theory of pain's badness — or might be entirely compatible with the facts, for all I know. I want to avoid both strawmanning a clinically useful idea and quietly accepting a philosophical claim the evidence does not support.

You are a philosopher of mind who works on pain and affect. Please think carefully, and before settling on any interpretation, brainstorm the distinct precise theses that "pain is just a signal" might be used to express — about what pain is, about its biological function, and about what its badness consists in — from the anodyne to the radical. Then lay those readings out as a menu with explicit verdicts: which are trivial or empirically well-supported, which are substantive but defensible, and which are probably false or overreaching. For each reading, say whether it is supported, undermined, or left untouched by the pain asymbolia evidence, and finally identify which reading or readings my psychologist would actually need for his clinical advice — that I should stop treating pain as an emergency and that the hurtfulness can change when I reinterpret the signal — to go through. I would rather have the full disambiguation than a single gloss, because I suspect different readings are doing different work at different moments in my sessions.
