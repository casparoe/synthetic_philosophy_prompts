Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory, with about 20 students, that makes participants feel the force of the Ellsberg paradox and then forces them to confront what their choices commit them to. The deliverable should be a runnable game or structured choice exercise, not a lecture.

The activity should run in two phases. In the first phase, participants make the classic Ellsberg choices — bets on an urn with a known composition versus bets on an urn with an unknown composition — with small real stakes, before they hear the name Ellsberg or the phrase ambiguity aversion. The payoff scheme should use points toward a small prize or some other non-monetary stake; no real money should be needed. In the second phase, participants face a structured follow-up designed to expose the fact that the typical pattern of choices violates Savage's sure-thing principle. For example, the follow-up could re-frame the earlier bets as acts over a single set of states, or offer a hedged portfolio of bets whose combined payoff table is dominated by an alternative the participant preferred to each component separately. The follow-up should present participants with a genuine choice: revise the earlier preferences, or pay a concrete cost in points to keep the ambiguity-averse pattern. The point is for the tension to arise from the choices themselves before the facilitator names it.

Specify the exact rules and materials (urns, colored chips, how many of each, how bets are recorded), the payoff scheme in detail, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal distinctions between risk and ambiguity, the sure-thing principle, and what giving up the principle would cost. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run — for instance, participants who do not display the expected pattern, or who rationalize their choices in ways that dodge the tension — and how the facilitator should handle each. End with an honest note about which feature of the philosophical issue the classroom game cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply in words.
