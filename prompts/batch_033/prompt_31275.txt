I have been trying to get clear about when I think it is legitimate to say that one person is better or worse off than another, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts. Here they are, going on gut instinct.

(1) If one person has a mild tension headache and another has a migraine, I have no hesitation saying that the person with the migraine is worse off. I mean this as a claim about how things are for them, not merely as an expression of whom I would rather help.

(2) If one person is in severe acute physical pain and another is in acute grief, I refuse to rank them. I do not merely lack information; I doubt that there is a single scale of badness along which the two states differ.

(3) For a single person across time, I make even cross-dimension comparisons without hesitation. I can say that the grief I felt after a bereavement was worse than a toothache I had the same year, and I do not think I am merely reporting which I would rather relive.

(4) When two people report their overall satisfaction with life, I generally trust the reports. If Ana says she is at 8 out of 10 and Ben says 6 out of 10, I take Ana to be better off, absent special reasons.

(5) But I do not trust reports of satisfaction that look adapted to deprivation. Someone who has spent years in severe poverty may sincerely say that they are content with very little, and I find myself thinking that they may still be worse off than a moderately dissatisfied person in comfortable circumstances. I cannot say what makes this case different from Ana and Ben's except that the contentment seems to have been formed by the deprivation itself.

(6) Across species, I am willing to compare within a single dimension. A dog with a broken leg is worse off than a human with a paper cut. But a dog's chronic separation anxiety and a human's mild depression feel like different dimensions, and I balk at comparing them.

(7) Yet I have no trouble saying that a person with clinical depression is worse off than a person with a stubbed toe. So I do not have a general taboo against comparing physical pain with emotional distress across people.

The closest I can come to a principle is this: comparisons require a shared dimension of experience plus some notion of intensity; self-reports carry weight unless the underlying preferences were shaped by the deprivation they report on; and a single person's own history provides a common scale that is missing when we move between minds. But that principle cannot explain (7), since a stubbed toe and depression do not share a dimension, and it may not explain why I am so confident in (1) while so resistant in (2).

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts, or most of them? State the best candidate precisely, say which clause is motivated by which verdict, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I would have to overturn. Also tell me what it predicts about cases I have not listed: comparing a person's physical pain with their own emotional distress years later; comparing two people's anxieties of apparently equal intensity; comparing an infant's suffering with an adult's; and comparing a satisfied person whose preferences were formed by indoctrination with a frustrated person whose preferences are authentically their own.
