For a graduate seminar handout on the nature of supererogation, please construct a taxonomy of positions on whether there are acts that are morally good without being morally required. The taxonomy should take the form of a branching classification in which each split is labeled with a concrete claim or case that forces a choice, and each leaf names a position (or marks it as logically possible but unoccupied).

The tree must include at least the following positions as leaves: (a) the view that all morally significant acts are either required or forbidden, with no third category; (b) the view that supererogatory acts exist and are distinguished from duties by the cost or sacrifice they impose on the agent; (c) the view that supererogation is grounded in the optional pursuit of ideals or values rather than in agent-sacrifice; and (d) the view that supererogation exists but only within the space of imperfect duties, not as a category independent of duty.

Use diagnostic questions that turn on structural features of the views rather than on labels. For instance, one split might be: "Is there any act such that failing to perform it would be morally criticizable, but performing it would not be morally praiseworthy?" (This distinguishes views that treat the required/supererogatory boundary differently from the forbidden/permissible boundary.) Another might involve a case: "A passerby sees a child drowning in a shallow pond and wades in to save her at the cost of ruining an expensive suit. Is this act supererogatory, obligatory, or merely permissible?" The questions should be answerable by someone who has never encountered the term 'supererogation' before.

For any logically possible combination of answers that no one defends, briefly note whether the position is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive for an identifiable reason, or because it simply has not been explored. Flag any pair of leaves that your taxonomy cannot cleanly separate and explain what additional question would be needed to distinguish them.

The audience is philosophy graduate students who have read the standard literature but have not yet mapped the logical terrain systematically. The purpose is to help them locate their own view by working through commitments rather than by recognizing labels.
