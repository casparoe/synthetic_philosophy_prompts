In a seminar I run on deterrence theory, several participants keep treating a commitment race — the escalating contest in which each side tries to make its threat more irrevocable than the other's — as interchangeable with the game of chicken: two drivers heading at each other, each better off if the other swerves first, and both ruined if neither does. From the analogy they draw a strong conclusion: in a commitment race the rational way to win is to make your own swerving impossible, or to make the other side believe it is impossible, because a rational opponent will then back down. I suspect the analogy is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear, and I would like you to audit it. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what in a real commitment race plays the role of the cars, the steering wheel, the decision to swerve, the onlookers, and the head-on crash. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. I especially want you to examine the payoff structure the analogy quietly imports — that mutual ruin is the worst outcome for both sides and that coming out on top is preferable to any negotiated compromise — along with the assumptions of simultaneity, symmetry, common knowledge of the payoffs, perfect observability of who swerved, and a single one-shot encounter, since real commitment races unfold over time, under private information, with domestic audiences and reputations that carry into later crises. Say clearly whether the conclusion survives the disanalogies once they are on the table, and if it does not, offer a better analogy or model for what a commitment race actually is — for instance, one in which each side deliberately delegates the final move to a partly uncontrollable process rather than simply trying to look fearless.
