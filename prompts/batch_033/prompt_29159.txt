I've been following a long-running exchange between two epistemologists whose debate has appeared in journals, conference panels, and a particularly tense symposium issue, yet never seems to reach resolution. One — call her the hinge epistemologist — works in the tradition of Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*. On her view, rational inquiry and doubt are only possible against a backdrop of commitments that are themselves exempt from rational evaluation: propositions such as "the earth has existed for more than five minutes" or "here is a hand" are not things we know on the basis of evidence, nor are they merely very confident empirical beliefs. They are hinges — the scaffolding that must stay fixed for the door of inquiry to turn at all. Their status, she argues, is arational: they are neither justified nor unjustified, but rather constitute the framework within which justification becomes possible. The other — call him the classical invariantist — regards this as a philosophical mistake, and a costly one. On his account, the so-called hinges are simply ordinary empirical propositions about which we happen to have overwhelming evidence; the difficulty of imagining their falsity is a fact about our psychology, not about their epistemological standing. If they cannot meet the ordinary standards of justification, that is a problem for our claim to know them — not an invitation to invent a new category of arational commitment.

They have now agreed, after some persuasion, to attempt an adversarial collaboration: a document they can both sign, laying out the terrain of their agreement and the precise topography of their disagreement. I would like to publish the result in the magazine I write for, which covers ideas at this level of resolution for a scientifically literate but non-specialist audience. Please draft this document. It should include: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties accept — these must be substantive, not mere platitudes (e.g., not simply "Wittgenstein wrote *On Certainty*"), and should capture what they jointly affirm about the phenomenon of bedrock commitments, about the structure of doubt, and about the limits of the traditional epistemological framework; (2) a disagreements table in which each row states a single, precise point of divergence, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would ungrudgingly endorse as its own; (3) a section identifying the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one party from their current position; and (4) for each crux, the kind of case, argument, or empirical consideration both would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about what such an inquiry would reveal. I am especially interested in whether any part of their dispute turns out to be verbal — a clash of terminology rather than substance — and if so, I would like that identified explicitly and separated from the genuine disagreements.
