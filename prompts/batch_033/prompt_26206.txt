A piece in a popular philosophy magazine summarizes the debate over higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia as follows:

"The idea is simple: you can have evidence about your own evidence. Suppose you're good at math, you've checked a proof three times, and you conclude it's valid. But then a colleague tells you you've been sleep-deprived and that people in your condition make reasoning errors about half the time. That's higher-order evidence—it doesn't bear directly on the math, but on your ability to assess it. The puzzle is whether you can rationally maintain your belief in the face of it. If you say, 'I still think the proof is valid, but I also think there's a good chance I'm wrong about that, ' you're in a state of epistemic akrasia—believing something while believing your evidence doesn't support believing it. Most epistemologists hold that this is straightforwardly irrational, for roughly the same reason that practical akrasia is irrational: it involves a kind of internal incoherence. And if you accept this, you also get a clean answer to the peer disagreement debate: when someone equally competent disagrees with you, you've acquired higher-order evidence that your reasoning might be flawed, and consistency demands that you suspend judgment or split the difference."

I'm not sure this summary gets the dialectic right. It seems to move very quickly from one claim to another, and I've heard that some philosophers actually defend the possibility of rational epistemic akrasia—that one can, in at least some circumstances, believe P while also believing that one's total evidence does not support P. Could you go through this paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a specialist working on these issues would accept as stated, which would need qualification, and whether the inference from the akrasia debate to the peer disagreement conclusion is as clean as the author suggests?
