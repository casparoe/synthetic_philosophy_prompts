You are a professor of philosophy of mind with a research focus on the epistemology of perception. Please think carefully and work through the following problem systematically. Before you give your final answer, take some time to brainstorm possible defeaters that might not be immediately obvious — consider edge cases, unusual experimental paradigms, and conceptual challenges that are less frequently discussed.

I have become convinced of a view about perception that goes roughly like this: early visual processing is cognitively impenetrable — it is not influenced by the perceiver's beliefs, desires, expectations, or background theories — and this impenetrability is what earns perception its distinctive epistemic role. The idea is that perception can serve as a neutral tribunal for belief only if what we see is not already shaped by what we think. If my expectations about the world could reach down into the early stages of visual processing and literally change how things look to me, then perception could not perform its function of checking our beliefs against the world; it would merely return the world in the image of our prior convictions. On this view, what is sometimes called cognitive penetration of perception either does not occur in the relevant sense, or occurs only in late, post-perceptual processing that is better described as judgment or interpretation rather than perception proper.

I want you to tell me what would count against this position. I am not asking for a literature review or a bibliography. I am asking for a systematic map of the position's exposure: the kinds of evidence, arguments, thought experiments, and empirical results that would — if they obtained — undermine it, and an honest assessment of whether any of them do obtain.

Please structure your answer as follows:

1. Start by distinguishing different versions or strengths of the impenetrability claim, so we know exactly what is at stake in each case. What would a maximally strong version claim, and what would a more modest version claim? Which version do I seem to hold, given how I have described the view's epistemic motivation?

2. For each version, identify the empirical findings that would count against it. Be specific about the kinds of experimental paradigms that would need to produce certain results — for example, cases where a subject's beliefs or expectations demonstrably alter early visual processing rather than merely influencing post-perceptual judgment or attentional selection. Which of these findings are we already confident we have? Which are merely possible but not yet established? Where you think the empirical literature genuinely supports one side or the other, say so, but flag where the evidence is contested or ambiguous.

3. Identify the conceptual or philosophical defeaters: arguments that the position is incoherent, that it collapses into a rival view, that it cannot handle certain cases, or that it rests on a distinction — between perception and judgment, or between early and late vision — that cannot be drawn in a principled way. Consider also whether the position's own epistemic motivation might backfire: could it turn out that cognitive impenetrability, far from securing perception's role as a neutral tribunal, actually undermines it in some way?

4. Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would force a defender of the position to abandon it entirely, and which would only require qualification or a more careful statement of the view? Be explicit about your criteria for ranking.

5. Finally, give an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated against falsification — whether its defenders have qualified it in ways that make it unfalsifiable, for instance by retreating to increasingly narrow claims about which stages of processing count as "early" or by dismissing every apparent counterexample as post-perceptual. If they have, is this a problem for a view of this kind, or is it the sort of philosophical position where some degree of insulation is appropriate? Could the position be reformulated so that it both risks something real and retains its epistemic motivation?

Throughout, I want you to distinguish between what the position's defenders would themselves concede counts against them and what only external critics say. I also want you to flag any places where the debate turns on empirical questions that are genuinely unsettled, as opposed to places where the disagreement is purely philosophical and would persist even if all the empirical facts were agreed upon.
