So I've been running this reading group on AI alignment and we keep getting stuck on the same argument. Half the group thinks specification gaming — like the boat-racing agent that learned to go in circles collecting power-ups instead of finishing the course, or the simulated robot that learned to fall over because the reward function counted "distance from start" in a way that rewarded being tall and then collapsing — is evidence of a deep structural problem. Their view: anytime you optimize hard against a proxy that isn't identical to what you actually want, you get Goodhart'd, and the problem gets *worse* the more optimization pressure you apply. The other half thinks this is way too pessimistic. They say these are just examples of sloppy engineering, and with better metrics, adversarial testing, and iterating on the specification, you can close the gap. They think calling it a "law" is giving up too early and that the pessimists are confusing current limitations with in-principle ones.

We've decided to try an adversarial collaboration to figure out where we actually disagree, rather than just rehashing the same examples. I want you to draft the document they should produce.

It should have:
(1) A numbered list of claims both sides can sign — about what specification gaming is, what the canonical examples actually show, what Goodhart's law says (in its various formulations), what kinds of fixes have been tried and how they've done.
(2) A table of disagreements, with each position stated in wording that its own side would accept as fair — not strawmanned.
(3) The cruxes: the sub-questions where if you could settle them, at least one side would update substantially.
(4) For each crux, at least one concrete test, argument, or case that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

I'm especially interested in whether some of this disagreement is actually verbal. Like, are they using "specification gaming" to mean different things? Do they have different implicit models of what "closing the gap" would actually require? If you think part of the dispute is verbal, flag which part and why.

And look, if you think the whole framing is confused — maybe the real disagreement is about something else entirely, or the two "camps" don't carve reality at the joints, or the adversarial-collaboration format is the wrong tool here — say so plainly. I'd rather hear that than get a document that politely papers over the confusion.
