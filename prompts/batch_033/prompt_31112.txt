I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and two weeks ago I attended a workshop where a molecular biologist and a philosopher of biology got into a long, candid argument over whether DNA is literally a code or whether "code" is just a metaphor that has outlived its usefulness. I took notes because I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the concept of genetic information, and the exchange crystallized the issue more sharply than anything I've read. I've been turning it over ever since and I cannot settle who has the better of it. I'd like you to adjudicate the disagreement move by move: who is right about which specific claim, where each argument is strongest and where it falters, and what, if anything, both parties are missing.

Here is roughly how it went, reconstructed from my notes.

The biologist opened by saying that calling DNA a code is not merely metaphorical. The standard genetic code — the mapping from sixty-four triplet codons to twenty amino acids and three stop signals — is a code in the strict information-theoretic sense: a rule for translating one set of symbols into another. If that is not a code, he said, nothing is.

The philosopher replied that this is too quick. All sorts of causal mappings satisfy that formal definition. The shape of a key maps to the pins in a lock, but we do not say the key encodes the lock's configuration. Her point was that when biologists say DNA "codes for" a protein or "contains the information for" an organism, they are invoking something richer than a bare mapping — something like semantic content or intentional specification. That richer notion is what needs defending, and gesturing at the formal codon table does not do the work.

The biologist pushed back: the richer notion is earned, not stolen. DNA does not merely correlate with proteins; the cell transcribes and translates it using dedicated machinery — polymerases, ribosomes, tRNAs with anticodons. There is a genuine separation of message and medium, which he called the hallmark of a code. The same DNA sequence, placed in a different cell, yields the same protein; the message survives a change of medium. That is not true of a key and a lock: the key does not carry a portable message you can read out in a foreign lock.

The philosopher granted that DNA has message-like features a key lacks, but argued that this still falls short. A genuine code requires a sender and a receiver who share the encoding convention. In the cell, there is no convention, no agent that agrees on the mapping — there is only chemistry. The ribosome does not interpret; it reacts. The mapping from codon to amino acid is sustained by the stereochemistry of tRNA charging enzymes and codon-anticodon base pairing. Calling it a code smuggles intentional language into a system with no intentions.

At this point the biologist said: if you insist that codes require conscious agents and conventions, then you have defined codes in a way that rules natural systems out by fiat. The history of science is full of concepts — force, computation, information — that began with agent-centered meanings and were then generalized. We now talk about the information a computer processes without implying it has a mind. Why not say the same about codes? The philosopher's definition is simply too narrow.

The philosopher answered that computation and information are precisely the analogies that should give us pause. When we say a computer processes information, we are still implicitly referencing the intentions of the programmer and the user; a computer alone, without a community of users, does not process information — it just runs currents through silicon. Similarly, DNA does not intrinsically carry information or constitute a code; we find it useful to describe it that way because we are agents with purposes. The danger is that the metaphor becomes invisible and we start attributing agency to molecules.

The biologist's final move was to say that this line of argument, taken seriously, would unravel molecular biology. If we cannot say that DNA codes for proteins, we also cannot say that genes carry instructions, that transcription copies information, or that mutations are errors. These locutions are not ornamental; they are load-bearing for biological explanation. If the philosopher wants to purge them, she owes us an alternative vocabulary that can do the same explanatory work. Does she have one?

The philosopher replied that she was not proposing to purge the vocabulary; she was proposing that we recognize it as a convenient and powerful metaphor and keep one eye on where the metaphor limps. The real danger is not the metaphor itself but forgetting that it is one.

The workshop ended there, and I have been unable to decide who had the better case. The biologist seemed right that the philosopher's definition of "code" was gerrymandered to exclude biology, and that the explanatory practices of molecular biology would collapse without code-talk. But the philosopher seemed right that there is a genuine risk of smuggling intentionality into molecules, and that calling the mapping a convenient metaphor we should not forget is a metaphor is a more honest position than insisting it is literal. I need to take a position in my dissertation and I would rather not get this wrong.

Please adjudicate: at each step of the exchange, who is right about what, where does each argument succeed and where does it fail, and are there considerations neither party raised that would strengthen or weaken their positions? I am asking for a committed response — I want to know which way you think the balance tilts overall and why, not just a survey of the options. And please write in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email — no headings, bullet points, or formatted lists, just paragraphs.
