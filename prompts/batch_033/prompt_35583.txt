I'm leading a graduate epistemology seminar, and in the next session I need to introduce the anti-luminosity argument associated with Timothy Williamson — the argument that no non-trivial condition is luminous, where a condition is luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. Don't walk me through the argument; give me a plan for teaching it. The students know the post-Gettier literature and can work with possible-worlds talk, but they have only met the KK principle in passing, and they tend to assimilate luminosity to infallibility or to the idea that the mental is transparent to itself. I have seventy-five minutes. I need the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why that order — what depends on what; the single example that should carry the argument, the one you would return to at every step, and what that example has to be able to do and what it must not quietly suggest; the misconceptions I should expect and at which point in the sequence each should be headed off; and one closing question or exercise that would show whether a student has grasped the structure of the argument rather than merely its conclusion. Throughout, mark clearly which features of the argument and its standard interpretation are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested — for example, its bearing on the KK principle, whether it rests on a sorites-style tolerance premise, and how much it shows about self-knowledge — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
