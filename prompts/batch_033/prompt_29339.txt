So I'm on the AI safety and ethics board at a midsize company building a long-term personal assistant agent — the kind that reads your email, drafts replies, and after a few months starts making small purchases and scheduling things on your behalf. We have to decide, before beta, whether the assistant should optimize for a user's actual, stated preferences or for something like their idealized preferences: what they'd want if they had all the relevant information, weren't impulsive or sleep-deprived, had time to reflect, and so on. Half the board wants a hard rule that the user's stated preference always wins, no second-guessing. The other half wants the assistant to aim at the idealized thing, because people ask for stuff they'd obviously regret, and the point of an assistant is to help you do what you'd actually want, not what you type at 2am.

I just got back from a long argument with a friend about coherent extrapolated volition, and I'm more torn than before. He thinks the whole notion is underdetermined — there's no fact of the matter about what I'd want if I "knew more and thought faster," because it depends on which idealizations you pick (more info about what? more reflective in what mood? the version of me who grew up differently is just a different person). He says idealized preferences are a blank check for paternalism dressed up as math. I pushed back that some idealization has to be doing work, otherwise we're just building a sycophant that helps someone drain their savings because they currently want to. We didn't resolve it.

So I need to write the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete policy for the beta launch in three weeks: which preference model the assistant uses, when (if ever) it may deviate from stated preferences, what the override conditions are, and what the user-facing explanation has to say. Don't gesture at "balance" — give us a rule we can actually hand to the engineering team. And take my friend's worry seriously, not as a strawman: if the idealized-preference view is underdetermined, what exactly are we telling engineers to compute?

After the memo, give me a JSON summary with these fields:
"recommendation": one sentence, the bottom line.
"decision_rule": the operational rule in one or two sentences, the way an engineer would implement it.
"losing_side": the strongest consideration against your recommendation and why it doesnt carry the day.
"residual_risks": an array of the top 2–4 failure modes your rule doesn't fully solve.
"test_cases": an array of two or three concrete user scenarios and what your rule does in each.
