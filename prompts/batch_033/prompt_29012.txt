I've been tracking this one appellate judge's opinions for a while now — a friend who clerked for her said her judicial philosophy is frustratingly hard to pin down, and I've been treating it as a clustering problem. She seems to toggle between hardcore textualism and purpose-driven interpretation in ways that don't fit any of the standard boxes. I pulled five cases where the structure was the same: a statute says X, the facts arguably fall under X but arguably don't, and something morally or practically significant is riding on it.

(1) *State v. Morrison.* An old statute made it a felony to "carnally know a child under the age of consent." A 19-year-old had consensual sex with someone two months shy of 18 who'd presented a fake ID. Ruling: the statute targeted predatory adults exploiting actual children; applying it here would be absurd. Acquitted.

(2) *City of Northport v. Brewster.* Ordinance required "any establishment serving food" to install a commercial grease trap. A tiny coffee shop that sells only black coffee and pre-wrapped pastries showed they produce essentially no grease. Ruling: "any establishment serving food" means exactly that; if the city council wanted exceptions, they'd have written them. Violation upheld.

(3) *U.S. v. Keller.* Federal statute criminalized "using or carrying a firearm" during a drug trafficking offense. Defendant had a handgun locked in a safe in the trunk during a drug deal in a parking lot. Ruling: "carry" requires immediate accessibility; a gun locked away in a trunk is stored, not carried. Conviction overturned.

(4) *Wildlands Conservancy v. Chen.* Regulation prohibited "motorized vehicles" in a protected wilderness area. Defendant flew a camera drone with near-silent electric rotors at 400 feet, arguing a drone isn't a "vehicle" under any ordinary definition. Ruling: the regulation's purpose — protecting quiet and wildlife — plainly covers drones; form doesn't trump function. Violation upheld.

(5) *Whitman v. Commissioner.* Tax code allows deduction for "educational expenses that maintain or improve skills required in the taxpayer's current employment." A software engineer deducted tuition for a philosophy master's, arguing it cultivates the analytical rigor her programming job requires. Ruling: the provision means concrete technical skills specific to the occupation, not general intellectual cultivation. Deduction denied.

Now the case headed her way: A 1973 statute criminalizes broadcasting "obscene, indecent, or profane material over the public airwaves." A website with no age gate streams hardcore porn, and a federal prosecutor wants to apply the statute, arguing "broadcast" has evolved to cover internet streaming to the general public. The defense says Congress in 1973 was plainly thinking of radio and TV, and stretching the term this way violates due process.

I can see at least two rules that fit her five prior verdicts perfectly. One: apply the ordinary meaning at enactment unless it produces an absurdity the legislature couldn't have intended — which predicts overturning the conviction, since "broadcast" in 1973 didn't mean internet streaming and Congress can update the statute if they care to. Two: identify the mischief the statute was meant to remedy and cover all cases that implicate that mischief — which predicts upholding, since making obscene content freely available to the public is exactly the harm Congress was targeting, and the delivery mechanism is incidental. But I keep wondering if there's a third, deeper rule — something about the relative determinacy of text versus purpose, or a criminal/regulatory asymmetry, or maybe she's doing a Dworkinian constructive interpretation of the whole legal order and the pattern I'm seeing is just an artifact of that. If you had to bet which way she goes, and you got to design one additional hypothetical to test against the existing five before committing, what case would you pick? I'm especially interested in whether her implicit theory is closer to Hart's (law as social fact, judges exercising discretion in the penumbra) or Fuller's (law as a purposive enterprise you can't interpret without reference to what it's for).
