I am on the committee that selects the recipient of our university's highest honorary degree. The front-runner is a retired head of state who, in the closing months of a war, ordered the bombing of two cities with the deliberate aim of destroying their civilian populations, on the calculation that this would shock the enemy into surrender and avoid an invasion that would have cost far more lives. The war did end, and the estimated death toll from a full invasion was plausibly much larger than the number of civilians killed. Many of my colleagues say this is exactly why we should confer the degree: he faced a terrible choice, made a hard consequentialist calculation, and on balance saved more lives than he took. I find I cannot vote for the degree, yet I cannot say why in terms my colleagues will accept. The cost-benefit reasoning feels to me beside the point, almost indecent, but when I try to articulate that I come across as merely squeamish. Is there a principled reason to vote against conferring the honor, or am I really just refusing to weigh the lives properly? Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email or a letter.
