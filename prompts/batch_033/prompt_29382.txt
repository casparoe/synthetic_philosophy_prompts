At my high school's philosophy club, which meets after school on Wednesdays, I sat in on a debate between two members that has now run for several meetings, and I have been unable to decide how to break it down. Maya opened with a version of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. She reasoned that what a person does is the product of how that person is — their beliefs, desires, temperament, and history — together with the situation they are in. For a person to be truly responsible for what they do, she argued, they would have to be truly responsible for how they are. But no one can be truly responsible for how they are, because any deliberate effort to shape one's own character would have to be made by some already existing self, and the same question would then arise about that earlier self. This pushes the demand for self-creation back forever, so the demand can never be met. She concluded that nobody is ever ultimately morally responsible for anything, and that blame, resentment, and punishment, as ordinarily understood, rest on an illusion.

Daniel objected that the argument only works by setting a standard nobody ever meant. When people call someone responsible, he said, they do not mean the person caused themselves to exist from nothing; they mean the action issued from the person's own deliberations and choices, in the absence of coercion or manipulation. That kind of responsibility, he said, is real, and it is all that ordinary blame and praise need. Maya replied that the ordinary practice is not as modest as Daniel makes it sound: genuine resentment treats the person as the ultimate origin of the action, not as the outcome of luck, so Daniel is quietly substituting a weaker notion of responsibility for the one actually in play. Daniel answered that Maya's notion of ultimate responsibility is the philosophical invention, and that a teacher or a judge is not implicitly claiming the student or defendant created their own character from nothing. Maya countered that if the judge's blame is fair only in some reduced, practical sense, then Daniel has conceded her deeper point and is now just arguing about which word to use.

I would like a move-by-move adjudication of this exchange. For each stage, say what is right and what is overstated or question-begging in what each person claims, and identify anything important that both of them are missing. Then say who is more right overall, and why.
