A colleague in my department teaches a graduate seminar on the analysis of knowledge. Over the semester, she has given her settled verdicts on the following seven cases:

1. **Gettier's original case:** Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get the job and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith infers that the person who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. As it happens, Smith himself gets the job, and Smith also has ten coins in his pocket, though Smith has no idea of either fact. Verdict: Smith does **not** know that the person who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket.

2. **Fake barn:** Henry drives through an area where, unbeknownst to him, the locals have erected many barn facades that look exactly like real barns from the road. Henry looks at the one real barn in the area and forms the belief "that is a barn." Verdict: Henry does **not** know that it is a barn.

3. **Lottery:** Jones holds one ticket in a fair lottery with one million tickets and believes, solely on the basis of the odds, that his ticket has lost. His ticket has in fact lost. Verdict: Jones does **not** know that his ticket has lost.

4. **Ordinary perception:** Maria looks out her window in good light and sees a maple tree in her yard. She believes there is a maple tree in her yard. Conditions are normal, her vision is functioning properly, and there is indeed a maple tree. Verdict: Maria **knows** there is a maple tree in her yard.

5. **Reliable testimony:** Amir asks a stranger for directions to the train station. The stranger is a lifelong resident of the city and gives accurate, clear directions. Amir believes the station is two blocks east. Verdict: Amir **knows** the station is two blocks east.

6. **Stopped clock:** Elena glances at a wall clock that reads 3:15. Unbeknownst to her, the clock stopped exactly twenty-four hours earlier. By coincidence, the actual time is 3:15. Verdict: Elena does **not** know that it is 3:15.

7. **Safe belief with an incidental falsehood:** David is hiking and sees what he takes to be deer tracks. He believes there are deer in the area. His belief is true: there are deer in the area, and in all nearby possible worlds there are deer and David would believe there are deer on the basis of similar visible signs. However, the particular tracks David noticed were made by a large dog, not a deer; his intermediate belief that a deer made those specific tracks is false. Verdict: David **knows** there are deer in the area.

Next week she plans to present an eighth case and has asked the seminar to predict her verdict before she reveals it. The new case is this:

**New case (misleading evidence about the causal path):** A fire investigator examines the scene of a warehouse fire. She finds traces of a flammable solvent near the ignition point and concludes that the fire was started deliberately using that solvent. Her conclusion is true: the fire was arson, and that very solvent was used to start it. Moreover, in all nearby possible worlds where she finds such traces at a fire scene, the fire was arson involving that solvent — the correlation is robust, and her methods are reliable. However, the traces she found were not left by the arsonist; they were left earlier that day by a cleaning crew using the same solvent for legitimate purposes. The arsonist happened to use an identical solvent from a different container. The investigator's evidence is genuinely connected to the truth of her conclusion, but the causal story she tells herself about how the evidence got there is mistaken.

Please do the following: identify the most plausible candidate rules or theories of knowledge that fit all seven of her verdicts; explain which of those rules diverge on the new case and what verdict each would predict; give your best prediction of her verdict with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others; and finally, name a further case — not among the eight described above — whose verdict would most decisively settle which rule she is actually running, and explain why.
