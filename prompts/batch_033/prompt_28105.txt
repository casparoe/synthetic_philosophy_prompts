Before I commit to any particular theory of cultural evolution—whether meme-based, dual-inheritance, cultural attractor, or something else—I want to know what the theory is supposed to do. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate Darwinian theory of culture must be able to deliver—the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the empirical results it must be consistent with. For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates treating it as a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one research program.

I have several specific concerns I would like you to address in the course of the list:

1. The replicator problem. Some argue that a Darwinian theory of culture needs a cultural analogue of the gene—a faithfully replicating unit of transmission. Others argue that cultural transmission is too high-fidelity to be mere contagion but too low-fidelity to support replicator-based selection, and that the demand for a replicator is itself a mistake imported from gene-centric biology. Is the existence of a cultural replicator a genuine desideratum, or is it a prejudice? If it is a desideratum, what constraints must such a unit satisfy?

2. The Lamarckian challenge. Cultural evolution appears to involve the inheritance of acquired characteristics: intentional innovation, directed variation, and horizontal transmission of traits within a single generation. A theory must either show that these features are compatible with a Darwinian framework or explain why Darwinian thinking remains useful despite them. What exactly is the requirement here, and is it non-negotiable?

3. The adaptationism question. In biology, not every trait is an adaptation, and there are well-developed methods for distinguishing adaptation from drift, constraint, and byproduct. What is the parallel requirement for cultural evolution? Must a theory provide criteria for determining when a cultural trait counts as an adaptation rather than, say, a frequency-dependent equilibrium or an epiphenomenon of individual learning biases?

4. Cumulative culture. One of the central explanatory targets is cumulative cultural evolution—the ratchet effect by which cultural traditions accrue complexity and functionality over generations without any single individual designing the outcome. What exactly must a theory explain about this phenomenon, and what would count as having explained it adequately?

5. Micro-macro mapping. A theory must connect individual-level psychological mechanisms (conformist bias, prestige bias, content biases) to population-level patterns (traditions, fads, institutions). What form must this connection take? Is it enough to show that certain biases could produce certain patterns under some parameter values, or must the theory make quantitative predictions?

6. The relationship to biological evolution. Must a theory of cultural evolution be consistent with the fact that the capacities for culture themselves evolved biologically? If so, what does consistency demand—merely not contradicting known facts about human cognitive evolution, or something stronger like integrated gene-culture coevolutionary dynamics?

I would also like to know: which pair of desiderata are hardest to satisfy jointly? And which single requirement, if you had to pick one, does the most to exclude the largest number of candidate theories?

A brief scorecard for the main frameworks (memetics, dual-inheritance theory, cultural attractor theory, cultural epidemiology) is welcome but secondary; the criteria themselves and their motivation are what I need.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the overall list and single out the point you are least sure about.
