I'm presenting a paper at a philosophy of language workshop next month. My thesis is that Davidson's principle of charity in radical interpretation is best understood not as a methodological heuristic or a pragmatic maxim for field linguists, but as a transcendental condition on the very possibility of identifying someone as a speaker with propositional attitudes. On my reading, the principle of charity is not something we choose to apply; it is constitutive of the interpretive stance. I argue that this reading makes better sense of Davidson's claim that radical interpretation begins at home — that the same constraints apply to understanding our neighbors as to understanding exotic strangers — and that it also explains why Davidson treats the connection between belief and meaning as non-negotiable rather than merely useful.

The audience will be primarily philosophers of language and mind, with the following composition: about half work in the Lewis-Stalnaker tradition of formal semantics and pragmatics, where communication is modeled in terms of common ground updating and possible-worlds content; several are committed Griceans who think that communicative intentions and recursive mindreading do all the work that charity is supposed to do; and two or three are sympathetic to Davidson but worry that the transcendental reading makes the principle unfalsifiable and therefore vacuous. There will also be a few graduate students who know Davidson mainly through secondary literature and are looking for reasons to invest in the primary texts.

Please prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I am most likely to face from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each objection, phrase it the way a member of this audience would actually put it, give me a two-minute reply, note where the reply is weakest, and flag which objections I should concede ground on rather than try to defeat outright. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
