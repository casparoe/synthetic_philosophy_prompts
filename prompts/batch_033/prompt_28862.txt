I've been reading about constitutional design on my own — not for a course, just out of curiosity — and I keep coming back to one case that I can't quite get straight in my head. After the Second World War, when West Germany adopted its constitution (the Basic Law), the framers included something usually called the eternity clause: Article 79(3) says that certain parts of the constitution may never be amended, no matter how large the majority in favor. The core commitments — human dignity, the democratic character of the state, the federal structure — are meant to be permanently locked. The framers had just watched the Weimar Republic get dismantled piece by piece through formally legal procedures, and they were determined not to let that happen again. Around the same time, I came across the story of Ulysses and the sirens, which some writers use as a picture of constitutionalism: Ulysses knows his future self will not be able to resist the sirens' song, so he has his crew tie him to the mast and stop their own ears with wax. On this view, a constitution is the ropes — a rational people binding its own future hands against moments of predictable weakness or madness.

I want to use the eternity clause to test the Ulysses model of constitutional precommitment, and the other way around. At first the fit looks neat: a democracy, foreseeing that it might one day vote itself out of existence, removes that option in advance. But as I have thought about it, the differences have come to seem more interesting than the similarities. Ulysses chooses his own binding, knowing the specific temptation and the moment it will pass; the German framers bound people who were not yet alive, against dangers they could not fully specify, with no provision for the ropes ever coming off. Ulysses is one person persisting through time; a polity is a succession of different people. And Ulysses gives the order while still rational — but the Basic Law was drafted under military occupation by a defeated and traumatized population, which raises the question of whether the "rational moment" the model requires was actually available.

What I am hoping for is a careful, two-directional analysis. First, map the episode onto the model: what plays the role of Ulysses, the sirens, the mast, the crew, the ropes? What does the precommitment framing illuminate about the eternity clause that a simpler historical explanation — say, that the framers were traumatized and overreacted — misses? And second, run it the other way: what does the eternity clause, as a real institution that has now functioned for over seventy years and survived reunification, reveal about the precommitment model's assumptions? Does it show that the model borrows ideas about personal identity and consent that break down when moved from an individual to a political community? I am less interested in whether the clause was wise policy than in what the strain between the model and the case teaches about the limits of precommitment thinking.
