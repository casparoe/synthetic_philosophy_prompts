I've been reading about advance directives and it's got me tangled up. Here's the practice: someone writes an advance directive saying "if I get severe dementia and can't recognize my family, don't treat my infections, let me go." Then they develop dementia. They seem happy — they enjoy pudding, they smile at visitors, they don't seem to be suffering. They get pneumonia. The advance directive says no antibiotics. But the person in the bed clearly wants to live. The legal and medical system almost everywhere honors the directive — it treats the past competent self as the real person and the current self as, I don't know, a diminished or inauthentic version whose preferences don't count.

What theory of personal identity or personhood does this practice presuppose? If personhood is relational, does the practice of privileging the earlier self's directive over the current self's apparent wishes still make sense? Or does it look like a very specific, Western, individualist metaphysics got baked into medical law without anyone noticing?
