I cover the science and ideas beat, and I've been working on a piece about how the philosophy of history has handled the problem of narrative explanation. On one side you have Carl Hempel's classic covering-law model: to explain a historical event is to show that it follows from general laws and initial conditions, the way an eclipse does. On the other, you have the narrativist tradition — Arthur Danto, Hayden White, Paul Ricoeur — arguing that historical understanding is irreducibly narrative, and that selecting, sequencing, and emplotting events into a story is not decorative afterthought but the very form historical knowledge takes.

It struck me that Kurosawa's Rashomon is, among other things, a dramatization of exactly this dispute. A samurai lies dead in a grove, his wife has been assaulted, and we are given four incompatible narratives of what happened — from the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai (speaking through a medium), and the woodcutter. Each account selects events, assigns motives, and constructs a causal sequence that renders the outcome intelligible. They all make sense. They cannot all be true. And the film notoriously refuses to hand us a definitive version — or rather, the version we are tempted to treat as definitive (the woodcutter's) turns out to be compromised in its own way.

On a first pass, Rashomon looks like an argument for narrativism: the film shows that narrative is inescapable, that we cannot make sense of events except by placing them in stories, and that there is no story-independent baseline of brute fact to which we can retreat. That sounds like White's claim that historical events are value-neutral and that the same events can be emplotted as tragedy, comedy, romance, or satire without violating the evidence.

On a second pass, the film looks like a devastating objection to narrativism: if four incompatible narratives can all render the same events intelligible, then narrative intelligibility is too cheap. It does not constrain. It does not track truth. It is a psychological need we have, not a route to knowledge. That is precisely the covering-law theorist's worry — that narrative substitutes aesthetic coherence for evidential warrant, and that explanation requires something sterner.

Neither reading feels quite adequate to the film, and I suspect the reason is that Rashomon is not only about the multiplicity of narratives but about why they diverge. Each character's story is self-exculpatory. The bandit casts himself as a swashbuckling rogue who fought honorably; the wife casts herself as a victim of fate; the samurai casts himself as a man betrayed who died nobly by his own hand. The woodcutter, who initially seems to offer the objective perspective, is revealed to have stolen the pearl-handled dagger from the scene — his narrative, too, serves an interest. So the film does not merely show that narratives differ; it shows that narratives are motivated, and that the motivation frequently is self-justification. That is a claim about narrative explanation that the classic philosophical literature, focused as it is on the logic of explanation rather than its psychology, does not always develop: the entanglement of making sense with making oneself look good.

I would like a reading of Rashomon through the lens of the covering-law versus narrative explanation debate that does justice to both the film and the philosophy. What does the film get right that the philosophical debate misses? Where does the philosophical literature supply analytic resources — about the difference between causal explanation and narrative understanding, about criteria for distinguishing better from worse narratives — that let us say something precise about what goes wrong in the grove? Does the film ultimately take a position on whether narrative can be genuinely explanatory, or does it refuse the question, and is that refusal itself a philosophically instructive move? Which scenes or structural features of the film would you point to as bearing the most weight? And if the honest critic must conclude that the film and the philosophy are ultimately doing different things — that Rashomon is about self-deception and moral failure rather than about the epistemology of historical explanation as such — I would like to hear that argued as well.
