A friend and I recently spent an entire evening arguing about what a translator of poetry owes to the original, and my friend kept invoking Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot to make his case. The book is built around a short sixteenth-century poem by Clément Marot and presents many English versions of it, several of which try to reproduce the poem's rhyme scheme, meter, and sound-play even when doing so forces the translator away from the literal sense. Hofstadter treats those formal constraints as central to what the poem means rather than as decorative extras. I found myself wanting to know how Vladimir Nabokov would have responded. Nabokov took a starkly opposed position: he argued that rhymed and metrical translations inevitably falsify the sense, and his own translation of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin is deliberately unrhymed, aggressively literal, and accompanied by a large scholarly commentary.

Nabokov died well before Le Ton beau de Marot was written, so this is necessarily a reconstruction. Had he read the book, which of his objections would have been most damaging to Hofstadter's project, and is there any element of Hofstadter's case for preserving form that Nabokov would have had to concede, however grudgingly?
