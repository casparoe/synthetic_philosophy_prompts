I keep ending up in the same argument on the forum: someone wants to say the self is an illusion, or at best a useful fiction, and they reach for Dennett's image of the self as a center of narrative gravity. The move goes like this. A chair's center of gravity isn't a real thing inside the chair — you won't find it by cutting the chair open, and nothing made of matter has to be sitting at that point. It's an abstraction defined over the whole mass distribution, and it earns its keep by letting you predict how the chair will behave. Likewise, the self isn't a real thing inside the skull; it's an abstraction defined over the story a person tells (and is told) about their life, and it earns its keep by letting us predict and coordinate with a person. From this the person concluded that there is no substantial subject persisting through time, that the self we blame, praise, and make promises to is an abstraction, and that worrying about whether the self survives uploading or teletransportation is like worrying about where the chair's center of gravity goes when you disassemble the chair — a category mistake.

Im actually sympathetic to alot of the deflationary conclusion, but the speed of the inference bugs me, so I want a gears-level audit rather than vibes. Give me the correspondence table first: narrative, remembered events, the narrator, the body and brain, other people's stories about you, the center that gets posited. Then go hard on where the mapping breaks. A physical body has a unique, observer-independent center of gravity given its mass distribution; is the narrative center of a life unique or observer-independent, and does it matter for the conclusion? A center of gravity has no first-person perspective, no causal powers, and no interests; is that a disanalogy that undermines the claims about blame and survival, or is it just the point of the analogy? What's the analogue of mass in the target — actual events, interpreted memories, speech acts, attention-weighted retellings? And once the disanalogies are on the table, how much of the deflationary conclusion actually survives, and does it survive because the analogy supports it or because there were independent arguments running underneath the whole time? If there's a better analogy for what Dennett is trying to do, say what it is.
