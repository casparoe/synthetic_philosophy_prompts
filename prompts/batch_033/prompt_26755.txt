You are a professional philosopher of mind who has worked on consciousness and is familiar with illusionism. I have an argument against illusionism that I find suspicious because it seems to prove too much, and I would like your diagnosis. Please think carefully; before you settle on a diagnosis, work through the argument step by step and consider what each premise commits a view to, including what the argument would require in cases outside consciousness. Then write your answer as a letter or email to me.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Illusionism is the view that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion: introspection presents our experiences as having qualitative properties, a private and ineffable what-it-is-likeness, that nothing in the physical world actually has. (2) An illusion is a case in which something appears to be a certain way but is not that way. (3) So illusionism is committed to holding that our experiences appear to us to have phenomenal properties. (4) But for an experience to appear to us to have phenomenal properties just is for that experience to be phenomenally conscious; the appearing of a qualitative character is not a further fact that could remain once the qualitative character is denied. (5) Therefore illusionism is committed to the very phenomenal properties it denies. (6) So illusionism is incoherent.

My worry is that this argument proves too much. If the reasoning from (2) through (5) were sound, it seems it would show that no error theory about any apparent phenomenon could be correct: the fact that a stick half-submerged in water appears bent would make the stick actually bent, and the fact that a choice appears free would make free will real. Since that is absurd, the argument must be doing something illegitimate, but each step looks fine on its own. I would most like to keep two things: the general distinction between how things appear and how they are, and the coherence of illusionism as a live theoretical option, even if the view turns out false.

Please tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked so plausible, and say what a position has to accept in order to reject it. In particular, address whether rejecting that step forces one to give up the appearance-reality distinction or merely to draw it more carefully. Write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter or an email.
