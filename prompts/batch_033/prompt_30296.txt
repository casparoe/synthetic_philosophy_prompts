In my recent reading in Bayesian epistemology, I have repeatedly encountered the Dutch book argument in a form that rests on a particular analogy. An agent's degree of belief in a proposition is treated as interchangeable with the price at which she would buy or sell a ticket paying one dollar if the proposition is true and nothing otherwise. The argument then shows that an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability axioms will accept a set of bets, each fair by her own assessment, whose combined payoff is a certain loss, and it concludes that rational degrees of belief must therefore obey the probability calculus.

I am less interested in the mechanics of the proof than in the analogy that carries it. What exactly is being mapped onto what when a degree of belief is identified with a betting price? A table of correspondences would be helpful. Where does the mapping break down, and which features of the betting context—money, a bookmaker, the assumption that one must accept any bet one deems fair—are being quietly imported into the theory of belief? Which of those disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion, and which are incidental? In particular, I want to know whether the step from the vulnerability to a sure monetary loss to the irrationality of the beliefs themselves can be made without the analogy supplying the missing premise. Finally, does the conclusion, probabilism, survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or has the analogy been doing the normative work all along? If a better analogy would do the same work more honestly, I would like to know what it is.
