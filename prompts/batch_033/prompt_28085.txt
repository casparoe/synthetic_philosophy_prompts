I am a novelist drafting a character, a scholar of early Chinese thought, who has become unmoored by the butterfly passage in the Zhuangzi, in which the dreamer cannot tell whether he is a man who dreamt of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming of being a man. I have formalized the character's doubt into the argument below, and I now suspect the argument proves too much: it seems to dissolve the distinction between dreaming and waking and then to saw off the branch it rests on. I cannot find the exit.

(1) Any experience I have while awake could in principle be reproduced in a dream so exactly that, from the inside, I could not tell the two apart.
(2) If two hypotheses would present me with exactly the same evidence, that evidence cannot justify me in believing one over the other.
(3) So no individual experience can justify me in believing I am awake rather than dreaming.
(4) Whatever could justify me in believing I am awake would have to be something available to me from the inside, such as an experience, a memory, or a felt sense of coherence, and by (1) and (3) no such item can favor waking over dreaming.
(5) Therefore I am never justified in believing I am awake.

(6) The same reasoning runs from inside the dream: the butterfly's experience of being a butterfly would be just as convincing to it as my waking experience is to me, and nothing inside the dream could justify the butterfly in preferring the human interpretation.
(7) So waking and dreaming are symmetric; neither state has any epistemic privilege over the other.
(8) But if neither state has epistemic privilege, the distinction between them is idle, a difference that makes no justificatory difference, and the categories of waking and dreaming collapse into one another.
(9) Yet that collapse is itself concluded by what I take to be waking reasoning, which by (5) I was never justified in trusting. So the argument undermines its own conclusion; it proves too much.

Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), which look like harmless platitudes, but I cannot see how to keep them and stop the slide to (8).
