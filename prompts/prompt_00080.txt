I've been sitting in on a philosophy of religion seminar this semester, and we just spent two sessions on Molinism, Luis de Molina's account of middle knowledge and how it's supposed to reconcile robust libertarian free will with meticulous divine providence. I understand the basic architecture: logically "prior" to God's creative decree, God knows all the true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (what any possible free creature would do in any possible circumstance), and he uses this knowledge to actualize a world in which free creatures do what he wants without their freedom being violated. What I'm stuck on is what actually follows from accepting this picture, especially given the grounding objection (that there's nothing that could make these counterfactuals true prior to the relevant choices actually being made) and Robert Adams's worries about their truth conditions.

Could you lay out the most important implications of Molinism if we grant it for the sake of argument? I'm especially interested in: (1) what it really buys the Molinist on the problem of evil and theodicy that a simple free-will defense doesn't already provide, (2) whether it collapses into a kind of theological compatibilism in practice even though it's marketed as preserving libertarian freedom, (3) what it implies about the coherence of counterfactuals of freedom themselves as truth-apt propositions with truthmakers, and (4) whether there are any interesting spillover implications for secular debates outside philosophy of religion, e.g., in decision theory or discussions of counterfactual reasoning about agents, where something structurally similar to "middle knowledge" of what an agent would freely do under various conditions gets invoked. I'd like the answer to go beyond the standard textbook summary and actually engage with why these implications are contested.
