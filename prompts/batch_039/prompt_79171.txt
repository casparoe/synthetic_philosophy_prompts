I'm in the second half of a graduate seminar in metaethics, and we have spent the past three weeks on evolutionary debunking arguments — the family of arguments that run roughly as follows: our moral judgments are produced by psychological dispositions that were selected because they made our ancestors better at cooperating, coordinating, and competing within groups; nothing in that selection story has any essential connection to the moral facts; therefore our moral judgments are unjustified, or at least we are not entitled to treat them as tracking anything real. I can rehearse the standard moves in my sleep, which is precisely what worries me. I have written a short position piece for Thursday's meeting that tries to take the debate somewhere less trodden, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuinely new move or a familiar one in new clothes. I would like you to comment on it. Specifically, I want four things: first, whether the evolutionary and psychological claims it leans on are stated in a way a careful biologist or psychologist would accept, or whether I have overclaimed what selection can be said to have done; second, whether the central distinction I draw is doing real work or merely relabeling a familiar response to debunking arguments; third, whether the conclusion actually follows from the premises as I have stated them; and fourth, whether there is an obvious objection I have failed to anticipate that would sink the whole thing. Please criticize the argument as written rather than summarizing the literature back to me — I know the literature; I want to know where this particular argument breaks.

Here is the piece, more or less as I intend to circulate it. It opens with a distinction between shallow and deep debunking. A shallow debunking explains why some particular person or culture happens to hold some particular moral belief — why a Roman found slavery unremarkable, or why I feel a pang about a promise I broke to a friend. Shallow debunkings are everywhere and mostly harmless, because they explain the occasion of a belief rather than its content. A deep debunking claims something far stronger: that the entire faculty of moral judgment was selected for its effects on fitness, and that the selection process was indifferent to whether the judgments it produced were true. I argue that the deep version requires a premise its proponents often slide past — call it the indifference premise — namely that the moral beliefs we in fact hold would have been the same even if the moral facts had been different. I then propose what I call the counterfactual range test. Instead of asking whether our moral faculty was selected for truth-tracking, which neither side can settle, we should ask how the moral code that would have been selected varies across the space of plausible ancestral environments. If, across a very wide range of selection pressures, the code that would have been favoured still includes some form of reciprocity, some prohibition on gratuitous cruelty toward those one lives among, and some special obligation to one's dependents, then those elements are not artifacts of one contingent history, and the indifference premise is not established for them. I anticipate the reply that a fitness-tracking process would also predict convergence across environments, so that the test proves nothing; my answer is that this reply only lands if one already assumes that the fitness-relevant features of ancestral life and the truth-relevant features of morality come apart, which is the very assumption at issue. My conclusion is that debunking arguments succeed, if at all, against the thick and parochial parts of morality — purity codes, sexual mores, the moral standing of outsiders — while a thin core survives, and that the interesting philosophical question is how much a realist can do with a core that thin.

Two things I will flag myself so that you do not have to. I do not know whether the counterfactual range test is a genuine test or just a vivid restatement of the claim that certain moral beliefs are evolutionarily inevitable. And I do not know whether the thin core I am left with is enough to sustain the position I want to end up in, or whether it quietly collapses into a reductive naturalism that surrenders everything the realist was after. Our convenor keeps insisting that we state our conclusion in a single sentence, so if you think I cannot, I would rather find that out now than on Thursday.
