Write a customer-facing FAQ page for a British liability insurer that has adopted imprecise probabilities as its official methodology and, as a consequence, has never issued a policy. The page should run to roughly 750 words, in the deadpan register of a corporate help centre: a short introduction, a heading, and eight to ten questions, the questions in the client's voice and the answers in the firm's, with the occasional cross-reference to a methodology note. It should be intelligible to an ordinary policyholder and genuinely funny to someone who has worked on imprecise credences, and it should never wink: the firm does not concede that its position is absurd, does not diagnose itself, and does not close with a moral.

Treat the following as given, and deploy it accurately rather than gesturing at it.

- The firm's belief about any event is a set of probability functions, which it calls its committee, each member of which is a complete and internally coherent assignment of degrees of belief. The figure the firm publishes for an event is the range spanned by the committee.
- A member's degree of belief in a proposition is the price at which it would buy or sell a £1 stake on that proposition, so the firm's published range is the band of odds at which it will neither buy nor sell. Inside the band the firm takes no position, and the page must present this as a considered policy rather than an evasion.
- A range cannot act, so the committee needs a rule for turning ranges into decisions. The firm's rule is unanimity: a policy is written only if every member finds the premium acceptable. Let it emerge that the credences themselves leave the choice of rule open, and that the rule, not the credences, settles what the firm does.
- Clients prefer the competitor, who publishes single numbers. The page attributes this to an aversion to ambiguity, and observes that preferring the urn of known composition, whether one is betting on red or on black, cannot be represented by any single assignment of probabilities.
- The firm must field the objection — dilation — that its range for a hypothesis can widen when it learns something it takes to be irrelevant to that hypothesis, and must treat this as a feature of its humility rather than an embarrassment.
- The firm must also field the objection that its range is merely a compressed way of reporting a single, sharp credence about the range itself, and must resist the reduction without quite explaining what is lost by it.
- The page should distinguish the firm's imprecision, where every member is perfectly precise and the firm has merely not decided which member speaks for it, from vagueness, where there is no fact of the matter what the firm believes. The distinction should be doing more work than the reader can comfortably accept.
- Where the firm does commit itself, it should do so with relish: it refuses a uniform assignment, for instance, because uniformity presupposes a partition of the possibilities, and different partitions give different answers.

The target is the practice of presenting interval-valued uncertainty as an epistemic achievement — a way of never being wrong — and of letting the choice of decision rule do the real work while the credences take the credit. Every absurdity on the page should be an exaggeration of a real feature of that practice rather than an invention, so that a specialist finds the satire fair; a reader should be able to tell what is being satirised without being told.

Constraints: name no real philosopher, paper, theorem, firm or product, and offer no references or further reading; use quotation marks only for phrases the firm itself is coining; keep the firm internally consistent throughout, and in particular never let it quietly become an ordinary Bayesian with a wide prior over which committee member is right. At least one exchange should make plain what the policy costs a client who simply wants to know whether her house will flood. British spelling throughout.
