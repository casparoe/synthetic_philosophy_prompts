I sit on the small committee that decides how to spend a modest research fund—enough to support three or four philosophers for about two years, with no clinicians and no laboratory attached—and this cycle we have narrowed the topic to a single syndrome and what it might teach us about the badness of pain. The phenomenon is pain asymbolia: patients with certain brain lesions, classically in the parietal region, who appear to detect and localize a noxious stimulus, and who often still describe it as painful, yet who show none of the usual aversive reaction—they may fail to withdraw, may even smile, and report that although it hurts they are not bothered by it. The standard reading pulls the syndrome apart into two dimensions that ordinary pain bundles together, a sensory-discriminative one and an affective-motivational one, and locates the badness of pain in the second. A colleague on the committee, though, has been pressing a distinction I now want the whole agenda to reckon with: when we say a pain is bad, we might mean that a certain phenomenal quality is intrinsically disvaluable; or that the state is bad because it is conatively unwanted, badness being a matter of the subject's opposition to it; or that it is bad only derivatively, through what it makes the subject do or endure. To force these apart she offers a thought experiment—a subject who insists the pain is as bad as it ever was, perhaps worse, while being entirely unmoved by it, who feels no distress at it and will not lift a finger to end it—and asks whether that is pain that is bad but no longer aversive, or a subject misdescribing a sensation that has lost the very thing that made it bad.

What I want from you is not a survey but a defended agenda. Of the open questions this material generates—whether asymbolia shows pain's badness to be separable from the sensation or instead shows that pain names a composite whose components come apart without remainder; whether the lesson is metaphysical, about what the badness consists in, or merely functional, about which processes mediate aversion; how much any of it should change how we treat pain in patients who cannot report it, such as infants, non-human animals, or people with advanced dementia; whether the syndrome bears on the moral status of experiences we cannot access from the inside; and anything else you think the fund should be looking at—I want a shortlist of the three or four most deserving of two years of a philosopher's attention, ranked, with the ranking defended. Make the criteria explicit and say how they trade off: which questions are upstream of which, which are answerable with conceptual tools rather than awaiting new data, which are neglected, and which look important but would change nothing downstream if settled. Tell me which one you would put first and why, which you would deliberately leave alone, and what argument or finding over the next couple of years would make you reorder the list.
