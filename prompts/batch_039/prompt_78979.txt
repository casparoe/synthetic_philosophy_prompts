My half-formed idea is this. The dispute over whether culture can be a cause seems to me to run on an assumption both sides accept without argument: that for values to explain conduct they must enter the causal history of individual actions as motives — as things people held and acted on. That assumption, I suspect, is what makes Weber's thesis about the Protestant ethic and the rise of capitalism look either untestable or trivially true. My hunch is that the explanatory work in that thesis is done elsewhere: values fix the space of options a population takes seriously, so that certain courses of action never come up for deliberation at all. If that is right, a value explanation need not be a story about anyone's motives, and the familiar objection that the thesis merely redescribes the conduct it was supposed to explain may or may not still land. Two further things should be taken seriously. First, doctrine and disposition can come apart: the conduct in question need not be accompanied by the belief that supposedly produced it, and the belief may persist without the conduct. Second, the thesis looks like a claim about a difference rather than an existence — about why rationalised conduct took hold in one setting rather than another — and I do not yet know whether that makes the explanatory burden lighter or heavier. My rough formulation of the question, which you should sharpen or replace if you can do better, is: can values explain conduct by constraining what a population treats as an available option rather than by figuring in anyone's motives, and if so, what must be true of such an explanation for it to be a causal explanation rather than a redescription?
