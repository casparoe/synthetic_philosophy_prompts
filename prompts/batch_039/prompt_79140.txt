I have been trying, with a friend, to write down an argument we both half-believe and that seems to us to prove more than we can accept. I would like your diagnosis of it. Two pieces of machinery first, so that the steps read as we intend them.

The provenance condition: for a feature of a person to be a basis of desert, the person must be its author — she must deserve to have it.

The endowment lottery: before anyone is born, each of us chooses her own constitution — her talents, her temperament, and how much effort she will find it natural to expend — from a common menu, and only then is born into her circumstances. We introduce the lottery to block the obvious reply that the arbitrariness of our actual origins is a contingent fact about this world rather than a feature of desert as such.

The argument:

1. (Provenance) A person deserves a good on the basis of a feature only if she deserves to have that feature.
2. No one deserves her constitution, and no arrangement of origins could change this: to deserve the constitution she has, she would have to have chosen it, and to deserve the constitution with which she did the choosing, she would have to have chosen that, and so on. Every genealogy of a self begins somewhere unearned.
3. Talents, temperament, and the propensity to exert effort are parts of a person's constitution.
4. So no one deserves to have the features on which assessments of merit rest.
5. So no one deserves anything on the basis of merit — not the job, not the prize, not the salary.
6. Nothing in this changes when we turn from what a person has to what she does. Whether she keeps her promises, whether she is cruel, whether she exerts herself on a stranger's behalf: all of it issues from a constitution she did not deserve to have.
7. So nothing anyone does makes her deserve anything — praise or blame, reward or punishment, decent treatment or the reverse.
8. But a man who tortures for his amusement does not deserve to be treated as the equal of a woman who has spent her life nursing strangers, and the fact that neither chose her character is no reason to think otherwise.
9. Therefore (1) is false.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep (2) than (8); I do not believe in the self-made man, and I would give up a great deal before I gave up the thought that our starting points are unearned. But I cannot see how to keep (2) and drop (1) without losing the critique of merit that started us off. One of us says the escape is to distinguish deserving a prize from being entitled to it under announced rules; I cannot tell whether that answers the argument or changes the subject. Give me a verdict rather than a menu: name the step, explain why it looked compelling enough to get this far, and tell me what giving it up costs — including whether anything of the original complaint against meritocracy survives.
