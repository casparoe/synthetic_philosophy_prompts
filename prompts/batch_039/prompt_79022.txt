I'm a TA for a grad seminar in formal ethics -- 14 students, 90 minutes, one room with a whiteboard and a stack of index cards. They've all done expected utility theory and they've read Rawls, and the session I'm building is on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. What I want them to feel in their bones is the move that does all the work: put everyone behind a veil of ignorance, let them rank policies from a position where they have an equal chance of ending up as any one of the affected people, and -- assuming their preferences satisfy the expected utility axioms -- they'll all agree on a ranking, and the ranking they agree on is the one that maximizes the sum of individual utilities. It looks like it proves utilitarianism from a premise most of them think of as anti-utilitarian, and every year they nod along and then can't say why it doesn't just settle the question. I also want them to run into the thing that happens afterwards: the same people, once they know which life they actually landed in, stop agreeing with the choice they unanimously made ex ante. So the unanimity behind the veil can be produced entirely by everyone having the same attitude to risk rather than by any real agreement about who should get what, which is a weirdly thin kind of agreement to build an ethics on. I don't want to announce either point. The word "utilitarian" shouldn't come out of my mouth until the debrief.

So design me a runnable activity, not a lecture. They should be the ones who generate the unanimity -- I want them to write their rankings down and then discover the agreement is already there, and I want the ex ante / ex post crack to open up from something that actually happens in the room, not from me telling them about it. No real money; points or chits are fine, and if there's randomization it has to be something they can watch happen (drawing cards, a spinner, whatever). It has to survive 14 people in one room for 90 minutes, and it has to be honest about what it can't show -- I'd rather have a field telling me which piece of the theorem the game just can't reproduce than have the game quietly pretend to do the proof.

Give me the whole thing as a JSON object, each field a string unless I say otherwise: "activity_name"; "overview" (2-3 paragraphs of prose on how it runs, start to finish); "participants_and_timing"; "materials" (array of strings); "setup_script" (what I say before anything starts, which must not name Harsanyi, utilitarianism, or the veil); "payoff_scheme" (the chits/points scheme, how a person's score gets determined, and exactly what's random); "timeline" (array of objects with "minutes", "beat", "what_happens", "what_i_do"); "the_moment_it_lands" (where the unanimity should show up and what I should be looking for on the board); "the_second_moment" (where the ex ante / ex post split should bite and how to stage it so they feel it instead of hearing it); "debrief" (array of strings, in order, each tied to something that actually happened in the room); "predictable_failures" (array of objects with "what_goes_wrong" and "what_to_do", at least three, and be specific -- I want to know the one where two students just refuse to play along); "what_the_activity_cannot_reproduce" (specific and honest); "one_line_thesis" (the sentence I want them walking out with).
