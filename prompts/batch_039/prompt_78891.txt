I have been trying to state precisely the duty to remember — the thought that a community which forgets certain of its past wrongs has failed at something more than record-keeping — and I have arrived at an argument that seems to dissolve the duty entirely. Every step looks defensible to me; the conclusion I cannot accept. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine, and what giving it up costs.

The plain case first. A country keeps one national day of mourning and has two atrocities in its past: one it suffered, one it committed. Both were grave; in both, the dead went unacknowledged. Whatever the country does with that day, it will honour one and leave the other in silence.

Now the argument.

(1) If a community owes remembrance to the victims of some wrong W, it owes it in virtue of a general feature of W — its gravity, the fact that its victims were wronged and never acknowledged, the fact that forgetting it makes repetition likelier. The duty cannot rest on W's merely being ours, on pain of being not a duty but a preference for our own.

(2) Many wrongs besides W possess that feature at least as fully.

(3) Remembrance is not free: it draws on attention, curriculum hours, public money, calendar days, and the emotional capacity of the people asked to do the remembering. What is spent on W is not spent on the rest.

(4) So if the feature grounds a duty to remember W, it grounds a duty to remember each of the others at least as much.

(5) No community can remember everything; so every act of commemoration — the very act the duty demanded — is also, by the same principle, a dereliction of other duties.

(6) A principle that makes every dutiful act simultaneously a wrongful one is incoherent.

(7) Therefore there is no duty to remember. Commemorating one atrocity rather than another is a matter of taste, resources, or politics, and not of obligation.

I cannot accept (7). A state that quietly erases a genocide it committed has done something worse than a state that prefers cricket to opera, and I do not think that verdict is mere sentiment. Yet I cannot locate the break. If it is (3), tell me what remembrance is like such that it is not zero-sum — and be specific, because attention and days of the year are finite. If it is (5) or (6), tell me what kind of ought survives unsatisfiability, and whether it keeps the force I want: can it still condemn the eraser, or does it thin out into a regret the eraser can shrug off? If it is (1) or (2), tell me whether the duty survives once made impartial, or whether, made partial again, it collapses into the preference (7) describes.

Two further matters, if they are not already the crux. First, the argument assumes that the duty to remember W′ competes with the duty to remember W in one currency; perhaps the remembrance owed to a wrong one has suffered is a different kind of thing from the remembrance owed to a wrong one has inflicted, drawing on a different reserve. If that is the way out, say what the two kinds are and why scarcity in the first leaves the second untouched. Second, I have assumed the duty runs to the victims. Suppose instead it runs to the community's own future — we remember so as not to repeat. Does the argument survive the substitution, or does the duty become a mere instrument, hostage to whatever pedagogy happens to work?

Rank the exits by cost and say which one a defender of the duty ought to take.
