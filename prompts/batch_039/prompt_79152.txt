My discussion group has spent four meetings on whether white lies are wrong and we are going in circles. Roughly, the camps are: lying is wrong because it wrecks the whole practice of being able to believe each other, and a kind motive doesn't launder that; versus speech is mainly for holding relationships together, so a lie that spares someone pointless pain is speech doing its job, and the person demanding the brutal truth in that moment is the one being unkind. Then the two members who've lived in both Tokyo and Amsterdam keep pointing out that in one place you're expected to just say the true thing and let people handle it, while in the other the polite reply is basically a fixed form that everyone recognizes as a form, so nobody feels decieved, which they think makes the whole question parochial. Annoyingly, both of the first two camps keep citing those same examples as evidence for themselves and neither seems to notice. And when we try to settle the factual part -- do the polite formulas actually fool anyone, does bluntness actually damage relationships -- we find we don't even agree on what counts as damage. My hunch is the fight isn't really about lying at all; it's about who should eat the cost: the speaker, who has to either lie or hurt someone, or the hearer, who has to either get hurt or do the work of reading the situation. Is that the right diagnosis, or is the real fault line somewhere else -- an empirical disagreement in disguise, a disagreement about what even counts as a lie (true-but-misleading, saying nothing, letting someone keep a false belief), or two different questions hiding under one word? And whatever you decide it is, tell me what would actually move us: a distinction, a way of splitting the question, or nothing at all, because it's bedrock intuition on both sides and no argument reaches it. Don't give me a both-sides-have-a-point answer. I want the diagnosis, and an honest call on whether progress here is even possible.
