My mother died in the spring, and since then I have spent a great deal of time reading about time. What I keep coming back to is the standoff over the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. As I reconstruct it, the argument runs from the relativity of simultaneity to eternalism: if no inertial frame is privileged, and simultaneity is frame-relative, then the set of events that are real cannot be identified with the present of any one frame without arbitrariness; and since reality is not the sort of thing that can be relative to a choice of coordinates, all events must be equally real. Those who defend becoming reply in one of two registers: either that the inference is invalid, on the ground that relativity constrains simultaneity relations rather than existence, or that the block picture, however complete as physics, leaves out presentness, which we know otherwise. The exchange has run for decades and neither side moves.

I do not want a survey of the positions. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement. My hypothesis, offered to be tested rather than adopted, is that it is neither empirical nor merely verbal but a disagreement about what a physical theory is in the business of asserting: the eternalist reads the geometry as a complete inventory of what there is, the becoming theorist reads it as a description of relations among things whose existence is otherwise given, and each is unable to argue the other out of his conception of what physics is for. Two further suspicions belong to the same hypothesis. First, if becoming can be had together with a block — the moving spotlight, say — then relativity cannot refute becoming, which suggests that what is really at issue lies elsewhere. Second, the argument asks me to accept a move I would refuse elsewhere: no one infers from the indexicality of here that every place is equally real, yet the parallel inference from the indexicality of now is supposed to compel assent.

Please tell me what kind of disagreement this is, with reasons drawn from what each side actually claims, and whether it is better described as a dispute about facts, about what counts as an explanation, or about which questions are well formed. Then tell me what would count as progress. Would the discovery of a preferred frame settle anything, or would both sides absorb it without strain? Is there a distinction — between existence and predication, or between what a theory represents and what it presupposes — that would move the debate, or is the honest answer that the impasse should be expected to persist? And since I am asking partly for my own sake: if the disagreement turns out to be about what physics is for rather than about time, is my sense that something is at stake in whether the past is real simply a confusion?
