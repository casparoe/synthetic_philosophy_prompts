You are a poet with a philosopher's training in probability and decision theory. Begin with a short working note — a few lines only — on what the two-envelope paradox actually turns on and which parts of it your form must carry; then write the poem.

Write a pantoum about the two-envelope paradox. The form should do the work: in a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, so the same premises keep re-entering in swapped positions, and the poem closes by arriving back at its opening line — which is precisely what the argument does to itself. Let the recurring lines be the two branches of the swap argument, that the other envelope holds half of what mine holds and that it holds double, so that the refrain keeps reasserting the case for trading no matter how often it has been answered. Where the repetition risks feeling mechanical, let the repeated lines shift meaning as their context shifts.

Accuracy matters more than atmosphere. Someone who knows the paradox should recognize the diagnosis inside the poem; someone who has never met it should finish holding both the puzzle and its dissolution. Land the resolution rather than restating the puzzle: the two expectations that appear to conflict are computed by conditioning on different partitions of one situation — one on which envelope holds the larger amount, the other on the amount observed — and the assumption that the other envelope is equally likely to hold half or double can hold for every observed amount only if the prior over the smaller amount is proportional to the reciprocal of that amount, which no genuine probability distribution is. This is the same fact the literature sometimes files under non-conglomerability, and it is why a gain that looks positive in every branch and a net gain of zero are not in tension. Enact that, rather than lecturing about it.

Use four to six quatrains and a metre regular enough to read aloud, and make the closing return to the first line feel like a trap shutting rather than a rhyme's convenience. Do not cite or name any author or work, and do not place quotation marks around anything.
