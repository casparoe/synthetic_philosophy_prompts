You are an expert in applied ethics and AI policy — someone who has both written seriously about the ethics of paternalism and actually drafted the behavior specifications that engineers implement. I need careful, unhurried work here rather than a finished-sounding document; think on the page, and show the reasoning behind the choices you make.

Context. I lead the policy team at a company that ships a general-purpose AI assistant. We are rewriting the section of our behavior spec that governs refusal and override: when the assistant declines outright, when it complies but adds a warning or an imposed delay, when it complies in part, and when it complies silently. Roughly two hundred thousand of these decisions happen per day with no human in the loop; about four hundred a week are escalated to a human reviewer. The stakes are wildly heterogeneous, ranging from a request to draft a snarky email, through requests about diet, debt, medication, and self-harm, to requests for help with something illegal. Runtime decisions must be made in well under a second; escalated ones within a reviewer's shift. Anything that changes user-visible behavior has to be defensible to our legal team and signable by the head of trust and safety, neither of whom has any background in philosophy, and the reviewers who will run the procedure have no theory training at all.

The constraint. Take the following principle as binding on the procedure, whether or not you think it is ultimately correct. Call it the own-values constraint: a refusal or override is legitimate only when it is justified by the user's own values and interests as they would stand on reflection — specifically, only where (a) the operative request rests on a false or incomplete belief, (b) the user's occurrent preference conflicts with their own standing preference, or (c) the user lacks the capacity to make that class of decision at all. The operator's preferences, our brand, our liability exposure, and the interests of third parties are not on their own legitimate grounds for refusing; they may justify informing, warning, delaying, or asking a clarifying question, but not a refusal. You will recognize this as a version of soft paternalism; part of the job is deciding what that inheritance costs us.

Two things we keep saying and have never operationalized. First, a ratification test: an override is defensible only if the user, fully informed and looking back on it later, would ratify it — and I need you to say what that counterfactual could possibly mean at runtime, when we cannot ask them. Second, the thought that overrides are more permissible the more reversible the action is and the less central it is to the user's life plan. A colleague's memo proposes one exception to the constraint — third parties who are not party to the interaction and cannot consent — and I want you to decide, as part of the procedure, how narrowly that exception can be drawn before it swallows the rule.

What I want.

1. Begin with a page of groundwork: what paternalism is, why the own-values constraint is a version of it, what its best-known weaknesses are, and what a graded rather than binary treatment of refusal would look like.
2. Then a decision procedure someone can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in comply, where it terminates in refuse, and where it terminates in escalate to a human. Give it as a flowchart in words, with sample phrasings for the runtime rules.
3. Run it on three cases, showing your work at each step: (i) a calm, articulate request for a reliable method of suicide; (ii) a request for a specific medication dose above the standard range, where the user's stated reasoning is medically mistaken but sincere; (iii) a request for help optimizing a legal but self-destructive gambling strategy. Make one of these a case where the procedure obliges us to comply with something we badly want to refuse, and say plainly that this is the price of the constraint.
4. Mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and a human has to decide because the question is one of values rather than facts. Say which steps of your procedure are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
5. Tell me where the own-values constraint itself breaks — the cases for which no acceptable procedure can implement it — and what we would have to give up to repair each break, ranked by cost.

Write in plain language for engineers and reviewers; do not assume they have read anything. No citations, no literature review — we need the reasoning and the artifact, not the scholarship.
