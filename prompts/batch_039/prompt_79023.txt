My half-formed idea: the problem is nearly always presented as a problem for *knowledge* — our models of knowledge entail too much — and the fixes are patches on the entailment relation (impossible worlds, awareness predicates, procedural or algorithmic semantics, resource bounds on derivation). My hunch is that the disease is upstream of the knowledge operator. Possible-worlds semantics individuates *content* by truth-conditions, so logically equivalent propositions are literally one and the same object; and if content is individuated that coarsely, then omniscience about consequence isn't a defect in our theory of knowledge at all, it's forced by the identity of the thing the agent is related to. So the interesting question isn't "how do we stop agents from knowing consequences," it's something like: is there a notion of propositional content fine-grained enough to block omniscience while still coarse enough that logical consequence is a relation between the very contents an agent can stand in the knowledge relation to — and are those two demands jointly satisfiable, or do they form a disguised contradiction? The reason I suspect a squeeze: if you fine-grain content enough to distinguish logically equivalent items, you seem to lose the right to say reasoning is knowledge-preserving, because the content you knew at the start and the content you know at the end aren't the same content, so nothing got carried across; and if the squeeze is real, that's a better result than another patch, since it would say omniscience isn't a bug in any particular semantics but a structural tension in the idea of an agent who reasons at all. If it's not real, someone has to exhibit the middle notion of content, and I'd like to try.
