A colleague in my pragmatism seminar keeps invoking a line she credits to Rorty — that 'true' is 'a compliment paid to sentences that seem to be paying their way' — and last week she used it to close off a question I take to be real: whether there is any fact of the matter about an event in the distant past that left no trace whatever, her reasoning being that where nobody could ever be in a position to pay the compliment there is nothing for it to attach to. I want the slogan broken into the distinct claims it could be making — about the meaning of the word 'true', about what earns a sentence the compliment, about whether any property is being tracked, and about who does the paying — laid out as a menu I can take to the next session. Mark each reading as trivial, as substantive but defensible, or as false; say which of them Rorty would actually own and which he is usually misread as holding; and say which of them, if any, would give her the conclusion she wants about untraceable facts, together with whatever further premise she would need in order to get from there to 'there is no fact of the matter'. I cannot tell how much of her conclusion the slogan is doing and how much is arriving from somewhere she never says out loud.
