I'm a second-year CS student who got dragged along to a philosophy of science reading group by a friend, and I've somehow been volunteered to run next weeks session on idealization in modelling, on the grounds that I do cryptography so I must have thought about this. The trouble is that the more I try to explain what my field actually does, the less I can defend it. Here's the practice. If I want to show that some encryption or signature scheme is secure, I basically can't prove anything about the real hash function it uses, say SHA-256. That thing is a fixed deterministic function: same input, same output, always. There's no randomness in it at all, and any two of its output values are about as far from independent as it's possible to get, since each one is completely determined by its input. So instead, in the proof, I swap the hash out for a fictional black box that returns a fresh uniformly random answer the first time it's asked about any input, with all those answers independent of one another, and I then show that no adversary can break the scheme in that fictional world. Then I publish the real scheme, the one with SHA-256 in it, and treat the proof as evidence that the real thing is fine. It's called the random oracle model, and the standard line is that such a proof is better than no proof at all. I also half-remember hearing that people have built schemes that are provably secure in the fictional world but trivially broken no matter which concrete hash function you instantiate them with, which feels like it should be a scandal but everyone just shrugs.

What I want isn't the slogan version of why this is OK, I want the actual commitments. Is there a coherent position on which a proof about the fictional world, where all hash outputs are independent, is evidence about the SHA-256 world, where they're maximally dependent? Does that position require me to believe SHA-256 is 'random enough', i.e. a claim about the statistics of the hash, or is the independence assumption doing something else entirely, like marking which attacks are out of scope, or moving where the burden of proof sits, or working as a heuristic in a sense that has nothing much to do with probability? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, for instance does it rule out the view that a proof only ever tells you about the model you proved it in? And is the practice even internally consistent, given that the same community will say in the next breath that a security proof is worthless if the model doesn't match reality, and that we should be honest that we're making an assumption?

The bit I actually care about: does this practice rest on a presumption that independence can be assumed by default, absent positive evidence of dependence? If so, is that presumption a probability claim, a convention about what counts as a security claim, an inference to the best explanation of why the field's schemes have survived decades of attack, or something else? Tell me which parts of this are strictly presupposed by the practice and which are just things that happen to fit it, and where the practice is underdetermined, so that several different theories would license the same behaviour. And what would have to change in cryptography if we dropped the presumption and required independence to be established positively instead?
