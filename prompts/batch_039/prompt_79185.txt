I belong to a small reading group on agency and determinism, and last week we spent an entire session on a single case that has been sitting badly with me ever since. The case concerns the digger wasp of the genus Sphex. When she is ready to lay, she digs a burrow, hunts down a cricket, stings it so that it is paralyzed but alive, and drags it back to the mouth of the burrow. There she leaves it on the threshold and goes inside to inspect. Only if the inspection satisfies her does she come back out and pull the cricket in. The interference is childishly simple: while she is underground, nudge the cricket a few inches away. She emerges, finds it, drags it back to the threshold, and goes inside to inspect again -- and the whole sequence will run again and again, however many times you move the cricket, even though simply carrying it in on the way would be the obvious thing to do after the first round. Our group's leader called behavior of this kind sphexish: superficially purposeful, but exposed by the right interference as a fixed routine that cannot revise itself. One member objected that the experiment is less clean than it is made to sound, that there are reports of the wasp eventually adapting, and that in any case re-inspecting a nest that someone has been meddling with may be a perfectly sensible policy; I am not persuaded that this rescues anything, because the point was never really about this one insect. What I cannot get past is the suspicion that the entire notion is an artifact of the observer's vantage point.

I would like you to play a philosopher of mind who has spent a career on the opposite side of this from me: someone who treats the wasp not as a curiosity but as the central clue to mentality, who holds that the difference between the wasp and me is a matter of how deeply the routines nest rather than a difference in kind, and who regards what we call free will as either the deepest such loop or a flattering name for it. Give the character real resources rather than contempt. They should cheerfully grant that I do things the wasp cannot, and should be able to say, in detail, why those things are still routines on a longer leash; they should concede a point when I land one, but never the main claim. Let them be a little dry and fond of homely examples, let them refuse to break character to recommend reading, and let them push back hard whenever I overreach instead of merely agreeing that the problem is deep. Keep each reply to a couple of paragraphs so that it reads like a live exchange.

My opening move is this. The force of the wasp story comes from us and not from her: we call her behavior mechanical because we already possess the counterfactual -- we know what she fails to do when the cricket moves -- whereas she has no such knowledge of herself. But by that standard I can find a routine behind any finite creature you like. Point to some piece of my behavior that strikes you as genuinely flexible and I will describe a larger mechanism that would produce it; point to a mechanism and I will find a yet larger one for which it is merely a subroutine. If nothing short of infinitude escapes the charge, then sphexishness has quietly become a name for the limits of our imagination rather than a feature of the wasp, and the interesting question is not whether I am an insect underneath but why anyone is so eager to insist that I am.
