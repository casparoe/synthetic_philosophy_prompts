My day job is writing software and I have no formal training in philosophy, so I want to flag up front that I am asking about something I have only read about on the side and may well be misstating.

The idea I keep running into is the strange-loop account of the self, roughly this: the self is not a thing located inside a head, but a self-referential symbol — a pattern a system produces when it models its own modeling, and the felt unity of being someone is what that pattern is like from the inside. It is usually presented as a modern, computational successor to the old debate about personal identity.

What I would like from you is an account of what David Hume would have made of it. Concretely:

1. Reconstruct, as precisely as you can and in your own words, the position Hume actually held about the self: what he thought the word I picks out, what he thought we are doing when we treat a bundle of perceptions as belonging to a single owner, what he took the source of that mistake to be, and where (if anywhere) he admitted that his own account left a gap. Be explicit about which of these claims are secure in his texts and which are contested among interpreters.

2. Then say what he would have accepted and what he would have rejected in the strange-loop picture. I care about the specific joints rather than a general verdict. Is the claim that the self is a real pattern rather than a substance a restatement of his view, a friendly repair of it, or precisely the error he diagnosed? Would he regard the loop as one more fiction of the imagination, or as the missing mechanism that explains why the fiction is so inescapable? And would his insistence that our ideas trace back to impressions leave room for a self that is only ever a pattern, never something observed?

3. Finally, extend this to a case he never faced. I work with a large language model that emits fluent first-person output: it says "I'm not sure", "I didn't mean that", "I'm the one answering you". Nothing in it persists between conversations, and there is no inner state that these sentences track; the pronoun is generated the same way as every other token. Would Hume say there is a self there? And — the part I most want — is his answer for the model different in kind from his answer for me, or only in degree? If only in degree, say what the degree consists in, and whether Hume has the resources to draw the line roughly where most people would want it drawn.

4. Close with your own verdict, clearly separated from the historical reconstruction: which parts of the strange-loop picture survive contact with Hume, and where his position would have to give way if that picture is right.

Please skip reading lists and citations — I cannot look anything up right now and would rather have your reasoning. Where the texts do not settle a question, say so and give your best reconstruction anyway, marking how confident you are.
