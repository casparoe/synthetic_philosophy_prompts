I am a third-year undergraduate in an upper-level metaethics seminar, and our convenor has set the class a task I would like help with. Each of us has to draft one examination question for the end-of-unit assessment, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The best draft will be used, with credit, in the paper the cohort actually sits. My question must fall within the unit on moral particularism and generalism, and the convenor has attached two conditions that I am finding difficult to satisfy at the same time.

The first is that the question must not be answerable by reciting the standard summaries. Anyone who has done the reading can reproduce the claims that reasons are holistic, that moral judgement is uncodifiable, and that generalism overstates the role of principles; she has warned us that a question answerable by that recitation will be marked down as a question, whatever the student writes underneath it.

The second is that the paper is closed-book and library-free. There must be no bibliography, no citations, and no credit for dropping names, so the question has to be self-contained and has to reward reasoning rather than recall.

To force the issue, she has circulated a handout of material she wrote herself for this exercise, and has asked us to build our question around it. It consists of a stipulation and three cases.

The stipulation. Holism about reasons is the claim that a feature which is a reason of a given valence for an action in one case may be a reason of the opposite valence, or no reason at all, in another case. Generalism denies this: it holds that a feature's valence is fixed wherever the feature occurs, and that any apparent reversal is always to be explained by some further feature acting as an enabling or disabling condition.

The first case. You have a free afternoon and could spend it practising the cello, which you enjoy. The fact that you would enjoy it is a reason in favour of doing it.

The second case. A torturer enjoys inflicting pain on a victim, and the fact that he would enjoy it is a reason against his doing it.

She asks us to treat these as the same feature — the act would be enjoyable by the agent — occurring with opposite valence.

The third case. A colleague has, as a matter of fact, always behaved generously towards you. In one case you know this; in the other you have exactly the same evidence about her past behaviour, but the evidence is misleading and her past generosity was feigned. The handout presents this as a second reversal, in which the very same feature is a reason to trust her in one case and a reason not to trust her in the other.

Her final instruction is that our question must require students to say precisely what has to be held fixed for the holism claim to be more than a verbal trick, to adjudicate whether the first two cases support it, to diagnose what is wrong with the third, and to say whether the generalist can simply absorb the whole business by writing a hedged universal principle — one whose exception clauses are long and disjunctive — and thereby have everything a principle was ever supposed to provide.

Please write my question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and the marking scheme. The question should be pitched at upper-level undergraduates, answerable in about fifty minutes, and should not reward name-dropping. The marking scheme should total 25 marks, and should consist of the specific moves a strong answer must make, the marks each is worth, and, for each, a one-line note on the characteristic error students make at that point. I would also like a short closing note on where the boundary between an A and a B answer falls. Neither the question nor the model answer should presuppose any particular author, title, or text.
