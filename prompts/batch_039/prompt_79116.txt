I teach a third-year undergraduate course on computability and its philosophical significance, and I have been thinking about the Church–Turing thesis in a way I did not expect. My own background is in software, where I treated the thesis as simply a fact about computation; now that I have to teach its epistemic status, I find the standard treatment unsatisfying in a subtle way. The thesis is not a theorem and, as far as I can see, cannot be one: it identifies a pre-theoretic notion — what a person can compute by following a finite set of mechanical rules — with a formal one, Turing-machine computability, and because the notion on the left is informal there is nothing from which to prove the equivalence. What supports the thesis instead is a convergence: independent attempts to make mechanical procedure precise, arriving from different directions, all pick out the same class of functions; and a smaller literature tries to improve on this by extracting axioms from our idea of a computation step and deriving the equivalence from them, which still begins from an informal reading of the axioms. The convergence is genuinely impressive, and it is not a proof. A student asked me last month what would count as evidence against the thesis and I gave a poor answer, and I have wanted to repair that ever since — by getting the class to feel both halves of the situation rather than take my word for either.

Please design the session. It is ninety minutes, twenty students, a room with a whiteboard and index cards and no computers; the class is mixed, some computer science and some philosophy, and all of them have met the halting problem but little else. I want the convergence to be something they produce in the room rather than something I announce: they should build two or three different rule-based devices of their own, of visibly different shapes, and then establish by their own work that whatever one can do, the others can do as well. I want that to feel like an achievement, and then I want the moment where the achievement turns out not to license what they will want it to license. Arrange the rules, roles, materials, and any scoring scheme so that this happens on its own; do not have me name the thesis until the debrief. Give me a timeline, and tell me what materials I need to prepare in advance.

The debrief should map what actually happened onto the two claims — the convergence and the absence of proof — including the places where the students' convergence was too easy to mean much (if all their devices were secretly one device, or if the tasks I set never needed more than a single primitive, the exercise establishes nothing) and what a better-designed version of their own experiment would have to look like. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time; I expect at least one group to build a device with a magical primitive, and I would like to know how to handle that without either blessing it or dismissing it. Finally, be frank about which feature of the thesis's epistemic standing the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief myself.
