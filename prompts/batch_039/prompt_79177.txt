I am a lecturer in philosophy at a university in a country whose parliament is currently debating a formal acknowledgment of atrocities committed by a predecessor regime. The debate has become personally urgent for me: my grandfather served as a mid-level administrator in that regime, and my students keep asking whether the acknowledgment should say that we are guilty or merely that we are responsible. I have been developing a distinction in lectures that I now want to test. I call it the asymmetric inheritance thesis (AIT): a nation can inherit responsibility for a historical wrong, but not guilt, because guilt is first-personal and non-transferable, whereas responsibility attaches to institutional roles and can be passed down. I want the final exam for my upper-level course on collective responsibility and historical injustice to contain one essay question that cannot be answered by reciting Jaspers's four categories.

The question should ask students to evaluate AIT against Jaspers, especially the category of metaphysical guilt, and to say what a nation could coherently mean when it acknowledges guilt if AIT is true. It should also require them to distinguish guilt as an emotion, guilt as a status, and guilt as a verdict; to explain whether collective guilt across generations is ever possible or only collective responsibility; to address whether a nation can be a collective agent capable of guilt at all; and to say whether the death of the perpetrators changes the metaphysics of guilt or only the practicality of blame. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes.
