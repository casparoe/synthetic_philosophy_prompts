I'm a graduate student in a group that works on cooperation between artificial agents, and my advisor has spent about a decade on program equilibrium. Her standing setup is a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma in which each player submits a program, the two programs are run once, each may read the other's source before choosing, and the source that is read is the source that runs. She always asks whether mutual cooperation is an equilibrium there, not merely whether it happens to occur. Her verdicts on the cases we have fought over:

1. Both players submit the same program. It is written to cooperate if and only if the opponent's program cooperates when run against it, and each copy settles that question by checking whether the opponent's source is identical to its own, which terminates. Verdict: mutual cooperation, and it is an equilibrium, because a player who swapped in a program that defects against everything would be met with defection and come out behind.

2. Two different programs, each the plain conditional, with no identity check and nothing else to break the circle. Verdict: no. She refuses to count the pair as cooperating at all, on the ground that there is no fact of the matter about what it does.

3. Full mutual access, but one program's choice turns on a private coin flip that the other can never observe. Verdict: no.

4. One side can read the other's source; the other cannot read back. Verdict: no.

5. Both sources are published, but with probability ε the opponent's published source is a decoy and the program that actually runs always defects. Verdict: cooperation is an equilibrium exactly when ε falls below a threshold computable from the payoffs, and not otherwise.

6. The conditional program is paired with one that cooperates unconditionally. Cooperation occurs, but she insists it is not an equilibrium, since the unconditional cooperator is being farmed.

Whether the identity check in the first case is doing the essential work, or is only the easiest way to settle a question that some other sound method could settle just as well, she has never said, and that is much of what I am after.

The new case: the two programs are not copies of one another, and neither contains any test of whether the opponent is a copy of itself. Each is written to cooperate only if it can establish that the other cooperates exactly when it does; Alice's program establishes this by simulating Bob's, Bob's by searching for a proof of the corresponding fact about Alice's. The routes differ, and I have convinced myself that both succeed and that the pair cooperates.

Tell me what she will say. I want her verdict, the rule she is running, and the other rules that fit her six verdicts just as well, including which of them this new case actually decides and which it leaves open. Give me your best guess with your reasons for it, and name the further case that would settle whichever rules are still standing. I don't need references; I want the argument.
