I'm not an academic, I just read a lot of this stuff on my own, and something in a government document has been bugging me for months.

Where I live, when the government wants to decide if a new regulation is worth the cost, they run a cost-benefit analysis, and the official instruction is that a benefit in the future is worth less than the same benefit now. The rate is a few percent a year. So roughly: a rule that saves a life this year gets full value, a life saved in thirty years is worth maybe a third as much on the spreadsheet, and in two hundred years it's basically nothing. I think the schedule actually steps down over time — something like 3.5% for the first few decades, drifting down to 1 or 1.5% out past a few centuries — but the point is the same either way.

Then in the same document there's a carve-out. For certain irreversible things, wiping out a species and that kind of thing, they say you shouldn't discount at all, or you should treat the loss as if it were happening now.

I cant square those two. On one side they're telling me a life three hundred years out counts for almost zero, on the other they're saying the extinction of some snail is a full-strength cost. And here's the other thing that gets me: nobody would accept this arithmetic across space. If the guidance said a life saved in another country counts for a third as much, there'd be outrage. Across time it's just standard practice.

I want you to act as a moral philosopher who also knows welfare economics well — someone who can move between the technical discounting machinery and the ethics of it. Please think carefully before you answer, and maybe start by brainstorming the different things that rate could possibly be doing before you settle on anything.

What I want:

1. What does this practice actually presuppose? Is it committed to the idea that future welfare matters less just because it's future, or could it be that future people will be richer so a dollar does less for them, or that the benefit is uncertain, or something else entirely? I suspect a few of these are getting smuggled in at once without anyone saying so. Tell me wich.

2. Separate what the practice strictly needs from what it just happens to fit. In particular I've heard that a discount rate that declines over time can fall out of plain uncertainty about future growth, with no time preference at all — is that right, and if so does the declining schedule stop being evidence for time bias?

3. Here's a puzzle I can't get past: discounting a future benefit by a few percent a year is mathematically indistinguishable from saying there's a few percent chance per year that the beneficiary will never exist. If that number on the spreadsheet is really just a probability of existence rather than a judgement that future lives are worth less, does that rescue the practice? Does it change what the practice presupposes?

4. The extinction carve-out. What view does that presuppose — that some losses aren't commensurable with ordinary welfare, that irreversibility has a special kind of value, that there's a duty not to destroy rather than a duty to maximise? Is the carve-out consistent with the discounting, or is it a straight contradiction someone papered over?

5. Finally, if the presupposed view were wrong and we replaced it — say with strict impartiality across time, where a life is a life whenever it happens — what concretely has to change in the guidance? What would the numbers have to look like, and what would the politics of that look like, given that the costs land on people who are alive now?

No need to cite papers or give me a reading list, I won't be able to find them. I want the reasoning spelled out in plain language. If some of my assumptions above are confused, just say so directly.
