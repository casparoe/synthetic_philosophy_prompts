I am in a reading group on moral anthropology, and last week we spent three hours on a comparison that left me genuinely unsure. The setup was a three-way contrast among cultural logics for handling a person's standing. In the first, worth is held by others and lives in one's reputation: an insult is a real loss, not being taken seriously is an injury, and the fitting response to a slight is to answer it, sometimes violently, because the alternative is to become the sort of person who can be slighted with impunity. In the second, worth is intrinsic and equal, so reputation is a distraction: slights are to be shrugged off or routed to impersonal procedures, and the person who fights over status has simply misunderstood where their worth comes from. In the third, worth is something one can press a claim on: the characteristic move is to name the wrong one has suffered and address it to a wider audience -- bystanders, institutions, the public -- which is then expected to recognize the wrong and remedy it. We could not agree on whether any of these is morally better than the others, and the discussion ended in the usual place, with everyone agreeing that each has costs and benefits and nobody willing to rank them.

A friend then proposed a frame that I would like to see tested. She said the three are not really three theories of value but three answers to a procedural question: when my standing is contested, who is the court of appeal? Under the honor logic it is the community, and its verdict is final; under the dignity logic it is me, since my worth is not hostage to anyone's opinion and the community's judgment is therefore not even admissible; under the victimhood logic it is a third party -- an audience asked to adjudicate the claim and obliged to respond. On her view the moral question then becomes which court should have jurisdiction, and the answer turns on which courts are trustworthy, which is a contingent social fact rather than a moral one. She offered a second distinction that I find more interesting still: whether the logic treats standing as positional (I have more of it when others have less), unconditional (insensitive to comparison), or as a claim on other people's attention and resources (so that recognition, not esteem, is the scarce good being allocated).

Write an essay on this controversy. Weigh the arguments on both sides, and reach a conclusion you are prepared to defend. I want you to address, at minimum, three things. First, is her procedural frame a good one -- does it capture what these logics actually disagree about, or does it distort them by making a substantive disagreement look like a jurisdictional one? Second, grant the frame for the sake of argument: is any of the three logics morally superior? Be explicit about what you mean by superior -- better as an ideal of how people should relate to one another, better in its consequences for the worst off, or better as a way of coordinating a pluralistic society -- since I suspect the answer differs across these readings, and a verdict that quietly slides between them would be worthless. Third, confront the objection that each logic's characteristic pathology is the mirror of its virtue: honor yields feuds and violence but also courage, candor, and thick solidarity; dignity protects the vulnerable from the tyranny of reputation but may be blind to status as a real and ineradicable human need; the third gives the weak a lever to press claims but rewards the display of suffering and can inflate the currency of recognition until nobody's grievance is worth anything. If you think that symmetry makes a ranking impossible, say so and argue it; if you think one logic survives the symmetry better than the others, say that instead. A concrete case may help you keep the essay honest: take a person publicly humiliated before their community, work through how each logic would handle it, and say what is gained and what is lost in each handling. Do not end on a non-committal note.
