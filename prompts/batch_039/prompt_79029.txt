I have no training in statistics or in philosophy; what I know about either I have picked up from reading on my own and from a long-running argument in an online group I belong to. The group exists mainly to argue about cases where two sets of numbers seem to tell opposite stories, and one member, a woman named Ruth who spent her working life in local government finance, has become our unofficial judge. When someone posts a comparison in which one option comes out ahead overall and the other option comes out ahead inside every smaller group, Ruth always rules, and she always gives a reason. I have written down three of her rulings. I would like you to work out what she is doing, and then tell me how she will rule on a fourth case that has just been posted.

Ruling 1. Two maths coaching programmes in one city, Northgate and Southgate, each with 200 pupils who sat the same exam. Northgate: 128 passed; of its 120 pupils who were already strong at maths, 108 passed, and of its 80 who were weak, 20 passed. Southgate: 85 passed; of its 40 pupils who were already strong, 37 passed, and of its 160 who were weak, 48 passed. Ruth said Southgate was doing better, on the grounds that Southgate takes far more of the pupils who need help and still gets more of them through.

Ruling 2. Two job-search schemes, Step-Up and Bridgeway, each with 400 clients. Step-Up: 220 clients were in work six months later; of the 200 who had got an interview in their first month on the scheme, 140 were in work, and of the 200 who had not, 80 were. Bridgeway: 195 clients were in work; of the 100 who had got an interview in the first month, 72 were in work, and of the 300 who had not, 123 were. Getting people into interviews is one of the things both schemes are meant to do. Ruth said Step-Up was the better scheme.

Ruling 3. This one is a slightly different shape, because here the two smaller groups disagree with each other as well as with the whole. Two diets, X and Y, were tried on children with poor growth. Overall X did better: 180 of its 300 children improved, against 165 of Y's 300. Among the children who were already overweight, Y did better: 90 of its 150 improved, against 30 of X's 60. Among the children who were not overweight, X did better: 150 of its 240 improved, against 75 of Y's 150. The study happened to contain mostly children who were not overweight, which is why X came out ahead overall. Ruth's ruling was that for the clinic that had asked us, where most of the children are overweight, Y was the right choice, and that the overall figure was a fact about who happened to be in the study rather than a fact about the diets.

The new case. A friend of mine works for a small charity and has to choose between two mentoring schemes for young people who left school with no qualifications. Halden and Ives were each run for a year in similar towns, with 400 young people in each. Under Halden, 244 of the 400 were in work or training a year later, and 100 of the 400 attended the sessions regularly; of those 100, 55 were in work or training. Under Ives, 220 of the 400 were in work or training a year later, and 250 of the 400 attended regularly; of those 250, 175 were in work or training. My friend's director has said that what matters is which scheme does better for the young people it actually reaches. The group is split. One of us says Ives is plainly better, since among the young people who actually turn up it is 70 per cent against 55. Another says that is nonsense, because the whole point is what happens to all 400, where Halden wins, and anyway the young people who turn up to Halden are the hardest cases in the town. Someone else has pointed out that Halden's non-attenders did rather well and Ives's did rather badly, which nobody can explain.

What I want from you is not really the answer for its own sake. I want to know what rule or rules Ruth's three rulings are consistent with, what each of them predicts about the new case, which prediction you would bet on and why, and what further case would separate the rules if they come apart here. If her record genuinely does not settle the new case, or if my friend's question as it stands cannot be answered from these numbers at all, I would rather be told that than given a verdict.
