My problem is that every version I write collapses into explain the gas gauge case and say whether reliabilism survives, and they just recite Vogel and Cohen and walk out with a B+.

So I want the question built on a case I've made up. Use it as given:

Mira has noticed that some of her dreams seem to anticipate the next day — she dreams about a flat tire and next morning the tire is flat, she dreams about a friend she hasn't thought about in years and that friend texts her that afternoon. So she starts a journal: every morning she writes down the night's dream, and every evening she marks whether anything in it matched the day. After a year she counts up the entries that have a recorded match, finds a few dozen of them, and concludes that her dreams are weakly predictive of the following day. On that basis she starts treating a vivid dream as evidence about tomorrow — she cancels a flight because she dreamed about a crash. Her flatmate says this is circular, she's using the dreams to license the dreams. Mira says no, it's just an ordinary track-record argument, same shape as the one that establishes a thermometer works.

What I want back: the essay question, a model answer of roughly 600-700 words, and a marking scheme.

The question has to make them commit to a verdict on Mira and defend it, not discuss. It also needs to force out two things that reciting the standard cases won't give me: first, exactly what is and isn't wrong with Mira's reasoning, and second, what a track-record argument for that same conclusion would have to look like in order not to be bootstrapping. And I'd like it to make them say whether the objection they're wielding proves too much — whether it would eat memory, or induction, or testimony along with Mira.

One more thing: I've deliberately put a detail in the case that I think shifts the verdict away from the standard gas-gauge setup. Build the marking scheme so a good answer has to find it.
