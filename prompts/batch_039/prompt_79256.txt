My mother turned eighty last month and has adopted as her motto a line she attributes to Cicero's dialogue on old age: that an old man plants trees which will benefit a later generation. She uses it to answer the suggestion that her remaining years are a diminishing return, and I have been asked to respond at her birthday lunch. I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply repeat it. Begin with a correspondence table: what plays the role of the planter, the sapling, the soil, the harvest, and the later generation. Then say where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the work the analogy is doing — answering the charge that old age is worthless because it has no future — from those that are merely decorative. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion Cicero puts in Cato's mouth, that old age is not to be pitied or feared, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying more weight than it can bear. Note in particular whether the analogy sits easily with Cato's other claims that old age has its own intrinsic goods, or whether it concedes that the value of old age is merely instrumental. If there is a better analogy — in the dialogue or elsewhere — say what it is and why it does the job better.
