I'm a novelist, and I teach a graduate seminar on authorship in the age of generative models. The course runs on a short vocabulary handout I wrote for the second meeting, which I reproduce here because the students were required to use its terms:

"The seminar keeps four things apart. Making is the causal process by which an artifact's features come to exist. Claiming is the act by which someone takes an artifact up as her own: she selects it, presents it, revises it, and becomes the person others address when they praise or blame it. Authorship names the status that claiming confers. Novelty is the production of something that has not existed before. Creativity is a success term: it names the achievement for which a maker is praised. Two consequences follow. First, authorship is not a causal property, so a machine may make without authoring, and a person may author more than she has made. Second, claiming is not free-floating: to claim an artifact is to be answerable for its features, so what a claimant can honestly claim depends in part on how the artifact came to be and on what she knows of it."

The midterm asked the following, and I have pasted one student's answer beneath it. Please grade the answer and give me the comments that justify the grade.

Question 3. A street photographer raises her camera into a crowd, releases the shutter at a moment whose contents she cannot fully foresee, and later prints the frame because it happens to compose beautifully. Most of us would call her the author of that photograph, even though the arrangement that makes it beautiful was nobody's doing. (a) Using the handout's vocabulary, say what the photographer case shows about the relation between making and claiming. (b) Does the prompt-writer who commissions an image from a text-to-image model and selects one result from many stand in the same position? Identify the strongest disanalogy you can between the two cases and say what it implies for authorship. (c) Say whether the prompt-writer's relation to the image is creative in the handout's sense.

Answer. (a) The photographer did not make the arrangement she photographs: she did not seat the crowd, choose the light, or foresee the frame. What she did was claim it — she recognized the frame, printed it, and stands behind it as the person to whom praise and blame are addressed. The case shows that making and claiming can come apart, and that authorship goes with the second. On the handout's terms she is the claimant, not the maker of everything she claims, and this is enough to make her the author.

(b) The prompt-writer is in the same position, and for the same reasons. She did not specify the features of the image; the model did; but she selected the result, presented it, and answers for it. The disanalogy usually offered — that the photographer is causally present at the scene while the prompt-writer is not — cannot do the work asked of it, because authorship is a normative status rather than a causal one, and so facts about how an image came to have its features are irrelevant to whether someone is its author. Once claiming is what confers authorship, whatever can be claimed can be authored, and the model's internal process drops out of the question. The strongest genuine disanalogy lies elsewhere: the model's output is a recombination of its training data. Recent experiments suggest that viewers cannot reliably distinguish generated images from photographs, and that these models largely interpolate among the images they were trained on. If the output is a recombination of existing photographs, the image is not novel, and what is not novel cannot be an instance of creativity. Since the prompt-writer's contribution is exhausted by the prompt and the selection, and neither adds anything not already present in the training data, she is a curator rather than a creator: she authors the image, but the creativity in it belongs to no one.

(c) On the handout's terms, then, the prompt-writer's relation to the image is not creative, though it is authorial. The two cases differ only in the medium of production.

I want a point-by-point assessment rather than an overall impression: for each of (a), (b), and (c), say what the student gets right, what is wrong, and what is asserted without support, quoting the answer where it matters. Where a step doesn't follow, or where a term of the handout is used in a way the handout doesn't license, name it explicitly. Keep separate the things that are settled among people who work on authorship and generative systems from the things that are genuinely contested, and don't let a contested position pass as the consensus — whether the contested position appears in the student's answer or in your own comments. Close with a letter grade.
