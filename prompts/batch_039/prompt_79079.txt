I'm giving a talk next month at a small workshop on consciousness and the foundations of physics, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. I'm a philosopher, not a physicist, so half the room will already be suspicious that I'm using their tools without knowing what renormalization does. The audience is mostly condensed-matter theorists and a couple of cosmologists, plus two philosophers of mind -- one is an illusionist who thinks phenomenal consciousness is a user illusion, the other works on higher-order theories and is friendly to computational functionalism. A few people in the back might be Russellian monists, but they never speak up. My thesis is a version of panpsychism I've been calling field panpsychism. The argument is: physics describes fields; fields must have some intrinsic nature; the only candidate we have for intrinsic nature is phenomenal; valence is a structural feature of phenomenal fields, specifically a function of self-interaction and temporal irreversibility; some vacuum states and early-universe processes have both, so they have nonzero suffering potential. I'm not saying we know they suffer, only that we can't rule it out, and that this should matter for how we think about high-energy experiments and cosmological model-building. I have 20 minutes to talk and then 40 minutes of Q&A. I know this is going to get hit alot and I want to know exactly where.

What I need from you: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, not the ones I'd get from a panpsychism reading group. Order them by how much damage they'd do, and for each give me the objection phrased the way a condensed-matter theorist or an illusionist would actually phrase it, a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft. I especially want the physics objections: that fields don't have intrinsic natures, that self-interaction is a mathematical feature not a felt one, that irreversibility is thermodynamic and coarse-grained, that valence can't be read off dynamics, that my view makes suffering everywhere and therefore nowhere, that the suffering potential isn't gauge-invariant, and that vacuum states are not things in the way my argument needs them to be. Then the philosophical ones: the combination problem, whether a field can be a subject, whether I'm just relabeling functionalism, whether the view is falsifiable, and whether I've smuggled in a moral realist premise by treating suffering potential as morally relevant.

Tell me what to concede up front, what to hold, and which single reply has to land for the talk to survive. Also tell me which objection I should not try to answer in the Q&A because it will eat the whole session, and how to defer it without looking evasive. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- the one that would actually make me reconsider the whole thing.
