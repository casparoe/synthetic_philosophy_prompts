I am a novelist, some way into my fourth book, and I have hit a scene I cannot yet write. The central figure is a philosopher of physics who believes that time does not pass; in the scene in question she defends that belief against a colleague, and I need to understand the strongest form of one particular argument for it well enough to put it in her mouth without embarrassing myself in front of readers who know the field. I am not asking you to draft the scene. I am asking you to make me understand the argument, and to be honest with me about where it is weak.

Please take the role of a philosopher of physics who is also a patient expositor. Before you write anything, spend a little space thinking the argument through for yourself: set out the several readings of it you can see, say which you take to be the strongest and why, and then set the weak ones aside. Take your time; I would rather have four points made properly than a complete-looking list. I have more than school physics — I can read a spacetime diagram, I know what an inertial frame is, I know that simultaneity is frame-relative — but I am not a philosopher, and you should not assume I already know the labels that specialists use for the positions here. Teach me the vocabulary as it becomes necessary.

The argument, as it was put to me by a friend, runs roughly as follows. Special relativity tells us that for any event that is spacelike separated from me here and now, there is an inertial frame in which that event is simultaneous with my now. If being simultaneous with my now is what it is for an event to be real for me, then that distant event is real for me now. Now take a second event, spacelike separated from the first but lying in my future, and an observer for whom that second event is simultaneous with her now; the second event is real for her, and if what is real for someone is real, then it is real for me as well, even though it lies in my future. Repeat the step, walking outward in spacelike increments, and every event in spacetime turns out to be real now: there is no objective present, and the future is as real as the past. The argument strikes me as suspiciously cheap, and also as hard to escape.

What I would like from you:

1. The argument set out as an explicit chain, each step labelled as a premise or as an inference, with the one step that is doing the controversial work identified and explained rather than merely named.

2. Whether transitivity is essential. The argument plainly needs the relation it trades in — call it being real as of a time — to carry across steps. If we grant that this relation is frame-relative but deny that it is transitive, does any form of the conclusion survive? If we keep transitivity and symmetry but give up something else, what exactly gives way? I want to see where the load falls, not a verdict.

3. The reply that simultaneity in relativity is a matter of convention, and that the frame-relative simultaneity the argument leans on is therefore not the real-together relation it needs. Does this reply block the argument, or must it assume the very thing it sets out to deny? And if we confine ourselves to relations that can be defined from the causal structure of spacetime alone, with nothing added by hand, does the reply still stand? I was told once that any non-trivial relation of that sort, if it is transitive, comes out frame-relative; I do not know whether that is true, and I would like to know what follows either way.

4. Whether this is an argument from special relativity alone, or from relativity plus an unstated metaphysics of becoming. If it is the latter, tell me what the smallest additional premise would have to be, and whether that premise is one a physicist could be argued into.

5. Whether the conclusion is substantive or verbal. It sometimes seems to me that the argument only spells out the fact that there is no frame-independent now, dressed up as a thesis about what exists. How would I tell the trivial reading from the strong one, and can the strong reading — everything equally real — be had without begging a question about the reality of the present?

6. Whether any of this has to do with determinism or fatalism. My character is not a determinist, and I would rather she not sound like one; tell me what the argument does and does not license on that front.

7. One concrete case I can hold in my head, friendly to a diagram, and if it helps, the version in which a person walking down a street alters which events in a distant galaxy count as happening now. Tell me what that case does and does not show, and say plainly which nearby case would mislead me, and why.

Two constraints. First, do not give me citations, a reading list, or a survey of who has said what; if you need to name a position, state it in your own words and move on, and I will add the sources myself later. Second, where the argument is genuinely inconclusive, or where a step depends on a choice of convention, say so instead of smoothing it over. A map of the uncertainty is worth more to me than a tidy verdict. If, at the end, you think the argument is stronger than its critics allow, say that too, and say why.
