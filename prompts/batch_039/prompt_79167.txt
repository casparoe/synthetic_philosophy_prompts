My friend and I argued for four hours last night about multipolar traps — races to the bottom, the thing people call Moloch — and I'd like to see what a genuine adversarial collaboration between our two positions would produce, since we kept talking past each other. Call us the trap camp and the rivalry camp. The trap camp holds that when agents compete in a commons without a binding coordinating authority, selection pushes them toward outcomes none of them want, that the signature of a trap is an outcome stable both against unilateral defection and against everyone's preferences, and that real remedies operate on the structure of the game — enforceable coordination, a monopoly over some decisions, exit that is actually available. The rivalry camp holds that the same competitive pressure is a discovery procedure that surfaces things no planner could specify, that many alleged traps are mispriced externalities or races to the top in disguise, and that the remedy is better-defined rights and more entry, not less competition.

Draft the joint statement we could both sign. Include: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims about the mechanism, the scope of the problem, and what would and wouldn't count as a trap; (2) a disagreements table giving, for each live dispute, the question at issue, each side's formulation of its own view in wording that side would accept, the crux, the case or argument both sides agree bears on that crux, and each side's prediction about it in advance; and (3) a note on which apparent disagreements are merely verbal — in particular whether 'coordination' means only centralized enforcement or also decentralized mechanisms like norms, standards bodies, and reputation. I suspect one crux is whether a fix that works by centralizing a decision is itself a trap, since it buys coordination at the cost of putting the ecology's selection pressure on whoever holds the lever; if that's right, treat it as a crux rather than a sidebar, and where a dispute can't be settled by any evidence either of us could imagine, say so and label it a weighting difference rather than a factual one.

Invent the test cases yourself, small and concrete — a norm that degrades because no individual can afford to be the one who stops complying — and don't cite literature or offer reading lists. Answer in American English.
