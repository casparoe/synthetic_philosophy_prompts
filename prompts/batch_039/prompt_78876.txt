Last night a friend and I had a long, slightly drunken arguement about whether you can bring interest rates down simply by making money more plentiful — his line was basically "print more, rates fall," and I said that's the sort of thing the older writers already took apart, and then when he pressed me on why, I realized I couldn't actually reconstruct the arguement, only wave in its general direction. So I went back to Hume's essay on interest, which I'm fairly sure is the one where he opens by saying that lowness of interest is usually put down to plenty of money but that he reckons the real cause is something else, and there's a great bit where he asks whether, if all the gold in England were annihilated at once and twenty-one shillings substituted for every guinea, money would be more plentiful or interest lower. That bit I think I get — nothing real has changed, you've just swapped yellow coins for white ones. But then I hit this and I've read it about five times and it still won't go in:

"High interest arises from three circumstances: A great demand for borrowing; little riches to supply that demand; and great profits arising from commerce: And these circumstances are a clear proof of the small advance of commerce and industry, not of the scarcity of gold and silver. Low interest, on the other hand, proceeds from the three opposite circumstances: A small demand for borrowing; great riches to supply that demand; and small profits arising from commerce: And these circumstances are all connected together, and proceed from the encrease of industry and commerce, not of gold and silver."

What I can't make work is the middle one, "great profits arising from commerce." It's listed as one of the three things that push interest up, and then he says all three are "a clear proof of the small advance of commerce and industry." But if commerce is throwing off great profits, isn't that a sign commerce is doing well?? My friend pounced on exactly this — he says it shows the whole thing is muddled, that high profits obviously mean a booming economy, and that at least the money story is simple. I'd definately like to be able to answer him, but right now I can't even lay out what Hume is claiming. Can you walk me through it — what he actually means by "great profits arising from commerce," why he thinks that goes together with "small advance" rather than against it, how it's supposed to lock in with the "great demand for borrowing" and "little riches to supply that demand" parts, and why on his view the quantity of money drops out of the picture entirely? And there's one more thing that nags me: in the same essay he seems to concede that adding money "may have some influence, by exciting industry" while prices are still catching up, before it goes neutral. Doesn't that hand my friend back exactly the short-run lever he wants, or is Hume entitled to that concession without giving up his main claim?
