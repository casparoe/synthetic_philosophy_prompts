At a reading group last term, two friends of mine, Dana and Miguel, spent most of an evening arguing about the objection to utilitarianism that both of them attribute to Bernard Williams — the drowning-wife example, and the further charge that utilitarianism cannot accommodate a person's integrity or their ground projects. I stayed out of it, partly because I lost track of what exactly they were disagreeing about. I have set out their exchange below as faithfully as I can. I would like you to referee it move by move rather than simply declare a winner. Do not quote or paraphrase any text as though you had it in front of you; work from the arguments as my friends state them.

Dana (1): The example is decisive against utilitarianism as an account of how a person should think. The man whose wife is drowning does not need, and should not have, the extra thought that saving her is morally permitted here, or that it is the impartially best available option. He saves her because she is his wife. A theory that requires the extra thought has alienated him from his own attachment, because it makes her claim on him conditional on a permission issued from a standpoint that has already set her aside as one person among others.

Miguel (2): That is not an objection to utilitarianism; it is an objection to the very idea that an agent should be able to say why what he did was right. The Kantian has the same extra thought — that saving her is what duty requires — and the virtue theorist will say that saving her is what a person of good character does. If any reflective endorsement counts as alienation, the objection indicts every moral theory, and the reflective agent, rather than utilitarianism. And notice what the utilitarian actually holds: the criterion of rightness concerns outcomes, not what passes through the agent's head. The husband may think only that she is his wife, and act rightly. A utilitarian who understood psychology would positively want him to have that disposition, since people who run the impartial calculation about their own families are worse at loving them, and the world is worse for it.

Dana (3): You are flattening the extra thoughts. The Kantian's extra thought is about what she is owed; it is a thought from inside the relationship, about her. The utilitarian's extra thought is a thought from outside it, about the total. That is why a permission has to be issued at all: from the impartial standpoint, her being his wife carries no weight until that standpoint licenses partiality as a good-producing device. So the complaint is not that there is an extra thought; it is what the extra thought is about, and what that reveals about where the theory locates the reasons. And this is the same complaint as the integrity one. What makes the theory uninhabitable is that the final authority on what he may do is a standpoint he cannot occupy as the person he is.

Miguel (4): Then drop the deliberation version entirely and say the objection is to the criterion — that utilitarianism gives the wrong answer about which reasons there are. But the example does not establish that; it establishes that a certain way of deliberating is unattractive. If the criterion is right, then in the drowning case it will license saving her, and the husband's unreflective rush to the water is exactly what a good utilitarian agent does. You are treating a claim about the theory's decision procedure as though it were a claim about its account of rightness, and these come apart. The integrity objection is a separate argument and needs a separate case: it needs someone whose life's work the theory can demand that they abandon. The drowning case is not that. He is not being asked to give up his life; he is being asked to justify an attachment.

Dana (5): Two replies. First, self-effacement is not a rescue. If the theory has to tell the agent not to consult it, that is a fact about the theory that stands in need of defense, not a way of making the objection go away — and the defense has to be conducted from the impartial standpoint, which is precisely the standpoint whose authority the objection denies. Second, the two objections do support each other, because in both cases what is at issue is whether the theory can be the agent's own point of view. Integrity is not only about large projects; it is about the relation between the agent and the source of the demands he acts on. If the ultimate source is an impartial calculation he cannot identify with, then even the case in which it happens to tell him to save her leaves him standing outside his own action.

Miguel (6): Now you are making the integrity objection do all the work and the extra-thought complaint do none. They come apart in both directions. You can have the extra thought with no threat to any ground project — the drowning case, on your own telling, concerns an attachment, not a life's work. And you can have the threat with no extra thought: a utilitarian can tell someone to go on doing what they were doing, and the theory will still be able to demand that they stop, and that possibility is what the integrity objection is about. So each objection needs its own case, and each has to survive an obvious reply: to the first, that the theory is a criterion and not a script; to the second, that a theory's being able to demand something is not yet a reason to think the demand would be wrong. I also do not think you have decided whether Williams is saying that the utilitarian gets the answer wrong or that the utilitarian cannot describe the situation correctly. Those are different charges, and one might be true while the other is false.
