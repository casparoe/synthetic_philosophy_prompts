I'm a philosophy professor at a mid-sized department, and I keep getting dragged into the same fight about the is-ought gap. A colleague of mine — a philosopher of biology, very empirically minded — uses 'you can't get an ought from an is' as a conversation-stopper. Last week a grad student in our metaethics reading group presented a paper arguing that facts about what participants in a practice accept can generate oughts that are internal to that practice: if the rules of chess say the bishop moves diagonally, then the fact that we're playing chess plus the fact that this piece is a bishop seems to yield a claim about how you ought to move it, and likewise for the norms internal to a proof system or a legal code. My colleague's response was basically, 'That's just changing the subject; you still can't get an ought from an is.' He also uses 'that's the naturalistic fallacy' as if it were the same objection, and I suspect it isn't. I want to write a handout for the seminar that lays out the different precise claims the slogan could be making, from the trivial to the radical, and says which of them would actually support his dismissal of the student's project. I've been thinking alot about this and I'm less interested in the history of the slogan than in the conceptual geography; don't send me citations or a reading list, because I already know the literature and I want the map.

The key thing I need separated is this: what exactly is the 'is' and what exactly is the 'ought' in the slogan? Is it about logical derivability from non-deontic premises? About the semantics of normative predicates? About a missing bridge principle? About the impossibility of reducing normative facts to natural facts? About the fact/value distinction as a claim about motivation or about reasons? And where does the naturalistic fallacy come in — is it a claim about definability, about inference, about the open-question argument, or something else? I also want to know which readings are compatible with the student's practice-internal oughts and which readings would rule them out. Some of these readings seem like platitudes that no one denies, some seem like substantive metaethical theses, and at least one or two might be false. I want the menu, with verdicts, not a single favored interpretation.

Give it to me in five sections, in this order. 1) 'The menu' (~600 words): six to eight numbered readings of the slogan, each stated as a precise thesis, with a one-line verdict on whether it's trivial, substantive, or false. 2) 'What each reading would license' (~400 words): for each reading, say whether it blocks the inference from facts about what we evolved to want to claims about what we ought to do, whether it blocks the practice-internal ought, and whether it gives my colleague the conclusion he wants. 3) 'The naturalistic-fallacy detour' (~250 words): separate the claims hidden in that label and say which, if any, are just the is-ought claims under another name. 4) 'Which reading is doing the work' (~200 words): your best guess at what my colleague probably means when he says it, and what he'd have to accept if he meant the stronger version. 5) 'Confidence and the soft spot' (~150 words): say how confident you are in the main verdicts, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
