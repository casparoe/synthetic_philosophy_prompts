I am the compliance officer at a mid-sized medical device manufacturer that sells in eight countries. Several times a week I have to rule on gifts, meals, travel and hospitality: things our sales staff want to give to customers and hospital purchasing staff, and things they want to accept from suppliers. The range runs from a sandwich to a week at a resort, and getting it wrong can mean criminal liability for the company and, in some places, for the individual whose initials are on the form. I have about five minutes per request, no legal training beyond our own policy, and no authority to give case-by-case legal advice. Anything above the written threshold goes to the general counsel, who also has to approve the wording of the policy itself, and an external auditor will eventually read that policy and ask why the numbers in it are the numbers they are. I would also like a first-line version I can hand to two junior staff who will field most of the questions before they reach me.

I have been reading about vagueness, and I think I finally understand why every draft of the policy I write comes out either uselessly vague or dishonestly precise. The difficulty is not that I don't know the law. It is that the predicate at the centre of the whole exercise — bribe, or inducement, or improper benefit, whatever word the local statute uses — seems to have no sharp boundary, and I am being asked to write a sharp boundary into a document and then apply it. A cup of coffee is not a bribe. A briefcase of cash is a bribe. Take the briefcase and remove one dollar, then another: I cannot say where the change happens, and no one I ask can either. My drafts have done one of two things: a numeric cap that is plainly arbitrary at the margin, or a list of factors followed by a note that each case must be judged on its merits, which is exactly what I had before I wrote anything. I have seen four ways of talking about this and I don't know which one I am implicitly relying on: that there really is a line and we simply cannot find it; that there is no line at all and the predicate is neither true nor false of the borderline cases; that the line exists but moves with the context of the person applying it; or that the predicate comes in degrees and nothing needs a line. I suspect the choice matters for what I write, and I want to know whether it matters in practice or only in how I justify myself afterwards.

What I need is a procedure, not a discussion. Specifically:

1. A decision procedure in words — a flowchart in order — that I can run on a single request in a few minutes. For each question: what counts as a sufficient answer, what I do when the answer is unclear, and where the procedure stops. Distinguish the stopping points clearly: a clean no; a clean yes; a no I will have to defend to an annoyed sales director; and a hand-off to a person. For each hand-off, say whether it is a hand-off because the question is one of values rather than facts, or because the procedure has run out of information I can get in five minutes.

2. A method for setting the numbers and categories in the written policy itself, not merely for applying them. This is where I need the philosophy rather than the law. If the predicate has no sharp boundary, what is the status of a rule saying that nothing above X may be accepted? Is it a discovery of where the line always was, a stipulation, or something in between — and does the answer change what the policy's preamble should say the rule is for? State plainly which of the four positions above the procedure presupposes, and whether a procedure built on a different one would behave differently in practice or only in its rhetoric.

3. Two failures I expect from any single threshold, and what to do about them. First, the continuum does not run only along the size of one gift: there is a second one, the accumulation of many small things from the same source over years, and a cap on individual gifts does nothing about it. Second, the boundary appears to move with context — the same bottle of whisky means something different from a purchasing clerk, from a ministry official, and from a customer who has bought from us for a decade; and something different again while we are bidding for a contract than after we have held it. I want the procedure to handle both without collapsing into 'it depends'. I am also aware that the offence is not purely a matter of degree: there is an intent element that looks all-or-nothing, and I do not want a numeric cap to swallow it.

4. A rule for the middle. Every draft I have written has an obvious gap between clearly fine and clearly not fine, and the gap is where all the real requests live. I do not want that gap filled with 'use your judgment'. I want a default, a reason for the default, a rule for when it can be overridden, and a specific provision for the case where the person asking me is my own manager.

Then run the procedure on the following, in this order, showing your work at each step:

(a) A supplier offers one of our sales representatives two tickets to a sold-out concert, face value 180 each, three weeks before that supplier's contract comes up for renewal. The representative does not vote on the renewal but is the person the supplier deals with day to day.

(b) A hospital department head with whom we have no contract invites our regional manager to a weekend at a golf resort, roughly 2,500 all in, described as relationship building, while we are preparing a bid.

(c) A customer who has bought from us for fifteen years sends a box of chocolates, about 25, to his regular representative at the new year. The policy as drafted says staff may not accept anything of value from a customer. The representative will be upset, every competitor does this, and I have already told two other representatives to decline similar boxes this month.

I expect the procedure to give me an answer I do not like at least once, and I would rather find that out now than in an audit. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which steps are judgments dressed up as procedure, where it will misfire, and what I should stop expecting a written policy to settle. I do not want a survey of the literature and I do not want citations; I want the thing I can use on Monday morning, together with an honest account of what it is.
