I'm a first-year PhD student who has somehow been handed the Liar session at our logic and metaphysics reading group, and the format I've been asked to use is a one-page decision tree the others fill in as we go, rather than a list of names on a slide. Last year the discussion died the usual death: everybody could say gaps and gluts, and nobody could say what they had bought into by saying it. Two friends and I have now spent a month arguing about whether the liar is best blamed on the truth schema or on self-reference, which is exactly the kind of disagreement I want the tree to settle by forcing us to answer the same questions in the same order. So please build me that tree.

The starting point should be a commitment a reader can recognise in themselves rather than a label, something like: I accept the ordinary truth schema, that a sentence is true just in case things are as it says they are, and I take the liar sentence to be a genuine problem for it. From there, every node should be a question about the phenomenon rather than about a school, and no node should be answerable by naming one. Among the distinctions I need the tree to draw: whether the paradox is a symptom of a defective premise to be located and given up, or a sound piece of reasoning whose conclusion we must learn to accept; whether the culprit is self-reference or circularity, the schema itself, the logic, or the assumption that the truth predicate applies uniformly across contexts; whether the repair leaves classical logic intact, revises it, or gives up a structural rule that lets the argument run at all; whether the truth schema is kept for every sentence, restricted to a fragment, or held to be correct but inexpressible in the very language it governs; whether the theory can state its own verdict on the liar in its own words without a revenge sentence immediately outflanking it; and whether ungrounded sentences come out valueless, come out both ways, or come out ordinary because truth was never a substantive property in the first place. I want at least these leaves, labelled with whatever standard names fit: the hierarchy that refuses a language its own truth predicate; the fixed-point approach that lets truth be partial; the paracomplete approach that keeps gaps but adds a conditional able to survive them; the paraconsistent line that lets some contradictions be true; the revision-theoretic line that treats truth as a circular concept needing no fixed point; the contextualist line on which the liar shifts the context and the paradox is equivocation; the deflationary line that restricts the schema because truth is only a device of generalisation; the substructural line that keeps both classical values and the schema but sacrifices a structural rule; and, as a leaf I suspect is thinly populated, the view that the paradox shows truth to be an incoherent notion to be dropped. Each named leaf should carry one sentence on the commitment its defenders find hardest to live with; where a leaf has no defender, leave it unnamed and say in a clause whether you think it is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexamined.

Several cases have to be worked through at the nodes, and I care more about these than about the labels: the truth-teller, which has no more grounding than the liar and yet nobody wants to call it paradoxical; an infinite descending chain of sentences each saying that the next is not true, in which no sentence refers to itself; a sentence that only becomes a liar because of how the world happens to be, so that blaming self-reference looks hopeless; the Curry sentence, which seems to reach any conclusion at all from the schema without using negation; and the revenge sentence that says of the theory's own verdict that it is not true in the sense the theory allows. Please also tell me which pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate — I expect, for instance, that a gap theory and a glut theory can answer every question I have listed identically and come apart only on some further question you will have to supply — and mark them as such rather than forcing a distinction. Aim for eight to ten leaves and no more than seven or eight branch nodes, so that it fits on one side of A4; the audience is graduate students who know first-order logic and have read nothing on truth, so keep the nodes free of jargon and assume they cannot tell where a question is heading. Don't bother with references or with attributing views to particular authors: if a standard name for a position helps, use it, but the tree must stand up even where the attributions are wrong.
