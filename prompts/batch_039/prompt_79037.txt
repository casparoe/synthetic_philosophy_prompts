A foundation that supports work in formal epistemology has asked me to allocate a small budget: four full-time researchers, three years, no empirical component, on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. I need to hand them a prioritized agenda rather than a survey, and I would like your help constructing it.

What I want is a defended ordering of the open questions in this area, ranked by the expected value of working on them. Treat that value as a product of at least three things, and be explicit about how you weight them: how many other questions turn on the answer (upstreamness); how tractable the question is with the tools a philosopher or logician already has; and how little attention it has actually received relative to its importance. For each item, say why it is upstream of the others, what the most promising routes to progress look like, and what would move it up or down the list over the next two or three years. Where a foundational question is unlikely to be settled and a narrower one could plausibly be closed out, say which you would fund and why. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the reasoning.

Three readings of the two paradoxes are commonly floated as ways of reorganizing the field, and I want a verdict on each as part of your answer, including whether any of them should displace the ranking you would otherwise give: (i) that both paradoxes are artifacts of trying to locate a single numerical threshold at which credence becomes outright belief, so the agenda should be about what, if anything, replaces the threshold picture; (ii) that they show only that a rational agent's beliefs need not be stored in one logically closed set, so the agenda should be about the structure of belief fragments and the conditions under which they may be jointly consulted; (iii) that they dissolve once belief is treated as a phase of inquiry that terminates rather than a state an agent is in, so the agenda should be about stopping rules and the norms of settling a question.

Your ranking should also say what it does with the following case, which I want the agenda to accommodate. A regulation requires that a filed ledger be both deductively closed and consistent: anything that follows from what is filed must also be filed, and nothing filed may contradict anything else filed. A bookkeeper must file a ledger of a thousand entries. For each entry, her evidence makes it overwhelmingly likely, though not certain, that the recorded amount is correct; and from long experience she knows that a ledger of this size almost always contains at least one error, though she has no idea which entry is wrong. She must either file, for each entry, the claim that its amount is correct, or omit that claim. Is any rational filing available? If not, does the fault lie with the regulation, with her evidence, or with the assumption that what she files is what she believes? Say how the questions you ranked highest bear on this case, and, conversely, whether the case should change the ranking.
