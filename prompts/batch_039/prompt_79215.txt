In my history seminar we spent a week on the origins of the Cold War, and the discussion split exactly as I gather the professional literature has split for decades. One side held that the United States bore the primary responsibility: the delay in opening a second front, the atomic bomb, the Marshall Plan as an instrument of influence. The other held that Soviet conduct was decisive: the imposition of communist governments across Eastern Europe and the pressure on Turkey and Iran. What struck me is that both sides cite the same handful of events and neither budges; the same evidence is consumed over and over.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My hypothesis is that the two camps are not answering one question but two, and that the phrase does the conflating: one sense of starting is causal (what set the confrontation in motion) and another is normative (who is answerable for it), and perhaps the causal question is largely settled while the normative one is not. A second possibility is that the impasse reflects a deeper disagreement about what a historical explanation should even look like — the deliberate decisions of statesmen versus impersonal forces such as ideology, economics, and geography. A third is that the question is ill posed: the Cold War may be a label historians apply retrospectively to a long rivalry, so asking who started it may resemble asking who started the Renaissance. Which of these, or which combination, best describes the disagreement, and why?

Finally, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a reformulated question, evidence neither side has used, or nothing at all — if the impasse is simply what happens when historians bring incompatible political and moral commitments to the same facts, say so and explain why.

Answer in 400 words or fewer, and put the diagnosis itself in the first two sentences.
