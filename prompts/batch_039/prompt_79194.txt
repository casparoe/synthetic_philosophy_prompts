I retired a couple of years ago from a career in population biology — thirty-odd years of life-history work, mostly reproductive strategies in insects and fish — and since then I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar in the philosophy department at the local university as a non-degree student, which has been equal parts humbling and wonderful. This term it's population ethics, and I have to write a term paper (6,000 words, one semester, due at the end), and I want to spend it on the procreation asymmetry — the Benatar-ish claim that it's wrong to bring a miserable person into existence but not required to bring a happy one. What nags at me is that everyone states this as though a birth is a countable event that brings a determinate new individual into being, and my entire working life was spent on organisms for which that's just false: clonal reproduction, budding, fragmentation in marine invertebrates, parthenogenesis, monozygotic twinning, chimeras, colonial organisms where you genuinly cannot say whether you're looking at one organism or many. And alot of the boundary cases aren't exotic curiosities, they're the bread and butter of my old literature. So the question I want the paper to answer: does the asymmetry survive once you take the indeterminacy of biological individuality seriously, or is it actually a thesis about something else — about the direction of concern, say, or about an asymmetry of needs, since coming into existence creates needs that can be frustrated whereas not existing leaves no needs to satisfy — rather than about the ethics of creating particular people? My old co-author thinks I'm overthinking it: she says an organism is whatever biologists find it useful to count as one, a bookkeeping matter with no ethical upshot. I don't buy it, because the asymmetry seems to need the counting to be a fact about the world rather than a convention, but I can't yet say why.

What I want from you is a proposal for the paper, not the paper itself. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, then explain why the positions people actually hold in this debate leave it open — I know the standard machinery (the person-affecting restriction, total versus average views, the thought that non-existence can't be a harm because there's nobody there to be harmed), so don't just list it back to me; I need to see why none of it settles my question. Then the method: what I'd actually do across the semester, in order, week by week if that's useful — which cases to work through and in what sequence so the biological material is doing real argumentative work rather than being colorful, which objections to my framing I should try to answer and which I should simply concede. Tell me what a result looks like (the thesis the paper would end up defending, in a sentence or two) and what would count as the project failing; I'd rather learn that now than in month three.

No reading list or citations, please — I can find the literature myself and I'll add the references at the end, I just don't want a bibliography standing in for an argument. Same for the metaphysics-versus-convention point — take a side on whether my co-author is right. Scope the thing honestly for one semester of part-time work by somebody who reads philosophy slowly and writes it even slower.
