So I've been going down a rabbit hole on Portugal's drug setup and there's one bit I cant stop chewing on. Personal possession of basically any drug isn't a crime there — it's an administrative offence. If the police catch you with under roughly ten days' worth, they confiscate it and refer you to a dissuasion commission. Which, as I understand it, is a small panel of about three people, usually mixing legal and clinical expertise — a lawyer plus a doctor or psychologist type, that sort of thing. It isn't a court. The toolbox seems to include fines, suspension of the right to practise a licensed profession, bans on frequenting certain places, orders to report in periodically, and it can also just suspend the whole process if you agree to go into treatment. Repeat appearances seem to escalate things, and I think failing to turn up at all is itself treated as an offence.

Here's what I want from you. Reconstruct the philosophical commitments this institution presupposes. Not the ones its architects announced — the ones baked into the machinery, the ones you'd have to accept for the thing to hang together as a coherent practice.

Some threads I keep pulling on:

It's still formally illegal to possess drugs there. Use remains an offence, just an administrative one. So the practice seems to presuppose that you can keep the legal status of an act while aiming the state's actual response at the person's health. Is that stable? What view of what law is *for* does it need?

The name is dissuasion, which is a deterrence word, but the sanctions are things like treatment referral, fines and professional-licence suspensions — levers aimed at pushing someone into care. So it looks like it presupposes a forward-looking, consequentialist theory of sanctioning. But specifically it's using the sanction to deter *the person being sanctioned*, for that same person's own good. That's an odd beast: coercion in service of the coerced person's welfare. What theory of paternalism, and what theory of the state's standing to intervene where no third party is harmed, does that require?

And the treatment offer — you can have proceedings suspended if you go into treatment. That only works if the alternative is bad enough to move you. Which means the practice presupposes that consent extracted under threat of sanction is good enough consent for medical treatment. Does it? Or does it presuppose something weaker — that we don't need consent for treatment, only non-refusal? What's actually doing the moral work there?

Then there's the addiction angle. The whole thing seems to turn on a distinction between the addicted user and the recreational one, with different responses for each. That presupposes addiction is a distinct normative category — that it changes what you're responsible for, or how much your choices count as choices. Is that a compatibilist move (the addict still chooses, just under bad conditions) or does it need something stronger (the addict isn't really choosing)? And if it's the former, why does the state get to be so much more intrusive here than with, say, someone eating themselves into heart disease?

Finally, the ten-day supply threshold. That's a quantity standing in for an intention. What does that presuppose about how we know what someone's up to, and about whether use and supply are really separable kinds of act?

Tell me which of these the practice *strictly* needs, which it merely fits nicely with, and where it's underdetermined — where two or three different theories would all license the same commission. Then tell me what would have to change in the institution if we swapped out whichever presupposition you think is doing the most load-bearing work.

British English please. And dont be shy about calling the thing incoherent if you think it is — I'd rather have a real verdict than a both-sides shrug.
