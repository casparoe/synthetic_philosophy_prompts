I teach a paradoxes seminar for upper-level undergrads, 14 of them, one 75-minute block, and next week I want to do the two-envelope problem as something they play rather than a puzzle I explain from the front. What I'm after is the moment where the swap argument feels obviously right while you're actually holding the envelope, and then the moment it collapses when you notice that the person holding the other envelope can run exactly the same argument back at you. I want them to feel that collapse in their hands, not hear me describe it.

Here's what I've been kicking around, though throw it out if youve got something better. Each pair gets two sealed envelopes, one holding twice what the other holds, and nobody opens anything until the very end. I act as a broker who will swap their envelopes for a small fee, and who pitches the swap the obvious way: whatever's in your hand, the other envelope holds either double or half, you've got no reason to think one is likelier than the other, so the other one is worth 1.25 times yours on average, and the fee is nothing next to that. I want the fee to do the real work here -- someone who buys the argument will keep trading and keep bleeding, which lands harder than being told the argument is bad. I'm also tempted to require that a swap only happen if both partners independently agree to pay for it, since then they can both talk themselves into it at the same instant, which should be impossible if the reasoning were sound. Its the symmetry I want them to bump into.

So design the whole thing. Rules, what goes in the envelopes, what the stakes are (play money only, and I want the amounts set so the double-or-half arithmetic stays easy in their heads), the size of the fee, how the broker phrases the pitch without tipping the answer, a timeline for the 75 minutes including setup, and something workable for tracking who's swapping. I'd also like a second phase where I reveal the distribution I actually drew the smaller amount from and have them redo the calculation knowing it, because I want them to see that a flat "it's fifty-fifty either way" can't survive contact with a real prior -- and I want them to find the boundary cases themselves, the highest and lowest amounts where the fifty-fifty assumption visibly gives out. Then a debrief that walks back through the game and lines up what happened with the actual structure of the puzzle: where the expected-value reasoning smuggles in an assumption, why the symmetry between the two players settles it, and what precisely goes wrong with the claim that the other envelope is equally likely to be double or half no matter what you see.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm worried about pairs who refuse to trade on principle and never feel the pull, students who've seen the puzzle before and blurt the answer, and the awkward chance that the fee makes them suspicious of me instead of suspicious of the argument -- but I'd rather hear what you think the real failure modes are and how to handle them mid-session. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce, the part I'll have to just tell them because no arrangement of envelopes will show it.

Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables -- just paragraphs I can read straight through like an email. American English please.
