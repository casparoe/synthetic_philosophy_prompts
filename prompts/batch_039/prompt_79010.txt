The rule-following argument that comes from Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein has been contested for decades without converging, and what I want is a diagnosis rather than another attempted solution. Some readers take it as a reductio of every attempt to explain meaning in non-semantic terms — dispositions, dispositions plus idealization, primitive facts about what a speaker means; others take it as a genuine skeptical paradox whose only honest response is a non-factual account of meaning in terms of what we are licensed to assert, anchored in communal agreement; a third group holds that it is a misreading of Wittgenstein and never was a problem at all. Is this one disagreement or three sharing a single name? My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the parties do not differ over any fact but over what would count as an explanation of meaning at all — whether treating meaning as primitive is an admissible stopping point, whether the demand for a non-semantic reduction is legitimate, and whether a theory of meaning is answerable to that demand. I come to this as a programmer with no philosophical training, and what drew me in is that a finite test suite never determines which function a program implements; I cannot tell whether that is this same problem or merely its cousin. If the hypothesis is right, say what progress would even consist in, and whether any of the familiar moves could amount to it. If it is wrong, say where the real crux lies and what kind of disagreement this turns out to be.
