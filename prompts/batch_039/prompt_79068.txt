Two people in my reading group got into a proper tangle last week and I definately couldn't referee it. We'd been looking at pain asymbolia — the syndrome where someone feels pain, can say where it is and how strong it is, but just doesn't seem to mind it; they might even reach for the painful thing instead of pulling away. (1) J said this shows the badness of pain can't be intrinsic to the pain sensation: the sensation is there, but the badness isn't, so the badness must come from the suffering or distress that normally comes with it. (2) R said no, it shows the opposite: the patient still calls it pain, and what makes pain pain is that it presents itself as bad; what's missing is the motivation to avoid it, not the badness. (3) J said if it's bad, why don't they mind it? Badness that gives no reason to avoid it is doing no work. (4) R said the patient can still judge it's bad without being moved, like someone depressed can judge something bad without feeling it; asymbolia breaks the link between badness and motivation, not between pain and badness. R also brought up the standard distinction between the sensory-discriminative side of pain — where it is, how intense — and the affective-motivational side — how unpleasant it is, how much you want it gone. Asymbolia looks like it leaves the first intact and disrupts the second, so the badness is in the affective side. (5) J said that's just relabelling the distress as a 'dimension'. If you strip away the distress and the avoidance, what's left that's bad? The patient doesn't report suffering, they report pain. So pain is present and badness isn't. (6) R said that supports a dissociation between pain and suffering, sure, but not between pain and badness. It shows badness can come apart from motivation. And if the affective side is disrupted, you'd expect the badness to be reduced, but the patient still identifies the state as pain — so maybe 'pain' names a sensory state that's intrinsically bad but not necessarily motivating. (7) J said or maybe 'pain' names the whole experience, in which case the patient doesn't have pain, just something pain-like; or it names only the sensory component, in which case pain isn't bad. Either way the badness is in the affective response. (8) R said that's treating badness as if it must show up as distress, which is exactly what's in dispute.
