My thesis: the whole debate about advance directives is fought on the wrong axis. Everyone argues about identity -- is the demented patient the same person who signed? does psychological continuity survive? -- and then concludes that if the earlier self is "still in there" the directive binds, and if she isn't, it doesn't, or only weakly. I think the directive's authority never came from identity in the first place. A directive is an instruction issued to agents who have taken on a role. It binds the nurse, the physician, the daughter who agreed to be proxy, because those roles are constituted partly by an undertaking to follow the patient's instructions -- not because the signer and the person in the bed are the same person. Three things I want to run with: (a) the directive binds the *agents* more firmly than it binds the *later self*, so a demented patient's present wish to be fed doesn't by itself cancel it; the real question is whether the agents have been released from the undertaking, which takes something like a change in circumstances the directive itself contemplated; (b) it explains why some directives are simply unenforceable without any failure of autonomy -- a directive demanding a service nobody is in the role of providing has no addressee, which is a different diagnosis from "the earlier self's will was defeated"; (c) it dissolves the then-self vs now-self framing, because the later self isn't a rival author whose preferences get weighed against the earlier self's, she's a party affected by how the agents discharge the role, and that's a different kind of weight.

Case I want to hang it on: a woman signed a directive ten years ago refusing artificial nutrition and hydration if she ever reached severe dementia. She's now severely demented, mobile, cheerful, and eats happily when spoon-fed. Her named proxy, her daughter, wants to stop the hand-feeding per the directive. The nursing staff refuse: hand-feeding is care, not treatment, and the patient is content. My view says the question is what the daughter undertook, not whether the woman now "really" wants to eat.

The position I'm attacking, stated as strongly as I can: what grounds the directive is autonomy over a whole life -- a life has a shape, the earlier self is the one entitled to set it, and overriding the directive wrongs the person whose life it is. My reply is that this view has to explain why a past preference outranks a present one when the present one isn't the product of the past one's plan but just of the same organism decaying, and that the trusteeship picture answers that more cleanly.

What I want from you: (1) am I misrepresenting the autonomy/narrative view -- I don't want to knock down a straw man; (2) does the role-based move actually do the work, or does it just relocate the puzzle, e.g. by making a directive's authority hostage to institutional facts (no proxy ever named, no doctor yet in the role), which smells like a reductio; (3) is this interesting, or am I reinventing something everyone in the field already settled; (4) what should I cut.
