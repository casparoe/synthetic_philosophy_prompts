I just got voted onto the standards committee of my professional association (nursing, about 4,000 members in the state chapter) and I am definately in over my head. Here's the problem. Two years ago we finally passed a real ethics code after the whole mess with the previous board. Now people keep filing complaints about things members did *before* the code existed. Technically allowed back then, or at least nobody ever wrote down that it wasn't. And I keep noticing that the people pushing hardest for us to go after the old guard are a lot of the same people who were in the room while all of it was happening. The full board has to ratify whatever we recommend, and half of them would honestly rather we buried the whole thing.

I've been reading about Nuremberg and Tokyo on my own time because it feels like exactly our problem, and now I can't unsee it. The charter they used was written after the acts they were judging, which is why the defense kept yelling ex post facto; the answer was that the 1928 treaty renouncing war as an instrument of national policy had already made aggression illegal, plus the idea that some acts are criminal under general principles no matter what the local law says. The defendants tried tu quoque -- you did it too -- and got nowhere. One judge at Tokyo thought every single defendant should walk and basically called the trial the winners taking revenge; he pointed out that Allied firebombing and the atomic bomb were not on the charge sheet and that no judges from the losing side sat on the bench. Also, superior orders was not a defense, only something that could lighten a sentence, and there was a conspiracy charge that let them convict the people who planned it rather than the ones who pulled the trigger. Later international law swung back the other way and made non-retroactivity a hard rule again.

So can you turn this into a decision procedure my committee can actually run? I get maybe twenty minutes per case and I have no legal training. I need the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where it stops and says no, and where it has to stop and hand off to a committee vote because its a value call and not a fact call. Don't send me to any sources or give me a reading list, I can't look anything up -- just build the ideas into the steps.

Then run the procedure on the three complaints we're sitting on: (1) a member who harassed a junior colleague in 2019, back when our old code was silent about it and everyone knew it; (2) a complaint against a former officer for taking industry gifts that three people currently on my committee also took; (3) a complaint against a department head who designed the production quota that led to the falsified records, even though he never falsified a single one himself. And tell me honestly where the procedure spits out an answer I won't like, and what it just can't do.

American English, please.
