I teach a 200-level "evidence and inference" course that's cross-listed between philosophy and stats — 18 students, mixed majors, and I get exactly one 75-minute session to do Simpson's paradox with them. Last year I put the Berkeley admissions table on a slide and it died on contact: half of them had already seen the trick in some stats YouTube video, so they could recite "the trend reverses when you disaggregate" without feeling a thing, and when I asked which table they'd actually act on they just picked whichever one they thought I wanted. This year I want them to manufacture a reversal out of their own decisions, argue about it while it's still live, and only get the name at the very end, in the debrief, after they've already staked out positions they have to walk back. No lecture up front, no slides of someone else's data, no computers — printing and index cards only.

Here's the half-baked mechanic I sketched in a notebook, and I'd like you to build the real thing around it (fix the numbers if mine are off, but keep the shape). Everyone is a gladiator in a two-tier league: in each round you choose whether to fight a Novice or a Champion. Each gladiator has a hidden form card giving their personal win rates against each tier, and instead of coin flips you hand them a physical deck of 10 cards per matchup type, shuffled and drawn, so the rates come out exact and the arithmetic is clean on the board. A win over a Novice is 1 point, over a Champion is 3. The scoreboard tracks wins, losses, and points. My suggested cards: a strong gladiator wins 8 of 10 against Novices and 4 of 10 against Champions; a weak one wins 5 of 10 and 1 of 10. Do the expected-value arithmetic and the strong gladiator rationally hunts Champions (0.4 × 3 beats 0.8 × 1) while the weak one farms Novices (0.5 beats 0.3) — so the strong gladiator's pooled win rate comes out at 40% and the weak one's at 50%, even though the strong one beats the weak one in both tiers. I want the room to first crown a champion by win rate, then have to defend it, and then have to say what they think "better" means here and why the question they were asked wasn't the question they answered. I'd also like a second phase that reuses the exact same table under different stories: the tier is picked by the gladiator (as in the game), the tier is assigned by a matchmaker after a fitness check the gladiator can't influence, and the tier is partly a consequence of how the gladiator did in earlier rounds — same numbers, three different right answers, and nothing in the table to tell them which story they're in.

So what I need from you is the full runnable design: rules and scoring, the materials and what gets printed, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, any roles (I like the idea of a Commissioner who has to declare a champion, and a couple of Scouts who only ever see the pooled table), what the facilitator says and when, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the idea — composition, confounding, and the fact that whether to aggregate is a causal question, not a statistical one. Two more things I care about. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — I can already imagine the strong gladiator's first pick being so obviously right that the choice feels trivial, someone shouting that it's rigged once they see the decks, and a stubborn student insisting the points leader is the best fighter and refusing to budge. Second, be straight with me about what this activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself: a friend of mine does epidemiology and keeps insisting that with odds ratios you can get a reversal with no confounding at all, something about non-collapsibility, and I don't understand it well enough to stage it or to explain it — if that's right, say so and tell me how to fold it in as a caveat rather than a demo. I also want an honest note on the version of the lesson I'm losing by building the reversal into the incentives, since a sharp student will eventually notice that I arranged for the composition difference rather than discovering it.
