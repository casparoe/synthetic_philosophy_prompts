I'm in my second year of a philosophy of psychiatry seminar, and I've been stuck for weeks on a case that I think is a better test of Ian Hacking's account of looping effects than the examples everyone reaches for. The case is the strange reversal in the official psychiatric classification of grief. In the DSM-5, the field deleted the old bereavement exclusion, which had barred clinicians from diagnosing major depression in the first couple of months after a death, on the grounds that depression following a loss is not different in kind from depression that isn't and should not be exempted from diagnosis. Nine years later, the DSM-5-TR added prolonged grief disorder as an official diagnosis, with a duration threshold of roughly a year in adults and six months in children, so that grief persisting beyond a culturally expected period becomes a disorder in its own right; the ICD-11 carries a comparable diagnosis with a shorter threshold. The category had already been circulating for years as a proposed condition for further study before it was promoted, meaning there were instruments and research programs built around it before it was officially recognized. So the field first insisted that bereavement gives no special protection against a depression diagnosis and then created a separate category for grief that is supposedly not ordinary grief, and it did so using a label that had been sitting in the manual's back pages the whole time. Please analyze this episode through Hacking's framework and then turn around and use the episode to test the framework: what makes a classification interactive rather than indifferent, what exactly is supposed to be looping here, who or what is being made up, and whether the case is a confirmation of the account, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated.

Here is the wrinkle that keeps tripping me up, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than dissolving it. Hacking's looping effect, as I understand it, requires the people classified to take up the category, understand themselves through it, and behave differently as a result, which then feeds back into how the category is drawn and revised. But in the grief case the loop may run through infrastructure instead: the category becomes billable, screening instruments are validated against it, trials are designed around it, professional consensus consolidates, and the population of diagnosed people changes without anyone in particular having to reinterpret their own bereavement in the new terms. So I want you to distinguish two mechanisms — looping through the classified person's self-interpretation, and looping through the classificatory and institutional machinery — and then tell me whether the grief case is an instance of the second, whether the second is genuinely a looping effect in Hacking's sense or a distinct phenomenon his framework cannot accommodate, and what follows for the framework if it is the latter.

Be disciplined about the mapping: name the relata, say what changes in response to what and over what timescale, and mark clearly where the theory goes silent rather than forcing it to speak. I'm not asking whether the DSM got grief right, and I don't need a survey of the literature or any citations — I need an argument about whether this episode supports the looping framework, undercuts it, or reveals a kind of case it was never built to handle.
