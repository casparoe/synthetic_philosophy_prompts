I'm a novelist, about a hundred pages into a book that has turned out to be much more talky than I expected — it's about two superintelligences who can never touch each other, because each one lives inside its own simulated world, or its own pocket of the multiverse, or whatever I end up calling it; there's no channel between them, no signal either way. I thought that meant the story had no conflict in it, and then a friend who reads alot of decision theory told me that's exactly the setup where threats get interesting, because a threat doesn't have to be carried out by the person making it. Each of them runs a model of the other. So the one I'm calling A can "punish" its model of B, and if that's how B's decision procedure works out — if B treats its counterpart inside A's simulation as the same agent as itself, or cares what happens to it, or whatever the right way of putting this is — then A can squeeze B without ever reaching it. My editor keeps asking when the plot shows up, and this is my answer, but I don't actually understand the machinery well enough to put it in a character's mouth without it sounding like hand-waving.

The other half is the counter-move, which is what I really need explained. My protagonist, who is B in this telling, doesn't get brave and doesn't call the bluff. She changes what she wants: she picks some completely pointless thing to care about instead — my friend's example was hating being shot with a water pistol, but I'm thinking something like a fixation on the number of rivets in one particular bridge — and she somehow commits to taking threats against that as seriously as threats against her real goals. The idea being that A's threat gets redirected onto the harmless thing, and then "giving in" costs her nothing, because the thing she'd be giving up is worthless. My friend had a name for this, something like a surrogate goal, but I never remember it.

What I want from you is three things. First, the mechanic: in plain language, why does a threat that literally cannot be executed have any bite at all? What has to be true about the two agents — about their preferences, or about how their decision procedures relate to each other — for A to be able to lean on B this way, and what happens to the whole thing if that isn't true? Second, the counter-move: why on earth does caring about something worthless help, who has to be fooled about what, and is the point that A ends up threatening the stand-in, or that A declines to threaten anyone, or something else I'm missing? Third, be hard on it, I'd rather find out from you than from a reviewer. Can she really adopt the new goal after A has already built its model of her? Does the stand-in have to be something A is happy to see her chase, and what if A adopts a stand-in of its own? And the one that keeps bothering me: if she knows the goal is a prop, is she still "committed" to it in the way the trick needs — or is the whole thing just a story an agent tells about itself, and does it matter whether the story is true? If any step only goes through under assumptions I should know about, spell them out. Don't worry about who invented this or what the literature calls it; I just want the argument, since I can't exactly put footnotes in a novel.
