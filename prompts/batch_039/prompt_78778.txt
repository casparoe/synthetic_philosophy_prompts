I'm putting together a proposal for a small interdisciplinary grant — we're a tiny research group attached to a CS department, the money is real but modest, enough for me half-time for a year plus a workshop — and I want to work on the question of which computations can ground moral status. Here's the half-formed idea I keep circling. When people ask whether a system computes something that matters morally, they end up fighting about implementation: is the computation really in the system, or just in the eye of the beholder who picked the description? I think that fight bundles three seperate questions that should be pulled apart, and I want the proposal built around pulling them apart. First, grain: any physical system can be described as implementing an enormous number of computations, and the morally relevant one, if there is one, has to be the grain at which the system's states are causes and effects in their own right, not the grain some convenient re-description supplies. Second, robustness: an attribution should survive interventions on the parts — if I swap a component and the "computation" quietly becomes a different one while the behavior stays the same, I haven't found the system's computation, I've found a coincidence in my vocabulary. Third, activity: there's a difference between a system that performs a computation and a structure that merely encodes one — a lookup table with every answer already baked in, a frozen brain, a rule book. Does moral status need the first, and can we draw that line without smuggling biology back in as the hidden criterion? I want the proposal to argue that moral status requires executed, intervention-robust computations at the system's own grain, and then earn that by sorting cases where the three conditions come apart: a lookup table versus a lookup *process*, a whole-brain emulation, a slime mold deciding where to grow, a plant doing whatever it is plants do with damage signals, and a large language model whose concern-like computations might be attributed by us at a grain the thing itself doesn't use.

So what I need from you is the actual proposal document, maybe 1500–2000 words. State the question so that it has a determinate answer — right now "which computations matter morally" is so open it can't be wrong, and that's a problem. Say why the existing positions leave it open; I don't need a literature review with references, I'll add citations myself, just tell me what the standard moves are and where the gap sits. Lay out the method concretely — what I'd actually do month by month for a year, what the deliverable is at the end, and why this is a conceptual project rather than something I should hand off to an empirical lab. Then be honest about the ways it fails: what result would show the three-way distinction is just the old triviality worry in new clothes, and what would show the whole question is too big for a year and a half-time researcher. Give me a realistic scope too, I always over-promise.

One more thing: push back if the idea is confused. If "the system's own grain" is a phrase that doesn't cash out, or if the activity condition is doing all the work while pretending not to, I'd rather you say so in the proposal than write around it. I'm asking for your best judgment on whether this is a real research program or a nicely packaged muddle, and I don't want the polite version.
