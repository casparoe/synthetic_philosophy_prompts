I've been persuaded by the scale argument for insect sentience: granted the expected-value framework, even a very small probability that any individual insect has morally relevant experience is enough, because insects so vastly outnumber vertebrates that their expected welfare swamps ours, which makes insect-relevant interventions the top priority. What would count against this? Give me the defeaters ranked by how much they should move someone who already accepts the framework: findings about insect neurobiology and behaviour, arguments about how to weigh tiny probabilities against enormous stakes, doubts about whether "the probability that an insect is sentient" is a well-defined or evidence-sensitive quantity, and consequences the position is committed to that many would find unacceptable. Say which of these a defender would themselves grant and which they would wave away, what the view predicts that rival moral priorities do not, and whether it can be stated so permissively that no conceivable evidence tells against it -- and if so, whether that is a fatal defect or just what an expected-value argument of this shape looks like. I should add that the charge that the argument is insensitive to evidence about insects is the one I find hardest to answer, so tell me how much it should worry me.
