Question (worth 30 points; students were asked for roughly 400 words): Knobe's chairman case. Participants read about a company chairman whose vice-president for research tells him that a proposed new program will increase profits but will also harm the environment; the chairman replies that he does not care about harming the environment and cares only about profits, and the program goes ahead. In a matched version, the program helps the environment, the chairman replies that he does not care about helping the environment and cares only about profits, and the program goes ahead. Participants were asked, in the harm version, whether the chairman intentionally harmed the environment, and in the help version, whether he intentionally helped it. Most said yes in the harm version; most said no in the help version. (a) State the finding precisely, and explain why it is supposed to be puzzling for an account of intentional action on which an agent intentionally brings about an outcome only if he foresaw the outcome and either wanted it or was aiming at it. (b) What, if anything, does the finding establish about the folk concept of intentional action, as opposed to about the correct analysis of intentional action? (c) Experimental philosophy treats ordinary people's intuitive verdicts as evidence about the concepts those verdicts express. Does the Knobe effect support that methodology or embarrass it?

Student's answer: The chairman case asks whether a side effect that the agent foresees but does not care about counts as intentional. Knobe gave participants a story about a chairman who is told that a new program will raise profits and harm the environment, and who says that he does not care at all about harming the environment and only cares about profit. He is then asked whether the chairman intentionally harmed the environment, and most people say that he did. When harm is replaced by help, so that the program helps the environment and the chairman says he does not care about that either, most people say that he did not intentionally help it. Knobe (2003) called this the Knobe effect; it is also called the side-effect effect. This is puzzling for the simple view because the chairman's psychological states are the same in the two versions. In both he knows the program will have the environmental effect, and in both he is indifferent to it and acts only for profit. If intentionally bringing about an outcome required only that the outcome be foreseen and either desired or aimed at, the two versions should elicit the same verdict, since foresight and desire are held fixed. The verdicts differ, so something other than the agent's psychology must be doing the work, and the obvious candidate is the moral status of the outcome: people are willing to call a foreseen side effect intentional when it is bad and unwilling when it is good. It is worth noticing, though, that the two stories are not really parallel. In the harm version the damage to the environment is part of how the chairman gets what he wants, since the program he adopts is the very thing that damages it, whereas in the help version the environmental benefit is a by-product that plays no role in his reasoning. So the asymmetry in the vignettes, and not the moral valence by itself, may be what drives the responses. The finding has also been extended beyond morality narrowly understood, which suggests that what matters is norm violation rather than badness as such. For experimental philosophy the case is a paradigm. The worry is always whether a survey response reflects the participant's concept or a performance error — whether we are measuring competence or noise. Here the responses are large and orderly enough that they look more like competence than performance, so the method is vindicated: the folk concept of intentional action turns out to be sensitive to moral considerations, which is something no amount of armchair reflection would have told us. But the conclusion is uncomfortable for philosophy of action. If two people with the same information can correctly say different things about whether the chairman acted intentionally, then there is no fact of the matter about what he did intentionally; intentionality is relative to the judge's moral outlook. The chairman case therefore does not merely correct the simple view; it dissolves the question that view was trying to answer.
