I've been designing puzzles for a small escape-room company for about three years, and I've run into a vocabulary problem I can't seem to solve on my own. Here's the thing I keep needing to talk about. When a player walks into one of my rooms, some information gets across just from the fact that a thing in the room was placed there on purpose -- the padlock on the drawer, the book left face-down on the table. Nobody had to agree on a code for that to carry anything; it carries something because a person put it there and the player knows a person put it there. Then there's a second layer, information that only lands if the player and I already share a convention -- that the dial runs 0 through 9, that a scrap of paper with three digits on it is probably indexing something three digits long, that red usually means stop and blue usually means go. And then a third layer: the actual content, the combination, the word, the thing the puzzle is *for*. In a room that works, all three are running at once and nobody can tell them apart. When a room fails, it's usually because I skipped one, and it's almost always the middle one -- I assumed a convention the players had no reason to have.

What bugs me is that I need to say these three things separately and I have no words for them. "Clue," "hint," and "answer" all cut straight across all three. I did a linguistics minor in college so I've poked around alot of the obvious places -- the natural-versus-deliberate meaning thing Grice does, Peirce's icon/index/symbol split, information-theoretic talk about how much a signal carries -- and none of them is quite it, because what I'm pointing at isn't about *kinds of signs* or about *amounts of information*, it's about layers of shared background that all have to be in place before a message does anything at all.

Is there a settled name for this stratification? I'm definately not just after a label, though -- what I actually want is to know where the seams are. For instance: if a convention gets universal enough (that a locked door is meant to be opened, say), does it stop being middle-layer and become bottom-layer? Is the bottom layer really one layer, or is it secretly a stack of its own, since noticing that something was placed on purpose already requires me to know what "on purpose" looks like? And what about the case where the middle layer swallows the others -- where the fact that I chose to write a clue in French, or to leave a conspicuously fake red herring, is itself the message and the content is nearly beside the point? Same phenomenon, or does that want a fourth slot?
