I teach a ninety-minute session next week in a graduate applied ethics seminar, and the topic is consequentialist cluelessness. There are about fifteen students; they are comfortable with expected-value reasoning and have enough ethics behind them to be quick and a little overconfident. Several do effective-altruism-adjacent work, so they arrive believing uncertainty is essentially solved: assign credences, multiply, compare, proceed. Do not explain cluelessness to me. Plan the explanation.

The session has to be built around a distinction I want to draw, and I need the plan to test it rather than assume it. The first half is causal cluelessness: we cannot trace the path from act to outcome because the chain is long, chaotic, or open-ended, so we cannot assign probabilities to outcomes in the first place. The second half is evaluative cluelessness: suppose the outcome distribution were handed to us, and we still could not compare the outcomes, because the comparison needs a common measure we do not have — the values at stake are not commensurable, or the moral status of the beings in the relevant outcomes is itself unsettled. I want students to leave able to say why cluelessness threatens the action-guidingness of consequentialism rather than its truth, and able to distinguish it from three neighbours they will inevitably collapse it into: ordinary empirical risk, moral uncertainty, and plain ignorance that better research would cure.

If my distinction does not survive contact with a ninety-minute session — if one half collapses into the other, or into moral uncertainty, or if the two halves need the same examples and the same defusing moves — then say so plainly, tell me which half to drop, and tell me what to teach in its place.

Structure the response under these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths.

**Starting point** (about 150 words). The one premise that must be in place before cluelessness is even visible to this audience as a problem, and why nothing later in the session works if this premise is not accepted first.

**Order of construction** (about 500 words). The sequence in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency made explicit at each step: what has to be understood before the next thing can be stated, and why the obvious alternative order fails. Include where the two halves of my distinction enter, and where — if anywhere — the standard replies should appear. I want the problem to have bitten before any reply is offered; tell me if that is a mistake at this level.

**The carrying example** (about 350 words). My candidate is a grantmaker choosing between a deworming programme with well-measured near-term effects and a campaign to shape AI policy whose effects run through decades of political contingency. Give me a verdict on it: can it carry both halves of the distinction or only the causal half? What must the carrying example have, and what must it lack, for the whole session to hang on it? If it fails, tell me what to replace it with and why the replacement works better.

**Misconception map** (about 400 words). The misconceptions this audience will bring or form, each with the point in the sequence where it should be headed off and the specific move that defuses it. I am expecting at least these: that cluelessness amounts to knowing nothing and therefore to paralysis; that expected value absorbs it, since one can always widen the credences; that cluelessness just is moral uncertainty; that it is an objection to consequentialism specifically rather than to any act-based view; and the overcorrection that we should therefore stick to near-term measurable interventions. Add any I have missed. Say which single misconception, left unaddressed, poisons the rest of the session.

**Check for understanding** (about 200 words). One question to put to the room at the end, what a good answer contains, and what an answer that sounds right but has not understood the distinction contains. Also one thing to watch for during discussion that would tell me the idea has not landed even if nobody objects.

**Deliberate omissions** (about 150 words). What to leave out at this level and how to say so without seeming evasive — I would rather name the thing I am skipping and give the reason than have someone raise it and derail the last ten minutes.
