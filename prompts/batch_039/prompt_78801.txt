I have been in a long-running argument, of the kind that flares up every few months in the forums I read, about whether it is ever correct to say that a language model believes something — that it believes the meeting is on Tuesday, that it believes it is being evaluated, that it believes the answer is forty-two — and one phrase keeps getting used to end the argument rather than advance it: it is just predicting the next token. Someone offers a belief ascription; someone else says the model is only predicting the next token; and the room treats the objection as decisive. I suspect the phrase is being made to carry several incompatible claims at once, that at most one or two of them are true, and that none of them entails the conclusion everyone takes them to entail. I would like the readings laid out as a menu.

For each distinct reading, give me: the precise claim, stated as a single sentence; what kind of claim it is (about the mechanism that generates the outputs, about the functional role of the model's internal states, about what those states represent, about consciousness, or about the propriety of the explanatory vocabulary we use); whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false; and whether it would in fact license the inference that the model has no beliefs, noting where the inference breaks if it does not.

I am especially interested in the readings that are easy to run together, so please separate at least these threads:

- whether 'predicting' names a goal the system is pursuing at run time, a training objective that shaped its weights, or merely a description of the operation it performs on each forward pass, and what follows from each of those;
- whether the slogan, on its most natural reading, is itself stated at the intentional level and therefore presupposes the very belief-talk it is meant to debunk — the homunculus worry;
- what account of belief the slogan is quietly assuming: a functional-role or dispositional account, on which a belief is a state that disposes the system to act in certain ways and to revise in the light of evidence, versus an account that requires a stored, sentence-like representation — and whether the slogan comes out true on one and false on the other;
- whether the conclusion needs the model to lack original intentionality, or only derivative intentionality, and whether 'just predicting' is even the right contrast class;
- the instrumentalist reading, on which the intentional stance is a useful predictive fiction whose success shows nothing about what is really there, together with the reason that reading proves too much, since it would equally debunk belief ascriptions to other people;
- the reading on which the model's dispositions are calibrated to what is likely in a corpus rather than to what is true, and whether that is a defect in the model's beliefs or a fact about the direction of fit between its words and the world;
- the reading on which there is no persisting subject of belief across time, only a single forward pass, and what that would and would not imply.

At the end, tell me which single reading is doing the work when the phrase is used to shut the ascription down, which reading I should simply concede because it is true and I should stop talking as though the model believes things, and which I should resist because it is false or a non sequitur.
