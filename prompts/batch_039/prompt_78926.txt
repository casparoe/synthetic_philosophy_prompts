I'm drafting a position piece on the concept of disease for a philosophy of medicine seminar, and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to it. Here is where I have landed.

(1) There are objective, value-free facts about malfunction. An internal mechanism is malfunctioning when it cannot perform the function it was biologically designed to perform, and whether it can is a question for biology, not for anyone's preferences or social attitudes.

(2) A condition is a disease only when it involves both a malfunction in that sense and a harm to the person who has it. The malfunction supplies the factual component; the harm supplies the normative one.

(3) It follows that psychiatric classification is legitimate only where the categories track real malfunctions, and that the diagnostic manual is best understood as a set of administrative conveniences rather than a series of discoveries about nature.

(4) I take the standard lesson of the case of homosexuality to be that statistical atypicality cannot ground disorder. It was atypical, and its classification as a disorder was a straightforward scientific error, corrected once medicine stopped mistaking social disapproval for biology.

(5) A condition can be a disease even if it causes no distress and no impairment, provided the underlying mechanism is genuinely broken — an early, asymptomatic tumour is a disease. Conversely, great suffering is not a disease if nothing is broken.

(6) I see no principled line between treating disease and enhancing: if a drug safely improves memory or mood, whether the relevant mechanism was broken beforehand strikes me as irrelevant to whether the use falls within medicine's remit.

Which of these actually pull against one another, and how badly? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, say which pairs are only apparently in tension and why they dissolve, and for each real tension identify the cheapest repair and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set.
