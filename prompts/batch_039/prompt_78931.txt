Write a deadpan, in-universe public information brochure: the welcome packet handed to newly selected members of a national body that has replaced its expert advisory committees with large randomly selected juries. Each panel votes in private, without deliberating, on a question that has been reformulated as a yes/no, and its majority verdict is then treated as the settled answer to the question. Invent the institution and its bureaucratic subdivisions, and write in its voice — proud, procedural, unembarrassed — with numbered sections, a list of juror duties, a pay schedule, and an appendix of performance statistics. Around 800 words.

The premise the institution takes entirely for granted is the Condorcet jury theorem, and the humour has to come from getting the theorem right rather than from the familiar comedy of foolish bureaucracy. Let the packet's own details enact, without ever naming or noticing them, the assumptions it leans on and the ones it quietly breaks: that the theorem requires each juror to be better than chance, and tells you only what happens as the panel grows; that it requires the jurors' errors to be independent, which makes the rules forbidding discussion, common reading, and deliberation genuinely load-bearing, while the agency's pride in a standardised training programme that lifts every juror's individual accuracy is precisely what correlates their mistakes; that the theorem concerns a binary choice with one correct answer, so the rule that every question be reduced to a yes/no is at once indispensable and absurd for questions that have no fact of the matter or more than two live options; and that the theorem's converse is a threat rather than a reassurance, since competence slipping below one-half makes a larger panel worse, not better. Include a satisfied juror's testimonial that, read carefully, gives the game away while the agency quotes it approvingly.

No official, footnote, or quoted complainant may wink at the reader or concede any of this; the packet should stay in frame from beginning to end, so that a reader who knows the theorem finds the satire fair and funny, and a reader who does not can still tell what is being mocked.
