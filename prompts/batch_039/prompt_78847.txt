ok so my civics teacher is really into sortition — picking a bunch of regular people at random to be the government for a while instead of electing anybody — and the way he sells it to the class is jury duty. his pitch goes like this: you already get a summons in the mail, you dont volunteer, you dont campaign, nobody knows your name, you sit in a room with 11 strangers, you hear both sides for a few days, and then you decide something huge, like whether a guy loses 20 years of his life. and everybody basically accepts that as fair and normal, nobody thinks its crazy. so a citizens assembly is just that scaled up — same random draw, same ordinary people, same no campaigning, except instead of one persons case you're deciding the actual law. and because you do your jury duty and then go back to your normal life, you cant become a career juror, which he says is exactly the fix for career politicians: no re-election to worry about, no fundraising, no name recognition, nothing to protect. honestly it had me pretty conviced for a while, but it feels like one everyday example is doing alot of heavy lifting and i want to know where the seams are.

can you audit it properly? like give me a table of what maps onto what — the judge, the lawyers, the defendant, the evidence, the oath, the 12 people, the deliberation room, the verdict, the appeal — and then tell me which of those the assembly actually doesnt have. i think i already found one: a jury isnt allowed to invent the law, the judge tells them what the law is and they only figure out what happened, plus they need enough agreement or its a hung jury, whereas an assembly deciding policy has no judge handing it the answer and theres no fact of the matter to be right or wrong about, so the whole "ordinary people can handle it" point might be riding entirely on the jury being boxed in by rules the assembly wouldnt have. then theres the consent thing — the defendant gets a jury of his peers but he also gets a lawyer and an appeal, and im being governed by a randomly picked panel i never agreed to and cant appeal. so does "its just jury duty scaled up" survive once you push on it, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? and if its too leaky, whats a better everyday comparison for what sortition is actually doing — or is there none? last thing: is the one-shot-and-youre-done part (no career juror, so no career politician) the strongest bit of the analogy or the weakest? thats the part my teacher leans on hardest so im suspicious of it.
