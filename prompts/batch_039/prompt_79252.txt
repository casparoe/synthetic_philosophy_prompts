I keep running into summaries of Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture" and I want to know how much of this one to trust. A newsletter I read put it like this: Scheffler asks us to imagine that humanity will be wiped out in thirty days, or that we've all become infertile so the people now alive are the last generation. In either case, he says, everything we care about — art, science, love, justice — would stop mattering to us, because these things get their value from being handed on to the future; with no future there's no point to any of it. That's supposed to prove that human extinction is bad in a way that goes beyond the deaths involved, since it strips every living person of meaning, which is a harm done to them. And because meaning depends on there being future generations, we're obligated to keep humanity going forever — that's the moral he wants us to draw.

Something feels off about this. Can you seperate what a careful reader of Scheffler would accept, what's a subtle distortion, and what's just wrong? I'm especially curious whether the conclusion about a duty to survive actually follows from the conjecture, and whether the infertility case is really interchangeable with the thirty-day one. I'm inclined to think the duty part moves too fast, but I don't know the view well enough to say where the slide happens. And if the summary gets the "why extinction is bad" part wrong, tell me what the better version is, and what a defender of Scheffler would push back on hardest.
