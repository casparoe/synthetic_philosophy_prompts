Ok so I need to turn a half-formed idea into a short research proposal — like two pages, for a small workshop our department runs, not a formal grant application. Topic is the self-sampling vs. self-indication business (SSA vs. SIA) and specifically the presumptuous philosopher style argument, where a miniscule chance of some astronomically large population of observers swamps everything else and you wind up concluding you should be nearly certain you're one of a staggering number of people. I'm less interested in relitigating whether that conclusion is absurd than in figuring out what the disagreement is actually about.

My hunch, and I'd like the proposal built around testing it: the SSA/SIA fight is doing less work than it looks like, because two other things keep getting folded into it. First, how much of the action is really in the prior over world-sizes — how many observers a world contains — rather than in any principle about self-locating credence. Second, how much is a gauge choice, i.e. how you scale the measure you assign to observers inside a world. If you're allowed to rescale that measure world by world, as long as you stay consistent, then a lot of the SSA/SIA contrast seems to wash out, and what's left looks more like a dispute about priors plus a dispute about whether decisions should be made 'updateless' — from a position that doesn't privilege the index you happen to occupy. But I genuinely don't know if that's right, and I don't know whether anything interesting survives once the gauge is fixed.

So write the proposal. What I want in it: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the existing positions — plain SSA, plain SIA, and the "you're all missing that this is really about decision theory" line — each leave it open; the method, meaning what I would actually do with a semester's worth of time, stage by stage, and why that beats the obvious alternative of just collecting more intuition pumps; what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing, or as my hunch being refuted; and an honest scope check, including what I should cut if it turns out to be too big. I'd also like it to name the strongest objection to the gauge idea — probably that it's just relabeling and can't change any decision — and say how the project would try to answer that rather than dodge it. The audience is a workshop of decision theory and formal epistemology people who mostly don't work on anthropics, so pitch the framing accordingly. Don't bother with citations or a literature section, I'll fill those in myself. American English please.
