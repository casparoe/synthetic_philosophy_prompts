I chair a committee at my university that's meant to replace our annual research-output and teaching-quality numbers with a proper outcomes dashboard, and I keep having the same fight with the provosts office: they treat Goodhart's law as a slogan you nod at and then get on with it. I want to hand them a procedure instead. Write me an ordered checklist the committee can run on each proposed indicator before we adopt it — five or six tests, in order, with what counts as a clear fail on each and which failure mode that test is there to catch. Use the four-way split that's kicking around in the alignment literature (Garrabrant's, written up by Manheim) as the spine — the proxy degrading as you push on it, the tail behaving unlike the middle, the proxy being causally downstream of what you actually care about, and people gaming the number on purpose — not just the measure-becomes-target line. Mark which steps are genuine technical checks and which are really judgements about what kind of department we want to be, and say where a test is inconclusive and a person has to decide. Then run it on three candidates: publications weighted by journal prestige, mean student evaluation scores, and a peer classroom-observation scheme. At least one of them should come out fine by the procedure even though I won't like the answer, and tell me straight what the procedure can't settle. Two weeks, half the committee are humanists who distrust numbers, and the provost has to sign off. And be explicit about which bits here are settled among people who study measurement and gaming and which are genuinely contested — don't hand me one camp's line as the consensus.
