For the past term my reading group has been split on the Sleeping Beauty problem, and the two sides have stopped engaging with each other's arguments. One side holds that there is a determinate fact about what the agent's credence in heads should be, fixed by a general principle for updating on self-locating evidence, with the betting and planning arguments merely downstream of that credence. The other side holds that the credence question is unsettled until we say what credences are for — that the betting arguments do not test a prior credence but constitute it, so "what should she believe?" is answerable only relative to a decision problem, if at all. I have been asked to draft the joint statement both sides could sign.

Produce it as follows. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: about the protocol and what the agent knows at each awakening; about which probability claims are well-defined here; and about what each of the standard arguments (indifference over awakenings, no-new-information, conditionalization on centered evidence, the betting and Dutch-book considerations) does and does not establish. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording the camp that holds it would accept, with the crux for each. Third, for each crux, a case or argument both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance.

Both camps have also agreed to be tested on a variant we devised, for which neither has a stock answer: she is told the whole protocol in advance; a fair coin is tossed; on heads she is woken once; on tails she is woken once, and then a second fair coin is tossed, and she is woken a second time only if that coin lands heads; there is amnesia between awakenings, and no clocks or other cues. Each camp should state its principle as a general rule that delivers an answer for this variant, rather than as an intuition about it, and should say what it would take for that principle to be refuted. Say finally which parts of the dispute, if any, are merely verbal, and what evidence or argument both camps would agree should move the debate. Keep the document free of citations; the group knows the literature.
