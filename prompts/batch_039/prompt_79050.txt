For the past year or so, a small group of friends and I have been running what we have taken to calling a moral-trade circle. The premise is that we do not all rank the world's problems in the same order, and that this need not be a reason for argument: I am most concerned about the risk of a catastrophic pandemic and regard animal suffering as a real but lesser matter, while a close friend in the group holds the reverse ranking, and so each of us can do the other a service by directing some of our giving toward the other's priority. Concretely, I now give more to animal welfare than I otherwise would, and she gives more to pandemic preparedness than she otherwise would, and we both believe that the resulting pattern of giving is better by our own lights than the pattern each of us would have produced alone. I have found the arrangement unexpectedly clarifying, and I have begun to wonder how far the underlying idea can be pushed.

That is where the proposal I would like your help with comes in. Another member of the group, who has been reading about how to make decisions under moral uncertainty, wants us to go a step further and make our trades conditional on the resolution of the factual questions that separate us. Under his scheme, a trade would be written so that its terms change if, say, the scientific question of whether insects can suffer is eventually settled one way rather than the other: if the evidence goes one way the animal-welfare side of the ledger grows, and if it goes the other way it shrinks. He argues that this would let each of us hedge against being wrong, and he adds that a larger version of the scheme could invite people who care about neither cause to act as intermediaries, buying and selling such contracts purely to profit from disagreements about their prices.

Please set out the most important implications of the idea of moral trade — the general claim that two people with divergent moral views can each do better, by their own lights, by exchanging moral concessions — for how I should think about this proposal and about whether to take part in it. I am most interested in what the possibility of gains from moral trade implies about the nature of moral value: whether it presupposes that different people's moral priorities can be placed on a common scale, whether anything is conserved when value changes hands, and what it would mean for moral value to be tradable in this sense at all. Beyond that, I want to know whether making my commitments tradable — and still more, conditional on the settlement of a factual dispute — damages their sincerity or my integrity; where the analogy with ordinary economic trade holds and where it breaks down; and what difference it makes if the parties to a trade are wholly indifferent to the goods being traded. Finally, please tell me which of these is the deepest issue, and which consideration should actually settle whether I join.
