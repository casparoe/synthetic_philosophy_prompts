I've been reading a chapter on conditionals in a book aimed at non-specialists — written by someone who's clearly a real philosopher of language, so I assume he knows what he's talking about — and the bit on Lewis's triviality results has left me unsure what to actually believe. Heres my reconstruction of the chapter's argument, which I think is pretty faithful:

The thesis, which the chapter credits to Adams and Stalnaker together, is that the probability of an indicative conditional 'if A, then C' just is the conditional probability of C given A — P(if A then C) = P(C | A). That's supposed to explain a lot about how we reason: it's why you can be confident that the picnic will be cancelled if it rains while having no particular view about whether it will rain, and why it would be incoherent to be near-certain of 'if A then C' while thinking A and C are each unlikely.

What Lewis proved is that this can't work. Take a language that contains conditionals and is closed under conditionalization — so a conditional can sit inside another conditional — and let the probability function be defined on all of it. Then the equation forces P(C | A) to collapse into plain P(C): on any probability function that satisfies it, the antecedent and the consequent come out independent for arbitrary A and C, which is absurd. The later triviality results just tighten the screws — no amount of tinkering rescues the thesis, and even if you insist the conditional is neither true nor false when the antecedent is false, the collapse goes through anyway. The engine of the whole proof, the chapter says, is that conditional probability is undefined when the antecedent has probability zero, which is why the thesis turns out to be so fragile.

So the upshot is that Adams's thesis is refuted, and that Lewis was right to conclude that the indicative conditional is just the material conditional, true whenever its antecedent is false. There isn't really a logic of indicative conditionals, and the same thing happens with counterfactuals like 'if Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have.'
