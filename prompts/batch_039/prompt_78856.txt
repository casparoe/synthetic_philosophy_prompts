I'm in a seminar on the history of economic thought and I've gotten stuck on a comparison nobody else in the class will take seriously. Keynes, in Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, predicted that within a century we'd be down to something like a fifteen-hour week, and that the real difficulty would be learning to use the freedom well — he expected the love of money as a possession to be recognized eventually as a semi-pathological thing, and in My Early Beliefs he sketches the conception of the good life he absorbed from Moore and the Apostles, where what mattered was states of mind, love, friendship, aesthetic enjoyment, and where the whole utilitarian apparatus of obligation and calculation got thrown out. Meanwhile in the same year, 1930, W.K. Kellogg in Battle Creek replaced three eight-hour shifts with four six-hour shifts, as I understand it held wages roughly where they were, and took back laid-off workers — and the thing actually ran for decades, with a workforce that was mostly women fighting to keep the six-hour day long after management wanted to go back to eight, until the last of it was gone by the mid-1980s.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions, and I want the awkward parts left in. First, read the Kellogg episode through Keynes's own conception of the good life: by his standards, what had those workers actually achieved? Much of the evidence is that they used the extra time for family, church, community, gardening, fishing, hobbies — not for the contemplative or aesthetic pursuits Keynes personally valued, and not obviously for the kind of higher states of mind he and his friends thought were the point. Is that a confirmation of his picture (people choosing time over more income, the money motive failing to grip them) or a quiet embarrassment of it (they were living a decent life, not his good life, and his framework has no real place for it)? Second, reverse the direction: what does the case expose about the limits of his conception — too intellectual, too individualist, too dependent on a particular class position, too quick to treat leisure as a problem of self-cultivation rather than of solidarity and rest? And what do we do with the ending, where the workers themselves resisted the return to eight hours and lost anyway — does that tell against his prediction that the love of money would fade, or does it just show that the prediction was about the wrong actor, since it was management and the wider labor market, not the workers, that reimposed the eight-hour norm? I'm after the residue as much as the fit: which features of the case his framework handles cleanly, which have to be squeezed to fit, and which simply have no place in it at all. No bibliography or reading list please, I don't need cites, I need the analysis.
