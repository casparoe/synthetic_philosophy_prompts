I'm a second-year PhD student in early modern metaphysics, and I've been asked to open next week's seminar on Leibniz's Théodicée (1710) — a session that will in practice be about the problem of evil, since that is what everyone in the room actually cares about. In the discussion after my last presentation, a colleague cut short a line of objection by saying that if there is a God then this is the best of all possible worlds, and that our impression that things are not going well is therefore not evidence about anything at all; several people nodded, someone else called that a Panglossian absurdity and precisely why the whole position is dead, and it became clear that nobody in the room was using that sentence to mean the same thing. I would like to come back with a map. What I want is not a defence of Leibniz or a survey of the secondary literature, but a careful inventory of the distinct propositions that one sentence could be expressing, set out as a numbered menu with a short label and a one-sentence statement for each, ordered roughly from the readings that are almost trivially true to the ones that are genuinely radical, with a verdict attached to each.

To make the inventory usable I need the axes of variation made explicit rather than folded together. In what currency is "best" being measured — aggregate perfection, variety combined with order, the balance of good over evil, total creaturely happiness, something else — and is the optimum supposed to be global only, or also local, i.e. does the slogan entail that any particular event or any particular person's lot is for the best, or merely that no better total arrangement of the whole was available? For whom is the world supposed to be best: the sum of creatures, each individual creature, or the whole considered as a system? What does "possible" range over — logical consistency alone, compossibility with everything else in the world, or the narrower set of worlds God could actualize without overriding creaturely freedom? That last reading looks far weaker and far more defensible than the unqualified one, and I want to know whether the slogan survives the substitution. Is the sentence an evaluative claim about where this world sits in a ranking, an explanatory claim about why God chose it rather than another, a claim about God's nature, or a disguised definite description — "the world God actualizes" — in which case it is analytic given theism and does no argumentative work at all? I would also like the modal presuppositions surfaced: does "best" require a unique maximum, and what becomes of the slogan if the ordering of worlds admits ties, or admits an infinite ascent so that for any world there is a better one?

Then, for each reading, I want the verdict: analytic or trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or merely rhetorical; which readings are mutually exclusive and which are compatible; and which are only verbal variants that should be collapsed into one entry. Above all I want it said which readings would actually license my colleague's move — treating the apparent mismatch between the world's evils and a good God as no evidence at all — and which would leave that move unsupported, or make the slogan absurd in the way the people who quote it to sneer assume it to be. Finally, a hedged judgement, where hedging is honest, about which reading the earnest repeater and the mocking repeater probably each have in mind, and where the dispute stops being about what the sentence means and becomes a disagreement about the value ordering of worlds itself — since that, I suspect, is where the seminar will actually end up, and I would rather be the one who names it than the one who is caught by it.
