I'm a third-year student in a political philosophy course on war, military service, and the state, and for our final assessment I have to produce a handout for the seminar: a decision tree that sorts the positions on whether a democratic state may compel military service, and on what follows if it may. I would like you to design that tree and write its content, rather than explain the debate in prose. Each internal node should be a question my classmates could answer without already knowing which position it leads to; each leaf should carry the name of a position, or be marked unnamed, with one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Build it primarily around these cases rather than around the standard labels: an all-volunteer force that recruits disproportionately from the economically worst-off; a universal obligation that can be discharged either in the military or in civilian service at equal pay, which some defend as fair burden-sharing and others attack as a tax on the young paid in years of life rather than in money; the Swiss and Finnish militia models, in which staying outside alliances is paired with a peacetime obligation on most adults and a large trained reserve; and a lottery with no student deferments, which removes the class skew but leaves the few selected bearing a burden everyone benefits from.

Three distinctions should be visible in the structure even if that means more nodes than leaves: the authority question of whether the state may compel service at all, as against the distribution question of who bears the burden; whether a position's case for conscription survives the loss of the empirical premise that a professional army could not defend the state as well; and whether conscription is defended as a way of winning wars or as a way of making the public bear the cost of the wars it tolerates.

Tell me how confident you are in the specific calls the tree forces, and single out the one node you are least sure about, with a note on what would settle it.
