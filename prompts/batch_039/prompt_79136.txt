I teach the second half of a graduate seminar in social choice and normative economics, and I have just come back from a very long dinner at which a colleague from the law faculty and I argued about Amartya Sen's liberal paradox. His position was that the whole thing is a parlour trick: it only bites because we let people hold preferences about other people's business, and any serious liberal simply denies that the prude's preference about the lewd's reading has standing in the social choice. I don't think he's right, but I couldn't get him to see why, and the exchange left me convinced that our exam is worthless as it stands, because the question we have been setting for years — state minimal liberalism and the weak Pareto principle, exhibit the prude-and-lewd case, conclude impossibility — is answered perfectly by every student and understood by almost none of them.

So for this year's take-home I want a single essay question on the paradox, pitched at students who have already done Arrow and can tolerate a little formalism, that cannot be answered by restating the theorem. It should force them to say precisely what minimal liberalism commits us to as a condition on a social choice rule — in particular whether it constrains the assignment of decisiveness over pairs of alternatives, or whether it is really a substantive claim about what counts as a person's own business — and then to work carefully through the two standard lines of escape: first, that the paradox dissolves once individual preferences are allowed to be conditional, so that the prude's preference is properly about the combination of who reads what rather than about the lewd's reading in isolation; second, that the paradox is an artefact of welfarism and evaporates once rights are modelled as constraints on outcomes rather than as something aggregated from private preferences. I want students to have to say which of these is a genuine resolution and which merely relocates the difficulty, and what each route costs the liberal who takes it.

Please write the question itself (no more than 150 words), a model answer of roughly 700–800 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks — for instance, students who read the theorem as showing that liberalism is incompatible with democracy, or who treat the conditional-preference fix as free. Add a one-line note on how candidates typically get each must-hit point wrong, and please make sure nothing in the question or the model answer depends on knowing a particular paper, page, or edition; my students have the Sen chapter in the reader and nothing else.
