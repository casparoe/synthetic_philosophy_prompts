Dana and I ended up in a two-hour argument after a party last week about whether the cogito proves anything at all, and I've definately been chewing on it since. Her line is that Descartes is only entitled to something completely impersonal — that doubting is happening, or that there's a thought around here somewhere — and that the I is just a bit of grammar we can't help bolting on; she says the second you add the I your smuggling in a persisting person, a soul, the whole Cartesian dog and pony show, none of which the premise pays for. I went in sure she was wrong and came out unable to say what I actually believe, so I want you to take my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and tell me what principle, if any, they add up to — and if there's no single one, which verdict I should give up. Here they are, as honestly as I can state them. (1) Descartes alone in the study, doubting everything, concluding that he exists: that works for me, and what it proves is that he exists — him, the same guy who'll still be there tomorrow, not just an anonymous episode of doubt. (2) Me in a dream, thinking to myself that I'm thinking: that proves the dreamer exists, I'd say, but not the person in the dream who seems to be doing the thinking — the dream I is a fiction, and the fact that a thought happens in a dream doesn't make the dream-self real. (3) A patient who says a thought was put into his head, that the thought about being watched isn't his: that proves a thought occurred, but not that he's the one thinking it, so it seems like I think doesn't follow just from a thought being in your head. (4) Someone thinking that this whole cogito business is worthless, or even thinking that they aren't thinking: contradiction or contempt doesn't matter, it still proves they exist. (5) Me right now, remembering that I doubted yesterday: that doesn't prove I existed yesterday, even though it's my memory of my own thinking. (6) A raw toothache, no words in it at all: something is hurting, but I don't feel like that proves a self in anything like the way the cogito is supposed to.

The most I can get out of these is something like: a thought proves that its own occurrence is real and that it comes in a first-person way, but nothing about who owns it or how long they last — and I can't accept that, because in case 1 it really does feel like it's me. So is there a principle that saves all six, or close to it (and if so, say precisely what it says about the dream case and the inserted thought, since those are where I feel the pull to go impersonal), or is the honest thing to admit that (1) has to go and the cogito only ever delivers a momentary subject of the current act? If you go the second route, tell me what your version predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a split-brain patient whose two hemispheres can each report thoughts — does the cogito run once or twice? — and a thought I have under anesthesia that I never remember having, which I suppose nobody was there to have. Also tell me whether your principle is doing any work Descartes wouldn't recognize, or whether I've just reinvented his answer with worse vocabulary.
