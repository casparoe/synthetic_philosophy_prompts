I'm a graduate student in a lab that studies gender and work, and for the past year two friends of mine have been meeting every other week to argue about the gender-equality paradox. The first, whom I'll call the disposition camp, holds that the pattern as it is usually reported — richer and more gender-egalitarian countries showing larger gaps between men and women in what they study and in where they fall on the people-versus-things axis that vocational interest inventories use — is what you should expect if loosening legal, economic, and social constraints lets choices track underlying preferences, and she reads it as evidence that culture is not the whole story, perhaps not even most of it. The second, the structure camp, thinks the very same pattern is what a thoroughly cultural and institutional account predicts, because affluence and legal parity reshape occupational prestige hierarchies, credentialing, parental expectations, and the labor market itself, and she suspects the disposition reading has been read off a correlation that is doing no independent work. They have now argued for a year, largely past each other, and I have offered to be the one who writes up what they should have produced together.

So I would like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the disposition camp and the structure camp — a document both of them could put their names to. Give me, first, a numbered list of agreed claims: how the disputed pattern is to be stated so that neither side is smuggling in a reading of it; what both sides concede the pattern does not by itself establish; and what both agree must be true of any measure before it could bear on the question at all. Second, the residual disagreements, each one worded so that the side holding it would accept the formulation as fair to its own view. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and for each crux a test or case that both sides agree in advance would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down before the fact. Make the tests concrete, and where you can, choose ones neither of them has already made up her mind about: what each camp predicts about a single country as its gender-equality index rises over a decade; what each predicts about the daughters of immigrants from less egalitarian countries, and whether their choices converge on the host pattern or the heritage pattern; what each predicts when the degree-field gap is recomputed with a within-occupation task-content measure instead of a field label; and what each predicts for a constructed pair of countries identical on every equality index but differing in whether technical occupations are credentialed through universities or through apprenticeships.

Finally, tell me which of the remaining disagreements you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, and if the two of them turn out to agree about the empirical pattern and to disagree only about what follows for justice — whether a genuine preference gap, if there is one, weakens or leaves untouched the case for policies aimed at equal representation — say so plainly and state that disagreement in the same neutral form as the others. Neither of us has the literature at hand, so do not lean on named studies or citations; write each empirical claim out in full, and mark clearly which of them are claims we would need evidence to settle.
