For thirty years I studied the nervous systems of invertebrates; since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind, and I now chair the standing committee at a coastal research institute that decides whether a given organism or engineered system is to be treated as a candidate for moral status — that is, whether we require anesthetic protocols, humane endpoints, and caps on the number of individuals used, on the assumption that the thing can be harmed. We take six to eight cases a year, two hours each, working from a two-page dossier. The committee is four biologists, an engineer, and a lawyer; none of us is a philosopher. Our recommendations go to the director and the funder's ethics board, and they are published, so they had better be defensible. We have been deciding by loose consensus, and it has produced two failures: we exempt small-brained animals wholesale without ever saying why, and we have lately been asked about cultured neural tissue and about a vendor's large language model that the vendor would like us to certify as a welfare subject.

Please turn functionalism, together with multiple realizability, into a procedure we can run. Take the commitments seriously: no verdict may rest on what the system is made of or on how much it resembles a mammal, and a state is to be identified by the causal role it plays. The difficulty is not the slogan but what the slogan leaves open, and the procedure must force us to be explicit about two things. First, the grain. A role can be drawn coarsely enough that the thermostat, the institution, and this committee all implement something, and finely enough that only a mammal qualifies; nothing in the theory picks the level for us. So the procedure has to make us state our grain, defend it, and say what follows when two of us defend different grains. Second, the bearer. A role may be implemented by an organism, a subsystem, a colony, or a process that stops and restarts, and functionalism alone does not say which of these is the thing that can be harmed. Nor do the cases arrive sorted: a snail, a shrimp, and a squid sit on a continuum, and I do not want a procedure that pretends the line is given.

Keep these six slots, which are my own distillation from my reading; do not substitute a different list. Decide their order, give each an operational definition a biologist can apply, and say how much of each is enough:

1. the system transduces a noxious input and its response is not a fixed reflex;
2. the state is integrated across more than one sensory modality;
3. it is modulated by endogenous factors — injury, deprivation, hormonal state — and not only by the input;
4. it outlasts the input and alters later choices, including choices in which it is traded off against something else the system wants;
5. it is available to many downstream systems at once rather than to one dedicated output;
6. the system can learn about the state or about the conditions that produce it.

The procedure must return one of four verdicts and no fifth: role realized; role not realized at the stated grain; indeterminate because the grain is contested; outside the procedure because what remains is evaluative rather than factual. It must also keep apart two things I keep conflating — the role that defines the state and the observable markers by which we detect that the role is present — and tell me which of the six slots belong to which category, and what we are entitled to conclude given that we can only ever test the markers. Say what to do when the slots disagree with one another, when the committee must stop and send the case to the director as a values question, and what record we should keep so that next year's committee can reconstruct our reasoning. Aim for one page of questions in order, plus a short gloss per slot.

Then run it on three dossiers: a crab in a study that requires repeated noxious stimulation; an octopus; and the vendor's language model, whose dossier argues that it satisfies all six slots — its outputs are shaped by prior descriptions of injury, its internal state persists across turns, and so on. I expect at least one verdict I will not like, and I would rather meet it here than in print; if the procedure licenses precautions my colleagues will call absurd, tell me whether it is the functionalist commitment, the grain, or the precaution we have layered on top that is doing the work. Finally, be plain about what the procedure cannot do: how much of it is a detection procedure and how much is a decision rule for acting under uncertainty, since functionalism tells us what a state is and not what to do when we cannot tell whether it is there; and which of our disagreements it will convert into technical questions and which it will leave exactly where they were.
