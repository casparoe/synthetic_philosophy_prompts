You are a historian of nineteenth-century political thought with a specialist's command of John Stuart Mill's political writings and of the scholarly literature on liberalism and empire. I am at work on an essay about Mill's imperial thought and I want your help with a consistency problem. Reason carefully before committing to anything: begin by setting out the candidate rules that could explain the record below, say which you discard and why, and only then reach a verdict. Work from the material in this prompt and from your own knowledge. Do not cite editions, page numbers, or secondary literature, and do not attribute any quotation to Mill beyond the passages I quote below.

The record I want explained is Mill's own, across six episodes.

First, India. Mill spent his working life in the India House, and when the Company's government of India was transferred to the Crown in the late 1850s he opposed the transfer, on the ground that the Company's officers, being further removed from parliamentary and missionary pressure, were better placed to govern with an eye to the interests of the governed than ministers answerable to an electorate at home. He held that India stood at a great distance from fitness for representative government and would need to be governed by others for a long time yet.

Second, the settler colonies. In the chapter on dependencies in Considerations on Representative Government, Mill divides dependencies into two classes: those "composed of people of similar civilisation to the ruling country, capable of, and ripe for, representative government," such as the British possessions in America and Australia, and those, "like India," which are "still at a great distance from that state." For the first class he holds that the true principle of government has at last been realised: full internal self-government, with the tie to the mother country reduced to the slightest kind of federal union.

Third, the limits of alien rule. The same chapter contains the line that "such a thing as government of one people by another does not and cannot exist," and, in the same passage, the warning that one people may keep another "as a warren or preserve for its own use, a place to make money in, a human cattle farm to be worked for the profit of its own inhabitants." Mill adds that where a country holds another in subjection, the Europeans who go out to make their fortunes are the class that most needs restraint, and that "of all the strong, the European settlers are the strongest."

Fourth, the West Indies. When Carlyle attacked the emancipated West Indian population as unwilling to work except under coercion, Mill answered him in print, refusing the account of their nature on which Carlyle's case rested and locating the explanation of their conduct in their circumstances rather than their character.

Fifth, non-intervention. Mill treats non-intervention as the general rule among civilised states, but allows an exception where a people is struggling against a foreign yoke; and he allows that despotism may be a legitimate mode of government in dealing with peoples he counts as barbarous, so long as the end is their improvement.

Sixth, Jamaica. After the repression that followed the Morant Bay rising, Mill chaired the committee that sought the prosecution of the governor, on the ground that the law of England ran in a Crown colony as it did at home and that no emergency licensed killing outside the law.

Now the case I want predicted. I have constructed it to isolate the variables, so I describe it without a name; it is not a historical colony and you should not give it one or supply it with a real history. The indigenous inhabitants of the dependency number about two million and form the great majority. A settler community of some forty thousand Europeans holds most of the arable land under titles the colonial courts have upheld; the indigenous majority disputes those titles, and disputes of this kind are the principal business of the local courts. The magistracy and the lower officialdom are drawn almost entirely from the settler community, and the record of convictions in land and labour disputes between settlers and indigenous inhabitants is, by any reading, one-sided. A small Western-educated indigenous professional class — clerks, pleaders, two physicians, a handful of journalists — has petitioned for a legislative assembly with a property franchise. The governor proposes to hand over internal affairs to just such an assembly; on the franchise he proposes, that assembly would be dominated by the settler community, and the indigenous professional class would hold perhaps three seats out of sixty. That professional class has asked instead for continued Crown administration, with a nominated indigenous element on the governor's council and a definite timetable for the transfer of power. The settler leaders argue, in Mill's own language about the North American and Australian colonies, that they are of the ruling country's blood and language and are ripe for representative government, and that the imperial government has no business meddling in their internal affairs.

What I want to know is what Mill would say about the governor's proposal, and what he should say if he is to be consistent with the record above: does he hand internal affairs to the assembly, refuse, or hand them over with conditions — and on which of his own commitments does the answer turn?

Please answer in the following five sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths.

The candidate rules (about 300 words). Set out two to four rules, each of which fits the whole record above, stated in the strongest form you can give it. Say which rules you considered and set aside, and why.

What each rule predicts about the case (about 300 words). For each surviving rule, give the verdict on the governor's proposal that it yields. Where a rule is indeterminate on these facts, say so, and say what further commitment would be needed to make it determinate.

My verdict on Mill's verdict (about 250 words). Give your prediction, name the rule you think is doing the work in Mill's own thought, and say how confident you are and what would lower your confidence.

The case that would separate the rules (about 200 words). Specify one further case, concretely enough to be judged, on which the surviving rules give different verdicts, and say what verdict on it would show which rule is operative.

The weakest link (about 150 words). Identify the step in your reasoning you would defend least confidently, and why.

Write continuous prose in each section. Use bullet points only where a list is genuinely the clearest form.
