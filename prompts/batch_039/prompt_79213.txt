I'm a science journalist — I write about AI and the mind, mostly — and I'm part-way through a longish piece on the idea that thinking is computation, the whole "is the brain a computer?" thing, and everyone keeps telling me it starts with Hobbes, so I've gone back to the source. I'm stuck on chapter 1 of his Elements of Philosophy, the one people usually just call De Corpore (English version, 1656), where he writes: "By RATIOCINATION, I mean computation. Now to compute, is either to collect the sum of many things that are added together, or to know what remains when one thing is taken out of another. Ratiocination, therefore, is the same with addition and substraction; and if any man add multiplication and division, I will not be against it, seeing multiplication is nothing but addition of equals one to another, and division nothing but a substraction of equals one from another, as often as is possible. So that all ratiocination is comprehended in these two operations of the mind, addition and substraction." And then the very next thing he does is try to show how we manage this "in our silent thoughts, without the use of words", with an example of a man seeing something from far off and compounding his ideas into one, the way the three names body, animated, rational compound into "man".

What I can't get straight, and I'd really like help with, is what's actually being claimed. Is this arithmetic on quantities, or just the weaker thought that any stretch of reasoning can be re-described as adding and subtracting ideas or symbols? Is it a claim about the machinery of the mind — which would make him a proto-computationalist a good three centuries before anyone built a general-purpose machine that could do it — or a claim about the form of reasoning, a logician's point about how inference is to be set out, with nothing mechanistic behind it at all? And if it is the mechanistic reading, why isn't this already the computational theory of mind, full stop? My half-formed hunch, which you're welcome to tell me is wrong, is that Hobbes's adding still needs an intelligent someone doing the adding, and that what the modern version adds is the thought that the adding itself can be done by something that understands nothing whatsoever — so the new ingredient is the program, not the arithmetic. I'd also like to know what that "without the use of words" example is doing: does it help the computational reading, since thought is compounding even without language, or hurt it, since then what exactly are the symbols?

I'm not after a reading list — I've got the texts, I just can't get my head round the arguemnt. Could you write it in British English, and say plainly what's fair to claim about Hobbes here and what would be overreach?
