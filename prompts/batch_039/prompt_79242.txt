I'm in a small reading group on early modern moral philosophy — we just finished up with Pufendorf and now we're into Hume's Treatise, and I'm stuck on a paragraph in the section he calls "Of the obligation of promises". I've read it maybe six times and I can feel myself going in circles, so I'd like you to help me understand it. Please take the role of someone who knows Hume's moral philosophy and the history of theories of promising inside out, and start by just laying out the argument of the passage in your own words, step by step, before you say anything evaluative — I want to see the structure on the table first. The bit that's killing me is this:

"The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions. It follows, therefore, that since the act of the mind, which enters into a promise, and produces its obligation, is neither the resolving, desiring, nor willing any particular performance"

— and the sentence then concludes that it must be the willing of the obligation itself that the promise creates.

I can follow the three eliminations (it's not a resolution, not a desire, not the willing of the action), but that last step feels like a magic trick: why does ruling out those three leave the willing of the obligation as the only remaining candidate, instead of just showing that the whole idea of a special act of mind behind the words "I promise" is confused? And then a bit later he says: "A change of the obligation supposes a change of the sentiment; and a creation of a new obligation supposes some new sentiment to arise." What exactly is the absurdity he's pointing at there — is it just that the will can't manufacture new sentiments, or is there a further point about what kind of thing an obligation is in the first place? One person in the group claims the argument is basically circular, because the premise that we have no motive to keep promises apart from a sense of duty is doing all the work and the rest is window dressing. I'm not sure if thats a fair reading or if theres a stronger one.

The thing I actually care about, though, is whether the argument is as broad as it sounds. A friend of mine keeps saying that promising is a normative power — a capacity we have to change what other people are owed just by exercising it, the way someone with standing authority can impose a requirement on you — and that this is a perfectly respectable way of thinking about it. If Hume is right that the act of mind behind a promise is unintelligible, doesn't that sink any view like that? Or is his target much narrower (say, only the idea that there's some natural act of will attached to the utterance, independent of convention and the interests of society)? Please lay out what you take the strongest version of Hume's argument to be, then tell me where you think it breaks, and feel free to brainstorm a couple of rival readings before you commit to one.
