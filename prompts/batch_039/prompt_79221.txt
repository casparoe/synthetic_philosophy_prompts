I am assembling a teaching dossier on anachronism for a postgraduate seminar in historical method, and I would like the centrepiece to be a satirical document. Please write it.

The document is a practice direction issued by a fictional regulator of scholarly practice, the Office for Temporal Standards, and is titled Practice Direction No. 4: The Moral Assessment of Historical Conduct. Its governing rule is that no historian may assess a past actor by the standards of the historian's own time: conduct is to be assessed only against the standards in force where and when it occurred. The direction is drafted by a career administrator with no sense of humour, in numbered clauses, with a preamble, defined terms, commencement and transitional provisions, a licensing procedure, and an annexe of worked examples. The register throughout is formal and bureaucratic, never jokey: nothing in the document may acknowledge that it is funny, and nothing in it may be self-consciously paradoxical. Somewhere between eight hundred and a thousand words.

The comedy has to come from taking the rule seriously and following it out. I want the document to turn on at least the following difficulties, each of which should surface as a piece of drafting rather than as commentary:

- Contemporaneous standards were plural and contested, so the direction must say which of them count. It resolves the matter in favour of the standards actually enforced at the time, i.e. those of whoever held power, and does not notice what it has thereby done.
- The difference between the categories a past actor used of himself and the categories a historian would use of him, with the direction coming down firmly on the former.
- The impossibility of a principled cut-off date for when judgement becomes permissible, handled by a schedule grading permissible severity of judgement against elapsed time.
- The awkward case of the past actor whose own standards were better than those of his contemporaries. The direction must do something with such a person, and whatever it does should be deflating.
- The Office's own enforcement of the direction, which consists entirely in applying present-day standards to living historians. This self-exemption is the pivot of the piece: make it land in a single short clause rather than with a fanfare.
- The fact that the direction binds teaching and the writing of history for present-day readers, both of which the Office regulates anyway.

Any quoted matter must be the direction's own text or the speech of invented officials and invented petitioners. Do not put words into the mouths of real people, living or dead, and do not build the piece on real statutes, real institutions, or named real scholars. Where you need an example of regulated conduct, use invented jurisdictions, offices and cases, rather than real episodes; if you refer to real historical conduct at all, keep it to the most general terms and supply no dates, names or figures. The piece should read as bureaucratic comedy to someone who knows nothing of the debates, while the difficulties listed above should be recognisable to someone who does.

Commit to a definite line. Choose one target and hit it: the piece should satirise the demand that historians suspend moral judgement, by exhibiting that demand applied consistently. Do not hedge by having the document half-concede the absurdity of its own rule, and do not spread the irony thinly across several unrelated targets. After the document, in a note of about a hundred and fifty words, state plainly which position you have satirised, why the consistently applied rule generates the absurdities it does, and what you take to be the strongest reply available to the historian who insists on non-judgement; say also where the satire overstates for effect or leans on a contested reading of the debate. You may use the ordinary technical vocabulary of the debate in that note (presentism, anachronism, actor's categories, Whig history), but supply no citations, reading lists or attributions.

Write in British English throughout, including in the fictional document, and keep the document's register formal rather than jokey.
