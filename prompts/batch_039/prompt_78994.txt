I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar — 14 students, mostly seniors plus a couple of first-year MA students — and I've got a 90-minute session to spend on the dogmatism paradox. I've tried just putting the argument on the board and it dies every time: they nod, and then someone says something deflationary like "well that just shows you shouldn't be so sure of yourself," which is exactly the move I want to make unavailable to them. So instead of a lecture I want a runnable activity — a game, a role-play, a structured deliberation, whatever fits best — where they enact the thing before anyone says its name. What I'm after: they should arrive, through their own choices, at the position that once you take yourself to know something, you're entitled to treat every later piece of contrary evidence as noise; and they should have felt in their hands both why that entitlement is so seductive and why it's a machine for entrenching people who are simply wrong. Ideally they reconstruct the steps themselves (knowledge entails truth, so anything speaking against a known truth must be misleading, so if you know it's misleading you may ignore it, and you know all of this a priori) — I'd much rather they build the argument out of what just happened to them than hear it from me. You're a philosopher who's spent a lot of time turning abstract epistemology into things people can do with their hands, so take a moment to brainstorm three or four mechanically different activities before committing to one, then develop the strongest.

The structural feature I care about most is this: the license to ignore contrary evidence gets issued *before* anyone can tell whether they're in the good case (they really do know) or the bad case (they're confident and mistaken). If the design lets people look back afterwards and say "ah, but I was justified, I really did know," it hasn't landed — I want them to discover that the rule protecting the knower is the same rule arming the fool, and that from the inside they can't tell which one they are. Seed the activity with whatever the group has to be doing: a bet, a verdict, a diagnosis, a sealed envelope, a witness who recants, an expert who contradicts them, a slip of paper telling them what they're allowed to shred. Keep materials cheap and printable, no real money, and make it survive a couple of absences or one group finishing early. Nobody should hear the phrase "dogmatism paradox" (or "higher-order evidence," or "defeater") until the debrief — the mechanism is supposed to do the teaching.
