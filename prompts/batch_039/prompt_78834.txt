The course supplies the following apparatus, and students were told to use these terms and no others:

- Risk and uncertainty. An outcome presents a risk when it has an assignable probability; it presents uncertainty when its probability is not assignable, though the outcome is recognized as possible. Risk and uncertainty attach to particular outcome-probability pairs rather than to whole decisions, so a single decision can be a risk case on one side and an uncertainty case on another.
- Three readings of the precautionary principle.
  - PP-1: the absence of full evidence that an activity causes harm is not by itself a sufficient reason to postpone measures that are otherwise justified.
  - PP-2: the proponent of an activity bears the burden of showing that the activity is acceptably safe, rather than opponents bearing the burden of showing that it is harmful.
  - PP-3: where an activity threatens serious or irreversible harm along a plausible causal pathway, the activity is to be restricted or prohibited pending a demonstration of safety.
- The trigger for PP-3. PP-3 is triggered only when both of the following hold: the threatened harm is serious or irreversible, and there is a plausible causal pathway from the activity to that harm. Bare possibility, with no plausible pathway, does not trigger it.
- The default rule. PP-2 is procedural: it fixes who must argue and to what standard, and it says nothing about the substantive outcome. The substantive outcome comes from PP-1 or PP-3. PP-2 does not by itself make prohibition the default.
- The symmetry requirement. The harms of a proposed precautionary measure must be entered on the same footing and in the same currency as the harms the measure is meant to avert. A harm of the measure may not be excluded on the ground that it is of a different kind from the harm it is weighed against.
- The self-application problem. Because restricting an activity can itself produce harms, one may ask whether PP-3, applied to the restriction, requires restricting the restriction pending proof of its safety.

Midterm, question 3 (suggested time: 25 minutes).

The state agricultural agency must decide whether to register Microfix-7, a soil inoculant containing an engineered consortium of bacteria and fungi, for use on cereal farms. The registrant's three years of field trials show yields equal to or better than conventional practice at 60 percent of the nitrogen input. Two of the consortium's fungal strains colonize the same root niche as a native arbuscular mycorrhizal species, and the agency's soil ecologists have raised the possibility that the consortium could displace that species; no trial has measured any decline in native fungi. The ecologists judge that displacement, if it occurred, would probably reverse within about a decade after withdrawal, but they cannot rule out the permanent local loss of the species. The agency cannot assign probabilities to either outcome. The proposed precautionary measure is a five-year registration moratorium. The agency's own modelling estimates that during the moratorium farmers would keep applying synthetic nitrogen at current rates, so nitrogen runoff into the watershed would continue at current levels rather than fall; the agency treats that runoff as a harm to the watershed, and one that is reversible once loading falls. Using only the stipulated readings and requirements, say what kind of case this is; say which reading or readings of the precautionary principle apply, and why; apply the trigger for PP-3 explicitly; apply the symmetry requirement explicitly; address the self-application problem; and state what the agency should do.

The answer, reproduced in full:

This is a mixed case, and it matters which side is which. The agency cannot assign probabilities to displacement or to permanent species loss, so the inoculant's ecological effects present uncertainty. The runoff is different: the agency has modelled it, and it describes the effect as reversible once loading falls, so that side is a risk case. The course's point that risk and uncertainty attach to outcome-probability pairs rather than to whole decisions is what lets us say both things at once.

PP-1 is satisfied but does not decide anything. It tells us that thin evidence about native fungi is not by itself a sufficient reason to postpone a measure that is otherwise justified: a floor, not a decision rule. PP-3 is the reading that governs. The threatened harm is serious and irreversible if it occurs, since the permanent local loss of a native species is not recoverable. The pathway condition is met too, because the two consortium strains colonize the same root niche as the native species, so competitive displacement is a live mechanism rather than a bare possibility.

PP-2 applies as well, since the registrant is the proponent of the activity and must show that it is acceptably safe. It has not: three years of yield data say nothing about native fungi. The default under PP-2 is therefore to withhold registration, which here means imposing the five-year moratorium.

The symmetry requirement requires that the moratorium's harms be entered on the same footing as the harms it averts. The cost side includes the yield gains and the nitrogen savings that farmers give up for five years, which are real but recoverable. Weighed against the possible permanent loss of a native species, the species loss dominates: a recoverable five-year delay in cost savings cannot outweigh an irreversible ecological loss.

The self-application problem is where the principle comes under pressure, and I think it does not survive. The moratorium keeps farmers on synthetic nitrogen, and the agency's modelling says runoff would continue at current levels rather than fall. Runoff is a serious harm to the watershed, so PP-3 requires us to restrict the moratorium; restricting the moratorium is itself an activity with costs, so PP-3 requires us to restrict that in turn, and the regress never terminates. A principle that cannot be applied without an infinite regress cannot guide action, so PP-3 is unusable. If we fall back on PP-2, the outcome is the same, since the burden remains on the registrant.

The agency should impose the five-year moratorium.
