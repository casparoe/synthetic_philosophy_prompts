I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a small summer research grant — three months, enough for a philosophy grad student to actually finish something — and I have a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into a real proposal. The topic is Kant's universalizability tests. I've been reading around them and I keep hitting the same wall: the test gives the right verdict on the textbook cases (the lying promise, the deceitful deposit) and then either goes quiet or goes wrong everywhere else. Two things bother me in particular. First, the maxim-individuation problem — the verdict flips depending on how richly you describe the maxim, since "I will break a promise when it suits my interest" comes out contradictory but "I will break a promise on this one occasion because my friend is in trouble" seems to sail through, and I've never seen a non-ad-hoc rule for which description is the operative one. Second, the competition cases: maxims of competing for a scarce good — a scholarship, a job, a seat, the last concert ticket — look like they should be fine, but under some readings they generate a contradiction in conception, and I can't tell whether that's the test doing its job or the test misfiring. My hunch, which is what I want the proposal built around, is that both problems come from treating the test as a decision procedure that takes one maxim as its input, and that it behaves much better if you treat it as a consistency constraint on a whole maxim-set — i.e. stop asking "is this maxim universalizable?" and ask "could this maxim belong to a set of maxims that every agent could adopt at once?", where a maxim is represented as a triple of triggering condition / action / purpose, and "everyone adopts it" means "everyone meeting the condition acts that way with that purpose in view". Then individuation becomes the question of which maxim-set is the relevant unit, and the competition cases become a question about whether the purposes are jointly satisfiable rather than whether the action is repeatable.

I want you to write the proposal so it has: a statement of the question precise enough that it has a determinate answer (I don't want "explore the nature of Kantian universalizability", I want something a referee could mark as answered or not answered); an explanation of why the standard readings of the test leave it open — the logical-contradiction reading, the teleological reading where the contradiction-in-will test turns on some necessary end of rational agency, and the reading that treats the test as a procedure for generating duties rather than testing them — without turning into a literature review, since I'll add citations myself and don't want you inventing any; the method, concretely, in terms of what I'd actually do month by month, including what I'd need to decide first (how to cash out "purpose", whether purposes can conflict, whether "everyone adopts it" should be read as universal adoption or universal permission — that choice alone flips several cases); and then what a result would look like and what would count as failure. On that last point I have views I'd like reflected: a result could be a worked-out constraint that recovers the verdicts we want on a stated list of cases without special pleading, or it could be a no-go result showing that no constraint of this shape does, given a small set of independently motivated adequacy conditions — I'd be genuinely happy with the second, but I want the proposal to say so rather than pretend the first is the only good outcome, and I want it to be honest that failure would look like the set-based reformulation merely relocating the individuation problem, or only working once I tune the adequacy conditions to the cases I started with.

A few test cases to seed it, so it's not all abstractions: the jacket on the chair reserving a table in a café; using an ad blocker on sites that exist only because of ads; buying the last ticket purely to resell it at a markup; and voting strategically for a lesser evil rather than for the candidate you actually prefer. I think the voting one is the interesting one, because the purpose (getting the outcome you want) seems to be defeated when everyone does it, and it's unclear whether that's a contradiction in conception or just a coordination failure that Kant's test has no business ruling on — the proposal should commit to a line on which of those it is and why. Also, briefly, say something about second-order maxims, like "I will adopt whatever maxims are most expedient for me", since universalizing a maxim-schema rather than a maxim is exactly the kind of thing the set formulation might handle better or worse than the standard one. Keep it in the range a three-month project could plausibly touch, and be upfront in the scope section about what I'm deliberately not doing (I'm not going to settle the exegesis of any particular text, and I'm not trying to derive a full ethics from this).
