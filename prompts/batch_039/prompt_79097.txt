I write about science and ideas, and for the past several weeks I have been reporting on a consortium of independent laboratories, each of which holds, on its own, the authority to decide whether to run a particular class of experiment: no member can veto another, and no proposal requires anyone else's consent. A decision theorist who has been advising the group sent me the argument below. I have read it closely enough to find it alarming and cannot quite take it apart. Each step looks reasonable to me on its own, yet the last one is a conclusion I do not believe anyone, including its author, would defend. I would be grateful if you would tell me which step to reject, why it looked sound, and what rejecting it would cost.

(1) Each of the N member laboratories may, on its own authority, decide to proceed with a proposed experiment.

(2) Each laboratory's judgment as to whether a given experiment is worth running is formed independently of the others' and is better than chance, though fallible.

(3) A proposed experiment goes forward if at least one laboratory judges it worth running.

(4) Consider any proposed experiment that is in fact not worth running. Each laboratory has some probability p, greater than zero, of mistakenly judging it worth running.

(5) The probability that at least one laboratory so judges is 1 − (1 − p)^N, which rises with N and approaches certainty as the consortium grows.

(6) So the larger the consortium, the closer to certain it is that any given bad experiment, once proposed, will be carried out by someone.

(7) The same arithmetic ensures that good experiments are carried out more often as well, but the benefit of a good experiment is not of a size to offset the harm of a bad one, so the ledger worsens with size.

(8) For any member who cares about the outcome, therefore, the expected harm the consortium does rises with the number of its members.

(9) Hence a member should decline to act on his own authority, and should press instead for a rule under which nothing proceeds without the broad agreement of the membership.

(10) Hence, other things equal, the consortium should be kept as small as possible and, if the sole aim were to minimize the chance of a bad experiment being run, would best be replaced by a single decision-maker, whose error rate is at least one person's rather than the union of N people's.

Step (10) is where I balk. So far as I have ever been able to tell, the safe way to decide whether to run a dangerous experiment is not to hand the decision to one person; and yet (10) appears to follow from (4) through (6) by nothing more than arithmetic. If I must surrender something, I would rather not surrender (2): that the laboratories' judgments are independent, and individually better than chance, is the whole reason the consortium is worth writing about. So where does the argument go wrong, and what do I lose by fixing it there?
