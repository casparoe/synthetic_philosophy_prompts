I am a third-year PhD student in early modern European history, and I have been held up for months by a single sentence. In a historiography seminar last term, a colleague dismissed the reconstruction I had presented on the grounds that my evidence came almost entirely from the administrative records of the winning side in the conflict I was writing about: history is written by the victors, she said, so what I had was the winners' self-portrait rather than anything about what actually happened. I have since heard the same line used to do at least three quite different jobs — to impugn a particular source, to deny that historical knowledge can be objective at all, and to explain why a given national narrative has the shape it does. I am fairly sure these are not the same claim wearing one sentence, and I would like them separated.

Please set out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, and give a verdict on each: platitude, substantive but defensible empirical generalisation, claim that holds in some domains and fails in others, or simply false. Lay the readings out as a menu with reasons rather than selecting one and defending it. I would particularly like you to address the following, though you should add whatever further distinctions the material demands rather than treating this list as exhaustive:

1. The archival claim: that the documents survive because the winners kept them, destroyed others, and had the standing to record at all. What does this establish about the surviving record, and how much of the past it concerns left no trace in the first place — is the operative notion victory, or literacy, or state capacity, or simply survival?
2. The transmission claim: that even where losers' testimony exists, the categories, chronology and genre in which it is preserved are the winners'. Is this a claim about content, about form, or about what counts as a source?
3. The disciplinary claim: professional historiography is built on source criticism expressly designed to read documents against the grain of their producers. Is the slogan therefore false of academic history and true only of popular memory, or does the professional method fail in ways that vindicate it after all?
4. The counterexamples: narratives of defeat are sometimes the most influential histories we have — the Lost Cause account of the American Civil War and the stab-in-the-back myth in interwar Germany are the obvious cases. Say precisely what these do and do not show, and whether they refute the slogan or merely complicate it.
5. The relativist claim: that because every account is someone's, there is no fact of the matter about the past. Separate this from the weaker claim that some historical questions are underdetermined by the evidence.
6. The word 'victors' itself: is it doing real work, or would the slogan be more accurate — and more interesting — if rewritten around who controls the means of reproducing texts, or who gets to define the terms of the settlement afterwards?

Then tell me which reading most plausibly animated my colleague's objection, and whether that reading actually licenses it. Does a source's having been produced by the powerful make it worthless as evidence, or only make it evidence of a particular kind, requiring a particular manner of reading? And what would count as evidence for or against the empirical readings, given that a past which left no trace is not the same as a past which was suppressed?

Finally, be explicit about which parts of your answer are settled among historians and philosophers of history and which are genuinely contested: I want the fault lines marked, and I do not want a contested position presented to me as the consensus. Where the disagreement turns out to be partly about how a word is being used, say so. Write in British English. I know the literature well enough, so spare me the reading list — I want the sorting, not the citations.
