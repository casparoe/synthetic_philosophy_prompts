My oldest friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about a case I had always treated as obvious, and I came away unable to say which of us was right — which is a problem, because I have to make this kind of call at work, on a schedule, without the luxury of being unsure.

The case is this. A woman blind from birth, who has never had any visual experience at all, is given sight as an adult. In front of her, on a table, are a cube and a sphere; she has handled both for years and can tell them apart instantly by hand. She is allowed to look but not to touch. Can she say which is which? My friend says no, and thinks the answer is obvious: she has never had to learn what vision means, the light falling on her retinas is at first a smear with no objects in it, and she must go through a long apprenticeship in which touch teaches sight what it is looking at. I said yes, and thought it equally obvious: cubeness is one property, met twice; she has known cubes for years, and there is nothing left for her to learn. We could not settle it. I gather it is an old question with a name attached — Molyneux's problem — but I am not interested in the history. I am interested in the disagreement's practical shadow.

I run the clinical and rehabilitation side of a small company that builds sensory substitution and sensory restoration devices: vibrotactile and audio-vision displays, a retinal prosthesis, cochlear implant fitting, a haptic belt for orientation. Every capability on our roadmap forces a version of the question my friend and I could not settle: do we assume that a skill our users already have in an intact sense will carry over to the new channel, or must we build explicit training and interface scaffolding for it? We make this call roughly a dozen times a year, currently by whoever in the room feels most strongly, and a wrong assumption costs months of engineering and — worse — burns the early adaptation window when a user's new sense is still plastic. I need to stop improvising.

Please write me a procedure we can actually run. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no and the instruction to train the capability explicitly, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values — how much training burden we are willing to impose on a user — rather than of fact. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of perception and the study of cross-modal learning actually know about this, to the extent that knowledge can be made usable; I don't need names or reading lists, I need the distinctions that survive being turned into a checklist. It will be run in a one-hour design review by clinicians and engineers with no philosophical training. The medical director signs off. We cannot run new studies, but we can run up to two ninety-minute sessions with a user, and I would like the procedure to say when that is worth doing.

Three things I keep noticing and do not know what to do with. First, our team collapses three questions that are probably not the same: whether the user can tell the two things apart at all, whether she can name which is which, and whether she can act on it — reach for the cube rather than the sphere. Second, some properties — being to the left of, being more numerous, being larger — seem like things any sense could deliver, while others seem to belong to one sense and nowhere else, and I cannot tell in advance which list a given capability falls on. Third, my friend's word for the missing ingredient has stuck with me: calibration. Her claim is not that the skill is absent but that it is present and unusable until something teaches the new channel what its signals correspond to.

Run the procedure on three cases and show your work: a user of the haptic belt learning to orient toward a doorway; a newly sighted user with the retinal prosthesis learning to pick a cup out of a cluttered table; and a user learning to read facial expression through a vibrotactile display. At least one of the three should come out against what we currently do — I would rather learn that on paper than in the field. Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire.
