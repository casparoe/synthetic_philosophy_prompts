I'm putting togehter a handout for my applied ethics seminar, and I want the whole thing to be one decision tree that students walk down. It should start from the admission "okay, fine, my single burger / one short flight / one vote makes no perceptible difference to anything" and end at a position about what, if anything, an individual owes in these cases. Please dont organize it by the usual textbook -isms. Every branch node should be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and every leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody actually defends it) plus one sentence on the commitment that position has the hardest time defending.

Branches I want on the tree, roughly in this order. First: what is the unit we're even assessing -- a single act, one person's whole pattern of consumption and behavior over years, or a group's joint action? Second: can an act be wrong even when the difference it makes is imperceptible? Third, if yes, where does the wrongness come from -- the act's tiny expected causal contribution (as opposed to the contribution it actually turns out to make), the fact that it's a non-superfluous part of a set of acts that jointly do the harm, the fact that it violates a rule it would be bad for everyone to follow, complicity in or profiting from a practice you don't control, or the fact that doing it expresses what you value? Fourth: is the duty unconditional, or does it switch on only once enough other people are doing their part? And I want one more branch for the people who think the whole puzzle is confused, on the grounds that "the harm" isn't a real thing over and above a bunch of individual harms.

It has to handle these five cases, and I want students to be able to answer every node without already knowing where it leads: (1) a bucket brigade where the fire gets put out only if at least twenty people pass buckets and there are already nineteen of us; (2) same setup, but there are already fifty of us; (3) driving a car that emits an imperceptible amount of carbon; (4) one voter in an election that will be decided by thousands of votes; (5) being the first person on my block to stop watering the lawn during a drought, where nothing changes unless about half the street joins in.

Three leaves I definitely want to see, since they're the ones my students never come up with on their own: the flat-out "no individual duty, the objection is sound" view; the view that the duty is to work for coordinated change rather than to abstain unilaterally; and the deflationary one that denies collective harms exist in the first place.

For the leaves generally: use real position names where there are real ones, "unnamed" where there aren't, and flag any two leaves your tree can't pull apart. Also tell me which combinations of answers come out empty and whether that's because the combination is incoherent, or merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Aim for eight to twelve leaves, keep it in American English, and write it so it fits on two sides of a page.
