I'm putting together a handout for a graduate applied-ethics seminar on inheritance, dynastic wealth and equal opportunity, and I want it to be a decision tree rather then a list of camps — something students can walk down to find out where they actually stand, with every node phrased so it can be answered without knowing which position it leads to. Build it from the logical space first, then place the actual views in it: each node a single question or claim on which the positions genuinely come apart, each leaf a named position, or unnamed where nobody defends it, and each leaf carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Don't organise it by how keen someone is on taxing inheritance, that's the boring axis. Organise it by where they locate the wrong, if there is one — in the bequest itself, in the receipt, in the ongoing power a dynasty's wealth gives it over other people, or nowhere — and then by what they take the point of equal opportunity to be (a fair start, a fair race, or no inherited advantage at all), and by whether the family counts as a special case where partiality is allowed. It has to have a leaf for the Rignano scheme — the 1920s Italian proposal for a tax whose rate climbs each time the same pile passes down a generation, so the first bequest is cheap and the fourth is not — and a leaf for the view that the right instrument is an annual wealth tax instead, plus the abolish-it-entirely and leave-it-all-alone leaves. Ten-ish leaves is fine. Three cases have to fall out of the tree cleanly: the family house left to one child, the family firm handed to the next generation, and a trust going to a great-grandchild. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually separate, and say which combinations of answers come out empty and whether that's because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or just undefended. No reading list or citations, thanks.
