I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working mostly in metaphysics, and I'm applying for a small internal grant — three months of summer funding, enough for a self-contained piece of work rather than a chunk of the dissertation. The application wants a two-page research proposal, and I'd like you to write it. The topic I keep circling is the old puzzle about a statue and the lump of clay it is made of. As I understand the landscape, there are three broad families of answers: that there are genuinely two objects there, sharing all their matter at once but differing in their persistence conditions, so that squashing destroys one and not the other; that there is really only one object, described in two ways, with the appearance of two being an artifact of our talk; and that the disagreement is not substantive at all, the disputants operating with different counting and sortal conventions and talking past each other. The two-objects view then owes an account of what makes the two differ when they are materially identical, and the deflationary view owes an account of what, if anything, our conventions are answerable to. What bothers me is that all three families are standardly tested against the same few cases, cases largely chosen by the people who hold the views: a lump gets shaped, the statue is destroyed while the lump survives, the matter is replaced piece by piece. I suspect the debate looks more settled than it is because those cases are doing too much of the work, and I want to test it against cases from outside the debate — specifically, cases in which the sortal status of a portion of matter is unstable or reversible. A colleague in art history got me started on this. Three kinds of case interest me: works that exist as instructions and are realized only for the duration of a showing, with the material deliberately left as an undifferentiated mass in between; ordinary manufactured things that become artworks when someone exhibits them and can stop being artworks afterwards; and objects whose material is entirely replaced, for conservation reasons, while everyone agrees the work persists. Each of these puts pressure on a different member of the three-way division, and I want to know whether the pressure is real or whether each view can absorb the cases with a small adjustment.

I want the proposal to do the framing work I haven't done. State the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why each of the three positions leaves it open rather than already settling it, rather than just summarizing the positions; and say why a philosopher's answer here would matter to anyone outside metaphysics. Describe what I would actually do in twelve weeks — I imagine the bulk of it is stating the cases carefully enough that they aren't rigged in advance, working out what each view predicts about how we should describe and count in each case, and then asking whether the views can be brought into agreement by adjusting what they mean by 'object', 'sortal', or 'convention'. Be honest about what would count as success and what as failure: if every view turns out to absorb every case, that is a result, but a deflationary one, and the proposal should admit that this is a live possibility and still say why the project is worth funding in that case. Say what would make the project too big for twelve weeks and how I would cut it down. The panel is mostly historians and literary scholars, so the proposal has to be intelligible to someone outside metaphysics without being dumbed down.

Please structure the proposal as titled sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: Summary (about 120 words); The question (about 180); Why the existing positions leave it open (about 250); Method and plan for twelve weeks (about 300, with the work broken into phases); What success and failure would look like (about 200); Scope and risks (about 120). Leave out citations, a reading list, and any references to particular authors or works — I'll supply those myself, and I'd rather the space go to the argument.
