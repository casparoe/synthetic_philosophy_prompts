I spent thirty-five years in solid-state and physical chemistry before retiring, and I have lately been working through philosophy on my own and in a small reading group. One dictum keeps turning up in both places — 'necessary truths are knowable a priori' — and it is invariably asked to do the same work: to rule out the suggestion that an identification established at the bench could be a necessary truth, on the grounds that whatever is necessary must be knowable without experience, while whatever is established by experiment is knowable only through experience. I am told the position being dismissed turns on how substance terms get their reference, but that does not by itself tell me what the dictum asserts.

I would like the dictum separated into the distinct precise claims it could be making, presented as a menu rather than resolved in favour of one reading, so that I can see which of them are trivial, which substantive and defensible, which false, and which of them would actually license the dismissal it is used for.

Two cases from my own work should keep the exercise honest. The first is deuterium oxide, commonly called heavy water: it is not H2O, it differs measurably in physical behaviour and in its effects on living tissue, and it is nevertheless called water by nearly everyone, chemists included. The second is a mineral phase I worked on early in my career: we could identify it by its powder diffraction pattern and by nothing else, so we named it before anyone knew what it was made of, and its composition was settled years later in another laboratory.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should state one reading of the dictum in precise terms, give your verdict on that reading — trivial, defensible, or false — and say whether that reading would support the use the dictum was being put to; where one of my two cases decides the matter, say so in the same item.
