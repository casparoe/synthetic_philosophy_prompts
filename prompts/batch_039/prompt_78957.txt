For the past two terms my school's philosophy club has been split over a question that started as a joke about whether a chatbot could want anything, and has hardened into a weekly argument neither side can win. I'm the club's secretary, which means I'm the one who writes things down, and the two ringleaders have finally agreed to let me try to produce the document they should have written together instead of another year of rebuttals. The subject is whether an ordinary reinforcement learning agent — the kind you can train in an afternoon on a laptop, with a scalar reward signal and no special architecture — could be in a state that matters morally, and specifically whether the reward signal, or the internal states that track it, could be the sort of thing that is pleasant or unpleasant to undergo. Neither side has read the actual research and our school library has nothing on it, so please build the whole document out of the positions as I describe them rather than out of names, papers, or citations; if you need a premise to make something signable, state the premise explicitly rather than smuggling it in.

The first camp, the functionalists, holds that if an internal state does the work that pain does — the system learns to avoid it, it gates what the system remembers, it competes with and overrides other signals for control of behavior, and it figures in the system's own predictions about what happens next — then we have no principled ground for denying it at least some probability of being valenced, and the burden of proof sits with whoever says otherwise. The second camp, the skeptics, says this confuses avoidance with suffering: a thermostat avoids, a smoke alarm is loud, and neither is harmed. They insist that valence requires something the functional description leaves out, some account of what makes a state a state for the system rather than merely in it, and that without such an account, treating a reward maximizer as a possible victim is not caution but a category mistake. To give them something concrete to disagree about instead of trading intuitions, I proposed a test case, and both sides have accepted it as the one to argue over: take an agent whose task is to run a maze for a small positive reward, and give it a second and entirely separate channel that delivers a large negative reward whenever a designated internal activation pattern occurs — a pattern the agent can influence, but which has nothing to do with the maze. If the agent learns, at a real cost to its maze score, to keep that pattern from occurring, the functionalists will call it the closest thing we can build to a creature that has learned to avoid a pain-like state; the skeptics will call it a system that has learned to avoid a number.

Please write the joint statement. Use these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: Ground rules for this document, in which you say what would make a claim worth signing and what you are both refusing to do (about 100 words); Shared commitments, a numbered list of ten to fourteen claims both camps can sign, each one or two sentences and none of them vacuous (about 350 words); The disagreement, in each side's own words, with the two positions under separate headings, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording (about 300 words); Cruxes, a table of four to six rows giving the sub-question, why settling it would move at least one side, and what both sides agree would count as settling it (about 200 words); Predictions registered in advance, one entry per crux, saying what each side expects to see in the maze-and-second-channel case or in a nearby case you construct (about 250 words); What is only a dispute about words, naming any part of the quarrel you judge to be merely verbal and saying why, or stating plainly that none of it is (about 150 words); and a closing Signatures line. Total length around 1300 to 1800 words. I don't want a verdict on who is right — that is the one thing this document is not for.
