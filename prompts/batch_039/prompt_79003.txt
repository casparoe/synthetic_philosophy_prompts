My reading group is doing Ursula K. Le Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas,' and everyone arrives at the same verdict: the story is a reductio of utilitarianism, because it buys the happiness of the many with the misery of one child, and that violates the separateness of persons. I want the discussion to be less comfortable than that. The separateness-of-persons objection is really a cluster of complaints — that utilitarianism wrongly aggregates goods across distinct lives, that it licenses using some persons as means to others' ends, and that it locates value in impersonal states of affairs rather than in persons — and Omelas is built so that all three converge on the same child, which makes it a poor test of which one is doing the work. It also matters that the citizens' happiness is not merely weighed against the child's suffering but made conditional on it. So which version of the objection does the story actually dramatize, does that conditional dependence strengthen or weaken the anti-utilitarian reading, and what are the ones who walk away rejecting — aggregation, the use of a person as a means, or the thought that value resides in states of affairs rather than in persons? Read the story and the objection against each other, say plainly where the story resists the reading, and tell me what the story shows that the objection, stated in the abstract, cannot.
