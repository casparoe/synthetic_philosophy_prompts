I am a junior at Jefferson High School and, for the past four months, I have been the student lead of a civics-project campaign to rezone the Old Mill Road corridor in my city — fourteen blocks of mostly one- and two-story commercial storefronts, two surface parking lots, and one 1968 garden-apartment complex of 96 units that still rents at roughly 55 to 70 percent of what the newer buildings on the corridor charge. What we are asking for is a change to the code rather than approval of a single project: maximum height raised from 35 to 52 feet so four stories become legal, floor-area ratio raised from 0.6 to 2.2, and the 1.5-spaces-per-unit minimum parking requirement removed, in exchange for an affordability requirement of 20 percent of units at or below 60 percent of area median income, with the covenant running thirty years. In fairness I should say that the draft we were given also lets a developer satisfy that requirement by paying an in-lieu fee into the housing trust fund instead of building the units on site, and I genuinely do not know whether that is a fatal hole or a reasonable pressure valve. My three-minute statement will not be about the numbers. It is about who the current rules are for: a height limit, a floor-area ratio, and a parking minimum are not neutral, they are a way of allocating who gets to live here, and the people who would live in those four-story buildings have as strong a claim on this neighborhood as the people who already own houses on the streets behind it. What I am least sure of is what to say when someone asks me what kind of claim that is, and who exactly it is a claim against.

Next Tuesday is the planning commission hearing. I get three minutes at the microphone, and then the six commissioners may ask me anything they like, and then two hours of public comment runs and I have to sit through all of it. I have watched the last four hearings on video, so let me describe the room precisely. The chair is a commercial realtor whose first question in every case is whether the pro forma pencils out. Beside her is a retired land-use lawyer who runs a seminar on property theory and who asks structural questions — what right, held by whom, against whom, enforceable how. Then a civil engineer who cares about the level of service at the Old Mill and Ninth intersection and about stormwater and pervious surface. Then a former council member who is the de facto voice of the neighborhood association and who opens with "we are not against affordable housing, we are against this." Then the youngest commissioner, who ran on a housing platform and is more likely to attack me from the left than from the right: she will ask whether the affordability requirement is real given the in-lieu fee, and she will point out that the cheapest housing in the corridor — the 1968 complex — is exactly the housing that gets demolished first when land values rise. And finally a landscape architect who will ask about the tree canopy and shadow studies, and who is probably persuadable. In the audience: about forty residents, overwhelmingly homeowners from the four streets behind the corridor, several of whom will have printed traffic counts; the owner of the 1968 complex, who has been quietly buying adjacent parcels; two tenants from that complex; and one man from a taxpayers' group who will ask why the city is subsidizing anything at all. The county's parking occupancy study found peak utilization of 61 percent in the corridor's lots; the intersection sits at level of service D in the PM peak; the school district projects flat enrollment over the next decade. My family rents a two-bedroom on Old Mill Road.

Act as a veteran land-use planner and hearing coach who has prepped both developers and neighborhood groups for hostile public hearings, and prepare me for the questions I will actually get in that room — not the generic NIMBY-versus-YIMBY talking points, which I already know. Think it through carefully; the specific facts above should do real work in your answers, and I would much rather be told I am wrong about something now than be told I was impressive.
