You are an expert in formal epistemology and the foundations of decision theory, brought in as a neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers who have been arguing for years about whether a rational agent's credences can be imprecise.

The two parties are:

Party A: A fully rational doxastic state is a single probability distribution, or at any rate is always representable by one without loss. When someone reports that their credence in a hypothesis is an interval rather than a number, that is a report of indecision or of ignorance about which sharp distribution to adopt, not a distinct kind of rational doxastic state.

Party B: A rational agent's credences can be a set of probability distributions, no member of which is privileged by the agent's evidence. Representing one's state as a set can be rationally required rather than merely permitted, and imprecision is a genuine feature of rational belief rather than a defect to be repaired.

Both parties agree that the following case bears on their dispute, and neither denies its mathematics; they disagree about what a rational agent in it should believe and do. An envelope contains a ball that is either red or blue, and a machine beside it will either beep or stay silent. You are certain that the ball is red with probability one half, certain that it is blue with probability one half, and certain that the machine will beep with probability one half. You have no information whatsoever about the dependence between the two: the machine might beep exactly when the ball is red, exactly when it is blue, independently of the ball, or anything in between. A trusted assistant opens the envelope and reports that the ball is red. A bet is then offered that costs fifty cents and pays one dollar if the machine beeps; you have not yet heard the machine. Note that before the report, every single distribution consistent with your evidence gives probability one half to the ball being red and probability one half to the machine beeping. If you think the case is not well posed, that judgment is itself a crux to be recorded, with each party's stance on it.

Produce the joint statement these two should have written. It must contain the following sections.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both parties would sign, each stated precisely enough that neither is signing something vacuous. Include what they agree on about the case above.

2. Disagreements. A table with one row per live disagreement. Columns: the question at issue; Party A's position, phrased so that A would accept the wording as a fair statement of A's own view; Party B's position, held to the same standard; and whether the row is a genuine disagreement or a terminological one. Do not dissolve a disagreement by choosing a formulation each side can read its own way; if a row cannot be made non-slanted, say so and explain why.

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. I suspect these include: what a rational agent should believe and do in the case above; whether a body of evidence can ever leave it rationally indeterminate which sharp distribution is correct, or whether imprecision is at best a bookkeeping device for a committee of sharp distributions; which decision rule governs choice when credences are a set rather than a point; and whether the arguments that rational credences are the ones that minimize expected inaccuracy presuppose sharpness. Identify any further cruxes you find. For each crux, state the case, argument, or consideration both parties agree bears on it, and write down each party's prediction about it in advance, including their predictions about the bet in the case above.

4. Verbal disputes and open questions. Which parts of the dispute dissolve once the terms are fixed, and what, if anything, both parties agree would actually move the debate.

Constraints: no citations, no names of authors or works, no reading lists. If you invoke a standard argument, state it in your own words. Write for a reader who knows probability theory and decision theory but not this literature.

Before drafting, spend a short preliminary section mapping the dispute and marking where you take the real fault lines to lie; then write the statement itself. Think carefully at each step about whether an agreed claim is one a committed member of each camp would actually sign, and revise any formulation that a partisan could reasonably reject as a misstatement of their view. Both parties are highly competent and will refuse to put their names to a document that slants their position or papers over a real disagreement.
