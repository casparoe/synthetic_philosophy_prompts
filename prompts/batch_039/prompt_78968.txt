I'm a third-year politics student and I've ended up in the weird position of taking a democratic theory course and a formal methods module at the same time, so the two keep colliding in my head. In the formal methods half we proved the Condorcet jury theorem — the one where if every voter is independently more likely than not to pick the correct option, the majority is more likely to be right than any individual voter, and the probability of a correct majority goes to 1 as the group grows — and in the theory half we spent the entire term arguing about whether that theorem actually justifies majority rule. And that arguement has completely stalled. I don't mean people disagree; I mean it's the same four moves every single week. One side says the theorem is a non-starter because real electorates aren't independent and aren't reliably better than chance — everyone watches the same news, deliberates together, absorbs the same bad information. The other side says that's a category error, that a theorem is a model of an ideal rather than a prediction about Sheffield City Council, and that robustness results exist for correlated votes. Then someone says 'competence' is just the free parameter that makes the theorem come out true, so the whole thing is circular, and someone else says no, competence is a substantive empirical claim you could in principle measure. Term's nearly over and nobody's budged, and we've gone round this alot.

Here's my half-formed hypothesis, tell me where it's wrong. I think the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about independence or competence at all — they're disagreeing about whether there's a procedure-independent fact about what the right political answer is, and the defenders need one to exist while the critics think that's either incoherent or anti-democratic, and neither side will say it out loud. There's also a thing I noticed that nobody in the seminar will engage with: the same Condorcet gave us the cycling paradox, where majority preferences can go round in a circle, which seems to me to definately undercut the idea that majority voting tracks anything at all — so maybe the impasse is that one side keeps invoking one Condorcet and the other side keeps pointing at the other one. Is that a real tension, or am I confusing two different setups, and does it even matter for the diagnosis?

What I want: tell me what kind of disagreement this really is — empirical, formal, verbal about the word 'competence', or a fight about what would count as a justification of a decision procedure at all — and say which parts of my hypothesis survive contact with the actual positions. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress here: a theorem, a distinction, a better-posed question, or nothing, with a reason why nothing should be expected. Lead with your verdict on the kind of disagreement in the first two or three sentences, then the support, and keep the whole thing under 700 words.
