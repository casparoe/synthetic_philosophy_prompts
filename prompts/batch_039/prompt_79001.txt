I teach a graduate seminar on modality and the metaphysics of identity, and I want a paper from you that takes a position on a question we have been circling for two weeks: could you — the particular person now reading this — have come into existence from a different sperm and egg than the pair that in fact produced the zygote from which you developed? The thesis that you could not is the one standardly credited to Kripke, and my students can all recite the argument for it: the gametes that actually produced your zygote were numerically distinct from any other gametes, the resulting zygote was distinct from any zygote that would have resulted otherwise, distinct things are necessarily distinct, and since you are that zygote, no organism from other gametes could be you. What I want is an essay that decides whether this reasoning establishes its conclusion or merely dresses up a premise it needs. Reach a verdict and defend it; an answer of the form "it depends on what we mean by personal identity" will not do, though you may make the verdict conditional on a clearly stated principle — for instance, that what is essential is the token cell rather than the genetic material, or the reverse. Be explicit about which premise you accept and which you reject, and give the opposing side its strongest formulation before you attack it.

To keep the essay from turning into a recitation, work through the following three cases, which I stipulate rather than report. First, imagine a world in which meiosis proceeds without crossing over, so that two sperm cells from the same division carry exactly the same chromosomes and the same sequences; if fertilization in that world had been accomplished by the other, numerically distinct but genetically indistinguishable sperm, would the resulting person be you? Second, suppose the zygote that in fact became you had divided shortly after fertilization, as monozygotic twins do; then two people share your origin exactly. Is either twin you, are both, or neither? Third, suppose a cell with your exact genome had been assembled from scratch in a laboratory, with no sperm, no egg, and no fertilization; if that cell developed into a person, would that person be you? Say what each case shows about whether the essential thing is the particular zygote, the particular gametes, or the genetic material they carry. And address what I have been calling the vacuity worry: the standard argument seems to presuppose that a gamete can be individuated — picked out as this cell rather than that one — without already settling whose gamete it is, since if the reference of the phrase "the sperm that produced you" is fixed only by way of you, the argument cannot help but move in a circle.

Keep the essay to a tight argument rather than a survey. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I would rather see you reason carefully about the cases as I have stated them than attempt to reconstruct what has been published about them, and you should feel free to treat the stipulated biology as hypothetical rather than something to be verified. Conclude with a clear statement of the position you have earned and of what, in your view, the strongest remaining objection to it is.
