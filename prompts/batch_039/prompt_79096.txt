I'm a TA for a second-year ethics course and next week's section is on the nonidentity problem -- Parfit's cases, the teenager who has a child, the policy that makes us all better off now and wrecks things for people a century out, the works. The reading is dry and I told the students I'd screen It's a Wonderful Life in lecture and use it as the hook, which I now have to actually do, so I've been rewatching it and I think theres something in it that I want to say out loud but I'm not sure it holds up. George wishes he'd never been born, Clarence shows him Bedford Falls become Pottersville, and everything he sees is the same people, worse: Harry dead as a boy, Mary an unmarried librarian, his mother a sour boarding-house keeper, Bert and Ernie and the rest of them gone small and mean. Which is precisely the case the nonidentity literature says we're allowed to compare, because it's the same people, only worse off. So the film's entire emotional force comes from a counterfactual in which nobody's identity is ever in question. Suppose the world without George had been full of entirely different people, all of them happy -- would the angel's demonstration still work, and what could George have said then? And there's a second thing nagging me, which is that the person the argument is supposed to be for is the one who wouldn't have been around to have interests at all.

A friend of mine who did a masters in philosophy says the film is person-affecting all the way down and therefore can't engage the problem, because the angel's world is engineered to be the one case where you get to keep the people and make them worse off, so the film never faces what makes nonidentity hard. I don't think that's quite fair, but I can't say why, and the ending doesn't obviously look like a comparison to me at all -- it looks like it's about George finally seeing his own life from outside, and the neighbours turning up at the door. What I want is a reading of the film through the nonidentity literature that's honest in both directions: where it dramatises the problem, where it dodges it, whether the dodge is a flaw or whether the film is making a different point the literature has no room for, and which two or three scenes I should actually put in front of thirty undergrads when I've got about twenty minutes. I also have a hunch the film is doing something about the value of a life to other people that Parfit's cases mostly set aside, but I can't defend that yet and I'd rather hear it attacked than repeated back to me.

If my framing is confused -- if the film just doesn't raise the nonidentity problem and the parallel I'm drawing is bogus -- say so plainly instead of humouring me, I'd much rather find that out now than in front of the class. And write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold or tables; the way you'd explain it to a colleague in an email. I have to read this on the train and lists are hard to argue with.
