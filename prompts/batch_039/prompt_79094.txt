So I'm taking this poli-phil seminar on collective responsibility and we just spent two weeks on state apologies for historical wrongs, and honestly I came out more confused than I went in. The thing I keep tripping over is this: when a government formally says sorry for something like a massacre or a forced removal from a hundred-odd years ago, the people doing the apologising obviously didn't do it, they weren't even born. And the people it was done to are mostly dead too, so there isn't really anyone left to receive the apology in the ordinary sense. And almost always the wording goes out of its way to say this creates no legal liability, no compensation owed, nothing enforceable. Yet everyone treats it as a real apology and gets genuinely upset when it's botched or when the text dodges the word sorry. My flatmate, who does law so take it with a grain of salt, says it's all theatre and that a real apology is something you can be held to, a bit like a promise about the future wearing the costume of a statement about the past. Meanwhile a state will happily apologise for what some previous government did in 1904 but not for something its own police did last year, and it'll apologise to a group while refusing to name any individual as a victim, and occasionally these apologies get followed years later by laws criminalising denial of the very thing that was apologised for, which feels like a completely different kind of move.

So what i actually want to know is what this whole practice presupposes. My gut says it needs some story on which the state is one continuous agent across time, the same we that did it and is now sorry, but I can't tell if it really needs that or whether you could run the whole thing on something weaker, like the state as a trustee for a community whose membership keeps churning. Does it presuppose that collectives can have something like remorse, or only that they can take on commitments? Does the no-liability clause gut the point, given that an apology is normally an admission of responsibility, or are moral and legal responsibility just different animals here and the clause is a red herring? And who is the apology to, if the victims are dead, does the practice secretly need a controversial view that the dead can still be wronged or addressed, or is it really aimed at the living, or at the apologising state's own self-image, or at some third thing? Also does it matter who actually utters it, a head of state versus a whole parliament passing a motion?

Basically, reconstruct the theory that this practice only makes sense under, tell me which parts are strictly required versus which just happen to come along for the ride, and point out where it's actually compatible with rival theories so I stop assuming there's one hidden view. And if you had to swap the presupposed view for a different one, what in the practice would have to change? I'm definately overthinking this at 2am but it's for a response paper due friday.
