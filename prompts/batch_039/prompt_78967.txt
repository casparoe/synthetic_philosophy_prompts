I'm finalizing the take-home portion of the final for my upper-level seminar on mind, machines and moral status. The whole term has been about which computations, if any, make a system a candidate for mattering morally — the usual suspects (global-workspace-ish views, higher-order and self-model views, integrated-information-ish views, plus the deflationary line that says this is just the problem of other minds wearing a lab coat). The problem is that every question I've drafted so far can be answered by a student who memorized what the theories say without understanding a single thing about why anyone believes them, and I get 40 of those. So I invented a toy case and I want the question built around it. Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (20 total), with a one-line note per point on how students usually drop the mark.

Here's the case, which should go in the question itself. Take a robot vacuum with three modules. M1 is a simple evaluator: it maps sensor readings to a scalar badness score. M2 is a predictor trained by gradient descent to forecast M1's next several scores from M1's current internal state, with no other input. M3 reads M2's forecasts and uses them to adjust the robot's own learning rate — when M2 predicts a bad stretch coming, the robot adapts faster so it gets out ahead of the trouble. Now vary three things independently. (a) M2 is trained offline and then frozen, versus M2 trained online with its forecasts actually driving M3 in the loop. (b) M2's forecasts are genuinely about M1's internal states, versus M2 is just a stale copy of M1 running one step ahead of it. (c) The whole thing is implemented as a lookup table, versus a spiking network, versus a dish of cultured neurons wired up the same way.

What I want the question to force is: which of these variations, if any, could possibly make a moral difference, and why; what work the word about is doing in (b) and whether the student means it in a tracking/causal-covariance sense or a teleosemantic or predictive-processing sense, and whether their verdict changes if they pick a different one; and whether the variation in (c) is a red herring or the whole ballgame. The crux should be this: does moral status attach to the computational role a state plays, or to the kind of stuff realizing it, or to something that is neither of those — and can the student say what evidence would actually move them, rather than just gesturing at uncertainty. I want the A/B split to come from whether they can keep the role-versus-realization distinction and the descriptive-to-normative step seperate, and whether they notice that (a) is where the attribution question really bites: a frozen M2 makes forecasts that are about M1 without anything in the system using them as being about M1, and I want to see if they can say whether that matters morally or only epistemically. Don't let the model answer just list positions — it should take a side and defend it, and the marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer has to make, not just the topics it has to mention.
