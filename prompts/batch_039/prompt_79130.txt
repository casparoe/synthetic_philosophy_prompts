So I'm in this reading group and one guy keeps bringing up the Stoic archer picture — you do everything right, you nock the arrow, draw, aim, release, and then a gust of wind knocks it off course; the hitting isn't up to you, only the shooting is. I think Cicero reports it in his account of Stoic ethics but don't lean on that, I'm going off memory. He uses it to argue we should stop caring about outcomes altogether: if you didn't get the job, if the election went the wrong way, if your book didn't sell, then since the outcome wasn't up to you, feeling disappointed is a mistake, and the person who's really internalized the analogy feels nothing about the result. He also says this is why he's stopped bothering with activism — the results aren't in his control, so wanting them is the error. It's a pretty common move in the modern Stoicism stuff, and alot of it traces back to the idea that some things are up to us and some aren't.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a summary of Stoicism. What maps onto what — the archer, the skill, the aim, the wind, the target, the hit, the score, the spectators — a table of correspondences is fine. Then where does the mapping break? In particular: does the ancient version of the image actually license not caring about the target, or does it only license not staking your happiness on the hit? Does the archer who genuinely doesn't care whether the arrow lands actually shoot as well as the one who does? What happens when the wind is other people who are watching you shoot and adjusting their own aim in response, and when you adjust your next shot based on where the last one went, so the outcome is part of the process rather than just downstream of it? And what does the picture quietly smuggle in by making it a single shot at a static target at a fixed distance instead of a long series of shots? Once the disanalogies are on the table, tell me which ones actually matter for his conclusion and which are just trivia, whether the conclusion — no disappointment, no activism — survives, and if it doesn't, what a better analogy would be, one that keeps whatever real insight is in the archer picture without carrying his conclusion for him.
