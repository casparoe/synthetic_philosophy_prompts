I am the research lead for a small independent production company making a six-part documentary on the Late Bronze Age collapse. Our presenter, a historian with a taste for methodological slogans, has asked us to assess every factual claim in the series using a principle she calls the asymmetry of survival. As she states it, the principle says that for any claim about the distant past the first question is not whether the surviving source could be lying, but what the record would have looked like if the claim were false, and whether that counter-record would have been likely to survive. If the same processes that destroyed the counter-evidence—fire, the recycling of metal, the bias of archives towards royal and administrative centres, the accidents of excavation—would also have destroyed the evidence we do have, then the survival of the claim is weak evidence for it. If the claim's truth would have generated traces in several independent media that ought to have survived, and those traces are absent, then the absence is evidence against. She also insists on distinguishing three kinds of silence: expected silence, surprising silence, and silence manufactured by later selection. At the moment this is a slogan, not a method. I need you to turn it into a procedure my researchers can actually run.

The procedure will be used dozens of times per episode, under time pressure, by people with good history degrees but no training in formal epistemology or statistics. The stakes are not academic: whatever survives the procedure will be narrated in the presenter's voice, may be clipped and circulated online, and could end up in schools. The procedure has to fit on one side of A4, be expressible as a checklist or a set of tests to run in order, and be usable without any specialist literature to hand. Please state the questions in the order they should be asked, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should stop and return a verdict of unusable or usable only with qualification, and where it must hand the decision to the presenter because the remaining question is one of interpretation or editorial judgement rather than evidence. Be explicit about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure cannot do. No citations or reading lists, please; I need the method itself.

Then run the procedure on three claims we expect to face. First, a claim that a particular coastal city in the eastern Mediterranean was sacked by the Sea Peoples around 1200 BC, where the evidence is a destruction layer, a later Greek tradition, and an Egyptian inscription that may or may not refer to the same event. Second, a claim that a royal inscription's account of a victory is substantially inflated, where the evidence is the inscription itself, the absence of any mention of the campaign in the defeated power's own records, and the fact that the inscription was composed decades after the event. Third, a claim that a specific queen led a coalition against an invading army, which the presenter loves because it makes a dramatic opening sequence, but which rests on a single ambiguous text and no independent corroboration. I want to see the procedure give an answer the presenter will not like in at least one of these cases, and I want the write-up to say plainly where the procedure runs out and a human being has to decide.
