A freind of mine hosts a tiny design podcast and I've offered to write the questions for next month's episode. The guest is a type designer who takes a hard line on letter identity: she says an 'a' has no essence. What makes an 'a' an 'a', on her view, is a loose genre — a set of roles the parts play (something that encloses, something that bears the weight, a way of closing the top) — plus the fact that readers have learned to see it that way. The genre tolerates almost any amount of reshaping, so there's no shape that all 'a's share. She calls these letter genres, which I think she nicked from Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit thing, but don't hold me to that.

I want eight to ten interview questions that actually press her on this rather than letting her restate it. Please work in: (i) a question about why 'a' and 'e' don't just merge if the genre is that permissive, and whether letter identity is really relational — each letter being what it is only by contrast with the others in the same font; (ii) one on the single-storey 'a' versus the double-storey 'a', whether they're the same letter, and what she says about children who meet both at once; (iii) one on the looptail 'g', which people read constantly but mostly can't write — does that put the genre in the eye rather than the hand, or does it undercut her? (iv) one asking her to separate a badly drawn 'a' from a different letter altogether, and whether she's happy to say the category is vague, so that some marks are neither determinately 'a' nor determinately anything else; (v) one on convention — if the genre is held together by use, could a community just decide that what we call 'q' is now an 'a', and if so, why can't a reader re-identify letters at will? Each of the main questions wants a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. She says it's a genre, not a shape, about twice an episode, so build the sequence so that line gets harder to reach for each time.

Lay it out like this: Openers (2 questions, no follow-ups, one sentence each); The view, on its own terms (3 questions with a follow-up each, two sentences max per question); Where it pinches (4 questions with a follow-up each); The last question (1 question, no follow-up). British English please.
