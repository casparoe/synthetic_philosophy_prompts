Ok, I've been chewing on Ulysses contracts for years and I want to actually pin one down instead of hand-waving, so I want a precommitment case built to order. Specs below.

Setup: one person, present day, ordinary stakes — a career, a relationship, savings, a habit — nothing life-or-death. No sci-fi, no brain implants, no drugs, no enforcer with unusual powers. A friend holding money in escrow is fine, so is a contract, so is a public commitment. Give it names and numbers so it reads like an actual situation.

The part I actually care about: at the moment the person tries to get out of it, they are completely with it. Calm, informed, no craving, no temporary mood doing the driving, not depressed, not manic, nobody leaning on them, and they can give a perfectly articulate account of why they're backing out and what the earlier self was up to. They'd pass any competence test you want to throw at them. And I still want the intuitive verdict to be that the arrangement holds, or at least that the earlier self's say-so has genuine authority rather than being a promise the later self is free to shred.

I want that bindingness to fall out of one of these, and I want you to tell me which one is really doing the work:

(a) the person is one agent across time, and the later moment is a defection from a plan the agent as a whole endorsed;
(b) third parties rearranged their own lives around the commitment;
(c) the later self is trying to keep the benefits the commitment bought and skip the cost, which is a fairness/reciprocity failure rather than a competence failure.

I'm definately most interested in (c), because I've only ever seen that move in the political obligation literature and never in the personal case, and I genuinely can't tell if it transfers or falls apart.

Then give me a second version of the case that's identical except for one feature, where the revocation *is* valid — and say exactly what that one feature is. For both versions, run three views and state their verdicts: the separate-time-slices view (the later self is a different person and nobody can bind them), the extended-planner view, and the fairness view. For each, say where in the case the verdict comes from.

What I'm after at the end: which feature is load-bearing, whether pulling it out collapses the thing into a boring ordinary promise, and how someone who thinks the later self can always walk would attack the case. Also say whether the same structure would work for a constitution binding a later generation, or whether the dead-hand objection kills it there.

Two things I don't want you doing to make it work: don't let the earlier self have information the later one lacks, and don't have the later self's preferences be "distorted" in a way I'd call a competence failure. The whole point is that the later self is fine. If the case only comes out the way I want because you secretly made them not fine, just tell me that's what's happening, and if two of my constraints pull against each other, say so instead of quietly dropping one.
