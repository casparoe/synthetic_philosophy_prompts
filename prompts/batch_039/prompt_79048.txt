I sit on the ethics committee of my professional association. Until recently the subject of online sanctioning was abstract to me; then a six-week campaign to have me dropped from a conference programme and an editorial board taught me something I have not been able to set aside. What struck me was not the hostility but the shapelessness: no one could tell me what the penalty was, who had imposed it, or when it would end. The committee now hears roughly a dozen complaints a year, nearly all of them about things members have said or done online, and we dispose of them ad hoc, by feel, in monthly meetings. I want a procedure we can actually run. We are not a court and can compel nothing; the worst we can do is expel someone, the best is to say something publicly.

I have no philosophical training beyond one undergraduate course, and neither do the other eight members, so the procedure has to do the work a theory would do without our having to hold the theory in our heads. I am told the philosophy of punishment has something to say here, and I want the substance of it, not a reading list. Tell me which of the following distinctions can survive translation into a committee procedure and which must be abandoned: the difference between the hard treatment a sanction imposes and the censure it expresses; the thought that a punishment answers to a determinate authority, with a stated sentence and a terminus, whereas what happened to me had no author and no end; the difference between punishing an offence and taking protective measures against a risk; the requirement that the offence be against a rule that existed and was knowable beforehand; the requirement of standing; and the aggregation problem, in which thousands of small sanctions by unconnected people sum to a punishment no one imposed and no one can remit. Tell me also whether the punishment a member is already receiving from strangers should count toward what we impose, and in which direction.

Then set out the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Fix the menu of things we can do — a private word, formal censure, suspension, expulsion, removal from office, a public statement, nothing — and say how the procedure selects a quantum and an end date. Our recommendation goes to the board, and any public statement needs the president's signature.

Run it on three cases: a member who made a crude joke about a group on a personal account, which was screenshotted and circulated; a member accused of years of private harassment of a junior colleague, documented, with his employer already investigating; and a member whose conduct at the annual meeting has quietly stopped junior members from attending. I expect the procedure to tell me to do something in one of these that I will find hard, having been on the receiving end, and to decline in another where declining will be read as cowardice. Say where it will misfire, and how it will be gamed.
