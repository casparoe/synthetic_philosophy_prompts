I have been reading Michael Dummett on immigration and refugees, and I have settled on a package of views that I would like audited before I commit to it. Here is where I have landed. (1) A state's right to exclude prospective immigrants is not a basic moral right; the burden of justification falls on the state that would exclude, not on the person who would enter. (2) The distinction between refugees and economic migrants is not morally fundamental; a liberal state that admits refugees while turning away economic migrants is drawing a line its own premises do not underwrite. (3) Nonetheless, democratic self-government requires that a demos have some control over its own composition, since a community that cannot regulate who belongs to it cannot be said to govern itself. (4) Cultural continuity is not a legitimate ground for exclusion, because the same reasoning that would justify excluding immigrants to preserve a culture would justify excluding internal minorities whose presence alters local culture. (5) Wealthy states have duties of justice, not merely charity, toward the global poor, and restrictive immigration policy is frequently a means of evading those duties. Please tell me which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and which are mutually reinforcing. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say precisely which commitment would have to be weakened or abandoned, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs. I do not know which of these is load-bearing and which is merely decorative, so please be specific about what commits me to what rather than reassuring me that the package is coherent.
