I did a PhD in economics and now work on programme evaluation, so my entire mental furniture is potential outcomes, identification strategies, treatment effect heterogeneity, spillovers, partial identification bounds, that sort of thing. Lately I've been reading moral philosophy in the evenings and I keep bumbing into a worry that goes roughly like this: consequentialism tells you to do whatever has the best total consequences, but the total consequences of anything you do fan out into the future without limit — the fifty quid you give to a charity changes who meets whom, which changes which children get born, which changes the whole subsequent history of the world — so any attempt to actually compute and compare the total consequences of your options is hopeless. Some versions of the worry seem to be about us not being able to know all this; others seem darker, i.e. the suggestion that even with perfect information there'd be no fact of the matter about the total value, because it's the wrong kind of quantity to add up in the first place. I might be inventing that distinction, I'm not sure.

What I want is a translation exercise, done properly and at length. Take this worry — both versions of it, if there really are two — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: estimands, counterfactual outcomes, no-interference and SUTVA violations, equilibrium and general-equilibrium effects, external validity, model misspecification, partial identification, decision under ambiguity, heavy tails, and so on. Answer as someone who works seriously on both sides of this — a philosopher of science who has actually read the causal inference literature rather than just heard of it. Then give me the residue report, which is the bit I really care about: which parts of the philosophers' worry survive the translation cleanly, which survive only if you bend my vocabulary in ways I wouldn't accept at a seminar, and which have no counterpart in it at all. For that last category, say honestly whether the gap is a defect of my framework — something causal inference can't represent and ought to — or a sign that the original claim was vaguer or more rhetorical than it looked once you try to make it precise. Concretely: if I sat down and wrote out the estimand for the effect of my donating fifty quid on total welfare, would identification fail, would the bounds come out vacuous, or would the estimand itself fail to be well-defined? Those strike me as very different failures and I suspect the philosophical debate slides between them without noticing.

Please don't smuggle my assumptions in and call it translation — don't quietly assume the outcome variable is a scalar, or that interference can be handled as a known network effect, if that's exactly what's at stake. Start by brainstorming the mapping out loud, then do the translation, then the residue, and flag every place where you've had to fudge it.
