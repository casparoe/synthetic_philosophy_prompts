hey, I'm trying to finish a short essay for a friend's reading group and I'd love your comments on it before I send it round. it's about whether "corruption" is really one thing or two. here's the draft, warts and all:

The one-liner you always hear from the watchdogs and the development agencies is that corruption is the abuse of entrusted power for private gain. Everybody nods and moves on. But that formula is doing something sneaky: it treats corruption as a single kind of act (an abuse, i.e. a betrayal plus a concealed exchange) and then stamps that template onto everything.

I want to argue there are two quite different things travelling under the name. First, what I'll call predatory corruption: an official or a strongman simply takes, demands, or diverts, and gives back nothing in the way of obligation. The clerk who won't stamp your paper unless you pay him. The minister who wires the ministry's budget into his cousin's shell company. This is recognisable in every society I can think of; nobody needs a Western manual to see it as a wrong. The wrong here is extraction: it takes from people who have no claim on you and no reciprocal hold over you.

Second, what I'll call relational corruption: payments, gifts, favours, jobs, introductions, marriages, all running along a web of mutual obligation — guanxi, wasta, the whole gift economy the anthropologists have been describing for a century. Here the act isn't a one-way extraction, it's a node in a reciprocal system. The person giving expects to be able to call the favour in later, and the person taking knows they now owe. Judged from inside that web, the exchange isn't theft, it's duty. Judged from outside, it looks exactly like bribery.

Now the standard move at this point is the anthropology defence: you're just imposing Western norms. I think that's too quick, and the interesting work is in seperating the two. Relational corruption is genuinely objectionable — but not for the reason the watchdogs give. It isn't wrong because it's dishonest, or because someone betrayed a trust. It's wrong because it converts a public role into private property, and so distributes access by who you know. That's a fairness complaint, not an honesty complaint. The outsider with no uncle in the ministry is being excluded, and excluded by design. That seems like a real wrong, and it doesn't lean on any particularly Western ideal of the cold arm's-length bureaucrat.

So here's the test I want to propose, and I suspect it's the weakest part of the whole thing. Imagine the exact same act — same amount, same discretion, same consequences — but performed between strangers with no prior relationship and no expectation of ever meeting again. If it still strikes you as wrong, you're tracking the predatory core. If the wrongness evaporates, and it starts to look like a normal market transaction with extra steps, then what you were objecting to was the relational form, and you now owe an account of why the relational form is bad that doesn't secretly smuggle the stranger case back in. Call it the stranger test.

The test is meant to do two jobs. It's meant to stop people dressing up predation as cultural relativism — the clerk extracting from a stranger fails it and gets exposed as extraction. And it's meant to stop people dressing up a preference for impersonal bureaucracy as a moral universal, since a lot of what gets called corruption is really a complaint about intimacy, patronage and obligation, and we should just say so.

One more wrinkle, and I'm not sure it fits. The reciprocity has to be real. A lot of petty corruption gets defended as gift exchange, but it's one-directional — the official never gives anything back except not making your life harder. That's not a gift, that's a toll. The gift-exchange point that reciprocity only bites when both sides have something to lose means the anthropology defence, wherever it works at all, is far narrower than its friends think.

So, is any of this any good? Concretely: (1) is the predatory/relational split a real distinction, or have I just renamed bribery and clientelism and called it a day? (2) does the stranger test actually work, or can you think of a case where it gives the wrong answer — I worry it quietly assumes that only relationship-independent wrongs are real wrongs. (3) am I strawmanning the universalist? plenty of people who use the watchdog definition would happily agree fairness is the issue, in which case I've spent two thousand words arguing with nobody. (4) anywhere I'm just factually off about how these systems actually work? I've never lived anywhere with a functioning patronage network, so I'm going on reading. thanks!
