I've been reading LessWrong, SSC-adjacent blogs, and the old rationalist forums for like a decade, and I keep running into the one-liner 'you can't lie to yourself' -- usually deployed to end a conversation about self-deception before it starts. Last week it came up in a group chat about a friend of mine who spent two years telling everyone (and, as far as anyone could tell, sincerely believing) that his startup was about to take off, while simultaneously making backup plans that only made sense if he thought it was already dead. Someone said: 'That's not self-deception, you can't lie to yourself; he must have known and just been lying to us.' I'm definately not buying that as a conversation-ender, but I also can't articulate why. I want the slogan taken apart. Don't give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations -- I can't look anything up right now and I don't want a tour of the literature; I want the conceptual menu.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims 'you can't lie to yourself' could be making, from the boring/trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it's true, false, or just a restatement of a definition? I'm not asking for the empirical question of whether self-deception happens; I want the conceptual menu of what the slogan could even mean. I'm especially interested in which readings assume a single unified self doing the lying and being lied to, and which become live options once you replace that with something like a committee, a set of subagents, or a partitioned mind. There's also a guy in my Discord with this half-baked 'two-ledger' idea: one ledger for what you'd avow in public, one for what you actually act on, and self-deception is when the second ledger is written in a code the first ledger can't read. I don't know if that's a real position or just him being clever, but I'd like you to use it as a stress test: which readings of the slogan survive if the two-ledger picture is even coherent, and which ones does it refute? And which reading is the one people usually mean when they use the slogan to shut down the possibility of self-deception? Make it clear which readings would actually support the move 'he must have known and just been lying to us' and which wouldn't.

Give it to me in four sections, in this order: 'The slogan and the move it was making' (one short paragraph, just restating what it was being used to do in that chat); 'The menu of readings' (the bulk, maybe 700-1000 words, numbered, each with a one-line statement of the precise claim and a sentence on what kind of self/agency it presupposes); 'Verdicts' (a bullet list or small table, 200-300 words, saying for each reading whether it's trivial, substantive-and-defensible, or false, and whether it would actually support 'he must have known'); and 'What the argument actually needed' (150-250 words, saying which reading the slogan-user probably meant and whether that reading does the work). Also, if the two-ledger thing is incoherent, say so plainly in the menu rather than forcing it to generate a reading.
