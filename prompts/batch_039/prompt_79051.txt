A textbook presentation of the axiom of choice usually begins with a picture rather than a formula: given any family of non-empty sets, we are entitled to select one member from each. I would like a careful conceptual treatment of what that picture actually commits us to, and of where it fails to be a description of anything anyone could do.

To make the discussion concrete, imagine an agent — call it a sequential chooser — which, whenever it is presented with a non-empty set, successfully designates one of that set's members, and which can repeat this operation indefinitely, each designation being made only after the preceding one has been settled, so that it eventually produces an infinite sequence of selections. In Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory without the axiom of choice, the principle that such an agent can always proceed for countably many steps is available as a named axiom, is strictly weaker than the full axiom of choice, and is nonetheless strong enough to develop most of ordinary analysis; in a model of set theory satisfying it, every set of real numbers may be Lebesgue measurable. It is also a theorem that if the chooser's power is extended to arbitrarily long well-ordered sequences of choices, the result is equivalent to the full axiom of choice.

Please explain, conceptually rather than by restating the axioms in their standard formulations, why the sequential chooser's power yields the weak principle but not the axiom of choice. I would like the explanation to address at least the following three points.

First, what precisely the chooser cannot do once the family it faces is arbitrary rather than countable — say, the family of all non-empty sets of real numbers, or a family of unordered pairs whose members carry no distinguishing feature — and why the obstruction is not simply that the chooser would need more time, more steps, or a longer sequence in which to finish the job.

Second, why the obvious repair — let the chooser work through the members of the family one at a time, however many members there are — is unavailable as stated. What would have to be assumed about the family's index set for the iterative picture even to be well formed, and why that assumption cannot be had for free? In particular, why does the well-ordering theorem fail to license the iteration, given that it is equivalent to the very axiom whose iterative reading is in question?

Third, what the chooser's shortfall reveals about the status of the axiom of choice. Is it a claim about which objects there are, or a claim about which selections can be specified, singled out, or defined by a rule? Does that distinction survive scrutiny once we notice that in ZF a choice function for a definable family — the smallest member of each set of natural numbers, the left member of each pair of shoes — is often provable outright, while for an arbitrary family no formula can be exhibited at all?

Please close by saying whether the sequential chooser is a coherent model of a mathematical agent or whether the picture is an artefact of imagining set-theoretic existence as a kind of activity performed in time. I am after the diagnosis of how simultaneity, definability and existence come apart here, not a survey of the axiom's uses and abuses.
