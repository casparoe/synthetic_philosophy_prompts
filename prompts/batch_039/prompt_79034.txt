I am a graduate student in environmental history, and I have been trying to get clear on the status of the Anthropocene as a historical period. As I understand it, the question has been settled at the geological end: the Anthropocene Working Group proposed a formal epoch beginning in 1950, with Crawford Lake in Ontario as the golden spike, and the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the International Union of Geological Sciences ratified that proposal. If that is right, then the only remaining disputes are downstream ones—about whether historians should adopt the term, which earlier start dates are rhetorically useful, and whether the concept is too totalizing to be explanatory. But in my seminar, the debate keeps reopening: people propose 1784, 1945, the Great Acceleration, the Columbian Exchange, and even the Neolithic, and they argue about whether the Anthropocene is a period at all or rather a way of periodizing that smuggles in a political agenda.

What I cannot tell is whether this is just a case of humanists refusing to accept a scientific consensus, or whether there is something about the geology itself that keeps the question open. I have also come across a distinction between chronostratigraphy and geochronology, and the suggestion that the Anthropocene might be better understood as an event than as an epoch, but I do not know how that distinction bears on the question of when it started or who decides. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is—whether it is a factual dispute about the stratigraphic record, a conceptual dispute about the units of geological time, a political dispute about authority over periodization, or several of these at once—and say what, if anything, would count as settling it? Please be explicit about how confident you are in your diagnosis and identify the point you are least sure about.
