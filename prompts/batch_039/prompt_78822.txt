So I'm a third-year PhD student in logic — proof theory, so I'm not the philosophy person — and my department is putting on a two-day workshop on Godel's philosophical views next month. The whole thing was my supervisor's idea, mostly an excuse to get a dozen people in a room to argue about whether the Platonism is seperable from the theism. He was supposed to give the after-dinner remarks at the closing dinner and has since realised he'll be at a wedding abroad, so I've been volunteered. It's a long table in the hall with all the portraits, about forty of us, and the room splits cleanly in two: on one side the logicians, including two people who think Gödel's philosophy is a mild embarrassment and would rather be talking about forcing; on the other side a couple of historians of maths, my partner, my partner's parents (who are lovely and believe I do something with computers), my supervisor's retired collaborator, and five undergrads who came for the free wine. It has to work for both halves.

What I'd like from you is the speech, five or six minutes. The thing I really want it to do is take Gödel's belief in an afterlife seriously as philosophy — not as a charming quirk of a great man, not as something we smile at and then move past — which means showing how it hangs together with everything else rather than floating free: the Platonism, the rationalism, the fourteen-point creed he left in his papers, the theism he said was Leibniz's rather than Spinoza's, the letters where he argued his mother into it, and the modal proof he sat on for about thirty years and finally let Dana Scott copy out in 1970 when he thought he was dying (that whole episode I find quietly moving and I'd like it in there, though the speech obviously can't turn into a lecture about the proof itself). It should also be honest that almost nobody in the room, me included, believes any of it — and honest about that in a way that isn't a wink.

The thing I'm most worried about is the ending drifting into 'there are truths we can't reach, and maybe that's where he is now' — the incompleteness-as-metaphor-for-the-beyond move, which every after-dinner speaker reaches for and which conflates a limitative theorem with an epistemic gap. I want the speech to inoculate the room against that move explicitly, then say where the actual weak link in his chain of reasoning is (name it, don't hand-wave it), and then say why that doesn't spoil what we're doing tonight. Please don't quote him — I can't check quotes and I refuse to be the guy who misquotes Gödel at a Gödel dinner, so paraphrase. Finish with a toast: one line, short enough that the non-philosophers feel it and the logicians can't get me for it. Oh, and my supervisor gets a copy, so nothing about him.
