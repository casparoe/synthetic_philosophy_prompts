I am putting together a ninety-minute seminar meeting on the moral status of fictional beings, and I want to spend it on Undertale. Assume the group knows the game — its routes, its endings, its mercy system — so there is no need to summarize the plot, and please do not quote dialogue; I want criticism, not exegesis.

The question I want the session organized around is the one the general topic usually blurs: what exactly is being claimed when we say that video-game characters matter morally? At least three separate claims get run together — that wrongs can be committed inside the fiction's own moral world; that the player's real actions, or attitudes, are assessable in the real world even though the characters are not; and that the game, as an artifact, can address a moral demand to a real person. Read Undertale as an argument about which of these it is making.

Its mechanics should count as part of the argument: the mercy and act systems; the renaming of the standard RPG statistics so that one turns out to be measured in executions and the other to stand for a level of violence; a save file that functions as a moral record and is remembered across resets; the endings, in which a save file can be spent or destroyed and in which the being addressed is not the avatar but the person holding the controller. Bring the philosophical literature to bear only where it genuinely fits — the paradox of fiction, Walton on make-believe and quasi-emotions, Gendler on alief if you think the player's guilt is best explained by a state that is not a belief, the gamer's dilemma about virtual wrongs, and the fictionalist reply that no one is harmed because no one is there to be harmed. If a brief contrast with one other game sharpens the point — BioShock's Little Sisters, whose fate is wired into a resource economy, or the androids of Detroit: Become Human — you may use it, but keep the reading on Undertale.

I want the reading disciplined in both directions: where does the game hold a defensible position — say, that a game can legitimately hold you to account for the attitudes it asks you to take up even if no fictional being is wronged — and where does it equivocate, sliding between the claim that you did this and the weaker claim that you were made to feel that you did? Which mechanics and scenes should I put in front of the group to make that disagreement live, and what would a good philosopher of fiction say the game gets right that the papers on virtual wrongs do not, or that it exploits for effect rather than argues for?

Finally, since I will be teaching from this: mark what is settled in this area and what is genuinely contested among people who work on it — whether fictional beings can be wronged at all, whether virtual actions are morally inert, whether a game can put a real moral demand on a player — and do not let a contested position read as the consensus. Where your own reading is one of the contestable ones, say so.
