I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to two decades now, and the thing that keeps me coming back is the habit of asking what a philosophical puzzle would look like if somebody actually had to build the thing and sell it. I'm putting together a piece in that spirit for a small forum I write for, and I'd like you to draft the artefact itself rather than an essay about it: an online product page for a supertask device. The device is the lamp from the old puzzle. It is switched on with half a minute remaining on the clock, off with a quarter of a minute remaining, on with an eighth remaining, and so on; after one minute, infinitely many switchings have been completed and there is no last one, so the lamp is on after every odd-numbered switching and off after every even-numbered one, and no switching is the final one. The question the puzzle turns on is what state the lamp is in at the one-minute mark. I want the page to consist of a short spec sheet and six or eight customer reviews with star ratings, plus a couple of replies from the manufacturer. The humour has to come from the puzzle and not from zaniness: a customer whose claim was refused because the lamp's condition at the limit is not something the warranty covers; a customer who paid extra for the model where you stipulate the final state at checkout and now wants to know whether they have discovered the state or chosen it; somebody who has been using the lamp to run other infinite tasks and is irritated at being asked for evidence; a physicist complaining about the accumulation point, the unbounded acceleration of the switch, and the silence of the spec sheet on Planck times; and a reviewer who thinks the whole thing is a muddle of language and gives it two stars on that basis. Nobody in the piece should ever remark that this is a philosophical problem; the shop should behave exactly like a shop, and the manufacturer should be mildly defensive in the way hardware vendors are.

Around 800 words, deadpan from start to finish, in British English. The bar I'd like to hold you to: somebody who has thought about this puzzle should be able to tell from the reviews that you know the difference between a lamp that is in no state at the limit and a lamp whose state at the limit is merely undetermined, and that you treat the stipulation of a final state as a real reply rather than a swindle; somebody who has never met the puzzle should still be laughing by the fourth review. The satire should be aimed at the assumption that a complete description of a process must settle its outcome, and at the corporate instinct to convert an ill-posed question into a customer-service matter — not at any particular person. After the page, give me a JSON object with these fields: listing_title (string); reviews (array of objects, each with handle (string), stars (integer 1–5), gripe (one sentence), conceptual_issue (a short label for the issue that review turns on), and verdict (one of sound, confused, question-begging, stipulative)); manufacturer_position (the company's official line in one sentence); and hardest_open_question (the question the page leaves standing).
