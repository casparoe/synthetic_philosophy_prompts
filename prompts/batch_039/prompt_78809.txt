OK, I need a thought experiment built to spec for a chapter I'm writing on forgeries, and I don't want the museum-guide version everyone trots out.

The set-up: three paintings, indistinguishable by any test we could actually run — pigments, canvas, craquelure, brushwork, the lot. (1) The original, done by the artist in 1889. (2) A replica the same artist made a year later for a patron, authorised, identical in every respect, same hand and same materials. (3) A copy made in 1925 by a skilled restorer using period materials, which then passed as the original for sixty years. Here's the twist I need: it's the 1925 copy the tradition actually absorbed. Everyone who mattered copied it, taught it, painted against it. The genuine original sat in a crate and was never exhibited. So if there's such a thing as an object's causal footprint on the later history of painting, that property belongs to the fake and not to the original. Keep it realistic, no sci-fi duplicating rays, and the scenario itself in one paragraph.

Then make the case earn its verdicts instead of just asserting them. Run through what each of these says about the three objects and about what a fully informed viewer ought to get out of them: (a) someone who thinks aesthetic value supervenes entirely on what's experienceable; (b) someone who thinks being by the artist's own hand is itself aesthetically relevant; (c) someone who thinks what matters is the object's causal role in the tradition. I need (b) to land in an awkward spot over the authorised replica, and (c) to end up ranking the forgery first — if either doesn't fall out of the case, fix the case rather than stipulating it. Then tell me which single feature is doing the work, what happens to the whole thing if I delete that feature, and how the defender of whichever view comes off worst will say I definately rigged it.
