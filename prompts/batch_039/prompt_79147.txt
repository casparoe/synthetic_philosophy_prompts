I'm prepping a session for a reading group on why historical analogies go bad — Munich, Vietnam, the Thucydides trap, all that — and a friend of mine keeps insisting the real argument is sitting in a couple of sentences in Thucydides, Book 3, the Corcyra civil war bit, and that everything anyone writes about the "lessons of history" is downstream of it. I dont really see it. The bits he means:

"In peace and prosperity states and individuals have better sentiments, because they do not find themselves suddenly confronted with imperious necessities; but war takes away the easy supply of daily wants, and so proves a rough master, that brings most men's characters to a level with their fortunes."

and a bit further on:

"Words had to change their ordinary meaning and to take that which was now given them. Reckless audacity came to be considered the courage of a loyal ally; prudent hesitation, specious cowardice; moderation was held to be a cloak for unmanliness; ability to see all sides of a question inaptness to act on any."

What I want help with, concretely: I can see that the second passage is about language getting bent out of shape while a city tears itself apart. What I can't see is how that's supposed to be an argument about historical analogies. My friend's claim is that the usual objections to Munich-style reasoning — the cases aren't similar enough, hindsight bias, the analogy is really just a policy preference in costume — all miss a sharper point, which is that some analogies are self-sealing: once you've invoked one, the words you'd need in order to argue against it have already been redefined, so "let's slow down" now just means "appeasement" and the argument is over before it starts. He calls this vocabulary capture. Is that actually in the text, or is he reading 20th century debates back into a 5th century civil war?

I'd like three things out of this:

1. A patient walk through what each passage is doing in its own setting, and how they're related. Is the bit about words a consequence of the bit about war as a rough master, or a further stage, or something else entirely? I keep going back and forth on whether Thucydides is making a causal claim about scarcity and fear, or a claim about what people do with language when they're frightened, and I'm not sure the text decides.

2. A judgment on whether "vocabulary capture" is a fair extraction or a distortion. If it's fair, what would it predict about a concrete case — say the way "who lost China" style framing made negotiation look like surrender in the early Vietnam years — without just repeating the standard disanalogy complaints. If it's a distortion, say exactly where the extraction goes wrong.

3. Two or three diagnostic questions I could hand the group, so they can take some live analogy (a current one, not Munich) and ask whether the vocabulary has been rigged, as opposed to the analogy just failing on the facts. And one example where the honest answer is the second one, so they dont walk away thinking every analogy is a trap.

No bibliography please, I just want the reasoning. And flag anything in here that would make a classicist wince.
