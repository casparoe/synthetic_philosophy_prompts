Second-year undergrads, 18 of them, one 75-minute slot, and the topic is the problem of induction -- Hume first, then the green/grue business. The textbook version definately lands flat: they nod along at the future-resembles-the-past thing and then go right back to expecting the sun to rise. What I want is for them to feel the squeeze in their hands, before I name anything: (a) they can't help projecting, (b) the data they've got doesn't pick out the rule they project, and (c) the only thing separating the rule they like from the ones that make them uneasy seems to be something about us and our habits, not about the world.

So design me a runnable classroom activity, not a lecture. Here's the shape I'm imagining -- change whatever needs changing if you've got a better mechanic, but keep the load-bearing bits:

- They make real predictions with real (small) stakes -- tokens, points, whatever, no money.
- At least one prediction has to resolve inside the session, so it's genuinely checkable, and I need to be able to say honestly that the outcome was fixed in advance and I didn't decide it on the fly.
- Somewhere in the middle the participants themselves have to manufacture the rival hypotheses rather than be handed them. I want each group to take the rule they're betting on and produce, from a template I give them, a rival that fits every observation so far and disagrees later -- then have the room verify that the rivals fit just as well. The move I'm after is the moment they say that's cheating and can't say what cheating means without already assuming the answer.
- There should be a bit where the only difference between two candidate rules is a record of past usage -- one has been used forty times over the term, the other never -- and they have to try to justify preferring the used one without appealing to its being the natural or real one.
- Optional, if it's worth the shopping trip: I can get thermochromic beads or chips, green at room temp and blue once you've held them a while. If that lets you build a version where the rival hypothesis is not merely consistent with the data but actually true of the objects, and the flip is a real event in the room, do that. If not, give me the version where the outcome sits in a sealed envelope and I'm honest about that.

What I need back: materials list (cheap, classroom-available), the rules and payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact wording for the few moments where I have to speak, and the debrief -- which has to map specific things that happened (that group's sealed card, the flip, the score) onto the parts of the problem, without needing any reading list or names.

Two things I want you to be honest about instead of papering over: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm expecting at least one group to insist that any rival is ad hoc and to treat that as a knockdown, and one group to just game the tokens), and which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. My guess is it's the entrenchment thing -- in the room every rival is equally new and equally artificial, so the asymmetry that makes the ordinary predicate feel privileged has to be asserted by me rather than felt. Tell me if you think that's wrong, or if there's a way around it.

Also, please don't lean on any particular published version of the puzzle. I'd rather the students build the structure themselves than recognise it.
