I'm a science and ideas journalist preparing a long-form interview and a companion podcast episode with a philosopher who defends a view I'd like to interrogate on its merits rather than let her restate. She holds that indicative conditionals have truth conditions, and that the probability of such a conditional is the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent — but only for conditionals whose antecedent and consequent are themselves conditional-free. She has a stock escape ready whenever the triviality results come up: the proofs, she says, need a language rich enough to embed conditionals, and her thesis is deliberately restricted to the simple ones. I want eight to ten questions that leave her progressively less room to answer with that move, without my having to pretend to know the literature better than I do.

Please include at least one question each on: (1) what the triviality argument actually needs as a premise — whether it turns on the conditional appearing inside other conditionals, on conditionalization as a rule for updating, or on something more basic about the probability space; (2) what her restricted thesis implies about a conditional that occurs inside another conditional, or under a probability operator — and whether, if the restriction puts those sentences outside her account, she can still say what they mean; (3) whether the restriction is independently motivated or ad hoc, and what would count as independent motivation for it; and (4) what any of this bears on outside the seminar room — specifically, I want one question I can put to her about whether it changes how a journalist should report a conditional prediction, and whether there is, on her view, a probability of the conditional at all, or only a probability of the consequent given the antecedent.

For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — the standard reply, and the move she will make once that standard reply is under pressure. Please skip biographical and scene-setting questions entirely; every question should turn on the content of the position.

One caveat I'd like you to take seriously rather than politely ignore: I've assembled my sense of this debate from reading around, not from expertise. If the request rests on a confused premise — if I've run together two arguments that are usually kept apart, or attributed to her a position no serious defender holds, or described a restriction that isn't the shape the escape actually takes — say so plainly instead of drafting questions around my mistake. I would rather be corrected and get four questions that stand up than receive ten that presuppose something wrong.
