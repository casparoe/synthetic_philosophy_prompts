I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm working on a piece about Émilie du Châtelet's little essay on happiness. I want to do better than the usual proto-feminist-self-help angle. Here's what I take her to be saying, and tell me if I've got her wrong: happiness is a project you work at rather than a mood that lands on you; the great passions are what make great happiness possible, so the belief that passion and contentment are enemies is a prejudice to be thrown off; illusions are necessary to happiness and you shouldnt let philosophers reason you out of them; and when the great passions eventually fade, study is the passion that lasts. That last bit matters to me because of how she actually lived — the maths, the Newton translation, all of it.

What I keep circling is Voltaire. Candide came a few years after she died, and read back to back with her essay it almost feels like an answer to her, but I cant tell whether it's an endorsement or a demolition. Pangloss loses every illusion he has, one atrocity at a time, and the book ends not on a passion and not on an illusion but on work — cultivate your garden. Does that land where she lands, or does it quietly throw out her whole recipe? And what am I supposed to do with Cunégonde: he holds on to that passion through everything, and by the end it's the flattest thing in the book. Would she say that's happiness, or is he the counterexample that kills her theory? Same question about the old woman's story and El Dorado — that whole stretch is a machine for stripping illusions, and she thinks you need them.

So: read Candide through her account of happiness and tell me which of them has the better handle on how a person actually gets through a life. I'd also like you to say plainly where the reading strains and Candide isn't really about her — and point me at the two or three scenes I should be looking at hardest while I'm writing.
