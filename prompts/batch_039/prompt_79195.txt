Ok so I teach 9th grade social studies at a mid-sized public high school, and our admin just rollled out a "Compassion Cup": the kids who do the most impressive acts of kindness get a donation made in their name to a charity of their choosing, plus their name on a plaque in the front hall. I somehow ended up on the comittee that picks the winners, and I've been reading about something called the wrong kind of reasons problem that I think is exactly what's bugging me about the whole thing. As I understand it, the idea is that you can have a perfectly good reason to be in some state without that being any reason at all for what the state is about: someone threatening you gives you a reason to admire them, but nothing about the threat makes them admirable; paying me to believe something gives me a reason to believe it but is no evidence that it's true. And then the hard part is saying what actually makes a reason "the right kind" in the first place, or whether you can even build a theory of value on that distinction. So: what are the most important implications of this problem for how I should run the Compassion Cup — for how I judge entries, for whether I should announce the prize before or after the fact, for whether swapping cash for a charitable donation really fixes anything, and for whether "genuine" kindness is even something a committee can assess? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question.
