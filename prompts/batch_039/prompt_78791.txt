I retired from soil science about four years ago (thirty-five years on nitrogen cycling) and I've been reading philosophy ever since, mostly badly and out of order. Here's the thing I can't get past.

Our council's policy on contested monuments is: the statue stays where it is, and a new plaque goes up underneath explaining who the person actually was and what they did. Fine. But the same council will rename a street overnight if two streets happen to share a name, and the museum down the road relabels its cases whenever the scholarship moves. So what does the keep-the-statue-add-a-plaque policy actually presupose? Is it treating a monument as a statement about the past — in which case a plaque can correct it — or as an act of honouring, in which case no plaque can correct it, only cancel it? And who is the plaque's author? If the panel says the man did monstrous things, is that the council speaking, the original donors, or the people walking past? The policy seems to need a view about who a monument belongs to, and I honestly can't tell whether it has one or just assumes one. There's a wrinkle I like: some campaigns now want the plaques taken down too, which makes me suspect the plaque isn't a correction at all but a second monument.

So: which commitments does the practice strictly need, which rival theories would license it just as well (my suspicion is it fits several and has simply never been forced to choose), and what would have to change if we adopted the view that commemoration is an act rather than a claim? Answer in British English please — I've definately gone transatlantic since I got a laptop.
