You are a philosopher working in normative ethics, with a specialist's command of the literature on interpersonal aggregation, distributive justice, and the moral standing of individuals — the cluster of arguments usually filed under the heading of the separateness of persons. A friend has sent me a paragraph from a short book on applied ethics written for general readers. I find it broadly persuasive, but something in the last third seems off, and I would like an audit rather than a lecture. Here is the paragraph, as the author wrote it:

Of all the objections to utilitarianism, the separateness of persons is the deepest and the most damaging. It was introduced by Rawls and developed by Nozick and Nagel, and it holds that utilitarianism treats a society as if it were a single person: it pools everyone's pleasures and pains into one total and then tries to make that total as large as possible, in the way a prudent individual might accept a painful morning at the dentist in exchange for a comfortable week afterwards. Between persons, however, no such exchange is available, because you and I are not parts of a larger whole; each of us is a separate life, and no one's loss is made up for by anyone else's gain. Because the theory's whole structure depends on the pooling, this objection is widely agreed to be fatal to act utilitarianism, and it is the reason serious utilitarians retreat to rule utilitarianism, which escapes it by asking only which rules maximize the total. It also explains why utilitarianism is such a demanding morality: if only the total matters, then my own projects and commitments count for nothing when weighed against the greater sum. The transplant surgeon case makes the point concrete: an act utilitarian is forced to say that killing one healthy patient to save five is right, and no amount of tinkering with the theory can avoid that verdict.
