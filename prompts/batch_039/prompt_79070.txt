I am a high school student, and as part of an independent-study project in our philosophy elective I have been asked to write the long-answer question for our unit test on the Mencius and Xunzi debate about human nature, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, so that my teacher and I can grade the class consistently. Our unit used only short translated excerpts, so the question has to be answerable by a student who understands the two positions and the arguments for them but who cannot quote or cite anything, and I would rather it not depend on anyone recalling the Chinese terms we learned.

What I most want to avoid is a question that can be answered by repeating that Mencius thought people are good and Xunzi thought they are bad. So I have worked out a distinction that I want the question to force students to use. There seem to be at least three things someone might mean by a person's nature: what they are like at birth, before any teaching; what they are disposed to become under ordinary conditions; and what they would be like if left entirely without teachers, ritual, or good examples. My hypothesis, which I want the question to test rather than assume, is that Mencius and Xunzi agree far more than the usual summary suggests about the first of these, and that their real disagreement is about the second and third.

I also want the question built around a concrete case, so that students have to apply the theories instead of summarising them. For instance: one student who seems drawn toward fairness almost without effort; a second who became fair only after years of correction and of going through the motions; and a third figure, a child imagined to have grown up with no instruction, no ritual, and no good example. Students should have to say what each thinker would predict about all three, whether those predictions actually differ, and what kind of evidence could count for one view over the other.

Please write the essay question itself, a model answer of about seven hundred words written at the level of a strong high school student rather than a graduate one, and a marking scheme worth twenty marks. In the scheme, name the five or six moves a top answer must make, give the marks for each, and add one line per move saying how students typically miss it, plus a short closing note on what separates an A answer from a B one. I would like the scheme to reward a student who can explain why part of the disagreement may be about what the word nature is being used to mean, and in particular to reward anyone who notices that Xunzi may be denying only that our inborn dispositions are sufficient for goodness, rather than denying that we have any moral equipment at all. The whole exercise should fit a fifty-minute in-class essay.
