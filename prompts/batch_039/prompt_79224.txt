I'm a PhD student in history of science (with a philosophy MA, which is how I got into this mess) and I'm trying to write something about Brecht's Life of Galileo that isn't the standard science-vs-authority line, and I could use another brain on it.

Here's my angle. Galileo had an actual position on how to read scripture, and its a real philosophical position, not just "the Church was being dogmatic." Two premises: God authored both the book of nature and the book of scripture, so they can't contradict each other; but nature is just whatever it is, whereas scripture was written to accommodate itself to the capacities of ordinary people. Put those together and you get a rule — when a demonstrated result in natural philosophy clashes with a biblical passage, the passage has to be re-read, and the literal reading was never the right one anyway (God described as having hands, etc.). He lays this out first in the letter to Castelli around 1613, then expands it into the long letter to the Grand Duchess Christina around 1615. He even cites Cardinal Baronio for the line that the Holy Spirit's intention is "to teach us how one goes to heaven and not how heaven goes."

Now Brecht. My sense is that the play doesn't engage any of this: scripture shows up in it as an instrument of social control, and the only place where the question of how you're supposed to read the Bible actually gets argued is the scene with the Little Monk, where he says his family back in the Campagna grinds out an existence on olives and the only thing that makes it bearable is the belief that God is watching and will settle up later, that Galileo's universe takes that away, and that the consolation is worth more than the physics. Galileo answers with what knowledge could do for those people instead. (The rest of the arc I'm working with is the usual one — Padua and the money problem, the telescope passed off as his own, Jupiter's moons, the move to Florence, the plague, the Inquisition, the recantation, Andrea and the smuggled Discorsi at the end.)

What I want from you: read the play against the actual hermeneutics. Where does it hit it, where does it miss, and — the bit I keep going back and forth on — is the Little Monk scene actually staging the social cost of the accommodation doctrine? Because it seems to me that if you say scripture was accomodated to the ignorant, you've already made a claim about who can handle the truth and who can't, and the Little Monk is precisely the guy saying his own parents can't. Maybe Brecht stumbled onto the strongest objection to Galileo's theology without noticing it's a theological objection, and played it instead as "the Church needs the poor kept ignorant." Am I overreading? And where does the play resist my reading — is there anywhere Brecht's Galileo has something like a theology of his own, or anywhere the play lets the Church's side be more than cynical?

Then the part I actually need for the paper: is Brecht doing better or worse philosophy here than Galileo was? I have a nagging feeling the play gets one thing right that the letters don't — it makes you feel the paternalism baked into accommodation, and it treats truth-telling as a class act rather than a purely epistemic one — but that it also flattens Galileo into an Enlightenment rationalist who has outgrown the text, when the historical Galileo was trying to be a better Christian reader of it, not to retire it. If the play's political reading of scripture is a rival to Galileo's rather than a replacement, say so, and say what each one can and can't do. Dont give me a reading list and dont quote the play at me, I've got the text in front of me. I want the argument.
