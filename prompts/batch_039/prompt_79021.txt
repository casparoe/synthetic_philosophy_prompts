I'm writing a novel about a jury that gets stuck — eleven to one, and the one wont budge — and my editor keeps telling me the holdout has to be reasonable, not just pigheaded, or the whole thing turns into a morality play. So I've been re-watching Twelve Angry Men and reading up on Aumann's agreement theorem, and now I'm half convinced the film is secretly about common knowledge and half convinced it cheats.

Can you read the film through the theorem for me? Aumann's result, as I understand it, is that two people with the same priors can't knowingly disagree — if each knows what the other believes and they're both rational, they have to be sitting at the same number. I cant work out whether Twelve Angry Men dramatizes that or dramatizes why it never applies. The jurors definately dont share priors: Juror 3's prior is basically his son, Juror 4's is the stock exchange, the old man's is whatever he thinks he saw through the window. So is the film's turn really evidence pooling — the knife, the el train, the marks from the glasses — or is it just social pressure wearing the costume of argument? And take Juror 8: does he ever actually update anyone, or does he just keep manufacturing doubt until agreeing with him becomes the reasonable thing to do? Related: would it matter if the votes were taken in secret rather than by a show of hands? It seems to me the open vote is what makes everyones posterior common knowledge, which is exactly the condition Aumann needs, and the secret ballot is what lets them go on disagreeing.

What I want is the honest reading — where the film illustrates the theorem and where it has to fail in order for the story to work. Then the writerly question underneath it: Aumann tells me what rational people cant disagree about, but the film seems to show that people mostly disagree about what they can stand to believe about themselves. If thats right, does the theorem explain that, or does fiction get at something the math leaves out? Give me a reading I can steal from.
