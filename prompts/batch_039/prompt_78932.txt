I'm a philosophy PhD student (ethics, mostly), but I'm sitting in on a philosophy of physics seminar this term because I'm supposed to teach it next year, which was definately a mistake on someone's part. Anyway I keep running into an arguement with the same shape, and I want to know whether the shape has a name, because whenever I describe it people just say 'oh, that's the [famous thing]', and I don't think that answers my question.

Here's the shape. Take a theory. Write down one of its models -- a complete description of what there is and where it is. Now do something to that description that the theory itself swears makes no difference: every quantity you could ever measure, and every relation among the measurable things, comes out identical before and after. But the operation moves the underlying scaffolding around -- the points, the labels, the bare places that the stuff is attached to -- so the second description isn't just the first one reworded. If you ask 'what is at this particular place?', you get a different answer. And then someone says: so the theory doesn't choose between these two descriptions. Either the scaffolding is real, in which case the theory is indeterministic about it, or the scaffolding isn't real, in which case we shouldn't have been treating the two descriptions as two.

I've seen this run three ways. (1) Spacetime: take a region, smoothly re-label the points inside it, and keep everything outside the region exactly as it was, dragging the fields along with the labels. Outside, nothing changes; inside, the values get shuffled. (2) Quantum mechanics: swap two identical particles. The labels are different, the statistics aren't. (3) I'm told Leibniz had a version where you slide the whole universe three feet to the left and nothing observable changes. Someone also mentioned gauge fields, where the potential and its transformed cousin are supposedly one field described twice, but I didn't follow that one.

So: is there a name for the general shape, as opposed to names for the individual instances? Is it actually one shape? A friend in the seminar says my cases are 'all the same argument', and I don't beleive her, because the first one seems to need the region to be a small one and the second doesn't seem to need anything of the kind. Is this the same as underdetermination of theory by evidence? It doesn't feel like it -- there aren't two rival theories, there's one theory and two of its own descriptions. Is it just the same as saying that you can describe one situation in two coordinate systems? My gut says no and that the difference is the entire point, but I can't say what the difference is. And someone else said it's the same as the old puzzle where a universe contains nothing but two perfectly identical spheres and you ask whether swapping them gives you a different universe; that one looks like it's about identity rather than about theories, so I don't think it's the same either.

I don't just want the label. I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, which of the cases I've listed are really the same phenomenon and which only look alike, and where the edges are -- for instance, is the small-region version doing any work, or is it just a dramatic way of making a point that the other cases make anyway?
