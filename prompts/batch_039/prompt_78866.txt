I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology, and the same argument keeps surfacing in our reading group: whether the Dutch book argument establishes that a rational agent's credences must satisfy the probability axioms. Nobody disputes the theorem. If your betting quotients violate the axioms, there is a finite set of bets at those quotients that guarantees you a net loss. The disagreement is entirely about whether that theorem bears on credence.

The defenders hold that the extra premise is thin. It is a requirement of practical rationality not to accept a combination of bets that cannot but leave you worse off; nothing essential hangs on the medium being money, since the depragmatized versions run the argument on nothing but the inadmissibility of a sure-loss commitment. On that reading the argument is not a claim about what pays. The critics hold that the thin premise is doing all the work, and that it is plausible only if you antecedently identify a degree of belief with the betting quotient at which the agent is indifferent — a substantive operationalism about credence rather than a neutral observation about it. They add that the apparatus presupposes credences with exact real values and an agent willing to take either side of any bet at any stake, so it cannot even be addressed to someone whose credences are comparative, vague, or imprecise; that even if it worked it would deliver consistency rather than correctness, since it gives no guidance among the many coherent credence functions; and that the same dispute recurs for the diachronic argument for conditionalization, where the additional assumption is that your future self will honour a betting policy fixed in advance. Someone also likes to observe that exploitability cuts both ways: an incoherent agent is as exploitable for a guaranteed gain as for a guaranteed loss, which he offers as a reductio of treating exploitability as a defect at all. And the accuracy-first programme, which was supposed to break the tie by replacing the bookie with an inaccuracy score, looks to me as though it simply relocates the contested premise: the assumptions that credences come in degrees, that inaccuracy is measured by a scoring rule, and that accuracy is the only epistemically relevant good are close to the framework being defended, so the non-pragmatic argument appears to assume what the bookie version was accused of assuming.

My tentative hypothesis, which I would rather have tested than accepted: this is not a disagreement about arguments but about what the word credence is for, and it stalls because the parties are answering different questions under one heading — the measurement-theoretic question of what structure a system of degrees of belief must have to be numerically representable at all, and the normative question of what a rational believer is required to think. If that is right, the repeated relocation of the contested premise, from money to accuracy, is a symptom rather than a coincidence.

Diagnose this for me. Is it a verbal dispute about a term of art, a substantive dispute about the nature of epistemic normativity, two questions running under one name, a hidden empirical question about whether credences and betting dispositions can come apart, or a sign that probabilism names a stipulation about how a technical vocabulary is to be used rather than a norm governing believers? Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a theorem that dispenses with the precision and willingness-to-bet assumptions rather than assuming them, an experiment, a change of question — or nothing, with an argument for why the impasse should be expected to persist. Lead with your diagnosis in two or three sentences, then give the case for it. I want the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of who said what. At most 700 words.
