For most of my career I worked on comparative physiology, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy seriously for the first time — the Groundwork and the ethical parts of the Metaphysics of Morals once each, the Critique of Judgment more haltingly. Kant's treatment of animals is the place where I feel most acutely that I am reading in a foreign language, and I would like you to do the translation explicitly. Take the position as a whole — that animals lack personality and are not ends in themselves, that we have no duties to them but only duties concerning them, that cruelty to them is condemned for what it does to our own humanity — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in: regulatory set-points and homeostasis, error and nociceptive signals, adaptive function and fitness, and above all the distinction I have spent my life on between a mere regularity (what a system in fact does) and a norm (what it must do to keep going). Bring in as well his account of organisms as natural purposes, which seems to me to sit awkwardly beside the rest, since a being whose parts are reciprocally cause and effect of one another looks considerably less like a mere means than his ethics requires. Then give me the residue report, and be honest about it: which claims cross over cleanly; which arrive only in a distorted form, and what exactly gets bent; and which have no counterpart whatsoever in my vocabulary — and for those last, tell me whether the gap reveals a defect in how I speak about living things or a claim in Kant that was doing less work than its prominence suggests.
