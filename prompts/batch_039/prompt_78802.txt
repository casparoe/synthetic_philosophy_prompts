For several years a small reading group I belong to has kept returning to Parfit's hitchhiker and to the wider family of cases in which it seems rational to become the sort of agent who would act irrationally in a one-off choice. The basic case is familiar: you are stranded, a driver offers a ride on condition that you pay later, you know you will never see the driver again and that no sanction can reach you, and you also know that only someone disposed to pay will get the ride in the first place. We have added two variants that have made the argument worse rather than better. In the first, the driver can see a cheap but imperfect signal of your disposition, so the question becomes whether the signal or the disposition is doing the rationalizing. In the second, the hitchhiker is not one person but a small group that must decide together whether to cultivate a reputation for paying, which raises the question whether the rationality at issue belongs to individuals, groups, or practices. The group divides into three camps and none has moved in months. The first says the case shows that decision theory must evaluate dispositions or policies rather than isolated acts, so paying is rational as part of the policy that secures the ride. The second says that is a confusion: the disposition may be causally advantageous, but the act of paying, once it is chosen, is still irrational, and the theory should say so plainly; what we have is a case where it is rational to be irrational. The third says both sides are answering the wrong question, because the promise is not a one-off act but a move in a social practice of promising, and the real question is what the practice requires of someone who has already accepted its benefits. Every reply is anticipated, and the exchange now feels less like an unfinished argument than like arguments that have stopped touching.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about the correct theory of rational choice, a verbal dispute about whether the word rational attaches to acts or to dispositions, or two or three different questions being asked under one heading—one about the causal efficacy of character, one about the logic of counterfactual commitment, one about the norms internal to promising? Is a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, perhaps about whether the disposition is detectable or about how to model subjunctive dependence? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the camps start from intuitions that cannot be argued for? I am especially interested in whether the impasse survives once we separate the question of what it is rational to do from the question of what it is rational to be, and whether the practice-based reading changes the kind of disagreement or merely relocates it. Please engage the camps as I have described them, and be concrete about what each side would have to give up for the debate to move.
