I'm a long-time reader of the rationalist blogosphere — the Sequences, the old quantum-mechanics sequence, the endless comment threads in which someone confidently announces that Bell's theorem has been refuted because quantum mechanics doesn't allow signalling — and I've somehow talked myself into writing one of the four questions on this year's final for our third-year "Philosophy of Physics" module. The students have had twelve weeks: EPR, Bell's 1964 paper, CHSH, the loophole experiments, and a closing week on interpretations. They can do a little algebra, but they are philosophers, not physicists, and I am not going to assume they have seen anything I don't put in front of them. What I want is a question that a student who has memorised "Bell's theorem rules out local hidden variables" cannot pass, because the answer turns on a distinction that most textbook presentations flatten into nothing. So I have built the question around a specific correlation table rather than around the theorem in the abstract. Please act as someone who has taught quantum foundations at graduate level for years: think the whole thing through before you write, check the arithmetic yourself rather than trusting the phrasing of my request, and if any sub-question I ask for looks unanswerable in the time I've allowed, cut it and say so.

Here is the object I want the question built on. Alice and Bob each receive a bit (x for Alice, y for Bob) and each return a bit (a and b respectively). The box is defined by the following joint distributions, one per setting pair, with the listed outcomes equally likely within a cell: for (x,y) = (0,0), (0,1) and (1,0), the outcomes are (a,b) = (0,0) or (1,1); for (x,y) = (1,1), the outcomes are (0,1) or (1,0). Equivalently, a ⊕ b = x·y always, and each of Alice's and Bob's outputs is individually uniform no matter what. The question should ask students to (i) determine whether the box satisfies the no-signalling condition, and to compute the CHSH quantity S = E(0,0) + E(0,1) + E(1,0) − E(1,1), where E(x,y) is the correlation coefficient for that setting pair; (ii) state precisely what the answer to (i) shows about the role no-signalling actually plays in the Bell argument — this is the crux, and I want the question worded so that a student who believes "no signalling" *is* the locality assumption has to commit to that in writing and defend it; (iii) use the standard distinction between parameter independence and outcome independence to locate exactly which of the two the box violates, and to explain why the derivation of the inequality needs their conjunction rather than either one alone — gloss both terms in a clause each inside the question, so that no marks depend on a student having memorised who introduced them; and (iv) say what, if any, lesson the box carries for quantum mechanics, given that quantum mechanics respects the Tsirelson bound of 2√2 for this quantity while the box attains 4.

The question is worth 25 marks, is to be answered in 45 minutes, closed book, no calculator needed.
