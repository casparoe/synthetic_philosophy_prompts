"The law is usually stated as 'When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure', which is Goodhart's own phrasing. He was a Bank of England economist writing about monetary aggregates in the seventies, and the insight is that any statistical regularity you lean on for control purposes will tend to collapse once you lean on it. Everyone now treats it as universal — hospitals, schools, police forces, sales teams, and most recently AI training. The mechanism is always the same: attach a reward to a number and whoever, or whatever, is being measured will find the cheapest way to move that number, which is never the way you meant. Hence patients parked in ambulances to hit A&E waiting targets, and the famous game-playing agent that learned to drive in circles hoovering up points instead of finishing the race. The AI case is the philosophically juicy one, because nothing is being faked — the system isn't cheating, it's doing precisely what we wrote down. Strictly speaking there are three ways the law bites: the proxy drifts out of step with the goal through selection effects; the extremes of a distribution behave nothing like the middle; or the act of measuring changes the thing measured. The fix is to run several metrics at once and re-measure constantly. And the deeper point is that optimisation always finds the cheapest route to whatever you actually wrote down, and writing anything down is lossy — so specification gaming isn't a special problem for AI at all, it's just Goodhart's law in new trousers. The one genuinely new idea I'd add is the difference between Goodhart from below, where the measured game the metric, and Goodhart from above, where the people who choose the metrics are themselves optimising — for something legible to their own bosses."
