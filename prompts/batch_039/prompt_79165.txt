I work in compliance for a mid-sized company — I read contracts and internal policies for a living, so I spend alot of my day arguing about what sentences mean — and there's a pattern I've never had a word for. I've asked two lawyers about it and they both said some version of that's just interpretation, which I definately don't think is the whole story.

It goes like this. A rule, read strictly, produces a result nobody could have wanted. Our building rules say no vehicles on the boardwalk, bicycles excepted. Taken literally, a bike is fine and the maintenance cart and the ambulance are not. So somebody sensible says, obviously they didn't mean that, they meant no motor vehicles, and from then on we all apply no motor vehicles. That's the first move. The second move is the one I actually care about. Five years later, no motor vehicles isn't a correction anymore, it's the rule. New hires are trained on it. Someone who points at the actual sheet gets told that's obviously what it means and it's always been that way. And the old words are still sitting there on the sheet, so when we finally do want to change the rule we have to amend a sentence that nobody is really applying, and we end up writing the correction into the text, which makes the text longer and less like what anyone meant.

Same thing with our expense policy. Receipts required over $25 — except that in practice airfare always needed one at any amount. By the time I got here that exception was just what the policy said, and when it was rewritten last year the exception went in as though it had always been there.

So two questions, and I'd rather have the carving than the label. First: is there a name for the first move — reading a text as saying something it doesn't quite say, because the literal reading is intolerable? I assume there's a family of ideas around that, so tell me which ones and which fits. Second: is there a name for the second move, where the repair stops looking like a repair, gets quoted as the text, and then becomes the baseline that the next repair has to work around? Is that just what precedent is for, or is it a different thing? Is it related to whatever it is when a contract gets corrected to match what the parties actually agreed — which I gather is treated as seperate from interpreting it? Or does the second move have no name at all, in which case give it one, and tell me where its edges are.

Concretely, I want to know: at what point does a hardened repair stop being a repair and become the real meaning, if ever? What separates a correction that has earned its place from one that's just been repeated for long enough that nobody remembers making it? And does this change the argument between my colleagues who say you go by the words and the ones who say you go by what the thing was for — because if the words have been quietly absorbing purposes for a century, I'm not sure the two camps are even pointing at different objects.
