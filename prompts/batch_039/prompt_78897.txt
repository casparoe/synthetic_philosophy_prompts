What follows is a list of bets and my verdicts on them. I want the principle behind the verdicts, not the name of the principle; I already know several of the names, and they have not helped.

A coin I know to be sixty-forty in my favor, paying even money, which I may flip as often as I like today, staking any fraction of my bankroll each time and reinvesting: I stake twenty percent a flip. I have no hesitation about this one.

The same coin, but I get exactly three flips and then the game is gone forever: still twenty percent. The shorter horizon does not move the number.

A different coin, returning plus fifty percent on heads and minus forty percent on tails, where I must stake my entire bankroll on every flip and may keep flipping all day: I refuse. My expected wealth grows without bound, and I refuse anyway, because I know what becomes of the single path I am actually on.

That same coin, entire bankroll, exactly one flip, and then never again: I take it. This is where I stop trusting myself. The reason I gave for refusing the series was about what happens over many flips, and that reason says nothing about a single flip, while the expected value is positive. Of all the cases here, this is the one I go back and forth on.

A one-shot bet that triples whatever I stake with probability ninety percent and takes the stake with probability ten percent: I stake about a tenth of my bankroll and no more. By the expected-log standard the right stake is close to eighty-five percent. I believe the arithmetic, and I still would not do it.

I owe one thousand and fifty dollars tomorrow and I have one thousand. Paying the debt is the only thing that matters; anything beyond it is worthless to me. I have the sixty-forty coin and may flip it as often as I like before the deadline. I stake the smallest amount I can manage and grind upward, even though twenty percent is the growth-optimal fraction, because what I want is the greatest chance of clearing the debt, not the greatest growth rate.

The principle I keep reaching for is this: act so as to maximize the expected logarithm of my wealth — informally, to care about the geometric mean of my outcomes rather than the arithmetic mean. It handles the first three cases cleanly. It cannot handle the one-shot cases, where there is no growth rate to speak of, and it appears to tell me the wrong thing about the debt. I suspect that maximizing expected log wealth and maximizing the growth rate of wealth are not the same principle, and that the difference is doing hidden work in my verdicts.

Give me the principle, or a small set of them, stated precisely, with the case that forces each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate. If the honest answer is that no principle gets all six, name the verdict I should give up rather than adding a clause per case. Then apply the surviving principle to a case I have not listed — choose one on which the candidate principles disagree, so the case does some work.
