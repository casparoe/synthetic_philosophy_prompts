I'm running a seventy-five-minute session of an interdisciplinary reading group — mostly philosophy graduate students, plus a couple of economists and one lawyer — on the question of whether "better than" is transitive, and I'd like you to plan the session for me rather than explain the topic to me. Assume the group knows what a relation is and has met the claim that rational preferences must be transitive, but has never considered whether the betterness relation itself might fail to be; nobody has read Parfit's material on the Repugnant Conclusion or anything in the recent literature on the intransitivity of betterness, and I don't want to send them to anything, because I want the whole thing to stand on its own from what is in the room. What I need from you is the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it: which distinction has to be on the table before the question even becomes well-formed (I suspect it is the one between the structural question about the relation and the normative question about rational preference, but tell me if something else has to come first, and in particular where the better-than / at-least-as-good-as contrast has to sit), how to set up the spectrum device so that the contradiction feels forced rather than clever, and precisely which step in the sequence is where each predictable confusion has to be killed.

For the carrying example I would rather not build the whole session on the population case, because I have watched it let students outsource the hard judgment to a doctrine: they either announce that the Repugnant Conclusion is obviously false or obviously true and then stop thinking. My own proposal is a mundane trade-off spectrum inside a single person's life — a chain of apartments running from a tiny flat in a perfect location, each step adding space and worsening the location by an equal notch, out to a vast flat in an awful location, with every adjacent step judged an improvement. Tell me whether that can carry the session, what it has that the population case lacks and what it lacks that the population case has, whether the ends really do reverse on it, and whether I should bring the population case in as a second instance or keep it out entirely. I have also wondered whether the sorites heap is a helpful analogy here or a trap — my instinct is that it hands them the vagueness diagnosis far too early — and I want your verdict on that too.

For the misconception map, the ones I am already braced for are these: that transitivity is simply a logical truth about any comparative, so the spectrum must contain a mistake somewhere; that a non-transitive betterness relation would mean there is no fact of the matter about which of two options is better, or that anything goes; and that the spectrum is just the sorites paradox in another costume, so it is a problem about vagueness rather than about the relation. Tell me where in the sequence each of those has to be headed off and how, which of my three is actually the most dangerous, and what I am missing — I have a suspicion there is a confusion between incomparability and intransitivity, and another about cyclic preferences and money-pumps, but I do not know which surfaces first in a room like this. Then give me a closing check for understanding that would separate a group that has genuinely grasped the structure from one that has merely learned to say that betterness might be non-transitive — ideally something that forces them to declare which of the rival diagnoses a fresh case supports. Finally, tell me what I should deliberately refuse to attempt at this level, the formal apparatus I would be tempted to reach for, and how I should say so out loud so that the group does not leave thinking they have been handed a watered-down version of something they are missing.
