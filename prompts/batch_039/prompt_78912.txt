I'm a philosophy professor and I've got a small internal grant application due — the humanities center here hands out a semester of course relief plus a bit of travel money, and the deliverable is one workshop and one paper — and I want to spend it on something I've been circling for years: the counterfactual about Hitler. Not the usual 'would there have been a Nazi Germany without him' as an essay prompt, but the prior question of what would have to be true for that question to have an answer at all. What I keep noticing is that everyone in this fight, the ones who say the man was the hinge of the century and the ones who say Weimar was going to cough up something like him anyway, is answering a different question than the one they think they're answering. If 'without Hitler' means he's never born, that's one world; if it means he's killed in the trenches, or stays in Vienna as a mediocre painter, or comes home from the war without the politics, those are three more, and I'm fairly sure the answers come out differently for each. So what I want to pitch is not another verdict on the history — I can't settle the history and neither can a committee reading this in ten minutes — but an account of when a counterfactual about one person's causal role is well-posed and what fixes the contrast class. The Hitler case seems like the right test case precisely because it's the one everybody already has intuitions about, and because the historiography is so saturated that any criterion I propose will get worked hard.

I also want the proposal to take the obvious objection seriously instead of waving at it: if history is chaotic in the technical sense, then the tiniest change to the antecedent swamps everything downstream and no counterfactual in this genre is stable, so the whole enterprise is idle. I think that objection proves far too much, since it would make ordinary causal explanation in history impossible and nobody actually accepts that, but I don't have a good story about why it proves too much and I'd like the proposal to commit to one. Related: the structuralists and the Great Man people often look like they're answering at different levels — one is saying what made the outcome likely, the other is saying who made it happen — and I suspect that once you pry apart 'necessary', 'one member of a sufficient set', and 'made a difference', the disagreement shrinks a lot without vanishing. What I want the project to get at is what's left after the shrinkage, and whether what's left is a disagreement about facts or a disagreement about what we wanted an explanation for in the first place.

Write me the proposal. Two pages, the kind of thing a humanities committee reads over coffee. I need the question stated so it has a determinate answer and so a reader can see it's not the same question everybody else is asking; why the existing positions leave it open; what I'd actually do with the semester, month by month (I read German slowly, and a colleague in the history department has agreed to sit in on the workshop — I want the room mixed and I don't want the historians feeling lectured at); the two or three ways it could come out and which considerations would decide between them; and an honest paragraph on what would count as the project failing, which I'd rather say up front than discover in June. No bibliography or reading list, I'll fill that in myself and half of what I'd want is in German anyway. And write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs, the way you'd write a letter. I know that makes a proposal harder to skim, but the committee is four people and they'll read it.
