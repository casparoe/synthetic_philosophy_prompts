I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of medicine, and I am writing the final exam. The unit on the definition of death has gone badly: my students can recite the three neurological criteria — whole-brain, brainstem, higher-brain — and can tell me that the circulatory and neurological routes to a declaration of death are meant to be two ways of establishing the same thing, but they freeze the moment I ask why the two routes are allowed to operate on different standards of loss. That mismatch is what I want the exam to test. A colleague in transplant ethics put the worry to me this way: the neurological route is normally taken to require an irreversible loss of function, whereas a patient may be declared dead on the circulatory route at a point when the loss is only permanent — where permanence means that the function will not return because no attempt will be made to restore it, or because it is past the point at which it could spontaneously return, and not that it could not in principle be restored. I want candidates to work out whether that gap is a genuine conceptual problem or a confusion, and what it implies for the rule that organs may be taken only from patients who are already dead.

Please write me one essay question that turns on that crux, together with a model answer of around 900 words and a marking scheme out of 20. The question should make candidates do at least the following: say what each route is trying to track about the patient, rather than merely restating the criteria; explain whether the two routes can be read as two tests for a single underlying concept of death or whether they covertly appeal to two different concepts — for instance, the death of the organism as an integrated whole as against the death of the person, or the loss of the capacity for consciousness; and take a defended position on whether the permanence/irreversibility gap puts pressure on organ procurement after a circulatory declaration, or whether it is a red herring. It should be answerable in ninety minutes and should not be passable by a candidate who has memorised the labels but has not thought about the concepts. For the marking scheme, name the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks — for example, treating permanence and irreversibility as synonyms, or treating the dead donor rule as a biological constraint rather than a normative one. Candidates have done the reading, so the question may name the distinctions I have described; the model answer should not depend on any particular source, and I do not want a bibliography.
