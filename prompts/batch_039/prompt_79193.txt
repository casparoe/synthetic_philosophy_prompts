In the epistemology of testimony and of disagreement, a certain ideal recurs under various names. Call it ascriptive universality: the thesis that a judgment counts as knowledge, or as warranted assertion, only if it is the sort of thing that any suitably situated inquirer could arrive at and endorse, so that its authority owes nothing to the particular position—social, historical, or institutional—from which it happens to be made. On this ideal, a warrant that cannot be reproduced from elsewhere is not yet a warrant; it is a local conviction wearing universal dress.

The dominance objection holds that this ideal is a self-portrait rather than a standard. What gets called the view from anywhere, the objection runs, is the view from the center: the requirement that reasons be positionally indifferent is in practice a requirement that they be phrased in the idiom of those who already hold epistemic power, and it functions chiefly to disqualify situated knowers by reclassifying their grounds as idiosyncrasy, bias, or mere experience.

Your task is to argue for a specific position: that the dominance objection succeeds only against a needlessly strong reading of ascriptive universality, and that a reconstructed version of the ideal survives it—one that relocates universality from the content of the warrant to the form of the challenge. On this reconstruction, a claim is ascriptively universal not because everyone could have discovered it, nor because its warrant is transparent from every standpoint, but because the claimant is bound to make the claim answerable to anyone who presses it, on terms the claimant must supply rather than presuppose. The universality lies in the standing obligation to keep the space of contestation open, not in the standpoint-independence of what is contested.

To keep apart the three things the objection tends to run together: content universality (the claim is true, or its warrant valid, for everyone), ascription universality (any inquirer could in principle recognize and reproduce the warrant), and entitlement universality (everyone is equally entitled to demand that the claim be made good to her on terms she can accept). Much of the objection's force comes from treating ascription universality as though it were content universality or entitlement universality. Your argument should show that the reconstruction escapes the objection precisely by refusing that slide, without thereby surrendering to relativism or to a complacent pluralism on which anything goes so long as it is sincerely held.

In making the case, do all of the following. State the reconstructed ideal precisely enough that it could actually be applied, and distinguish it from the strong reading it replaces. Anticipate and answer the strongest version of the dominance objection, including its most awkward form: the charge that even the form of the challenge—the demand that a claimant supply terms anyone could take up—is itself a culturally specific genre of reason-giving, so that the reconstruction has smuggled the original problem back in at a higher level. Construct at least one concrete case, of your own devising rather than borrowed from the literature, in which a situated knower is marginalized or silenced, and show what the reconstructed ideal would and would not license in that case. Finally, say what the reconstruction costs: identify at least one kind of epistemic authority or deference that it cannot underwrite, and explain why giving that up is acceptable.

Write a sustained argument of roughly 900 to 1,100 words in American English. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to specific authors or texts; the argument must stand on its own. Address the reader as a competent philosopher who does not already accept your conclusion.
