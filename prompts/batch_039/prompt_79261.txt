Ok so I've been drafted into writing a short piece on free-floating rationales for my department's reading group — the Dennett-ish idea that there can be a reason why something happens even though nothing anywhere represents that reason. And the longer I sit with it the more the whole dispute feels froze. One camp: obviously the cuckoo chick shoving its foster siblings out of the nest has a rationale — it serves a goal, that goal is what explains why the behaviour is there, and none of that requires the chick to have a thought in its head. The rationale floats free, and calling it a rationale isn't just a colourful way of saying 'it got selected'. Other camp: that's either trivial (you've pointed at a selection history, the intentional vocabulary adds nothing and can't be wrong about anything, so stop dressing it up) or it's illegitimate (a reason is the kind of thing that can be weighed, and can be better or worse, and weighing needs a weigher; take away the subject and you've got causes, not reasons).

My hunch — treat it as a hunch, I'm not confident — is that both sides keep sliding between three seperate questions, and that's why nobody's moved in decades: (1) there's a reason why the thing happens, plain explanation, no normativity in it; (2) there's a reason for the thing to happen, i.e. the thing can succeed or fail at something; (3) there's a state inside the system that means something. Pull those apart and is the fight partly just verbal — a tug of war over the word 'reason' — and partly a genuine fight about whether (2) can get any grip without a subject who is answerable to it? Or is my split a red herring, and it's a straight substantive disagreement about what an explanation of behaviour has to deliver?

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. What kind of disagreement is this actually — verbal, or a clash about standards of explanation, or two groups answering different questions under one label, or something with a hidden factual core? Where exactly does the verbal part end and the real disagreement start? Does my (1)/(2)/(3) split earn its keep, or does it just relocate the problem? And then the practical bit: what would count as progress here — a distinction, a case, an argument, a formal result — or should I just write the piece as 'this is a permanent fork and here's why'? Two test cases I keep tripping over, use them if they help: a bacterium swimming up a sugar gradient, and a river that carves its way to the sea. If your diagnosis makes those come out differently, say so and say why; if they come out the same, tell me whether that's a feature or a reductio. And if a thermostat gets the same treatment, great; if the diagnosis breaks there, tell me it breaks there. Deadline's Friday and I'd rather hand in a diagnosis I can defend than a fence-sitting summary.
