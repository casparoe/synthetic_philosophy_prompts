I am trying to understand the following passage from Aristotle's Physics, Book VI, chapter 9: "Zeno's reasoning, however, is fallacious, when he says that if everything when it occupies an equal space is at rest, and if that which is in locomotion is always occupying such a space at any moment, the flying arrow is therefore motionless. This is false, for time is not composed of indivisible moments any more than any other magnitude is composed of indivisibles." In the rest of the chapter he lists the dichotomy, Achilles, the arrow, and the stadium. The usual modern gloss—that at an instant a moving body is somewhere, and being somewhere at an instant is not the same as being at rest—seems to make Aristotle's appeal to the non-composition of time out of indivisible moments either unnecessary or too weak, since one can accept instants and still accept the so-called at-at theory of motion, on which motion just is the occupation of successive positions at successive instants. What I want is a careful reconstruction of Aristotle's actual response. Which premises does he attribute to Zeno, and which one does he deny? Is the denial that time is composed of indivisible moments meant only to rule out temporal atoms, or does it also commit him to a stronger thesis about the relation between motion and the instant? How does his conception of motion as the actuality of a potentiality bear on the arrow, if at all? And if we granted a discrete time composed of indivisible moments, would the arrow paradox then succeed, or would it still depend on an equivocation about rest and occupying a place? I have also heard it suggested that the arrow is really a problem about how to ascribe velocity at an instant rather than about the continuum; does the passage support that reading, or is it a later imposition? I would like the reconstruction to distinguish Aristotle's own solution from the at-at theory, and to say which of the arguments he goes on to list in the chapter is hardest for his solution.
