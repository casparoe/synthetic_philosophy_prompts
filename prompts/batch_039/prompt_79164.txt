I'm a novelist, three books in, and I'm stuck on the fourth in a way that's turned into a philosophy problem, so I'm asking you to settle it for me. The book is set in a big distribution warehouse outside a mid-sized city, and the character I care about is the engineer who installs the robots. I need to know what she actually believes, and right now I catch myself believing different things on different pages.

Here are my gut verdicts on automation. What I want is the principle, if there is one, that gets all or most of them. I've read enough Marx to be dangerous, so don't water the philosophy down, but the answer has to be sayable out loud by a woman in a hi-vis vest, so nothing I can't put in her mouth.

1. A robot takes over the roof-bolting in a mine, the job where men have died, same crew, same pay. Good, plainly good, no asterisk.

2. Same robot, but the mine lays off half the crew and the survivors supervise it, safer and slightly better paid. Bad. Not bad-but-worth-it. Bad.

3. The word processor. My writing life got freer, I write more, I'd never go back, and I feel no flicker of loss about it even knowing what happened to typing pools. Good.

4. A model that drafts my chapters. Structurally the same gain: less drudgery, more books, same money. Feels like something being taken from me rather than given. Bad, and I can't articulate why, becuase 3 and 4 look like the same case with the knobs turned up.

5. Self-checkout at the supermarket. Bad. The shop has handed me a job and called it convenience. I use it anyway, which I assume counts as data against me.

6. A warehouse where an algorithm sets the pace and logs every second of the picker's day. Worse than the old foreman — even though the foreman was a bully and the algorithm is scrupulously fair and never shouts.

7. A worker co-op buys the same picking robot as in 6, cuts the week to 25 hours, same pay. Good. But I'm suspicious of how good I think it is, because I worry my whole principle is just who owns the thing, and that that's either the entire answer or a dodge.

8. A friend's novel, half-drafted by a model, that is honestly better than her last one. I can't get a verdict out of myself at all on this one.

9. The Luddites. Smashing the frames was a rational move by people with no other move, and the machine wasn't the enemy. I hold both at once and don't feel any pressure to give one up.

The pairs I can't reconcile are 3 against 4, and 2 against 7. Everything else I'd defend, including 8 being a blank.

So: give me the principle, or a small set of rival principles if one won't do, each stated precisely enough that I can see what it costs me. For each clause, tell me which of my cases forces it. Tell me what it predicts about two cases I haven't listed — pick them yourself, one historical and one contemporary, and try to pick ones where the principle embarrasses itself rather than where it coasts. Then tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts it can't keep, and whether I should drop the verdict or add a clause.

One more thing, and I'd rather you tell me if it's a red herring: I keep coming back to that stretch of the Grundrisse where machinery and fixed capital take over the worker's knowledge, and where the whole thing is supposed to end with labour time ceasing to be the measure of wealth. Does any of that do real work in my warehouse, or is it decoration I should keep out of the book? Same question for the shift from formal to real subsumption — is that the distinction that actually explains 3 versus 4, or am I just reaching for a phrase?

Hard cap: 900 words. Lead with the bottom line — the principle in a sentence or two at the top, before you argue for it — and if you have to cut something, cut the theory, not the cases.
