Take the selected-effects (etiological) account of function at its strongest: a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected, and it is this appeal to a history of selection that explains the normative dimension of function talk — why a malformed heart is malfunctioning rather than merely doing something else. I want a map of what would count against this account, with the objections a defender should concede separated from those they can wave away, and ranked from fatal to merely awkward.

I am particularly interested in the cases the account seems to mishandle on its own terms: traits now maintained by selection for an effect other than the one that originally recruited them; traits that have lost the effect they were selected for and are now vestigial; and signals whose biological function arguably lies in what they do for the receiver rather than the sender. Does the account need to be supplemented with a causal-role or systemic-capacity component to cover these, and if so, does it forfeit the normative payoff that was supposed to be its chief advantage over the causal-role account? Finally, say what this account predicts that its rivals do not, and whether the version that survives the objections remains a substantive thesis or has been qualified into something close to empty.
