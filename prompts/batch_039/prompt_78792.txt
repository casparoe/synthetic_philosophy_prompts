My grandmother has advanced dementia, and there is a family meeting next week about whether to place a feeding tube. Years ago, well before any of this, she signed a living will refusing artificial nutrition and hydration in the event that she could no longer recognize the people around her, and she was emphatic about it in a way the whole family remembers as characteristic: she had watched her own mother spend two years in a nursing home and said she would rather die than live like that. My cousin, who works as a nurse on a long-term care unit, keeps saying that this is just a Ulysses contract, and she calls it that — the competent person has herself lashed to the mast so that the later person, who will want to steer straight onto the rocks, cannot; the crew rows on through the shouting, and the man himself is grateful afterward. That is what she uses the story for: to argue that my grandmother's present wishes should not count for much and that we should simply follow the document. Two things complicate it for me. The shouting is not temporary — whatever we decide, the later person does not come back to thank anybody. And my grandmother's wishes now come and go: on Tuesday she refused everything and asked to be let go, on Thursday she asked for the tube and said she wanted to live. So I am not even sure there is one later person for the mast to hold in place.

Could you audit the analogy rather than just telling me which side is right? I would like the correspondence set out explicitly — what is supposed to play the part of Ulysses, the crew, the ropes, the song, the mast, and the shore he was trying to reach — followed by the places where the mapping fails or has to be forced, with the failures that matter for the work my cousin is making the analogy do (licensing us to override a request my grandmother is making now) separated from the ones that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in what the story quietly imports that our situation lacks: one agent with a continuous memory, a state that ends, a hero whose own plan at the moment of binding was to hear the song and survive it, a crew with no stake of its own in the outcome. I would also like you to test whether the move my cousin makes against Thursday's request — that it is the disease talking — can be turned around against the document itself, since the woman who signed it had just watched her mother die and may have been answering that experience rather than anticipating this one. And tell me whether the analogy still does any work once the later self's preferences fluctuate, or whether that pushes us toward a different picture altogether: a will, a constitution later generations may amend, a trustee acting for a beneficiary, or something better suited than any of those. If the Ulysses picture carries less than my cousin thinks, say what it does still carry, and be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on advance directives and which are live disputes among them, rather than presenting one camp's answer as the consensus.
