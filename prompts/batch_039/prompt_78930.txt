I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy degree, and I've somehow ended up co-running the "practical rationality" week of a decision-theory module for final-year CS students (long story, someone went on sabbatical). So I have to write the final essay question and the marking scheme, and I'd rather not embarass myself in front of people who actually know this stuff.

The topic I've been handed is money pumps, and whether rationality is something that happens at a moment or something that happens across time. The reading group keeps saying "intransitive preferences are irrational, because you can money-pump someone who has them," and I want an exam question that makes that line do some actual work instead of just getting recited back at me.

I've made up a case I want the question built around, so it won't be sitting in any textbook:

Sam has a ticket to a gig on Friday. At any single moment, Sam's preferences are a perfectly ordinary, transitive ranking. But they shift predictably as the date approaches. Three weeks out, Sam would rather have £80 in hand than go. The night before, Sam would rather go than have £120. A scalper who knows all this buys the ticket off Sam three weeks out for £80, and sells it back to Sam the night before for £120. Sam goes to the gig having paid a net £40 for something that would've been free, and there's nothing special about gigs — the scalper can run the same play on Sam over and over with other events. Yet at no point were Sam's preferences internally inconsistent.

I want the question to make students work out what, if anything, that shows about Sam's rationality, and to notice that this pump and the standard one (cyclic preferences, a trader offering swaps in a single sitting) might not be showing the same thing at all. Specifically I want them to seperate out (i) what the pump actually establishes — that Sam is down £40, or something stronger than that — (ii) what extra premise you'd need to get from "Sam can be bled" to "Sam is irrational," and (iii) whether precommitment changes the verdict: if Sam could sign something up front that blocks the night-before swap, is Sam then rational, or just insured? And I'd like a part where they say whether my own hunch is right. My hunch is that getting ripped off shows nothing about rationality by itself — it's just being ripped off.

Constraints: one essay question, doable in about 60 minutes, worth 20 marks. The students are strong CS types with some decision theory and no philosophy of action. No citations, no reading list — it's closed book and I don't know the literature anyway, so keep everything to arguments. And please don't let it be answerable with "intransitive preferences are irrational because money pumps."

Give me the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words that actually takes a stand rather than listing views, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks for each (adding to 20), and a one-line note per point on how students typically botch it. Make the very last line of the whole thing read exactly \answer{...} with a short phrase inside giving the model answer's bottom line on whether being pumpable is by itself enough to show irrationality.
