I'm a novelist — three books, all of them secretly about grief, which my agent finds tedious — and I need a piece for a reading my writers' group is doing next month, maybe to publish after. The thing I want to make fun of is the problem of evil, but I don't want to do the obvious God-on-trial skit. I want it settled administratively, as if the whole business had been outsourced to an insurance company decades ago and nobody remembers it was ever a philosophical question. So I've invented a bureau — I've been calling it the Office of Providential Claims, but name it whatever's funnier and give it a slogan — that processes complaints about suffering the way a big insurer processes claims, and every denial it sends out is a theodicy wearing a form letter. What I want from you is the actual booklet: the terms and conditions / policy document that comes stapled to the denial letter. Maybe 700 words, numbered clauses with little headings, deadpan the whole way, no winking, no scare quotes, and never let the narrator step out from behind the desk to tell me it's a joke. The prose should be scarily plausible — the flat, blameless register these documents actualy have.

I know I want an act-of-God exclusion that the bureau cheerfully applies to damage caused by the manufacturer itself, since that's apparently what the phrase means in the trade. I want a free-will rider excluding loss arising from the exercise of anybody's agency — and that's the one I care most about: it has to be the strong version, applied to a case where someone else's free choice wrecks a life that had nothing to do with it, so a reader can't just laugh it off. Then something about losses that turn out to be character-formation and will be revalued on a longer schedule; a clause reserving the right not to disclose the reasons for a denial on the grounds that the claimant isn't equipped to evaluate them; one explaining that no better world was on offer because there is no best of all possible ones; and a clause deferring all compensation to an afterlife clearinghouse, payable only to the deceased. Please don't invent flimsy versions of any of these — if someone who teaches this stuff reads it, they should recognize each clause as a real position some serious person holds, just dressed up as a warranty. Don't cite anyone, don't name scholars, no reading list; I want it to read as if the bureau wrote it and had never heard of a university.

The claim that sets the whole thing off: a woman in a town that flooded is trying to collect for her nine-year-old daughter, who died of something slow and horrible, and the booklet arrives with the adjuster's cover letter. Use the town from my last novel if you want continuity, or just make up a dreary one. By the end I want a reader to have felt the real weight of her complaint and not merely enjoyed the joke — the way a good reductio leaves you stuck with the conclusion. And if you can sneak in one line from the adjuster suggesting he's starting to crack, without ever breaking character, that'd be great.
