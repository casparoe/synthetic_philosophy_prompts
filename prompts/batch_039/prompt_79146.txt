Ok so my philosophy club just did the Twin Earth thing where Putnam asks you to imagine a planet that looks exactly like ours, and the people there call the stuff in their lakes water, except it turns out their stuff isnt H2O, its some other chemical, and the question is whether their word means the same as ours. I get that the answer is supposed to be no, because what a word refers to gets fixed by the environment and not by whats inside your head. Fine, I buy it.

What I want is to run that machinery on something that actually happened: the 2006 IAU vote where Pluto got demoted. Heres the facts as I understand them, and if I've got them wrong just say so and keep going: to be a planet now you have to orbit the sun, be massive enough that gravity has rounded you out, and have cleared the neighborhood around your orbit of other junk. Pluto fails the third one because it shares its zone with a bunch of other icy bodies, so it got moved into a new box called dwarf planet, along with Ceres and Eris. Only a small fraction of the IAU's members actually voted, and plenty of planetary scientists, including people who study Pluto for a living, still call it a planet. So nothing out in space changed at all, Pluto is the same ball of ice it always was, but the word changed.

Analyze this both ways, the theory on the case and the case on the theory:

1. If externalism is right, was it true in 1990 that Pluto is a planet, and false now? Or was it always false, the way that whales are fish was always false? Which of those does the theory actually commit you to, and does it matter who's doing the talking, the experts or the rest of us who just go along with whatever the experts say (the division of linguistic labor thing)? And does it matter that the change came out of a vote, where a big part of the motivation was that we dont want forty planets in the textbooks? Is the reference getting fixed by nature here, or by us?

2. Heres the bit I actually care about. Suppose the IAU had voted the other way and Pluto stayed a planet. Same world, same objects, same orbits, only the committee vote differs. Does planet mean the same thing in that world? Environmental externalism seems to say yes, because the stuff out there is identical, so the extension is identical. But the Burge arthritis case seems to say no, because what your word means depends on the norms of your community, and communities can differ. So does this case support environmental externalism, support social externalism, or drive a wedge between them? Be specific about what each one predicts about the counterfactual IAU, dont just tell me both of them are externalist.

3. Compare it to cases where we revised a terms extension because we found out the world wasnt what we thought, like jade turning out to be two different minerals, or whales turning out not to be fish. Is the Pluto case that kind of case or a different kind? And if its a different kind, what does that tell us about whether planet is a natural kind term at all, or whether its a category we invented for convenience, and if its the latter then what was the externalist argument even about?

4. Then be honest with me: does this case definately confirm externalism, embarrass it, or is it something the theory never thought about and just goes silent on? If it goes silent, point at exactly which step has nothing to say here.

Dont give me a reading list or names of papers, I only want the argument, and dont just tell me its controversial which definition of planet is right. I know that. I want to know what the theory of meaning says about the controversy, and what the controversy shows about the theory.
