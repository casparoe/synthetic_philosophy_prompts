It follows that nobody can be wrong about what they have reason to do, and that moral criticism of anyone is impossible — the man who is serially unfaithful and untroubled by it simply has no reason to be otherwise, and Williams accepts that with a shrug. He also thinks the very idea of deliberating about ends is incoherent, which is why what he ends up with is a dressed-up version of the Humean doctrine that reason serves the passions. The view is now generally regarded as refuted: the standard objection — that it can't account for someone who is simply indifferent to his own future — is usually taken to be decisive, and Williams himself later qualified the thesis in response to critics, which most people read as a concession.'
