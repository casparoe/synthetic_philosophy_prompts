I'm a retired atmospheric scientist — thirty-odd years in numerical weather prediction — and since I packed it in I've been working through moral philosophy with a lot more enthusiasm than discipline. I've somehow got myself roped into running next month's session of a "philosophy for scientists" group at the local continuing-ed place: about fifteen people, a few engineers, two doctors, a retired chemist, nobody with any philosophy past one undergrad ethics elective. Topic I've been assigned is consequentialism and the problem people call cluelessness — the thought that for most of what we do the consequences run off into a fog so thick we can't say whether the act was good or bad, or even who it was good or bad for. Don't explain the idea to me, I more or less have it. Plan the ninety minutes for me.

What order do the pieces have to come in, and why does each one depend on the one before it? My instinct is that the thing they need first isn't the fog at all, it's the fact that ordinary uncertainty is not the same thing as this — that expected value eats ordinary risk for breakfast, and this is supposed to be something worse than risk. But tell me if that's the wrong opening, and if so what actually has to be in place before the problem even makes sense to them.

Then the example that carries the whole session. I was going to use my own home turf: one decision, say taking a long-haul flight to a conference, then tracing where it goes in the climate system, the butterfly stuff. I'm worried that's a trap, because a physicist hears "chaotic system" and thinks "unpredictable, but still governed by known equations and well-defined initial conditions", which is a different animal from not knowing what to even put in the ledger. If my example is bad, give me a better one, and tell me what it has to have and what it must not have — it must not, I think, be a case where we secretly do know the answer, or where the fog is just "nobody's done the study yet".

Misconceptions. The ones I can already hear coming: "so it's just uncertainty", "so consequentialism is useless, back to rules then", "so nothing I do matters / everything matters equally", and from the doctors, "this is why we stick to the guidelines". I'd like each one placed at the point in the sequence where it should be headed off rather than saved for the Q&A, and if there's one you think is likelier than any of mine and I haven't listed it, say so.

Two things that stopped me while reading around, and I'd like them worked into the sequence somewhere if they belong: first, that the fog isn't only about how much good or bad comes of an act, it's also about the sign — whether the thing is good at all; second, the part that really rattled me, that it isn't just "how much" but "who", because the people downstream who get affected or don't exist at all depending on what I choose, so I can't even line the outcomes up side by side to rank them. Tell me where those belong, or whether they're a distraction at this level.

Last two things: one question I can put to the room at the end that would actually tell me whether they've got it, rather than whether they can repeat it back to me; and what I should openly refuse to go into at this level, and say so out loud — I don't want to be the fellow who name-drops literature he's read three papers of. I've got ninety minutes and a whiteboard, definately no slides.
