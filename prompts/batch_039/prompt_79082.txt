I'm a philosophy professor who's been drafted into runnning a three-session module on epistemic game theory for our interdisciplinary MA. The room is a handful of philosophers, a few economists, a couple of linguists; nobody has seen a knowledge operator in their life, and everyone has heard "common knowledge" used loosely at a dinner party. The one idea I've been asked to make land is common knowledge of rationality — what it adds over everyone knowing that everyone is rational, and what it's actually for in the theory. Don't explain the substance to me, I know the substance; I want the teaching plan. Please take the role of someone who has taught this at graduate level for years and has watched the standard presentations fail, brainstorm a couple of possible arcs before you commit to one, and think it through carefully rather than giving me the textbook order.

What I need, in order. First, the sequence: which notion of knowledge, which iterated-knowledge step, which fixed-point idea has to be in place before the next thing can even be stated, and where in that sequence the students' existing intuitions are working for me rather than against me. Second, the carrying example, chosen and defended against the obvious alternatives: I keep going back and forth between the coordinated-attack / two-generals story, the e-mail game where the automated acknowledgements never bottom out, the muddy-children puzzle, and the guessing game where everyone picks two-thirds of the average — tell me which one to build on, why, and what it has that the others lack (and if the honest answer is "none of them, use something else", say so and describe it). Third, the misconception map: they will arrive thinking common knowledge just means "everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows, roughly"; or that it's a psychological claim about actual people rather than a condition on a model; or that more common knowledge can only ever help. Fourth, what to leave out at this level and announce that I'm leaving out — I'm guessing the full partitional/fixed-point machinery and anything touching correlated equilibrium, but correct me. And one closing question that would actually distinguish a student who has the concept from one who has a slogan.

Keep the settled and the contested apart. And if part of my framing here is confused, or if a standard teaching route would be a mistake for this particular audience, push back on me.
