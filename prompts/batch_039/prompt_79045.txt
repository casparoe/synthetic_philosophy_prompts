I cover science and ideas, and I'm writing a piece on why originals matter. I want a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors — neither a strawman, each answering the other's actual points rather than talking past them — about a case I keep coming back to.

The case: a painter scans one of her canvases at high resolution and prints a second object on the same kind of canvas using the same pigments; no test, including hers, distinguishes them. She sells one as the original and one as a copy, then loses the notes recording which is which, and later exhibits both side by side under the title Original and Copy.

One speaker holds that the two now have the same aesthetic value, and that whatever is left over — an interest in which object came first — is a different kind of value, of the same order as caring that a friend actually climbed the mountain rather than being airlifted to the summit. The other holds that the difference is real even where no one can detect it, and that the first speaker's account of aesthetic experience quietly helps itself to beliefs about what one is looking at.

Let them work the harder cases rather than the easy ones: a forgery that copies nothing, since the forger invented the composition; a copy that seems to be a quotation of the original and so to have only borrowed value; an original so heavily restored that little of the paint is the artist's; a work that exists only as a score or a recipe and so has no original token at all; and the destruction of the original while the copy survives. Let each concede something real along the way, and let them stop without a forced resolution if the arguments do not force one.

Begin with the dialogue itself — no preface, no closing commentary.
