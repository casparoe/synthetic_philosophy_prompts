I chair the standards and appeals committee for a national certificate in "applied reasoning" — it's the short exam a few employers and two professional bodies use as an analytical-skills gate, so our rubric wording matters to people's jobs in a way that a university exam probably doesn't. Last month a candidate appealed a zero on one of our deduction items. The item hands you two premises and asks for the conclusion that follows; the rubric, which we rewrote two years back, awards a mark for "stating the governing principle at each step." The candidate did write the conclusion, but under it wrote that the inference wasn't licensed by anything they'd been given: a rule of inference is not a premise, they said, so write the rule into the item as a third premise and they'll use it, but then we owe them the rule that carries them from those three lines to the conclusion, and so on down the line. They then left the reasoning blank and took the zero. Their appeal argues that the rubric's own letter asked them to state the principle rather than apply it, and I have to admit they're not obviously wrong about that. This is, I know, that Lewis Carroll bit with the tortoise who cheerfully accepts every premise you hand him and every conditional you hand him next, and never gets to the conclusion. Half my committee wants to deny the appeal on the ground that an exam in reasoning which treats that answer as compliant tests nothing — a candidate who describes the rule and declines to be bound by it hasn't shown they can reason, and if we start granting marks for describing inferences we may as well mark essays on our own rubric. The other half wants to grant it, because our item does demand that a candidate endorse a rule we never gave them and never defended, and "we've never had to defend it" is not an argument.

Draft the written decision I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: a clear recommendation — deny, grant, or grant in part with a rubric amendment — with the exam cycle by which it takes effect, and reasons the people who wrote the item could actually respond to rather than a restatement of the psychometric handbook. It should take the tortoise's point seriously enough that the memo would survive being read by the candidate, and take the committee's worry seriously enough that it would survive being read by our chief examiner; if you think one side is right, say plainly what the other side has to give up. I'd also like it to say something real about what's underneath this, not just about our paperwork: whether accepting a conditional premise and being bound by a rule of inference are the same sort of thing at all, and if they aren't, whether a stipulated rule — something like "this item is governed by: from 'all A are B' and 'x is an A,' infer 'x is a B'; candidates are deemed to have adopted it" — is a legitimate way to stop the regress or just a politer way of hiding it, and whether a rule can be the kind of thing that's true or false in the first place, or only the kind of thing one does or doesn't go in for. One more bit of detail in case its useful: we piloted a "licence box" on a handful of items last spring (candidates tick that they adopt the item's rule) and it went badly in both directions — the strongest candidates ticked it and then wrote the conditional out as a line anyway, and the weakest ones ticked it and then took steps the licence plainly didn't cover. Make of that what you can. No citations or reading list needed, we're not publishing it and I'd rather you argue than cite. Around 800 words, headed with the committee's name and a one-line recommendation.
