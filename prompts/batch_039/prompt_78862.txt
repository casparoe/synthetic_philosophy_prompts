I teach the final-year module "Welfare of Farmed Fish and Crustaceans" on our animal-science MRes, and I am setting the summer examination paper. It runs three hours, candidates answer two of five questions, and each question carries 40 marks. The difficulty I keep running into is that the obvious essay titles — "Can decapod crustaceans suffer?", "Should welfare law treat finfish and crustaceans alike?" — are answered well by any student who has memorised the standard two-sided summary of the sentience literature, so a candidate with no real grasp of the underlying methodological and ethical difficulties can pass on recall alone. I also want to avoid a question whose answer turns on knowing particular reviews, statutes or commission reports, both because the candidates' reading is uneven and because I would rather test whether they can reason with a policy instrument than whether they can remember who published what. So for this one question I have invented a fictional certification body and two rival instruments for it, and I would like the question, a model answer and a marking scheme built around them.

The scenario, which I shall print in full on the paper, is this. The Aquatic Welfare Assurance Board (AWAB) is a new international certification body covering salmonid farms and decapod farms — shrimp, prawn and crayfish. AWAB's majority proposal is the Sentience-Weighted Harm Index (SWHI). Under SWHI an expert panel assigns each species a Sentience Confidence score c between 0 and 1, and assigns each recurring welfare-relevant event e — a day at a given stocking density, a handling or transport episode, a specified slaughter method — an Aversiveness score a between 0 and 10. A farm's index is the sum, over all events and species, of c × a × n, where n is the number of individuals undergoing that event in a year. The farm's grade is set by the size of this total, and the Board's stated rationale is that a farm can lower its index either by treating its animals better or by keeping fewer of them. The minority on the Board proposes instead a Threshold Trigger Schedule (TTS): the panel asks only whether the evidence of sentience for a species is adequate, and if it is, the species is scheduled and a short list of practices becomes prohibited outright — slaughter without prior stunning to immediate insensibility, stocking densities above a stated ceiling, and live sale for domestic boiling — with no weighting, no summing and no offsets between species or between individual animals. To fix ideas, the paper gives one worked case. Brackenmoor Farm runs two lines: whiteleg shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei), 40 million individuals a year, harvested by immersion in ice slurry, an event the panel scores a = 3, with c = 0.5; and rainbow trout, 200,000 individuals a year, killed by carbon-dioxide narcosis followed by gutting, scored a = 6, with c = 0.9. On these figures the shrimp line contributes 60,000,000 to the index and the trout line 1,080,000, so Brackenmoor's grade is determined almost entirely by the shrimp, and replacing ice-slurry harvest with a method scored a = 1 would cut the index by some 40,000,000 — more than closing the trout line altogether.

Please write the examination question itself — I envisage a short stem and three sub-parts: the first asking candidates to say what SWHI is actually measuring and what its arithmetic commits the Board to; the second asking what changes, if anything, when the index is normalised per individual rather than summed; and the third asking them to adjudicate between SWHI and TTS and to state what they would put in the standard in place of either. Alongside the question, please supply a model answer of about 800 words and a marking scheme for the 40 marks. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks, with the marks for each must-hit point and a one-line note on the characteristic mistake at each. I am especially keen that it distinguish an answer that merely says "aggregating over individuals is controversial" from one that can say precisely what the aggregation assumes, when those assumptions fail, and what the alternative gives up in turn — and that it reward a candidate who notices that the two instruments disagree not only about the facts but about what a certification standard is for.
