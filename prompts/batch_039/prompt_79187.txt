I chair the stratigraphic nomenclature committee of our national geological survey. The remit is unglamorous but not inert: we maintain the official time scale that appears in map legends, in the national science curriculum, and in a handful of environmental regulations that fix their scope by reference to named units. A revised edition is due in 2027, and the Anthropocene is the one item I cannot get the committee to settle. I hold the casting vote at our next meeting, so I need to circulate a written recommendation beforehand rather than open the floor and hope that the room converges.

Three proposals are before us. Our Quaternary mapping group wants a formal Anthropocene with its base at the mid-twentieth-century radionuclide signal — the weapons-testing fallout that appears as a sharp, effectively simultaneous horizon in our own lake cores and in polar ice. Two colleagues who work on agricultural soils want the base pushed back several thousand years, to the earliest detectable global signature of land clearance and wet-rice cultivation; they argue that a boundary near 1950 converts a long, diffuse transformation into a single dramatic rupture, and that the rupture story flatters a claim about industrial modernity that our own field evidence does not support. A third member, a geomorphologist who has spent her career on diachronous surfaces, argues that we should define no boundary at all and instead register the Anthropocene as an ongoing geological event rather than a unit of the time scale — real and consequential, but legitimately smeared out in time and therefore unfit for a golden spike. Our standing rules for admitting a new unit are, in order: a primary marker demonstrably close to isochronous across the whole territory; a reference section that is physically accessible and can be legally protected in perpetuity; and demonstrable utility for mapping and for the regulations that lean on us. There is an unwritten fourth rule — avoid contradicting the international decision — but the international bodies have not settled the matter, and our deadline will not wait for them.

Two pressures sit underneath all of this. Our survey's archive holds a detailed record of a colonial-era land transformation in one province, and colleagues outside geology have already told us that whatever date we choose will be read as a verdict on who caused the change and who bears responsibility for it. And there is a view on the committee, which I do not share but cannot dismiss, that a stratigraphic body has no business deciding something that is really a question of historical narrative and political responsibility. Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend one of the three courses of action, or a fourth if you think the choice on offer is badly framed, and give its reasoning in terms the dissenters could actually engage with — the strongest argument against my recommendation stated at full strength, not dispatched with a paragraph of throat-clearing. It should also say what the recommendation commits the survey to doing concretely over the next five years: curation of the reference section, revision procedure if the international picture changes, and what we tell the curriculum office in the meantime.
