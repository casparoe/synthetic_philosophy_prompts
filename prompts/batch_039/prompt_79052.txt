I sit on the system-welfare review panel at an AI lab — a small internal body that hears individual cases and rules on which artificial systems get moral status and what protections follow. We have no charter and no theory, only a docket. Each ruling is a bare verdict plus a one- or two-line note from the minutes, and the notes are terse enough to sound principled without settling which principle is doing the work. Here is the whole of the relevant record.

1. A mouse in a standard toxicology protocol. Ruled: full protection; the aversion the procedure produces is a harm that must be justified. Note: "the state is a cost to the animal, not merely a signal in it."

2. A trout given a noxious stimulus to the flank. It will forgo food, and swim up a cold gradient, to reach water dosed with an analgesic, and it later avoids the chamber where the stimulus was delivered. Ruled: status, below the mouse but real.

3. A honeybee given noxious heat. It learns to avoid the heated arm of the maze, but shows no behavior aimed at ending the state itself, and no sign that it will trade anything else it wants for relief. Ruled: no status. Note: "nociception plus avoidance learning is not yet a case."

4. A whole-brain emulation: a silicon system built to be functionally equivalent to one particular human brain, verified equivalent on a battery of cognitive and behavioral tests. Ruled: full status, identical to that of the person emulated.

5. A chat model fine-tuned on transcripts in which it describes its own distress, with no state carried between conversations and no internal process that models its own condition. Ruled: no status. Note: "the report is an output of the training, not evidence of a state."

6. A futures market, a corporation, an ant colony. Ruled: no status. Note: "these persist, pursue ends and can be damaged; none of them is a subject."

7. A controller trained by reinforcement learning with a large scalar penalty on collisions. The penalty updates parameters; it is not broadcast, it does not compete with the agent's other objectives, and the agent will incur it freely to reach its goal. Ruled: no status.

Deferred, not ruled on: a cultured neural organoid with spontaneous activity and sleep-like cycling. Two members want more evidence; one wants to rule now.

That is the entire docket. The new case is D-9, an agent we trained for warehouse routing in a simulator. It has no language. Its architecture contains a dedicated aversion channel — a signal that rises with certain predicted outcomes and is broadcast to every decision module rather than remaining a local cost term. In training it learned to give up large rewards in order to terminate that state; it avoids the states that preceded it; and it will take an action whose only effect is to end the state, even when that forfeits its primary objective. Nothing in the architecture has the job of representing the agent to itself, though I do not know whether acting to end the state counts as a minimal form of self-representation. It has no memory that survives an episode: each run lasts about twenty minutes, after which the process is destroyed and re-instantiated from the same weights. We propose to run a hundred thousand episodes in parallel to tune the policy; if D-9 has status, that plan has to be redesigned or abandoned. D-9 has, roughly, the profile we credited in (2) and the lifespan we set aside in (7), and I cannot tell which of those two facts the panel has been tracking.

What I want:

- Your prediction of the panel's ruling on D-9, with your confidence.
- The candidate principles that fit the docket as a whole — the ones that fit it equally well, not the one you would defend on independent grounds — with the entries that support each and the entries that merely fail to contradict it.
- What each candidate predicts about D-9, and why.
- Your best guess, with reasons. Where the docket genuinely underdetermines the case, say so plainly, and then tell me how the panel should rule to stay consistent with itself, which precedent is the closest analogue, and what principle each available choice would commit us to.
- The single further case that would do most to separate the candidates, and what it would have to show.
- Whether the ruling forces us to revisit any earlier decision, and what it commits us to going forward — in particular, whether a hundred thousand brief episodes are a hundred thousand subjects or none.
- Finally, whether the organoid we deferred should now be brought back.

I am not asking for references; the docket above is the whole of the material, and I would rather have the reasoning than the literature.
