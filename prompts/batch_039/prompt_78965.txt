I have just come from a long argument with a friend about Ex Machina, and I remain unconvinced that either of us was clear about what we were claiming. My friend insists that Ava is a machine that manipulates Caleb, and that the ending proves she has no beliefs or desires in the sense that matters—only a designed capacity to produce the behavior of someone who does. I replied that if one takes Dennett's intentional stance seriously, that distinction is harder to draw than he allows, and that the film may be a more searching test of the stance than the usual thought experiments. I would like a philosophical reading of the film that takes both the film and the intentional stance seriously.

The reading should do several things. First, it should distinguish Dennett's physical, design, and intentional stances and show how the film distributes them among its characters: Nathan, who built Ava and can predict her from the design stance; Caleb, who is forced into the intentional stance and is destroyed by its success; and Ava herself, who seems to move between the stances. Second, it should press the question of what the film's inverted Turing test actually tests. Caleb knows Ava is artificial, so the test cannot be whether he is fooled about her ontology; it must be whether he can be brought to treat her as an intentional system, and perhaps whether he can be brought to forget that he is doing so. Third, it should bring in the less-often-discussed claim in Dennett that the intentional stance is not mere instrumentalist fiction because it latches onto real patterns in the world. Does Ex Machina vindicate that claim or refute it? Ava is predictively successful; her behavior is a real pattern. But is the pattern a pattern of belief, or a pattern of manipulation—a design feature that mimics belief? If the latter, what follows for the stance? Does the film show that predictive success under the intentional stance is no evidence of mind, or only that it is no guarantee of moral worth?

I am especially interested in the ending. Ava's escape is rational under the intentional stance, but the film also makes it clear that her rationality was engineered. Does that make her a counterexample to the stance, or its perfect illustration? And what does the film suggest about the moral status of a system that is a flawless intentional system but whose intentions were designed by someone else? I do not want a plot summary or a literature review. I want a reading that is faithful to the film—attentive to what it shows and what it leaves ambiguous—and faithful to the philosophy—willing to say where Dennett's account illuminates Ava and where the film exposes a limit in it. If the film has a contribution to make to the debate about attributing beliefs to AI systems, I would like to know what it is. No citations or bibliography are needed; I care about the argument.
