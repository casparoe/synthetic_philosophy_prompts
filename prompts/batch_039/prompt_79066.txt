I've been sorta convinced by lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. The way I understand it: there's a threshold of suffering such that once a being crosses it, no amount of pleasure anywhere else can compensate for that suffering, because it's lexically worse, but below the threshold ordinary trade-offs between pleasure and pain are allowed. I want a map of what would count against this view. Not just the usual instant-extinction objection, which I take the threshold to be designed to block. I mean: which cases, intuitions, or formal results should actually make me doubt it? What does the view predict that rival views don't? Does the threshold do real work, or does it collapse into either plain negative utilitarianism when the threshold is zero or something more like prioritarianism once the threshold gets fuzzy? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones defenders of the view would themselves concede rather than just what critics say. Also, I keep getting stuck on the commensurability part: if the threshold marks a point where suffering lexically beats pleasure, doesn't that presuppose pleasure and pain are comparable enough to locate the threshold in the first place? And if they're not commensurable, can the view even state its own thesis without smuggling in a common scale? I'd like you to sort that out instead of assuming one side.
