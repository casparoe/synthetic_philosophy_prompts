I have been rereading Franklin's account of his project of arriving at moral perfection for a seminar on eighteenth-century moral psychology, and the more closely I look at the actual mechanics of the thing, the less clear I am about what it commits him to. The practice itself is quite concrete: he draws up a list of thirteen virtues — temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and, added last, humility — each with a short precept attached, and then keeps a little book with a page for every virtue, ruled into seven columns for the days of the week; each evening he marks the faults of the day, concentrating for a single week on one virtue while still recording lapses in the others, so that one full pass through the list takes thirteen weeks and four passes make a year. The order of the list is chosen deliberately, with temperance placed first on the grounds that it keeps the head clear for the vigilance the remaining virtues demand, and he reports that order, which he included because his printing business needed method, is the one that costs him the most painful attention and that he never manages to acquire. At the end he concedes that he cannot claim to have acquired humility itself, only its appearance, and that the appearance has served him well; he adds that if he had ever succeeded in conquering pride he would probably have been proud of his humility.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes, as distinct from the ones Franklin happens to avow elsewhere in the book. Does the daily grid presuppose that the virtues are separable habits trainable one at a time, and does the persistent failure with order falsify that assumption or merely show that his circumstances were irregular? Does keeping a numerical tally of one's own faults presuppose a particular view about the reliability and the moral innocence of self-observation — that the scrutiny will not itself become a vice — or is there a self-referential difficulty here, given that pride and vanity are among the things being scored? Does the fact that the practice seems content with the appearance of humility mean that it is not really a perfectionist program at all but something closer to a program of reputation management, or can the appearance be a legitimate intermediate stage in the acquisition of the virtue? And does the whole exercise presuppose that virtue comes in degrees and can be approached asymptotically, or that it is a threshold to be crossed?

I would like the answer to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, since I suspect several quite different theories of character would license this same daily routine. In particular, tell me where the practice is underdetermined, and then what would have to change in it if we replaced the underlying picture with a unity-of-virtues view on which the virtues cannot be acquired piecemeal, or with a view on which stable character traits of the sort being tracked are largely an illusion and the relevant causes of behavior are situational. Would the little book have to be abandoned, or only reinterpreted?
