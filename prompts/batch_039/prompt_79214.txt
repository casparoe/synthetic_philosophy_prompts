My friend and I spent most of yesterday arguing about whether the MU-puzzle is genuinely a case of jumping out of the system or just an ordinary proof dressed up in formal clothes, and I am still not sure. I am now grading a homework question I set on exactly that. Below is the question and one student's answer. Please give the answer a grade out of 10 and write the comments that justify it, point by point. I am the instructor, so be direct about what is right and what is not.

Question. In the MU-puzzle, you begin with the string MI and may apply these rules: (1) if a string ends in I, you may append U; (2) if a string is Mx, you may replace it with Mxx; (3) you may replace any occurrence of III with U; (4) you may delete any occurrence of UU. The target string is MU. (a) Prove that MU cannot be derived. (b) Explain what is meant by jumping out of the system, and say whether your proof in (a) is an example of it. (c) Consider a variant with the same axiom and rules, except that rule (3) may be applied only to a III that is immediately preceded by M. Is MU derivable in this variant? Justify your answer.

Student answer. (a) The key is to find an invariant. The number of I's in any derivable string is never a multiple of 3. MI has one I, so it satisfies the invariant. Rule (1) adds a U and leaves the I-count unchanged. Rule (2) doubles the I-count, so if n is not a multiple of 3, 2n is not a multiple of 3. Rule (3) removes three I's, so it preserves the property of not being a multiple of 3. Rule (4) removes U's and leaves the I-count alone. MU has zero I's, and zero is a multiple of 3. So MU cannot be derived. (b) Jumping out of the system means stepping outside the formal rules to prove something about the system from a higher level. My proof is an example, because instead of trying to derive MU, I found a property that all derivable strings share. But it is not really a mathematical proof; it is a meta-level observation, and the point is that such jumps are always informal and cannot be formalized. (c) In the variant, rule (3) is restricted, so the invariant no longer applies. Since the restriction makes the system weaker, MU might actually become derivable, because the original impossibility proof depended on being able to apply rule (3) anywhere. However, I tried a few derivations and could not find one, so I suspect MU is still underivable, but the invariant argument does not settle it.
