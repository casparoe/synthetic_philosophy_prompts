Q4 (10 points). Bertrand's paradox is sometimes said to show that the principle of indifference is untenable. (a) State the paradox and give Bertrand's three ways of choosing a chord at random, with the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the inscribed triangle for each. Here's what the student wrote:

(a) Take a circle with an inscribed equilateral triangle; the side of the triangle is √3 times the radius. We want the chance that a randomly chosen chord is longer than a side.

Method 1, random endpoints: pick two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and join them. Rotate so one endpoint sits at a vertex of the triangle; the chord is long enough exactly when the other endpoint lands on the opposite third of the circle. So p = 1/3.

Method 2, random radius point: pick a radius, then a point on it uniformly, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius at that point. The side of the triangle cuts the radius at its midpoint, so the chord is long enough when the point falls in the inner half: p = 1/2.

Method 3, random midpoint: pick a point uniformly in the disk and take the chord having that point as midpoint. The chord is long enough just when the point lies in the concentric circle of half the radius, which has a quarter of the area: p = 1/4.

Bertrand set out these three arguments in Calcul des probabilités (1889), and he thought the random-endpoints argument was the right one; the other two were meant as illustrations of how easily one goes wrong.

(b) The lesson usually drawn is that the principle of indifference is inconsistent. The very same ignorance — we aren't told how the chord is picked — is compatible with three different answers, so a rule that tells you to spread probability evenly over the possibilities can't be a sound rule of inference at all. The standard escape is that the phrase at random is ambiguous, and the problem is well posed only once a physical procedure is specified (throwing straws at the circle, spinning a pointer, and so on). That much is uncontroversial.

(c) Jaynes's argument (1973) is that we shouldn't have to specify the procedure. Nothing in the problem fixes the size of the circle or where it sits on the page, so any objective answer has to be indifferent to size and position, i.e. invariant under scaling and translation. He writes down the integral equations expressing this and shows they have a unique solution, namely the random-midpoint method, so the answer is 1/4. Because Jaynes's argument removes the ambiguity instead of legislating it away, the 1/3 and 1/2 answers are now generally regarded as artifacts of badly chosen parameterizations.
