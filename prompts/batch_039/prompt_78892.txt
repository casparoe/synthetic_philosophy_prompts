I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works on the epistemology of testimony, and whose view is that the whole pramāṇa apparatus is a baroque way of saying that we reason, well or badly, from the reliability of our sources. I defended the position set out below with rather more confidence than I actually feel, and I would now like an audit rather than an exposition. Here is what I hold.

1. The Nyāya classification of the pramāṇas is, I think, on the right lines, and each of the four it lists is a distinct and irreducible route to knowledge: perception (pratyakṣa); inference (anumāna); comparison (upamāna), the stock case being the traveller who is told that a gavaya resembles a cow and who then, on seeing one in the forest, comes to know what a gavaya is; and testimony (śabda). I am also inclined to think that knowledge sources are not to be multiplied beyond need.

2. Testimony is not reducible to inference. When someone competent and sincere tells me something and I understand the words, I can come to know it without first reasoning from her reliability; the epistemic basis I have is simply her telling me. The corridor case is the one I care about: a stranger in the department office mentions in passing that the seminar has moved to Thursday, and I take it that I now know that the seminar has moved.

3. Testimony nonetheless yields knowledge only when the speaker is āpta, that is, competent and sincere. If I have reason to think my informant is guessing, repeating gossip, or lying, I do not know. I also do not think the source has to be a person: a printed timetable, or a properly calibrated instrument, can give me knowledge in the same way, so what matters is the reliability of the source rather than its communicative intentions.

4. I accept the default-and-defeat picture of justification, on which cognitions are innocent until reasonably challenged, and doubt requires a reason rather than the reverse. I do not accept the stronger Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta thesis of svataḥ prāmāṇya, that a cognition's validity is intrinsic and known by the cognition itself; a cognition can fail to be knowledge even when nothing has yet gone wrong for me. Error, I take it, is best explained along Nyāya lines as misattribution (anyathākhyāti): the rope is cognised, but as a snake.

5. I think the Mīmāṃsakas were right to insist on arthāpatti, postulation, as a pramāṇa in its own right. In the stock case — Devadatta is fat, and he does not eat during the day, so the only way to reconcile these is that he eats at night — the conclusion is genuinely known, and I do not believe we reach it by inference.

6. I am not persuaded, however, that non-apprehension (anupalabdhi) is a pramāṇa in its own right. Knowing that there is no pot on the table seems to me to be a piece of inference: if there were a pot I should perceive one, and I do not.

What I want is a consistency audit. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, and, for each real tension, what would have to give and how costly the cheapest repair would be. Order the tensions by severity instead of treating them as equally bad, and finish by naming the single revision that would clear up the most.
