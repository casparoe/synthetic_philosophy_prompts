I am in eleventh grade, and the essay below is the one I submitted to my school's philosophy journal. My teacher praised the organisation but would not tell me whether the argument is actually sound, so I would like comments from someone who will. Please judge the argument on its own terms; I have not read much of the literature and I do not have access to journals, so I would rather have explanations than pointers to sources.

**Which box should you take?**

Imagine two boxes. Box A contains a thousand dollars. Box B contains either a million dollars or nothing. A predictor, known to be highly accurate, has already filled the boxes, and it put the million into B if and only if it predicted that you would take B alone. You now choose: take both boxes, or take B alone. You keep whatever is in the boxes you take.

I think you should take B alone, for two reasons.

First, suppose the predictor is right with probability p. If I take B alone, I receive the million exactly when the prediction was right, so my expected value is p × 1,000,000. If I take both boxes, then I receive the million plus a thousand exactly when the prediction was wrong, which happens with probability 1 − p, and otherwise I receive just the thousand; so my expected value is (1 − p) × 1,001,000 + p × 1,000. Setting the two equal and solving gives p = 0.5005. Taking B alone therefore wins as soon as the predictor is better than a coin flip. Since the problem stipulates that the predictor is far better than that, anyone who takes both boxes must be claiming that the predictor's accuracy is irrelevant, which cannot be right.

Second, imagine that box B has a lid I am permitted to lift before choosing. If I look and see the million, I will take both boxes and end up with 1,001,000; if I look and see nothing, I will still take both boxes and end up with 1,000. A player who can look therefore always takes both boxes, the predictor knows this, and so it leaves B empty. The ability to look costs me the million, while being unable to look, and taking B alone, makes me rich. The winning thing is to be the kind of person who takes B alone, and that is what I should be.

The usual objections seem weak to me. That the money is already in the boxes shows only that my choice does not cause the contents; it does not show that my choice is not evidence about them, and evidence is what expected value runs on. And the complaint that I am ignoring free money in box A begs the question, since taking box A is precisely what makes box B empty.

Could you tell me whether this works? In particular: is my arithmetic right, and is the conclusion I draw from it, that a two-boxer must be denying the predictor's accuracy, fair to the two-boxer? Does my second argument really support taking B alone, or does it only show something about the version with the lid? And what would you cut, given that the journal limits me to a thousand words? Please give me a definite verdict rather than a survey: say which box you think a rational player takes in the original problem and why, and identify the single weakest step in what I have written. If you think part of my argument is right in a way that people usually miss, please say so as well.
