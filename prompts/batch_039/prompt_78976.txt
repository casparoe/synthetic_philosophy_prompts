I'm a final-year philosophy student with a minor in cognitive science, currently enrolled in a seminar on evolution, computation, and mind. I have one semester to work with, a supervisor whose background is in the philosophy of biology, and no capacity to run experiments — so anything I take on has to be argued rather than tested.

The cluster I keep circling is the one usually credited to Daniel Dennett: the idea that Darwin and Turing each inverted our ordinary picture of reasoning. Darwin, on this telling, showed how adaptive competence can be produced by a process that comprehends nothing of what it is doing; Turing showed how a system can reliably produce correct outputs without understanding the rule it follows. I would like to write my main seminar paper here, but the area feels like a long tail of loosely connected questions, and I cannot tell which of them are load-bearing.

Please give me a prioritised agenda of the open questions in this area: which two or three genuinely deserve a semester of a philosopher's attention, in what order, and why. I care far more about the reasoning than the list. For each, tell me what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone working conceptually rather than empirically, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what would move it up or down your ranking — including what argument or finding over the next year would change your mind. Also tell me which questions here are overrated, or look deep but turn out to be terminological.

A few framings I have been turning over, which you should feel free to reject, revise, or use as test cases for the ranking. First, that the two inversions may not share a shape: the Darwinian one is diachronic, explaining how competence comes to exist without a designer, whereas the Turing one is synchronic, asserting that a competence can be present at a time without comprehension. If that difference is real, does the generalisation into a single thesis about minds survive? Second, the suspicion that comprehension is doing one of two very different jobs in these discussions — naming a further layer of competence, or naming a genuine explanatory remainder that no amount of added competence would close — and that which job it does is rarely made explicit. Third, the thought that the interesting question is not whether some system comprehends, but what work the predicate is doing in our explanations, and whether it is discovered or conferred.

Please keep the answer self-contained, and do not give me a reading list, citations, or references — I cannot check them and I am not asking for them. Where you are confident, say so and say why; where the literature or the community is genuinely split, mark the split rather than smoothing it over. I would rather have a defended ranking with visible seams than a tidy consensus that does not exist.
