Question 3 (25 points). Schelling treats the deliberate creation of a shared risk of war as a way of making one's threats credible. (a) Distinguish a threat that leaves something to chance from two neighbours it is easily confused with: a threat backed by a stated probability of execution, and an automatic commitment of the kind people have in mind when they call forward-deployed forces a tripwire. (b) Explain why Schelling thinks a threat that leaves something to chance can be credible even when the act threatened would be irrational to carry out. (c) Assess the claim that brinkmanship is just a test of which side is willing to be more irrational.

Student's answer. (a) Schelling's central insight is that in a crisis the object of the game is not to win the military engagement but to manipulate the other side's expectations, and brinkmanship is the technique of doing this by creating a risk that both sides share. The key feature is that the risk is not fully under the brinkman's control; if he could turn it on and off at will, the adversary would simply wait him out. A stated probability of execution — say, announcing that one will launch with probability 0.3 — and a threat that leaves something to chance are therefore the same instrument, differing only in how the risk is generated: in the one case the risk comes from a deliberate policy of randomization, in the other from the possibility that events slip out of hand. What matters to the adversary is that the expected cost of standing firm is positive, so any device that puts a nonzero probability on the bad outcome will serve. It follows that brinkmanship is always available to a rational actor: he need only announce the probability, or arrange his forces so that the probability is visible to the other side. A tripwire is a special case of the same logic, since the troops are stationed forward precisely so that the adversary cannot attack without some chance of involving the guarantor.

(b) The credibility problem for a threat to do something irrational — say, to retaliate after an attack that has already destroyed one's cities — is that a rational adversary knows the threat would not be carried out, since carrying it out buys nothing. Schelling's solution is to make the carrying out of the threat no longer a decision. If the situation is genuinely out of hand, then the fact that execution would be irrational for the brinkman is beside the point, because no one is choosing. This is why the threat that leaves something to chance does not require the brinkman to be irrational, or to pretend to be. It requires only that the adversary cannot count on him to remain in control.

(c) On its face the claim is a caricature, but there is something to it. Schelling does describe crisis bargaining as a competition in risk-taking, in which the side that can tolerate more risk, or that can credibly convey that it is willing to go further, tends to come out ahead. If that is right, then a reputation for erratic and unpredictable behaviour is an asset, and the claim that brinkmanship tests who is willing to be more irrational is close to the truth. The practical implication is that a state should make its decision-making as opaque as possible, since the more the adversary is kept guessing about what one will do, the more credible one's threats become.
