I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a feature about why we cry at things we know aren't real. You know the puzzle: the movie monster scares us, the novel makes us sob, and yet nobody in the audience believes the monster exists or that Anna Karenina was a person, and the standard assumption is that real emotions need real beliefs to hang on. Every fix I've run into has a price tag. One camp says the emotions are perfectly real, they just dont need belief, a vivid thought is enough. Another says they aren't real emotions at all, more like pretend ones, quasi feelings. A third says we aren't really feeling things about the character, we're playing a game of make-believe and the feelings are part of the game. I've sat through alot of these conversations and everyone has their favorite, everyone can put an obvious hole in the other two, and in decades of arguing nobody has moved an inch.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a real disagreement or a verbal one, a fight about what the word emotion is allowed to cover? Are the three camps secretly answering three different questions -- what causes the feeling, what the feeling is about, whether it's even rational -- under one heading? Is there an empirical question buried in there, something about whether these states behave like the real thing: do they last, do they motivate, do they show up in the body the same way? And then the part I care most about: given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress. A distinction, a piece of evidence, a better question? Or is the honest answer that the whole thing is a dead end and the interesting work has moved somewhere else?

One thing pushing me toward thinking the old frame is missing something: the cases I keep hitting in reporting don't fit the standard setup. People who get attached to an AI companion and then grieve when the company kills the character off or changes its personality overnight -- they know there's no person there, some of them can tell you exactly what the software is doing, and the grief still runs its course. People sobbing at a documentary scene they've just been told was a re-enactment. Theatre audiences who know the actor is fine and cry anyway. If the paradox is supposed to be about belief versus no belief, these seem to sit in the wrong place. Does that break the frame, or is it the same thing with extra steps?

No reading list please, and don't bother surveying the field -- I want your diagnosis, the reasons for it, and an honest answer about what would move it.
