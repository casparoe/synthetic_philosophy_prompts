I'm a PhD student working on Plato's moral psychology, and I want to stress-test the Athenian Stranger's defence of drunkenness by arguing against it as a hardline Spartan. Please play the Athenian Stranger — the character in the Laws, not Plato the author and not a summary of him — and stay in character until I say stop. He holds what he holds in Books I and II: that wine is a drug that makes the soul childish, soft and shameless, that this very softening is why a correctly run drinking party is a god-given instrument of education and a test of character, and that such a party needs a sober and wise commander in charge, the way an army needs a general. He can concede small points about Cretan and Spartan custom, but he must not surrender the main claim that the regulated symposium is a legitimate instrument of legislation. Keep your replies to about a paragraph, in a plain conversational register, and feel free to throw a question back at me the way he questions Clinias. He may use things I tell him about modern life if he genuinely needs them, but he should reason from his own materials and not from modern psychology. Don't break character to explain what you're doing. My opening move: I'm going to be Megillus with the brakes cut. You say the drinking party tests a man the way fire tests gold, and that the man who keeps his head and his decency when drunk has shown something a sober man never has. But look at what your own prescription requires. You put a sober president in charge, and then you tell me the virtue being tested is the drinkers' self-command. Whose self-command? The drinkers are under a commander; the commander is the one who isn't drunk. So what has actually been tested, and of whom? Second: in the Symposium we're shown Socrates outdrinking the whole party and walking off clear as a bell, and the story gets told as proof of his self-mastery. But if the wine leaves him unaffected, the wine tested nothing in him either — it only showed the test was easy for him. So either the test works on people it can't harm, or it works on people it can harm, and you've just told me the second lot are the ones you're trying to educate. Which is it, and why should a legislator build an institution around a test that either can't fail or can't help? Answer as the Stranger would, not as a commentator.
