You are an expert in decision theory and in the practical ethics of extortion and ransom policy. I need you to think carefully and to brainstorm before you write, because the task is to turn a piece of abstract decision theory into something my colleagues can actually run. Before you write the procedure, list the key distinctions you think must survive the translation from theory to practice, and the ones you think can be dropped.

I am the head of policy at a mid-sized cyber-insurance company. Every few weeks one of our policyholders is hit by ransomware, and our claims team has to decide whether to authorize a payment to the attackers. The decisions are high-stakes (often several million dollars; sometimes a hospital's patient records or a city's personnel files), they are made under time pressure (the attackers set a deadline, often 72 hours), they are made with incomplete information (we rarely know who the attackers are or whether they will honor a deal), and they have to respect sanctions law and our own legal obligations. Our CEO signs off, but the claims team does the analysis. Right now we decide case by case, and I think we are being inconsistent and probably making things worse. Our decisions also affect the ecosystem: attackers learn which insurers pay, and because we insure many organizations, a payment in one case may raise the frequency of attacks on all of them.

The structure of these decisions, I think, is the structure of two thought experiments from decision theory. In counterfactual mugging, a perfect predictor flips a coin: if it comes up tails, it asks you for $100; if it comes up heads, it pays you $10,000, but only if it predicted in advance that you would have paid had the coin come up tails. You are only ever asked in the tails branch. Causal decision theory says refuse; updateless decision theory says pay. In the blackmail scenario known as XOR blackmail, a blackmailer threatens to reveal your secret unless you pay, but the blackmailer's incentives are structured so that the threat is credible without ordinary leverage -- they are made whole by exactly one of two outcomes, your payment or the revelation -- so the usual reasoning that the threat must be a bluff fails. In both cases, the question is whether to take an action that helps in the situation you are actually in but that changes what kind of agent you are, or what kind of agent you are predicted to be.

I want a reusable decision procedure -- a checklist or a flowchart in words -- that our claims team can run, in order, on any ransomware case. It should say which questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should be usable by someone who has never read the decision theory, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It should also say what to do when a key fact is unknown or contested.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a regional hospital: the attackers have encrypted patient records and threaten to publish them; the hospital's insurer (us) would cover a $2 million payment; the hospital says it will pay with or without us. Second, a city government: the attackers have stolen personnel files and are demanding $500,000; a well-known incident-response firm is advising the city to pay and has offered to handle the negotiation; the attackers have a track record of publishing data from victims who paid late. Third, a novel case I want you to reason about carefully: the attackers are sophisticated and are known to model their targets' published policies. They have structured the demand so that they are made whole either way -- if we pay, they keep the money; if we refuse, they have a standing offer from a data broker to buy the stolen files. They have told us this, and we believe them. So the threat is credible in the XOR-blackmail sense, refusing does not save the data, and the attackers appear to have chosen us as a target because they predicted we would pay. Does the procedure handle this, or does it break? What should it say?

At least one of your worked cases should come out in a way I will not like -- for example, a case where the procedure says do not pay even though paying would obviously help the victim in front of us, or a case where it says pay even though our policy says not to. Tell me what the procedure cannot do, and where it will misfire. Write in American English.
