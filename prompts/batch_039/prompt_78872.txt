Last night me and my friend Dana got into a stupidly long arguement at a bar about whether a legal system can pull itself up by its own bootstraps — specifically, whether a rule that says how rules get changed can be changed by itself. She said obviously yes, people amend constitutions all the time. I said sure, but not the clause that does the amending, not without some outside anchor, and we never actually finished the thought. Now I want a short story that works the thing out instead of hand-waving it.

So here's the setup I want you to use. It's an invented country, so don't go looking for it and don't swap in a real one. Keep the details as I give them.

The Republic of Marenya. Its founding charter contains an article, Article 61, that says roughly this (my paraphrase of my own invented text):

(a) Any article of this charter, including this one, may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the Grand Assembly.
(b) An amendment to this article does not take effect until it has been ratified by the Grand Assembly sitting one generation later, which for our purposes means twenty-five years after the vote.
(c) No amendment may remove requirement (b) with respect to itself.

The plot: a reformist Assembly, led by someone who has wanted to scrap that twenty-five-year delay her entire career, passes an amendment deleting (b). Two-thirds, clean vote, no procedural screwups, everything by the book. But under (a) that's allowed, under (b) the deletion only takes effect twenty-five years later, and under (c) maybe it can't be done at all, since removing (b) is precisely removing (b) with respect to itself. So is the amendment law the moment it passes, because it deleted the delay? Not law for twenty-five years, because the delay was still in force when it passed? Or void from the start, because of (c)? And the wrinkle I really want in there: when the ratifying Assembly finally sits, it's mostly the same people, since Marenyan politicians live a long time and the chamber is small, so the "next generation" gate may be a fiction everybody has been politely maintaining for a century.

What I want: a short story, 1500 to 2500 words, that puts this paradox at the center and actually works it rather than just gesturing at it. Somewhere in the middle I want a character — a clerk, a judge, an archivist, whoever you like — laying out the two or three horns clearly enough that a reader could argue any side. I want at least one scene where somebody has to act: certify the amendment, refuse to certify it, print it in the statute book, refuse to print it. And the story has to live with the consequences of that action instead of dissolving them. Do not end with a wise narrator telling us the paradox is deep. End with the thing done.

Two extras if they help. An archivist finds a note from the founders suggesting they believed (c) made Article 61 permanent and (a) made it amendable, and never noticed those can't both be true. And the court that ends up ruling on the amendment gets its jurisdiction from an article that was itself amended by the amendment in dispute. Use those or don't, but keep it about the self-amendment problem, not generic political intrigue.

Voice: plain, concrete, some dialogue, no lectures. Don't explain the paradox to the reader in a footnote-y way — let the characters fight about it.
