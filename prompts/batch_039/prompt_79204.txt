I am a novelist writing a book about a character who becomes preoccupied with the moral status of insects, and I keep encountering a pattern in the arguments around me. Someone will say that the chance an insect is sentient is very small—one in a million, say—but then note that there are incomprehensibly many insects alive at any moment, so the expected suffering is enormous and should dominate our priorities. Pressed on the probability, the person says it is not a frequency but a subjective degree of belief about a metaphysical question, so it cannot be dismissed as speculation; and the same structure appears with tiny probabilities of catastrophic outcomes multiplied by vast future populations. I want to know whether this pattern has a name. I know it is near Pascal's mugging and near the objection that expected-value reasoning is fanatical, but it seems to have a distinct feature: the small probability is an avowal of ignorance rather than a well-defined chance, and it is being multiplied by a number that is supposed to be empirical. Please act as a philosopher of mind and decision theorist. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the nearest concepts. Then tell me whether there is an established term for the exact move, and if not, coin one. In either case, define it precisely: what the move is, what it is not, how it differs from Pascal's mugging and the fanaticism objection, and where its edges lie—when is the calculation legitimate, and what would have to change for the small probability to be genuinely action-guiding rather than a way of laundering metaphysical uncertainty into a verdict? No citations or reading lists, please; I want the conceptual carving.
