I'm TAing for a probability-and-induction course and I keep getting into the same stupid fight with the students about Bertrand's chord thing. You know the one: take a chord of a circle "at random" and ask the chance it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle -- pick two endpoints on the circumference and you get 1/3, pick a radius and then a point along it and you get 1/2, pick a point in the disc as the midpoint and you get 1/4. Everyone can recite the three recipes, nobody can say what the lesson is supposed to be. I've got a suspician that the useful move isn't to crown a favourite recipe but to sit down and write out what any principle of indifference would have to do to deserve the name -- so that's what I want from you: the desiderata. What does a rule that says spread your credence evenly over possibilities you have no reason to distinguish have to deliver before it counts as adequate? Give me the list with the consideration behind each item, not a list of adjectives -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results it has to stay consistent with, the temptations it has to resist. And I want to be able to test a candidate against concrete cases, so here are the ones I care about: (1) the chord, above; (2) a machine cuts a one-metre stick at a random point and I want the chance the longer piece is at least twice the shorter -- does "random" mean uniform in the cut point, or uniform in the ratio of the two pieces?; (3) a factory turns out cubes whose edge length is known only to lie between 1 and 2 cm, so the edge, the face area and the volume are each equally unknown in a fixed interval -- over which do I spread credence?; (4) a bent coin I know nothing about: even over the bias p, or even over the log-odds?; (5) a point picked "at random" on a sphere, and then the question of what conditioning on it landing on one particular great circle even means, that being an event of probability zero; (6) a case where the number of possibilities is itself unknown -- an urn where I don't know how many colours or how many marbles I'm ignorant about.

Then the part I actually want. Say which of these requirements are genuine desiderata and which are just a camp's prejudice -- I've noticed the invariance people and the "indifference is a con" people seem to want opposite things and both act like it's obvious -- and, most important, flag any pair or subset of the desiderata I can't have together, because my hunch is that the list is where the real result lives: something like always-unique-answer and never-privileges-a-parameterization can't both be satisfied, which would make Bertrand's paradox not a puzzle about chords but a proof that we've been asking the principle for too much. If one requirement does the most damage to the popular proposals, name it and say why. I've been calling my pet version of the requirement description-invariance -- indifference is only licensed when the symmetry you lean on is a symmetry of the situation itself and not of the way we happen to have written it down -- so tell me what that rule buys, what it costs, and whether it's even formulable without circularity.

Finally, since I have to say something in class: two or three lines each on how the maximum-entropy/group-invariance approach, the "just use a reference prior that works in practice" approach, and the flat refusal to have any such principle at all do against the list. No history, no names and dates, no reading list -- I don't want a bibliography, I want the criteria and the tension between them. And be honest about which desiderata are only requirements if you've already decided the principle is meant to ground real statistical inference rather than serve as a teaching heuristic; that changes the list and I want to see exactly where.
