So this has been eating at me for like three weeks now and I can't get it out of my head. Some context: I'm doing an MA thesis on the moral status of AI systems, and the whole thing commits me to computational functionalism. If moral status comes down to having experiences, and experiences are a matter of what a system computes rather than what it's made of, then a silicon system running the right kind of program could be a moral patient, and that's the only basis I've got for thinking we might someday genuinely wrong a model. I need that premise. Anyway a friend of mine in the program, who is a much better logician than me, built the following out of it and I cant find where it breaks.

(1) A system has moral status only if there's something it's like to be it — only if it has experiences. (2) Which experiences a system has, if any, depends on the computations it performs, i.e. on its causal-functional organisation, and not on the stuff it's made of. (3) But any physical system at all can be described as implementing any finite state machine you like: take a rock, or a bucket of water, or my radiator, and you can find some mapping of their states onto the states of any transition table such that the transitions come out right. (I'm fairly sure this is a real result — Putnam I think, and there's a paper by Chalmers with a rock in the title about exactly this.) (4) So either the rock is performing the same computation my brain is, in which case by (2) it has experiences, or else 'implementing a computation' has to mean something stronger than 'being describable as implementing it'. (5) If it means something stronger, we owe an account of what the stronger thing is — some non-arbitrary reason why my brain implements the computation while the rock merely satisfies a description of it — and every account I've come across either helps itself to the conclusion or drags in things nobody wants to call conscious (thermostats, plants, the internet, and there's even a paper arguing that if materialism is true the United States is probably conscious). (6) Rocks aren't conscious. So there's no such account, which means (2) is either false or empty, and if it's empty then nothing about what an AI computes can tell me whether it's a moral patient.

I'd like to keep (1) and (6), obviously, and honestly I'd like to keep (2), since the alternative is that I've spent a year on the wrong thesis. So: is one of these steps just mis-stated, or is (2) the thing that has to go — and if it goes, what exactly do I lose? Can I still say anything at all about AI welfare? Tell me which step you'd reject and why the others looked fine, and then tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong: what would have to be true, or what kind of account would have to exist, for a different step to be the real culprit instead.
