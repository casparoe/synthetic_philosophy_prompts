A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about what licenses an assertion. His view is that the norm of assertion is belief: a speaker is entitled to say something just when she believes it, and knowledge and truth matter, where they matter at all, only because they tend to accompany belief. I think that is at best half the story, but every case I raised to show it played straight into his hands. The familiar counterexamples run in one direction only — a speaker who asserts what she believes without knowing it, because she is lucky or because her evidence misleads her — and these show only that belief is too weak a requirement. He concedes that cheerfully. What I could not produce was the case running the other way, and that is what I want from you: a case built to specification, which shows whether belief can be a necessary condition on licensed assertion or is at most a reliable symptom of it.

The case must have the belief norm as the sole dissenter. Of the three candidate norms — knowledge (assert only what you know), truth (assert only what is true), and belief (assert only what you believe) — the first two must permit the assertion and the third must alone forbid it. Concretely: the speaker must know the proposition she asserts, the proposition must be true, and she must nevertheless fail to believe it.

Specifications:

1. The knowledge claim must be earned rather than stipulated. Set out the speaker's epistemic position — her grounds, why they are undefeated, why the warrant is of a kind that yields knowledge — so that a reader can see why the knowledge norm permits. Do not let the case be a Gettier case. I am not interested in the direction in which the knowledge norm is the dissenter; that direction is well trodden and tells me nothing about the belief norm.

2. The absence of belief must be a fact about the speaker's doxastic state, not about what she is willing to say. Exclude cases in which she disavows belief while her behaviour betrays it, and cases that turn on her being insincere, joking, or playing a role. If you rely on her self-report at all, explain why the report is evidence of the state rather than a substitute for it.

3. Keep it ordinary: one person, one episode, no longer than a paragraph; no science fiction, no exotic psychology, no divided or hidden selves. No examinations or quizzes, and if your case makes the speaker merely an unusually modest judge of her own competence, say what besides the modesty secures the knowledge.

4. Do not secure the verdicts by decree. A case that works only because you have declared the speaker to know and not to believe is no use to me.

Then, under separate headings: (a) the scenario; (b) the proposition asserted and the assertion itself; (c) the verdict each of the three norms returns and the reason for it; (d) the single feature of the case that does the work — what must be true of the relation between knowledge and belief for the case to hold together at all; (e) what happens when that feature is removed, for instance if belief turns out to be a necessary condition on knowledge — does the case collapse, and into what; (f) how a defender of the belief norm would attack the case, and what the best reply would be; (g) your own verdict: is the assertion licensed, and which way does the intuition about the case actually cut? I care less about which theory you favour than about whether the case is decisive or merely a standoff.

If you conclude that no such case can be built, do not simply report the impossibility. Identify the premise doing the blocking — that knowledge entails belief, say, or that an utterance expressing no belief is not an assertion at all — relax that premise and no other, and build the closest case you can, stating precisely what it does and does not establish.
