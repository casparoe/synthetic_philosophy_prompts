I'm a graduate student working on climate ethics, and because the structure of the problem is the same I keep getting pulled into arguments about vegetarianism and about voting. Last month a friend in my cohort built an argument out over drinks that I have not been able to dismantle. He calls it the honest accountant's argument, and it runs like this.

(1) Whether a particular act is wrong depends only on what that act itself brings about; it cannot depend on what other people happen to be doing, because no one is excused or condemned by the choices of strangers. (2) My eating one more burger, taking one more flight, or casting one more vote brings about no harm: the outcome — the number of animals raised, the carbon in the atmosphere, the result of the election — is the same whether or not I act. (3) So my act brings about no harm. (4) So, by (1), my act is not wrong. (5) Nothing in the reasoning turned on the size of my contribution or the size of the group; it applies to every other person in the same position, one at a time. (6) So the same reasoning shows that none of them acts wrongly either, and the whole practice, though it produces enormous and predictable harm, contains no wrongful act. (7) But that is absurd — a practice that predictably wrecks the climate, and that nobody has any reason to stop, is not a practice without a single wrongful act in it. (8) So (1) is false.

He treats (8) as the punchline and thinks the argument refutes the individualist premise outright. I would rather keep (1) than (8) — I do believe my obligations shouldn't turn on what strangers happen to do — but I cannot make myself deny (7). I also notice that his argument doesn't even need the harms to be large: if only ninety people were in a position to do it and the harm required all ninety-one of us, my act would still make no difference and would still come out permissible, which is either a reductio or the price of honesty.

So: which step do I reject? Tell me which one, why it looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs. If it is (1), say exactly what replaces it and whether the replacement still leaves individuals answerable in the threshold cases, where no single act tips anything. If it is (2), tell me what my one burger brings about that is not merely a small increment of a harm. If it is something between (2) and (6), name the principle that blocks the slide and say whether that principle also lets the firing squad off the hook — five shooters, one victim, any four bullets would have sufficed — or whether it distinguishes the two cases. And if the argument is simply valid and I have to choose, tell me which claim I should learn to live without, and what the world looks like once I do.
