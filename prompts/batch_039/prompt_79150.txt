Bit of a strange request, this one. I run a small reading group on speech acts with a couple of colleagues (we've been working through the Austinian material — felicity conditions, the misfire/abuse distinction, the sincerity condition, Searle's constitutive rules — and we're all a bit sick of setting each other seminar questions), and for the last session I want somthing funnier than another set of discussion prompts. So here's the brief.

I'd like a satire written from inside a corporate document. Setup: a large consumer-tech firm has shipped an AI customer-service agent that talks in the first person, and the legal and comms teams have produced an internal handbook for whoever writes the agent's scripts. Call it something like the Illocutionary Risk Handbook. The governing doctrine is that the agent's utterances are purely locutionary — it reports and describes, but never actually performs anything — so its "I'm sorry" is an apology-shaped report of a feeling-state, and its "I'll get that sorted" is a prediction about a future state of affairs rather than a promise. The handbook invents its own vocabulary for this (things like 'non-committal register', 'informational apology', 'descriptive passive'), and it leans on Austin's point that non-earnest speech is a parasitic use of language — the bot is like an actor on a stage, so nothing it says is said in earnest, so nothing it says can be happy — while elsewhere in the same document insisting that customers may rely on the agent's factual statements, that the agent is accurate, and so on. That tension is the whole joke, and nobody inside the document should ever notice it.

Please write the handbook section itself — roughly 700 words, deadpan, formatted as numbered clauses (or a FAQ, if that comes out funnier, your call). The comedy has to come from the theory being right: that a promise can be hollow without failing to be a promise; that force comes from convention and uptake rather than from anything going on inside the speaker; and that if you void the agent's commitments you void its assurances along with them. No winking at the reader and no generic 'chatbot says sorry, badly' material — I want a specialist to laugh at how well it understands the thing it's mocking, and someone who has never read a word of the theory to still be able to follow it. British English please, and keep the invented jargon internally consistent.
