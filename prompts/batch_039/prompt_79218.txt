You are a poet who works in received forms and who also knows the history and sociology of science well enough to be trusted in a seminar room. Think carefully before you write: this piece has to do two jobs at once, and the second is harder than the first.

I am assembling a small anthology on multiple discovery — the fact that a great many discoveries and inventions arrive independently and more or less simultaneously in more than one place, so that the interesting datum for a historian is not the lone genius but the duplication. I would like a sestina for it. You may draw on the standard cases, the calculus in the seventeenth century and natural selection in the nineteenth, but do not name anyone.

The conceit I want the form to carry is this. The two people did not make "the same" discovery; they made two things that become one thing only when someone later translates them into a common notation — and the sestina's six recurring end-words are your version of that notation. The repetitions should therefore read as a retrospective achievement rather than a shared vocabulary: the same six words coming back, meaning slightly different things depending on whose mouth they are in.

1. Form: a sestina proper. Six stanzas of six lines, end-words recurring in the standard retrogradate permutation (each stanza's first line ends on the word that ended the previous stanza), then a three-line envoi using all six end-words, three of them at line ends. Unrhymed, as is normal for the form. No padding to fit a word in; if a word can only be got in clumsily, replace it.
2. End-words: six that are concrete and unglamorous — a thing, a mark, a verb of making, a word for a letter or a date — each of which could plausibly turn up in either person's account of what they did. Keep abstractions out: no truth, time, genius, legacy, eternity.
3. Voices: stanzas one to three in the voice of the one whose name the result did not end up bearing; stanzas four to six in the voice of the one whose name it does bear. Let the asymmetry show in what each means by the shared words — one may be describing an instrument where the other is describing an argument, one a method where the other a result.
4. The envoi belongs to a third voice: the person who comes afterwards and writes the two accounts into a single history. This is where such a poem will want to resolve everything; resist.
5. The poem must engage, without settling, three things that are usually run together: (a) the step from "it was arrived at twice" to "it was bound to be arrived at", and why that step is tempting and not compulsory; (b) the difference between being first and being credited — the fact that the name attached to a result so often belongs to whoever publicised it, or to whoever was already famous, rather than to whoever got there first; (c) the machinery by which people in that period staked priority claims at all — enciphered announcements, sealed envelopes deposited with a learned society, a letter that carried the status of publication, a date established by the seal. (c) is real history and worth using; where you are unsure of a detail, keep it general rather than inventing one.
6. Do not name the historical parties and do not cite anything. The poem should be legible to someone who has never heard of either case and unmistakable to someone who knows both.
7. It should not decide whether priority matters. I would rather end with the question genuinely open than with a verdict. Give it a title.

Before drafting, brainstorm in a few lines: your six end-words and why each can survive in both mouths; what stanzas one to three do that four to six do not; and what the envoi is refusing to say. Then write the poem, and after it add a short note, under 150 words, identifying the end-word scheme and saying what each half is doing, so that I can check the form quickly.
