Me and my flatmate Dan got into it last night about laws of nature and it ran until about 2am, and now I honestly can't tell whether I lost the argument or he just outlasted me. He's a Humean: laws are just the axioms of the best systematization of the whole mosaic of facts — the shortest and strongest true description of everything there is — no governing, no necessary connections between distinct existences, laws are contingent, and the whole question is just which regularities earn their keep in the system. I'm the other guy. I want laws to be what makes things happen, what the regularities hold *in virtue of*. Two things he said are still rattling round my head. One, every time I pressed him he'd say "thats a demand on a theory, not a fact about the world," and I'm now worried he's right that I've been smuggling my conclusions into my own criteria. Two, he asked me to name one thing a law does that a regularity doesn't, I said it supports counterfactuals, and he said fine — so why? Either laws support counterfactuals because of something over and above the regularity, in which case what, or else it's brute, in which case I've got magic. I don't have an answer to that.

So before I read one more thing on this, I want the spec written out. Give me the desiderata for a theory of lawhood: what does any adequate account have to deliver, with the case behind each requirement. I want the actual cases in there — the second law, statistical, violated all the time, still called a law; the Pauli exclusion principle, which feels like a constraint on what's possible rather than a force shoving anything; the ideal gas law, false of every gas that has ever existed and true only ceteris paribus; Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, a null model where nothing's happening so nothing changes, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a law or a bit of maths wearing a lab coat; the "all gold spheres are less than a mile across" sort of thing and why it feels different from a real law even if it's true and even if it would be an axiom of some tidy system; conservation laws, which seem to fall out of symmetries rather than being laid down separately; Newton's laws, which were laws, and then our best theory changed, and now they're... what exactly, when they're true of nothing? — plus the counterfactual-support requirement and the nagging intuition that laws govern.

Then the part I actually care about: say which of these are genuine requirements and which are just Humean or anti-Humean prejudice in disguise, and — this is the bit I'm really after — tell me which subset of them can't all be had at once. My suspicion is that "laws are contingent," "laws govern," "laws explain their instances," and "no necessary connections" is an over-subscribed menu, and if so I'd rather know now than after another two years of reading. Also Dan floated something I didn't follow: that the gold-spheres thing isn't a law because the generalization only looks good in a gerrymandered vocabulary, and that lawhood is really about robustness across ways of describing the world. Is that a real criterion or is he making it up on the spot? A quick line on how Humeanism and the anti-Humean options (dispositional essences, primitivism about laws, whatever else) fare against the list would be nice, but keep the weight on the criteria — I'll do the scoring myself.
