I'm the TA for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I'm drafting the essay question for the unit on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Last week I got into a long, slightly heated argument with a friend of mine—a computational neuroscientist who works on predictive processing—and she pressed a dilemma that struck me as much sharper than the usual complaint that illusionism is eliminativism in a trench coat. Her line was this: either the illusionist says that introspection seems to present us with phenomenal properties, in which case the seeming is itself a phenomenal state and the view is self-undermining; or the illusionist says the seeming is nothing but a disposition to judge or some other functional state, in which case the view quietly denies the very datum it promised to explain and has no account of why the illusion is so inescapable and so resistant to correction. I want the exam question to force students to confront that dilemma head-on rather than recite the standard talking points about the meta-problem, and I want the marking scheme to make explicit which moves separate an A from a B.

Please write three things. First, the essay question itself, suitable for a 50-minute in-class final and worth 20 marks; it should be answerable by a strong student who has done the reading but should be effectively unanswerable by someone who has only memorized slogans about qualia and the hard problem. Second, a model answer of about 650 words. Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, four marks each, with a one-line note for each point on how students typically get it wrong. In the model answer, please include a short closing paragraph on what would change its verdict—that is, which consideration would make my friend's dilemma fatal to illusionism and which would defuse it—and be explicit about where the model answer's reasoning is most likely to fail or to be contested. The question should not require students to know any particular author's terminology; if it helps to name a position or a distinction, do so in plain terms.
