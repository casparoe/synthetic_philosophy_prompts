I have drafted the following sketch of an essay on the semantics and pragmatics of slurs, and I would like your comments on it — chiefly on whether the argument is sound, and only secondarily on whether the position is one worth defending. I am not asking for a literature survey, a reading list, or references; I want an assessment of the reasoning as it stands.

Let S stand for a canonical slur for a group G, and N for the neutral term for the same group (I use the letters as placeholders rather than writing the word out). The question is what, if anything, distinguishes S from N at the level of meaning. The data I begin from are these. (1) The derogation S carries is difficult to cancel: an utterance of the form "she is an S, and there is nothing wrong with Gs" sounds incoherent, whereas "she is an S, and by that I mean only that she is a G" does not repair it. (2) The derogation survives negation, conditionals, questions, and embedding under non-factive as well as factive verbs: "there are no Ss in this department" and "if she is an S, we should reconsider" still convey it. (3) S can be uttered by someone with no derogatory attitude at all — a speaker quoting another, or an in-group member using it affectionately — and hearers still register the derogation.

My argument runs as follows. (i) By (2), the derogatory content is not at issue: it is not the part of the utterance put forward for assessment as true or false, but projective material. (ii) Projective material is not asserted content, and content that is not asserted is not truth-conditional; it is presuppositional, or else use-conditional in the way that expressives are. (iii) The only truth-conditional contribution of S, then, is the group-targeting condition it shares with N; truth-conditionally, the two are on a par. (iv) What is left is a norm of use: S is conventionally available only for expressing a derogatory perspective on G, and this is what explains (1) and (3). Call that residue the charge, and say that the charge belongs to the word's etiquette rather than to its meaning. (v) So there is no distinctive semantics for slurs, only a distinctive use-norm — and reclamation and in-group uses show that the norm is contextual rather than lexical. (vi) One consequence I am drawn to: because the derogation is not at issue, it cannot be contradicted. "That is false" cannot target it, and the only remedy available is to refuse the word rather than to dispute what it says.

Please tell me which steps are sound and which are conflations, and for each step what the strongest objection to it is. I am particularly unsure whether (ii) and (vi) are doing the work I need them to do. If you think the two-tier picture is the wrong way to carve the phenomenon, say what the right carve is and what would count as evidence for it; and if any of my distinctions are idle — between presupposition, conventional implicature, and expressive content, say — tell me which and why. Be argumentative rather than expository.
