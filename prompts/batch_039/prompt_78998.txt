Two friends and I have been arguing for weeks about whether death is bad for the person who dies. One of them keeps saying it can't be, since there's no one left around to be harmed; the other thinks that's obviously wrong but can't say why, and I've been stuck in the middle. I read the rationalist blogs alot and I've learned the hard way that my gut is more trustworthy than my theories, so before we start arguing about principles I wrote down what I actually think about a pile of cases -- and now they wont add up. A close friend of mine, Matt, 24 years old, was killed by a drunk driver a week before his wedding: that's the worst thing I can think of, a total loss for him. A four-month-old dies of SIDS: bad for the baby too, I think, but I notice I don't think it's as bad for the baby as Matt's death was for Matt -- which seems crazy, because if death is bad because of the life you lose, the baby lost way more. My great-aunt made it to 94, sharp as a tack the whole way, told everyone at her birthday party she'd had a full run and was ready, and died in her sleep: I feel like almost nothing was taken from her. My neighbor's father had advanced Alzheimer's for six years, didn't recognize anyone, had no wants left at all, and when he finally died I mostly felt relief, and I honestly can't say he lost anything. A guy I knew was diagnosed with a disease that would have left him in agony for years and then dropped dead of a heart attack before it got bad: I think that was, if anything, good for him. A historian I admire died at 60, and five years later a rival published a total fabrication that wrecked his reputation and his family half believes it -- he never knew about any of it, but I still think he was wronged, and that it was bad for him and not just bad for them. My nonexistence in 1850 is nothing to me; my nonexistence in 2250 is, as far as I can tell, exactly what's bad about dying tonight, and the only difference I can point to is that one is behind me and one is in front. Someone dies peacefully in their sleep at 40 with no idea anything was wrong: still bad, I think, so nobody has to feel anything for the badness to be there. And then the one I keep flip-flopping on: a woman of 45 who's done everything she ever wanted -- kids raised, book written, marriage good, no projects left and no desire for more -- dies suddenly. Some days that feels like my great-aunt and some days it feels like Matt, and I can't tell which way I actually come down.

My friend Dave proposed what he calls the unfinished-business view: you're harmed by death in proportion to how much of your life's work was left incomplete, not how much time was left on the clock. That seems to get the baby, my great-aunt, the Alzheimer's case, and maybe the flip-flop woman right, but it does nothing with the historian, and it has no story at all about the 1850/2250 asymmetry. My own instinct is that it has something to do with whether there's still a self around with a future it's invested in, but then the historian breaks that, and if I let harms outlive the self just to save him, I don't see why the Alzheimer's guy's death wasn't a harm to him too. So I'd like you to be my philosopher here.

Please take the role of someone who works on the metaphysics of death and harm and thinks about this stuff for a living -- but I have no internet access and I don't want a reading list or a canon of names, I want the gears, and if you can't derive it from my cases plus common sense then it isn't what I'm asking for. Think it through carefully, step by step, before you write anything. Start by restating my verdicts in your own words and telling me which ones are load-bearing and which are probably noise or a case I described badly. Then give me two or three candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that I could run it myself on a new case without asking you follow-ups, with the specific verdict of mine that motivates each clause, and for each candidate an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Then tell me which verdict I should give up, if any -- I'd rather pay a real price than keep a mess, so don't tell me everything fits if it doesn't. Finally, run your best principle on cases I haven't given you: someone who dies at 30 but whose life was going to get steadily worse from there; someone who dies and is completely forgotten within ten years versus someone who dies and is remembered for centuries, and whether that difference is part of the harm to them or not; a dog; and whatever case you think would most embarrass your favorite principle. And give me a straight answer on the flip-flop woman, since that's the one I actually need.
