Quine died in 2000, so he never saw LLMs, and I'm putting together something for a reading group where I want a serious reconstruction rather than a gag answer.

Setup: imagine Quine is alive now with basically the same commitments he always had — holism about confirmation, the idea that no statement is immune to revision, not even the laws of logic, the indeterminacy of translation, meanings-as-entities being a posit we can do without, naturalized epistemology where epistemology becomes a chapter of psychology, and his general behaviorism about language. Now show him a modern LLM stack. Four specific things I want you to run him through:

1. Embeddings. Tokens live in a high-dimensional space where similarity and distance are measurable. There are people saying this finally gives us a scientific handle on synonymy: two expressions are synonymous to the extent their vectors are close. Does Quine read this as a vindication, or as a new version of the mental-museum picture where meanings are objects sitting in some space, or as something else entirely? Note the tension here — he wanted synonymy to stay a real if unanalyzed notion for a behaviorist, but he also wanted meanings as entities ditched.

2. The gradient view of meaning: the claim that a token's meaning is fully given by the direction in which it would push the loss function under paraphrase, i.e. that inferential role is a differentiable function. Could Quine accept that, or does it smuggle back in exactly the analytic-synthetic distinction he attacked — a "meaning" fixed independently of which sentences turn out true?

3. Fine-tuning and RLHF as web revision. When you fine-tune a model you can shift its dispositions on one family of questions while leaving the rest alone, and you can also change what it treats as a valid logical inference. Quine said revision can go anywhere, even into logic. But is there anything in his picture that would let you predict which strand of the web a given training regime actually pulls? Or does that just reduce to the engineers' pragmatics — conservatism, simplicity, whatever keeps the benchmark scores up?

4. Indeterminacy of translation between two models. Two LLMs, different corpora, different architectures, functionally equivalent outputs on every prompt you can throw at them. Does the indeterminacy thesis say there is no fact of the matter about whether they mean the same thing? And does that thesis get stronger or weaker once you can read out the weights and compare the geometries directly? I think this is the crux — if the geometry is a fact about the model, maybe there IS a fact of the matter and Quine loses; if the geometry is itself theory-laden and chosen by us, maybe he wins a bit too easily.

For each of the four: what is Quine's most defensible position, where is the reconstruction shakiest, and does the modern development actually threaten one of his arguments or just dress it up in new clothes? Then one last thing I think is underrated — does his naturalized epistemology give him a reason to be happy about LLMs as models of reasoning, or does the fact that the training signal is next-token prediction rather than successful action in the world matter a lot here? Don't bother with page cites, I know I can't check them, I want the argument not the footnotes. Also if you think one of my four framings is just wrong-headed or rests on a misunderstanding of him, say so instead of humoring me.
