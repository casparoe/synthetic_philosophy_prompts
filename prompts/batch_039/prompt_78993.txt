I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been working through Marx's Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 in the evenings — slowly, and mostly out of a general curiosity about why the word "alienation" gets thrown around so freely in tech-industry discussions of work. There's a stretch near the beginning of the section on estranged labor that I keep bouncing off of, and I'd like help working out what Marx is actually doing in it. Here is the passage as it appears in the translation I pulled up online:

"We have proceeded from the premises of political economy. We have accepted its language and its laws. We presupposed private property, the separation of labor, capital and land, and of wages, profit of capital and rent of land – likewise division of labor, competition, the concept of exchange value, etc. On the basis of political economy itself, in its own words, we have shown that the worker sinks to the level of a commodity and becomes indeed the most wretched of commodities; that the wretchedness of the worker is in inverse proportion to the power and magnitude of his production; that the necessary result of competition is the accumulation of capital in a few hands, and thus the restoration of monopoly in a more terrible form; and that finally the distinction between capitalist and land rentier, like that between the tiller of the soil and the factory worker, disappears and that the whole of society must fall apart into the two classes – property owners and propertyless workers."

"[…]"

"Do not let us go back to a fictitious primordial condition as the political economist does, when he tries to explain. Such a primordial condition explains nothing; it merely pushes the question away into a grey nebulous distance. The economist assumes in the form of a fact, of an event, what he is supposed to deduce – namely, the necessary relationship between two things – between, for example, division of labor and exchange. Thus the theologian explains the origin of evil by the fall of Man – that is, he assumes as a fact, in historical form, what has to be explained."

"We proceed from an actual economic fact."

What I can't resolve is the apparent tension between the first and second of those paragraphs. In the first, Marx says he has "proceeded from the premises of political economy" and even claims to have "shown" a series of conclusions "on the basis of political economy itself," which sounds like he is accepting the economists' framework and reasoning inside it. In the second, he charges political economy with failing to explain private property and with assuming what it is supposed to deduce. Is the intended move a reductio, in which the economists' own premises are shown to generate conclusions they would not accept, or is Marx making the stronger claim that those premises are not merely incomplete but incoherent from the outset? I would also like the theological analogy unpacked: what precisely is the parallel supposed to be, and what exactly is being begged? And how much weight should I place on the short sentence that follows — is "an actual economic fact" meant to announce an empirical law about wages and output, or something closer to a conceptual consequence of the wage relation? Relatedly, the claim that the worker's wretchedness stands "in inverse proportion to the power and magnitude of his production" reads to me as a piece of economics, but I gather the surrounding discussion is about alienation; I can't tell whether Marx is describing exploitation, describing alienation, or running the two together, and whether that distinction even matters for how the argument is supposed to land. A friend in my reading group calls the whole thing a textbook case of "immanent critique," and I'd like to know whether that label is doing real analytical work here or is just a polished way of saying that he argues against the economists using their own assumptions.

I'd rather you be self-critical than settle the matter confidently. If you favor one reading of what Marx is up to in this passage, tell me what would change your mind — which further sentence in the surrounding text, or which consideration about the argument's context, would push you toward the competing reading — and identify the step in your reasoning you would trust least. Please write in American English.
