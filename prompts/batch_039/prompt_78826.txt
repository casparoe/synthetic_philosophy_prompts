I'm in a graduate seminar on the history and philosophy of science, and I've now spent two weeks arguing with a friend in computational social science about path dependence, and I'd like your credence on the question we actually disagree about. He has built an agent-based model of how a community settles on a shared convention: agents choose among options, options improve with use, switching costs rise once coordination has occurred, and each option carries a quality parameter. The headline result is that even when increasing returns are strong enough to make the community's eventual choice absorbing, the option that ends up dominant is nearly always one of the top two by quality, and he takes this to vindicate the deflationary view — that the outcome would have arrived anyway, and that path dependence is mostly a matter of which of several near-equivalent options you happen to land on. My objection is that the quality parameter is assigned by him, with hindsight and with the later consensus already in hand, so the model cannot distinguish a world in which the community was tracking something real from a world in which his scoring rule simply rewards whatever features the winner happened to have; the simulation then tells us about the structure of his scoring rule rather than about the counterfactual.

The case we keep returning to is the near-universal adoption, in quantitative fields, of a bright-line significance threshold: the practice of reporting a result as significant or not according to whether a summary statistic crosses a fixed level, with 0.05 the most common. I think this is a cleaner test than a technology, because there is no learning-by-doing story available for it and no manufacturing scale economy — only the coordination value of comparability, the inertia of training and editorial practice, software defaults, and the fact that once the convention is in place, re-testing the alternatives is costly and unrewarded. What I want is a number for a claim I will try to state precisely: in the nearest possible world in which that particular threshold was never canonized by the early methodological literature, so that the convention was never fixed at that level, some functionally equivalent bright-line reporting rule would nonetheless have become the dominant practice in the relevant fields within roughly two decades. My credence is around 0.3; his is around 0.75.

Give me your own credence, then decompose it rather than asserting it. I want terms for at least the following: the probability that the convention's dominance was driven mainly by structural pressures — the demand for a decisional rule in applied work — rather than by the specific contingent choices of a few influential writers and journals; the probability, conditional on that, that the functional role would have been filled by some bright-line rule at all, rather than by a norm of reporting estimates and uncertainty with no default cut; the probability that the resulting rule would have been close enough in stringency to count as the same outcome, if you think the counterfactual claim requires that; the probability that the counterfactual is truth-apt in the first place, that is, that the notion of the same outcome has determinate enough content for a probability to attach to it; and the probability that the absence of any serious re-testing of the alternatives after canonization reflects lock-in rather than genuine satisfaction. Then tell me how these combine, which single term your number is most sensitive to, and where you think my friend and I actually disagree — whether the disagreement is about the history, about the structure of counterfactuals, or about what counts as the same outcome. Finish by saying what evidence or argument would move your number most, and in which direction.
