I am in a small reading group working through Hobbes's philosophy of science, and we have bogged down over a reconstruction one of us built out of Hobbes's own doctrines — an argument which, if it holds, dissolves the special epistemic standing Hobbes gives to geometry and takes his civil philosophy down with it. The materials are these. Hobbes treats reasoning as a kind of computation, the combining and separating of names, and he treats demonstration as the derivation of consequences from definitions; the definitions come first, and the rest of a science is the working out of what follows from them. He explains why geometry can be demonstrated while natural philosophy cannot: the lines and figures we reason about are drawn and described by us, so we know their construction, whereas with natural bodies we do not know the construction and must instead hunt for causes from observed effects. And he says civil philosophy is demonstrable for the same reason, namely that we make the commonwealth. In the geometry, moreover, the definitions are generative rather than descriptive — a circle, for instance, is defined as the figure produced by the circumduction of a straight line about a fixed point, so the definition tells you how to make the thing. One of us then argued as follows. (1) A demonstration is a chain of consequences derived from definitions. (2) Definitions are stipulations that we ourselves lay down, and we could have laid them down otherwise. (3) A truth that depends on a stipulation we chose, rather than on something we discovered, is a truth about our notation and not about the world. (4) Geometry is demonstrable in precisely this sense; its certainty comes from our making the figures, not from anything we find in nature. (5) Therefore geometry tells us nothing about the world, and its necessity is only the necessity of consistency within a system of our own devising. (6) By the same route, civil philosophy — which Hobbes boasts is demonstrable because we make the commonwealth — is emptied of anything concerning how people actually are. (7) But Hobbes means both to be substantive knowledge, and he treats geometry as the model on which all the rest of science, including the mathematical natural philosophy he wants to build on it, is supposed to rest.

I cannot decide whether the argument is valid and one of (1) through (4) is simply too expensive to keep, or whether there is a slide somewhere in it; the two things I would most like to preserve are (4), since it is Hobbes's own account of why geometry is special, and (7), since without it there is nothing left of his programme at all. A colleague in the group wants to read the whole thing as a cautionary tale about the circle-squaring follies of Hobbes's later years, on the theory that a man who takes geometry to be something we make up will eventually believe he has made up a proof; I do not think that is an argument, but it shows how badly I need the diagnosis. So tell me which step to give up, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it costs Hobbes — if the price is that geometry loses its privileged standing after all, or that his contrast between geometry and natural philosophy collapses, I would like to know that in advance rather than discover it three sessions from now.
