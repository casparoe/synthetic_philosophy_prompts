You are a philosopher specializing in early twentieth-century analytic philosophy and in contemporary debates about meaning in life. I am a journalist working on a feature about the recent wave of cosmic-insignificance arguments—the claim that the vastness of the universe and the brevity of human existence render our lives meaningless. I want to focus on a less-discussed response from Frank Ramsey, the Cambridge philosopher and mathematician who died in 1930. In his posthumously published Epilogue, Ramsey argued, roughly, that the feeling of insignificance before the size of the universe rests on a mistake: we treat the universe’s perspective as if it were the authoritative one, when in fact it is just one perspective among many. Size, he suggested, is not a measure of value; a being that can think and love is not made less significant by the existence of something larger.

I find this attractive, but a philosopher friend has reconstructed Ramsey’s move into an argument that seems to prove too much. I would like you to diagnose it. Here is the argument as she lays it out:

(1) Any judgment that something is significant or insignificant is made from some perspective or standpoint.
(2) No perspective is metaphysically privileged over any other; the universe’s point of view is not more real or authoritative than a human’s.
(3) Therefore, the judgment “my life is insignificant because the universe is vast” is false: it illegitimately treats the cosmic perspective as privileged.
(4) But by the same token, the judgment “my life is significant because it matters to me and to those around me” is also made from a local perspective.
(5) Since no perspective is privileged, no perspective can ground a judgment of significance.
(6) Therefore, all judgments of significance are equally arbitrary, and the question of whether life is meaningful has no objective answer.

She says this shows that Ramsey’s deflationary move either proves too much—collapsing into a nihilism that Ramsey would have rejected—or it must be supplemented with something that explains why some perspectives do have authority. She would like to keep (2), because she thinks any attempt to privilege a cosmic or divine perspective is metaphysically suspect. But she cannot accept (6).

Your task: identify the step or steps where this argument goes wrong, if it does. Is there an equivocation between metaphysical privilege and normative authority? Is there a slide from a descriptive claim about perspectives to a normative claim about significance? Or is the argument valid, in which case which premise is least costly to give up, and what would giving it up cost? Be specific: name the step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives. If you think the argument is valid but unsound, say which premise you would reject and what that rejection commits you to. If you think there is a subtle flaw, say exactly where the reasoning slips and whether the slip can be repaired without abandoning (2).

Before you write your diagnosis, take a moment to brainstorm the possible points of failure. Consider, for example, the difference between a perspective being metaphysically privileged and its being normatively authoritative; the possibility that significance is not a predicate requiring a single privileged perspective; and the possibility that “arbitrary” in (6) does not follow from “perspectival” in (5). Then write a rigorous, structured response. Do not simply declare a fallacy; explain the repair and its costs.

Finally, in a brief concluding section, take a stand on the underlying question: if Ramsey is right that the cosmic perspective is not privileged, does that make meaning more democratic—every perspective counts equally—or does it make the concept of meaning evaporate? Can Ramsey’s view avoid both the cosmic insignificance it denies and the nihilism it seems to invite?
