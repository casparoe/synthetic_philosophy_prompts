I am writing a term paper for my philosophy of mind course, and I want to stress-test a view I have come to accept rather than defend it: that the brain is literally a computer, in the sense that its causal organization implements algorithms and that citing those algorithms explains cognition rather than merely redescribing it. What would count against this? Please rank the candidate defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, sorting them into empirical findings about brains, conceptual problems with the notion of implementing a computation, and intuitions about particular cases, and for each say whether a committed computationalist should concede it or can explain it away. I am especially interested in three lines. First, that on a permissive reading of implementation any physical system can be mapped onto some computation, making the thesis vacuous, while on a strict reading it may exclude the brain itself. Second, that the computational description is explanatorily idle, since every prediction it licenses is already available at the neurophysiological level. Third, a case I keep returning to: a system whose behavior we predict perfectly from continuous dynamical equations but which admits no privileged algorithmic decomposition. For each, tell me what the computationalist must show in order to survive it, and whether the view can be formulated so that no possible evidence counts against it -- and if so, whether that is a defect or simply the price of holding it.
