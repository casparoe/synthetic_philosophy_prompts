I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years at the bench, and for the past three years I have been teaching myself philosophy, mostly decision theory and epistemology. I would like you to act as an expert philosopher of decision theory with a solid formal background and help me with a problem I have not been able to settle on my own. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming on the page rather than committing to a verdict.

The problem is the theorem, usually credited to I. J. Good, that the expected value of information is never negative. As I understand it: if the information is free, if it reaches you before you must act, and if you will then act as a rational expected-utility maximizer, your expected utility cannot be lower for having received it than for not having received it. The proof, roughly, is that an informed agent can always ignore what she has learned and do whatever the uninformed agent would have done, so the informed agent's option set is a superset of the uninformed agent's, and her maximum cannot be smaller. Yet I keep meeting cases that look like counterexamples. People pay to avoid a genetic test. A patient says he would rather not know. In competitive settings, being the better-informed party looks like a liability rather than an asset. I also notice that the result seems to be proved twice over, once in philosophy and once in the statistics of comparing experiments, which makes me suspect I am misreading one of them.

Set aside the trivial case in which the information is not really free, since it costs time, attention, or money; I want the harder cases. Consider the following proposed ways of reconciling the theorem with the apparent counterexamples:

1. The decision problem is not held fixed. Learning something creates new options or changes the stakes (the test result opens up preventive treatments that were not choices before), so the with-information and without-information situations are not the same problem.
2. Preferences are over belief states, not only over consequences. If a person would rather not believe something — if ignorance is itself something she values — then the utility function the theorem holds fixed is not really hers, and receiving the information can lower her utility directly.
3. Strategic settings. When other agents condition on what you know, or can read your behavior, being informed can be a disadvantage even when your own payoff structure is unchanged; the theorem is a single-agent result.
4. Self-defeating information. Sometimes the act of acquiring or learning the information alters the very fact the information is about — a prediction that changes the predicted behavior, a measurement that perturbs the system — so the signal is not the passive arrival the theorem assumes.
5. The future self is not ideal. The theorem assumes your future self will update correctly and then choose the best action available; if your future self may reason badly, or may be unable to resist an action that is bad ex ante, information can hurt you.
6. Transformative information. Learning can change what you value, so ex ante and ex post utilities are not on a common scale, and the comparison of informed and uninformed states may be ill-posed rather than merely negative.
7. Unawareness. If the information may reveal possibilities you could not previously conceive of, then you had no well-defined prior over the enlarged space, and the expected-value comparison presupposes a fixed algebra of events that you did not have.
8. Degenerate expected utilities. The proof turns on a maximum over available actions; where expected utilities are infinite or undefined, or the action set is not well behaved, the argument can fail for technical rather than substantive reasons.

Please do the following, in order.

First, a short brainstorm: for each of the eight routes, two or three sentences on what it actually claims, and on what the theorem would have to assume for the route to count as a genuine exception rather than a change of subject.

Second, rate each route on a scale from 0 to 1 for how much of the apparent paradox it resolves, where 0 means it does not touch the theorem and 1 means it fully dissolves the tension. Give a number to two decimal places for each, with a short paragraph justifying the number. If you judge two routes to be not independent of one another, say so explicitly and explain how you handled the overlap in your ratings.

Third, sort the routes into tiers — A, B, and C, with A the tier you would trust most — and state in one sentence what separates A from B and B from C.

Fourth, answer the question directly: as I have stated it, is the theorem false, or is it true but easy to misread? If you think it is true, name the single assumption whose violation does the most work across my list. If you think my statement of it is already inaccurate, tell me how I have garbled it, since I am working from memory.

Fifth, give me the strongest case for the opposite verdict: the best argument that there really are cases in which a rational agent should prefer not to receive free, veridical information, even with the decision problem and the utility function held fixed. If you believe no such case exists, say so, and then give the best argument on the other side anyway.

Feel free to use technical vocabulary — I can follow it — but I want your reasoning rather than a survey of who said what, so please do not give me a reading list. Where a claim is your own reconstruction rather than something you take to be standard, flag it as such, and say how confident you are. Write in American English.
