I am an eleventh-grader, and I have somehow ended up running the philosophy club at my school, which meets in a spare classroom at lunch. Last week we talked about the old puzzle of the donkey that starves between two identical piles of hay, which I have heard called Buridan's ass, and the discussion went badly: everyone agreed the donkey is being ridiculous and then nobody could say what it should have done instead. Since then I have been keeping a list of my own verdicts on cases in this neighbourhood, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here is the list. (1) Two identical granola bars in the cupboard: I take the left one and eat it. Fine; I do not need a reason. (2) After weeks of comparing, two colleges are exactly as good for me on every dimension I care about, so I flip a coin. Also fine — I would defend the coin to anyone. (3) I flip a coin to decide between my grandmother's birthday dinner and a friend's tournament, when I know perfectly well that the birthday matters more to me. Not fine; the coin is a way of not owning the decision. (4) At a restaurant, two dishes I like equally, so I tell my friend to order either one. Fine. (5) Same friend, same two equally liked films, except this time she asks me to pick and I refuse, insisting that I really do not care. This feels mildly wrong, and I think it is because I have made her do the settling that I was just as able to do. (6) Between two apartments I had no preference about, I pick one, and a week later I genuinely prefer it; that preference seems real to me rather than a lie I am telling myself. But if I insist that the other one was worse all along, when nothing about it has changed, I think I am fooling myself, and I cannot articulate the difference between those two attitudes. (7) When a coin flip picks a school for me and it goes badly, I feel the coin flip itself was a mistake rather than mere bad luck; but when I later wish I had taken the other granola bar, I think that regret is irrational, because by hypothesis there was nothing to get wrong. Those two cases look structurally identical to me and I give them opposite verdicts. (8) I am the surgeon with one kidney and two perfect-match identical twins; a coin flip there seems not merely allowed but required. Yet a judge who flips a coin to pick a sentence inside the legally permitted range seems to me to be doing something wrong, and I cannot say why, since by construction there was nothing in the range to prefer. I should add one more case that makes me doubt the whole exercise: some pairs of options strike me as neither better nor equal — the phrase I have seen for this is that they are on a par — and in those cases it seems plainly wrong to say that it therefore does not matter. It does matter, and it seems to matter because I commit to it. If committing is what creates the reason, then I am never really in the donkey's position, and I want to know whether that is a discovery or a dodge.

My tentative principle is this: whenever I have no reason to prefer one option, settling the matter arbitrarily is perfectly permissible, and what goes wrong in the bad cases is never the arbitrariness as such but something else — either that I did have a reason and was ignoring it, or that I pushed the settling onto someone else, or that I later dressed the settlement up as a judgment. Please test that against the list and repair it. State the principle, or a small set of rival candidates, as precisely as you can; tell me which case each clause exists to handle; say what the principle predicts about cases I have not listed, at least one of which you should invent in the same spirit in order to strain the account; and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of them I should give up. My friend also insists that exact ties never occur outside of puzzles, so the whole question is idle, and I would like to know whether the principle you end up with depends on her being wrong.
