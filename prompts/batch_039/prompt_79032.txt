I'm writing a term paper for a seminar on decision theory and artificial intelligence, and the last few weeks have been on what the readings call embedded agency: the view that a decision-maker is a proper part of the world it models, so that there is no clean separation between agent and environment; that an agent therefore cannot in general hold a complete model of the world that includes itself, its own future computation, and the representations other agents hold of it; and that this self-reference generates its own problems — logical uncertainty about one's own future behaviour, the need for some form of reflective self-trust, and the inadequacy of the textbook picture of an agent optimising against a fixed, fully specified environment. (I mean that decision-theoretic framing, not the enactivist or situated-cognition sense of the word.) For an unrelated course I've been reading about market microstructure, and I keep coming back to the flash crash of 6 May 2010: in the early afternoon the US equity market fell roughly nine percent — nearly a thousand points on the Dow — in a matter of minutes, with some large-cap names printing at a penny or at five-figure prices, before recovering most of the loss by the close. I don't want you to adjudicate the cause, which is still disputed; the regulators' joint report afterwards located the trigger in a large automated sell order interacting with high-frequency market makers, while the later criminal prosecution of a trader for spoofing implied a messier picture.

What I want is an analysis that runs in both directions. In one direction: the mapping, done concretely — who or what is the agent here, what is the environment, and what does the claim that the two cannot be cleanly separated actually amount to? Is it only the banal fact that the participants' own orders moved prices, or something stronger, namely that each participant's model of the market had to include the models the other participants held of it, so that the environment was partly constituted by the agents' own representations? How far does the framework get in describing the mechanics — the hot-potato passing of positions, market makers pulling their quotes, the stub quotes that produced the absurd prints, the stop-loss and automated executions that turned one large order into a cascade — and where is it simply silent, because the explanatory work is being done by market microstructure, by incentives, or by the interaction of many agents rather than by one agent's self-model? In the other direction: what does the episode show about the framework? My suspicion, which I'd like you to test rather than assume, is that the interesting case is not one embedded agent but many mutually embedded ones, and I want to know whether that is a straightforward generalisation of the fixed-point problem or a different problem altogether; whether the framework's formal core — self-reference, self-trust, logical uncertainty — is doing any real work here, or whether the episode is better handled by rival lenses (reflexivity and endogenous risk, normal-accidents-style complexity, principal-agent problems), in which case I want that said plainly; and whether the post-crash tightening of circuit breakers is a case the framework never anticipated, since the regulator is itself an embedded agent with no outside vantage point. The Knight Capital deployment failure of 2012 — new code rolled out to most but not all of the firm's servers, a few hundred million dollars lost in well under an hour — is often told as the same kind of story; if it is a cleaner test of the single-agent version of the theory, use it, and if it is not, say why.
