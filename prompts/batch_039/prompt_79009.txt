I am the employee-elected member of the board of the Nordic subsidiary of an American software company, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's board meeting.

Management has put a single item on the agenda: rolling out in our region a programme the parent company already runs in the United States. It has three parts. Every quarter, a ranked list of the top ten per cent of employees by performance score is published to the whole region. The top twenty-five are sent to a three-day retreat with the CEO. And the next recruiting campaign is to be built around one employee who began on the support desk and is now a team lead, held up as proof of the parent company's slogan, that here anyone can make it.

Our Nordic employee council has voted nine to two against, and its written objection is one sentence long: that in Jante's town no one gets to stand above the others. Jantelagen, as I understand the norm they are invoking, is the old Nordic rule that you must not think you are anything special, or that you are better or know more than the rest of us; the same reflex, exported, is what elsewhere gets called the tall poppy syndrome.

Write the opinion I will circulate. It must recommend a definite course — approve as proposed, reject, or approve subject to conditions I can put in writing with a date by which they must be met — and it must argue rather than survey. I want the board to be able to interrogate it, so please do the following.

First, state what each side is actually claiming about what the company owes the people who work for it. Neither side is a caricature: the council is not simply against praise, and the parent company's slogan is not mere naivety.

Second, use a distinction I have found clarifying. Call recognition a prize when it is scarce and rank-ordered, so that one person's gain is another's loss, and call it acknowledgment when it is not scarce and can be extended to anyone who did the work. Say which of the two the programme actually implements, and whether the other would serve the purposes the company says it has.

Third, meet the strongest case for the losing side head-on. If the purpose is to motivate effort, does a rank-ordered prize do that, given that the people most likely to win it may be those who would have worked hard regardless? If the purpose is to advertise that the company rewards talent, whose interests does the advertisement serve? And say what would have to turn out to be true for the opposite recommendation to be the right one.

Fourth, say plainly what a published ranking does to the people who finish at the bottom of it, and whether the company has an answer to that.

Two details should be taken into account. The employee who would front the recruiting campaign said in the internal interview that the company's training fund and a manager who covered for her during a family illness were what made the difference; the parent company's version of the story drops both. And the council's objection was drafted by two of our most productive engineers.

No citations and no literature review — I want an argument in prose that a sceptical board can push back on, under nine hundred words.
