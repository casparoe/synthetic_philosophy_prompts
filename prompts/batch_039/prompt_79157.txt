I'm on the neuroethics committee at the epilepsy centre where my brother had his corpus callosotomy three years ago — long story, I kept showing up to his appointments with printouts from journals, so when a seat opened up they put me on it. I recused myself from anything touching his own file, but this is a policy question so I'm still in the room, and I've got to circulate a written opinion before Thursday.

The live case: 24-year-old woman, callosotomy eight months ago, seizures way down, doing well by every functional measure. She's enrolled in our own department's longitudinal study — divided visual field stuff, words flashed to one side, respond with the hand on that side, then say out loud what you saw. Two sessions ago she said, plainly, that she's finished, she wants out. But in the lateralized trials her left hand kept doing the task. Forty minutes, fast, accurate, not a single error on the hard block. The study coordinator says that isn't a decision, it's a reflex. My brother's neurologist, who has no dog in this fight, says a reflex doesn't run a forced-choice task for forty minutes.

Her mother is on the phone with us twice a week saying the study is the only thing keeping her motivated and that the hand is the real her, the mouth is just the part that's tired. That's the argument I'm most worried about, actually, because I think there's something to it and I don't want the opinion to just steamroll it.

The chair wants us to adopt what she's calling the one mouth rule: only what the patient can say out loud counts as her decision, everything else is data. It sounds clean until you notice which hemisphere usually has the mouth — and that in the exact trials we're arguing about, the side that's answering is the side that doesn't have it. So the rule would decide the case by construction. I don't think we can adopt it as written. But I also don't want to hand back "two subjects, two consents," because the science doesn't support it either, and the PI will quite rightly point out that the section isn't complete, there's residual transfer, so the two sides aren't as sealed off as the write-up implies and the disagreement might be an artifact of our own task design rather than a fact about her.

Draft me the short written opinion I can read out. Concrete recommendation — approve with conditions, suspend her enrollment, something else, your call — with a time frame, and if it's conditional, the conditions spelled out so the study team can actually act on them. Engage the one mouth rule on the merits rather than waving at personhood, because half the committee will vote for it if I don't take it apart properly, and take the mother's position seriously enough that she'd recognize it. Last thing: whatever we say here becomes the standing policy for the next patient who walks in, so give me the general rule too and tell me what would have to be true for you to give a different answer. No reading lists please, I need an argument I can read out loud in ten minutes.
