Ok, so I'm building materials for a grad seminar on compromise and moral trade, and I want a case built to order rather than the usual recycled stuff.

Here's the picture I'm working with. The gains-from-trade story about moral compromise goes roughly: you and I care about different things, so there's usually some package deal where I get my way on the thing I care about a lot and you get your way on the thing you care about a lot, and we both come out ahead by our own lights. Nobody has to say their values are commensurable, nobody meets in the middle, nobody abandons a belief. It's trade, not bargaining. I want a case that does that, and then a version of the same case where it breaks.

Specs for version one:

- Two parties, realistic setting. No trolleys, no desert islands, no immortal souls. An ethics committee, a coalition, a department, a family, a village water dispute, whatever you like.
- The compromise is a package across two issues, not a split down the middle of one. Nobody gets half. Half a pipeline is not a compromise in the sense I care about.
- Both sides come out ahead by their own lights, and I want you to actually lay out each party's ranking over the joint outcomes so I can check the arithmetic. State the implicit exchange rate — how much of issue B does this side give up to get a unit of issue A.
- Nobody gains because they were decieved or because they hold a false empirical belief. The gain has to be real, not epistemic.
- Neither side has to do anything it takes to be flat-out impermissible. We're trading inside the permissable set.

Then do the work: give each party's ordering, show the trade is a strict improvement for both, and tell me which feature is doing the heavy lifting — the difference in relative intensities, the seperability of the two issues, the completeness of each side's ranking over the joint outcome space? Which of those, if you took it away, kills the trade?

Now version two. Same parties, same two issues, but the trade collapses, and it has to collapse because of something inside one party's value system rather than an external obstacle — no third party vetoing it, no legal bar, no scarcity. Some candidate breakers: the value is holistic, so how issue A is settled partly constitutes how much issue B is worth to them (the Moore organic-unities thing); or the value is sacred, in the sense that its being tradeable at all destroys it; or one side's values are lexically ordered so there's no rate of exchange at all. Pick whichever honestly generates the verdict, say why the other two don't fit, and if two of them fit, tell me which is more interesting.

Then the wrap-up, which is the part I actually care about:

- Does version one show that gains from trade need less than commensurability — just a shared outcome space and complete rankings, no common currency — or does version two show that completeness was doing all the work and the "no commensurability required" line is hollow?
- If a defender of the trade picture says fine, in version two they should trade on some other margin, is that a rescue or an admission?
- And this is what worries me: in both versions, does the availability of the trade depend on how I carved up the issue space? Could a party redraw the boundary between issue A and issue B so the trade vanishes in version one, or appears in version two? If so, say so — I'd rather hear it from you than have a student spot it in week nine.

Finish with where you think I've rigged the case and what the strongest objection to it is.
