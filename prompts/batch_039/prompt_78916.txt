I'm a philosophy student auditing a graduate seminar in rational choice theory, and the unit on backward induction ended in a way I can't stop thinking about. The formal result itself nobody in the room disputed: in a finite centipede game, where the players alternate the chance to take a modest payoff or to pass and enlarge the total, the only subgame-perfect outcome is that the first player takes immediately. What struck me is that this settled nothing. One group treats the theorem as simply correct and the experimental subjects who pass for several rounds as confused, or as playing a different game than the one the experimenter set up — the experiments, at least as they were summarized to us, find a great deal of passing, and more of it the longer the game, which this group says only shows that the subjects haven't understood the tree. A second group says the theorem is best read as a reductio: common knowledge of rationality is an assumption so strong that no actual finite reasoner could satisfy it, and the unraveling is a proof that the assumption is incoherent rather than a prediction about agents. A third group keeps raising the off-path problem — the argument tells the first player that if she passes, the second will take, but that prediction is about a node the theory says will never be reached, and the reason it won't be reached is precisely that the first player accepts the prediction, so the whole thing seems to lean on counterfactuals about situations its own conclusion rules out. When someone proposed that a tiny probability of meeting a reciprocating opponent would make passing sequentially rational and stop the unraveling before it starts, the first group answered that this just changes the game rather than solving it. I have now heard this exchange three times with different participants and the positions have not shifted by an inch.

What I want from you is not a ruling on which faction is right but a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a substantive disagreement about the correct solution concept, a disagreement about what a model of rationality is for — prediction, normative advice, or explanation — a disguised empirical disagreement about how people in fact reason, or a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading, one asking what follows from axioms about preferences and beliefs and the other asking what a flesh-and-blood agent, who cannot hold an entire game tree before her mind at once, ought to do? My own hunch, which I would rather have tested than adopted, is that the first and third factions are not really contradicting each other, because they disagree about what the theorem is a theorem about; but I cannot tell whether that makes the dispute merely verbal or whether it conceals a real disagreement about the axioms themselves. Say what each side is actually committed to, and whether the dispute survives once those commitments are made explicit.

Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. I want to know which of the available moves would genuinely shift the debate — a distinction, a formal result, a revised equilibrium concept, a reformulation of the question — and, if you conclude that none of them would, why the impasse should be expected to persist rather than being a sign that everyone has simply been too slow. Please argue for your diagnosis rather than surveying the field; I don't need names, dates, or a reading list, since I'll be working from what you say and from my seminar notes.
