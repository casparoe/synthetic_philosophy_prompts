I am in a seminar on early modern political philosophy and have been assigned to present on Spinoza's defense of democracy. A classmate has handed me the following argument, which he says he reconstructed from Spinoza's Theologico-Political Treatise and the unfinished Political Treatise, and it has been bothering me for a week. Each step looks like something Spinoza either says or would happily accept, and yet the conclusion is one he would have rejected with some heat. I would like help finding the exit.

(1) For Spinoza, a thing's natural right extends exactly as far as its power: whatever a thing can do, it may do, and there is no further standard of permission hovering above nature. (2) When people join to form a state, they hand over to the sovereign the power of coercing and restraining one another, and the sovereign's right is measured by the power thus handed over. (3) Because right is coextensive with power, no covenant or constitutional limit binds the sovereign once the sovereign has the power to disregard it. (4) A sovereign that can effectively silence a preacher, seize a printing press, or punish a pamphleteer has the power to do so. (5) Therefore such a sovereign has the right to silence, seize, and punish. (6) Since the sovereign's right in this matter is exactly its power, the same holds for a democratic assembly: whatever a majority can do to suppress an opinion, it has the right to do. (7) So in a democracy there is no opinion whose expression is protected simply because it is true, or because it is the speaker's own considered judgment. (8) But Spinoza argues at length that a state which tries to control what people think is unstable, that no one can truly transfer to another the right to judge for oneself, and that democracy is the most natural form of state precisely because it leaves judgment free. (9) Therefore Spinoza's own equation of right with power licenses the very censorship he spent a treatise opposing.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the flaw is the slide from power to right, I need to know whether Spinoza has any non-power standard left with which to defend toleration, or whether his defense of free thought turns out to be prudential all the way down. If the flaw is instead in (2) or (3), I need to know what becomes of his naturalism about right, since that is the part of the view I find most attractive. I would rather keep (1) and (8) than keep (5), but I want to see the bill before I pay it, and I want to know whether the repair leaves him with a coherent position or merely a softer one.

Please structure your reply as follows. First, a section titled Diagnosis, in which you name the step or steps you would reject and state the error in a sentence or two. Second, a section titled Why It Looked Fine, in which you explain what makes that step initially compelling and why Spinoza in particular might have been tempted by it. Third, a section titled The Price of Rejection, in which you work out what giving up that step costs him elsewhere in his political philosophy. Fourth, a section titled Rival Repairs, in which you compare at least two alternative fixes and say which you prefer and why. Finally, a section titled Verdict, no more than a short paragraph, stating which premises of the argument survive and what Spinoza's position looks like afterward.
