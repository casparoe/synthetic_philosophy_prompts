I'm putting together a seminar on the epistemology of economic institutions, and I keep circling back to the interwar controversy over whether a centrally planned economy could allocate resources rationally once prices for capital goods and land had been abolished. Mises and Hayek held that the price system is not a mere accounting convenience but the only device by which dispersed, largely tacit, and constantly changing knowledge gets condensed into a form that individual actors can actually act on, so that a planning authority would be groping in the dark; Lange answered with a model in which a Central Planning Board plays the role of a competitive market by trial and error, raising prices in response to shortages and lowering them in response to surpluses, while the managers of plants follow two simple rules, producing at minimum average cost and expanding output until marginal cost equals price. Intellectually I follow both sides, but I can't feel the disagreement in my bones, and I suspect a story would do more for me than another round of exposition. So: please write a short story, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, that dramatizes this dispute.

The setting is a fictional island republic sometime in the middle of the twentieth century, with no meaningful foreign trade and a Planning Bureau that has abolished money outright. Allocation runs through published norms and an annual ritual in which every enterprise files a requirement return: a document stating, for each input it uses, how much of that input it could forgo and what the resulting loss of output would be. Please build the story around one concrete scarcity rather than a general panorama, for instance a single contested material with two rival uses, such as tarpaulin needed both to roof the warehouses and to mend the fishing nets, and let the theoretical disagreement surface through the ordinary mechanics of the Bureau's work rather than through characters who lecture. Two things I'd especially like the story to show, without resolving the debate on the reader's behalf: first, that the requirement returns are filed by people whose careers depend on them, so that a Bureau which sets next year's norms from this year's reported capabilities creates a standing incentive to report falsely, with the result that the aggregate picture drifts steadily away from what the island can actually produce; and second, that the Bureau's chief statistician tries to patch this by publishing a notional price list, shadow prices inferred from last year's shortages and gluts, and instructing enterprises to treat those figures as though they were market prices. I want the reader to see, in the specific fate of the tarpaulin, both why the statistician's fix is not obviously absurd and why someone on the island might reasonably doubt that any list the Bureau can print could carry the information a real price would carry. Please don't name Hayek, Mises, or Lange anywhere in the story, and don't end on a verdict; end on a concrete image instead.
