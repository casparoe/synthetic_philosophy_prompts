"Fitch's paradox of knowability. State the knowability principle, walk through the argument that takes you from it to the conclusion that all truths are known, then say which premise you think is doing the real work and what an anti-realist about truth — someone who thinks truth isn't independent of our capacity to know — should do about the result."

And here's what they handed in:

"Fitch's paradox starts from the knowability principle: for every truth p, it's possible to know that p. That's supposed to be the modest anti-realist thesis, since it links truth to knowability without claiming we're omniscient.

The argument goes like this. Take any truth that in fact nobody knows, and call it p. So p is true and p isn't known. The conjunction of those two — p is true and p isn't known — is itself a truth, so by the knowability principle it's possible to know that conjunction. But now suppose someone did know it. Knowing a conjunction means knowing both halves, so they'd know that p, and they'd also know that p isn't known. Knowledge implies truth, so knowing that p isn't known means that p isn't known — which contradicts their knowing that p. So it's impossible to know that conjunction. But the knowability principle said it was possible. Contradiction. So there can't be any unknown truth, if the knowability principle holds; i.e. if all truths are knowable then all truths are actually known. So the modest anti-realist thesis collapses into the omniscience thesis, which nobody wants.

I think the premise doing the work here is the transparency of knowledge — the idea that if you know something, you're in a position to know that you know it. That's a strong assumption and I'd give it up. Anti-realists should give it up too; nothing about tying truth to knowability forces you to accept that knowledge is luminous.

There's also something a bit fishy about how the principle gets stated. If you read 'all truths are knowable' as 'there is a possible world in which someone knows every truth' — one knower with the whole lot — then of course you land in omniscience. But the honest reading is 'for each truth, there's some possible world where that truth is known', and once you read it that way the paradox loses most of its bite. So I'd say the anti-realist position survives in a weakened form: it can't claim that some one person could know everything, but it can claim that each truth is knowable by someone.

The other option, which some people take, is to restrict the knowability principle so it only covers basic observational statements. That keeps the thesis modest, though I'm not sure how much of maths and theoretical science it leaves you with."
