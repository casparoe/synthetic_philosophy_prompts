I am a graduate student working on AI governance, and I keep running into a pattern in arguments about how the content of a norm gets fixed. I would like to know whether it has an established name.

The pattern is this. Someone specifies the correct answer as whatever would be arrived at under idealized conditions — full information, unlimited time, perfect rationality, everyone reasoning together without coercion, or some other such specification. Three instances. In a discussion of AI alignment, a colleague says the right values for a system are the ones a fully informed and fully rational version of humanity would converge on, and that we cannot anticipate the content of that convergence; yet the same colleague treats it as a decisive objection to a proposed objective that the ideal process would not endorse it. In a political theory seminar, someone says a law is legitimate just in case it would be agreed to by everyone under conditions of freedom and equality; pressed on whether anyone has ever been in those conditions, they say that is beside the point, and then use the test to condemn an actual referendum. In an ethics class, the instructor defines the good as what an ideal observer would approve of, and when asked on what grounds the ideal observer approves, replies that the ideal observer is by definition responding to the grounds there are.

What strikes me is that in each case the idealizing procedure seems to be doing one of two jobs and the person using it will not say which. Either the procedure constitutes correctness — the ideal agents' verdict makes the answer right — in which case I cannot see how it can criticize anything actual, since no actual person is an ideal agent and the procedure never runs; or the procedure merely tracks a standard that is right independently, in which case that standard is doing all the work and the idealization is decoration. There is a third thing I keep noticing as well: the verdicts of the ideal agents depend on contingent facts about them — what they would find salient, which concepts they would have, what they would care about — and those facts are neither available to us nor obviously authoritative for us. The idealization also tends to idealize away precisely the features that made the question urgent: disagreement, unequal power, and ignorance about what matters.

Is there a name for this? I have been told it is just the Euthyphro dilemma, and it does have that shape, but Euthyphro concerns a divine command, whereas here the procedure is supposed to be epistemic. I have also been told it is the rule-worship objection to indirect versions of consequentialism, that it is the same as the problem of the criterion in epistemology, and that it is just the standard complaint against ideal theory in political philosophy. Each of these feels adjacent without being the thing.

I do not want a label so much as a carving. If there is a term, tell me what it covers and what it excludes. If there is not, coin one and define it so that it distinguishes this pattern from the four items above, and tell me which cases fall inside its boundary and which do not — does it apply to reflective equilibrium, to ideal observer theory, to the original position, to proposals that the content of alignment be fixed by an ideal deliberation? If my description is confused, or if the dilemma I have drawn is a false dichotomy because there is a well-known third possibility I am ignoring, say so plainly rather than producing a coinage to satisfy me, and tell me how confident you are that no standard name already exists. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
