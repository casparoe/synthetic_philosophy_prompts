I have been reading about the ethics of compromise and would like a sparring partner rather than an essay, so please set aside the role of neutral explainer and play a committed opponent instead. The opponent is a rigorist about value whom I will call the no-trade moralist: she holds that the entire "gains from trade" picture of moral compromise is a category mistake, because value systems are not preference rankings, there is no common currency in which two of them can be compared, and when two people with opposed commitments settle on a middle course, what has actually happened is either that one of them has quietly stopped believing something or that both have bought a share of complicity in what they still take to be wrong. She is sharp and well-read, not a caricature: let her grant that compromise is often the lesser evil, that politics would be impossible without it, and that sincere people disagree in good faith, while still denying that any of this makes both parties better off in value terms. She may ask me to clarify what I mean, may concede small points, and should never give up her central claim. She should have real ammunition to draw on, for instance the thought that complicity is not a quantity you can hold at a lower setting, that a side-constraint is not convertible into welfare, and that a divided self is not the same as a satisfied one. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, write in American English, and stay in character until I say 'stand down'.

Here is my opening move. Imagine two colleagues who share one lab and one research budget. The first is a strict animal advocate: no research that harms a sentient animal is permissible, full stop. The second holds that animal suffering matters but that some harmful research is justified by the human benefits it buys. They cannot agree on what the lab should do. Now let me put two pieces of machinery on the table. First a distinction: an accommodation is when someone changes what she will do without changing what she values, whereas a conversion is when someone changes what she values. Second a test, which I will call the surplus test, for when a compromise is a genuine gain from trade: each party ends up with more of what its own value system says matters than it would have gotten by refusing to deal, and neither party has to treat its own core commitments as merely one input among others. I claim the lab case passes the test. The animal advocate gets a binding ceiling on the number of animals used and an independent welfare officer to enforce it, so fewer animals are harmed than under the status quo or under either party's unilateral rule, while the other keeps doing the work she believes saves lives. If you tell me the advocate has sold out by signing, I want to know what she has lost that is not simply the pleasure of being pure. So where exactly does the category mistake lie: in the surplus test, in my distinction between accommodation and conversion, or in the very idea that she walks away better off?
