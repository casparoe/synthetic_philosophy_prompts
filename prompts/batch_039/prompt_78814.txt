My friend Ana's divorce got finalized last week and she's asked me to say a few words at a small dinner — about twenty people, her parents, her brother, a few of his relatives, and nobody in that room agrees on whether she should have stayed. I want the toast to actually turn on something about precommitment rather than just being warm noise, so here's the deal. Eight years ago her and I made an arrangement: she could only leave the marriage if she said it to me, out loud, in person, twice, six months apart, and the second time she had to tell me what she'd learned in between. She did both, and then she left.

So what I want the toast to answer is what that arrangement actually was. The usual story is that a Ulysses contract works by taking an option away from your future self, and that anything you can walk out of isn't binding at all — it's a default, and defaults work by friction and inertia, which is a different mechanism entirely. On that reading she never built a channel she could only open from the inside; she just noticed the door was a door. On the other reading the thing was real, she was bound, and being bound is what made the leaving possible at all — the two conversations were the binding working, not an escape from it.

Pick one of those and write the toast on it. Don't hedge between them and don't give me two drafts. It's the same question as whether a constitution with an amendment clause is really a constitution, so you can nod at that if it fits, but the room isn't philosophers and it has to land as warmth toward her and toward the marriage, both families included. Three minutes. Then one short paragraph after it saying which claim in the toast you'd defend if my uncle corners you at the table, and where you're leaning on a reading that someone could reasonably contest.
