I'm a grad student in history and philosophy of science and I keep getting tangled up in something that I think people (including me) routinely conflate when they talk about Kuhn: the strong claim that two frameworks can't be compared at all, versus the much narrower claim that you can't translate one framework's central claims into the other's vocabulary without changing what's being claimed. I want a worked case that pulls those two apart, and honestly I'd rather build it from scratch than keep reaching for Galileo or phlogiston, partly because I'm tired of the standard examples and partly because I want to see the machinery, not the memorized episode. So take on the role of someone who teaches this material and is good at constructing examples, think it through carefully before you start writing, and sketch your plan to yourself first -- I'd rather you get the case right than get it fast. (And if you decide the four constraints below can't all hold at once, say so and tell me which one has to give; that's a real answer, not a cop-out.)

Here's what I need: a short fictional scientific episode -- one or two paragraphs, set in some made-up research field (something biology- or medicine-adjacent is fine, but don't use a real historical episode and don't lean on an existing term of art) -- in which two research traditions are incommensurable in the semantic sense only. All four of these have to hold simultaneously: (1) scientists on both sides can agree in advance on which observations would count for and against each side's central claims, so they share their standards of evidence and argument; (2) each side can predict the other's results and even learn to run the other's instruments, so nothing hangs on perception or on a 'gestalt switch'; (3) there's still one central classificatory term in each tradition -- something that sorts things into kinds -- such that no sentence in one vocabulary states what the other tradition's key claim states, and the failure isn't vagueness or ambiguity but that the two terms' extensions cut across each other; and (4) the shift from one framework to the other is nonetheless rationally forced by the shared evidence plus the shared standards, so nobody needs to be converted or struck by lightning.

Then do the audit. State the key claim in each tradition's own vocabulary. Attempt the translation both directions and for each direction say exactly where it breaks -- does it lose a distinction, smuggle in one the original didn't have, or mis-sort some particular case? Is the failure symmetric, or is one vocabulary strictly more expressive than the other? Then say what a Kuhn-style defender of semantic incommensurabililty would say about your case and what the usual "we can't even make sense of a wholly untranslatable scheme, and partial translation failure presupposes a lot of translation that works" objection would say (that's the Davidson line, roughly), and adjudicate the exchange honestly rather than splitting the difference. Finally, isolate the variable: which single feature of the case is doing the work of generating the incommensurabililty, what happens if I remove it (does it collapse into ordinary factual disagreement, or into a purely verbal dispute?), and what's the strongest way someone who thinks incommensurability is never more than a verbal dispute would attack your case? Please don't cite literature or give me a reading list -- I want the structure of the case, not the bibliography.
