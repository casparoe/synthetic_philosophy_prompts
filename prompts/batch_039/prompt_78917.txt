I retired five years ago after thirty-five years as an experimentalist in sensory psychophysics, where the whole job was to infer something I could not observe — a detection threshold, an internal noise parameter, the slope of a decision criterion — from something I could, namely button presses, reaction times and confidence ratings. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science in earnest, and this year I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on computational accounts of behaviour, which is where I encountered inverse reinforcement learning and had the strong impression that I had spent my career doing something adjacent to it without the vocabulary. I have been asked to contribute one of the three essay questions for the seminar's final paper. The paper runs three hours and candidates answer two of the three questions, so they will give mine about ninety minutes. I want the question to be unanswerable by reciting the standard setup — a reward function is inferred from demonstrations of near-optimal behaviour, the problem is ill-posed, one applies a regulariser — and I want it to force candidates to say where the identification actually comes from.

The question should be built around a small, concrete scenario of the following kind. A subject in a laboratory repeatedly forgoes a modest immediate cash payout in order to finish a long and tedious calibration task. Two inverse reinforcement learning models are fitted to the same demonstration set. The first infers a reward for task completion. The second infers a reward for the experimenter's evident approval, on the grounds that in this laboratory approval — a nod, a word of praise — accompanied completion every time and occurred in no other circumstances. On every demonstrated choice the two models agree, and both reproduce the subject's behaviour exactly on held-out trials drawn from the same distribution. From there the candidates should have to address what would have to change — in the data, in the model class, in the prior, or in the notion of preference being employed — for the evidence to bear on the choice between the two models; whether there is a fact of the matter about which model has captured the subject's preference, and what kind of fact that would be; and what the inverse reinforcement learning literature is entitled to claim when it reports having recovered an agent's reward function. I would also like a part that connects this to the older philosophical discussion of the underdetermination of theory by evidence, since I suspect the identification problem is a special case of it, but I do not want the wording to beg that question. Please write the question as it would appear on the paper, including the scenario, the marks available and the expected length (I am thinking twenty-five marks, with no citations required — candidates may name results if they wish, but I do not want marks to turn on recall of names); then a model answer of roughly six hundred and fifty words; then a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on the error that typically costs candidates that point. I would like the boundary between a strong answer and a merely competent one to be stated explicitly. The candidates are graduate students who will know the machinery perfectly well; the discriminator should be whether they can say what is doing the identifying work and whether they can hold apart a predictive claim from a psychological one.

Finally, a request about how to answer me. I am an outsider in this literature and I may be importing an analogy that does not hold. If the comparison with underdetermination is a stretch, or if I have conflated two different things — the non-identifiability of a reward function given optimal demonstrations, on the one hand, and the far more mundane observation that finite data leave parameters undetermined, on the other — then say so plainly rather than dressing up my framing, and reshape the question so that it targets the real crux. I would much rather have the question corrected than receive a polished model answer to a badly posed one.
