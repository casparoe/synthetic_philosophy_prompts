I run the collections and access unit at a national memory institution — a state archive with a public museum attached — and roughly twice a month I have to draft a recommendation on a contested holding for the director to sign. The cases come in four shapes, and I have come to think they are not four shapes of one thing: whether to accept custody of a collection that a community or a family offers us; whether material we already hold should be open, restricted, sealed for a term, or handed back to the people it is about; whether to digitise and publish; and whether to mark something publicly, with an exhibition, a plaque, an annual reading. I have a week per case, two junior archivists to do the legwork, no budget of my own, a legal office that will tell me what the statute requires and nothing about what is right, and a director who signs whatever I put in front of her and will be embarrassed in the press if I get it wrong. What I do not have is any way of knowing whether the answers I have been giving are consistent with one another, and the cases have lately come close enough together that I can see I have been deciding them by mood.

The two poles of the literature on this I can state and cannot reconcile. On one side, memory is a duty, owed to people who can no longer speak for themselves and owed first of all within thick relations — one's own family, town, nation — so that erasure is a second killing and a tidy archive is a lie told on behalf of the comfortable; on the other, the keeping of grievance is itself a harm, commemoration is nearly always a project of the living dressed up as a debt to the dead, and a society can be better off not carrying its worst material forward, especially where the people named in it, or their children, still live in the same street. I do not want you to settle that dispute. I want a procedure that can be run while it stays unsettled. Two things I have noticed on my own, because they are where my practice actually goes wrong, and the procedure has to accommodate them. First, remembering is three separable acts — retaining, allowing access, and publicly marking — and a decision to seal is not a decision to destroy, and neither is a decision not to commemorate; most of my inconsistency comes from collapsing the three. Second, in a paper archive the live options are not keep-or-destroy but keep-and-index, keep-and-bury, and keep-and-tell-the-people-named; digitisation moves everything from the second to the first, and a document that has been scanned cannot be un-remembered by any later decision, which makes the decision whether to digitise the one with no way back.

Please write the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers stop the case with a no, and which questions have to be handed to a person because they turn on values rather than facts — and, where that happens, who that person should be, given my setup. Then run it end to end on three cases and tell me plainly which of my instincts it overrules: a state security file naming dozens of low-level informers whose grandchildren still live in the towns they informed on, which a court is still using as evidence in prosecutions; a government that will pass an amnesty and fund an official history only if we agree to seal the relevant holdings for fifty years; and a family that asks us to destroy their mother's file rather than seal it, on the ground that a sealed file is a monument and destruction is the only mercy. Say where the procedure will misfire — I assume a rule that is right for a settled democracy's archive will be wrong for an institution whose holdings are still being used as evidence, or whose own staff are named in them — and be explicit about which of your steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.
