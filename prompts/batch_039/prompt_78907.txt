I run a monthly "big questions night" at the community centre near me — usually 12 to 16 people, ages 19 to 70, nobody with a philosophy degree (two retired teachers, a nurse, a guy who does spreadsheets for a living, a few students). We get 90 minutes and lately we just talk in circles, so I want to try something where people actually have skin in it.

The idea I've been chewing on for weeks: I read somewhere that if your beliefs don't hang together — say you'd give 30% to rain tomorrow and 30% to no rain, or you'd pay 60 tokens for one thing and 60 for another but only 90 for the two of them together — then somebody can come along and arrange a set of bets with you where you lose whichever way it goes. The person doing it doesn't need to know anything about the weather. They just need your numbers. I forget who came up with this; I think the name for the trick is a Dutch book. What I want is a game where everybody in the room writes down their own numbers and then the "house" — me, or a volunteer — builds a package of bets out of them.

So what I need from you is the game. Rules, materials, the ticket list, how the house decides what to offer, the token economy, how payouts get worked out on the night, and a debrief to run after. We get 90 minutes total and I'd rather spend the last half hour talking than on bookkeeping, so the arithmetic has to be doable in a couple of minutes by one person who isn't good at maths.

Things I've learned from the last time I tried something like this:

- People won't risk real money, so tokens plus one decent prize at the end (a nice bottle) and a conversion that keeps it interesting.
- The tickets have to be about something I genuinely don't know the answer to while I'm being the house, otherwise it's just me cheating. I was thinking a bag of marbles behind the bar, drawn at the end of the night.
- The moment I want people to feel is the one where the house hands them a slip and says: these are all your own numbers, you agreed to both sides of every ticket, here's what you win in each case — and the total is negative whichever marble comes out. I want them annoyed at themselves, not at me.

Fiddly things I want built in, because this is where I think it gets good:

1. Each ticket gets ONE number per player — the most they'd pay for it — and the house may either sell it to them at that number or buy it off them at that number. If people can charge more than they'd pay, or refuse the deals, the whole thing collapses. But I want them to notice that too: I want a moment where someone says "I'd never actually sell at that price," and we have to work out what their number was ever supposed to mean.
2. The house should be able to mix freely — selling someone the ticket for "not P" when they already hold P, or buying a share of "P and Q" while selling P and Q separately. But it should be following a short written recipe rather than me improvising, because I will definately get the sums wrong in front of everyone. Give me a table or worksheet.
3. I want at least one player to come out un-bookable — the house looks at their numbers and can't build anything that loses for them — and I want that person to be the hero of the debrief. Tell me how to check quickly who's safe.
4. Include a round where people price a ticket about something they don't know yet but will soon, something like "I'll think it's going to rain tomorrow once I've seen the marbles." I've heard there's a version of this about betting on your own future opinion and it seems like it should work, but I don't understand it — flag it for me.
5. I've also heard there's a version that needs an infinite number of bets to work, which sounds like a con. Don't try to stage that, just say something about it in the debrief.

For the debrief: map what happened back onto the idea. Which moment showed the rain/no-rain problem, which showed the "P and Q costs more than P plus Q" problem, why it matters that the house needs no knowledge of the world, and what exactly it proves about someone whose numbers get exploited. And be honest with me about what the game can't show. My worry is that all of this only proves something if you already accept that your number is a price you'd bet at both ways, and that being certain to lose is the bad thing. Couldn't someone just say "so what, I lost some tokens" or "I'm not a betting machine, my 30% isn't a price"? I want to know which objections the game really answers and which ones I'll have to admit it doesn't.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time. I already expect somebody to quote 50 on everything to play safe, somebody to refuse to play at all, and an argument about whether me looking at their numbers before I pick the bets is fair.
