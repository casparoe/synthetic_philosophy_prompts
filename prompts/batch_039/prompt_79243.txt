I am defending the following thesis at a departmental workshop next month, and I need a question-and-answer prep sheet aimed at the specific people who will be in the room.

The thesis: the long-running dispute between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony has no single right answer, because it equivocates between two different things testimony does. On what I call the transmission dimension, a hearer comes to hold a proposition true on a speaker's say-so, and here the reductionist is right — whatever warrant she acquires must be reconstructible from reasons she possesses, or at least from a track record she could in principle audit. On what I call the accountability dimension, the speaker stakes her standing on the claim, thereby giving the hearer a right to demand reasons, to hold her to it, and to resent her if it proves false; here the anti-reductionist is right, because the hearer's entitlement to rely does not wait on her having audited anyone's credentials. The debate went wrong, I argue, by insisting that one answer cover both. I defend two corollaries: the dimensions can come apart in both directions, so there are cases of credibility without accountability (the reliable but anonymous source, the well-calibrated system that cannot be answerable) and of accountability without credibility (the speaker who takes full responsibility for a claim she has no standing to make); and the real philosophical work lies in saying what, if anything, the accountability dimension contributes to knowledge as opposed to merely to interpersonal relations.

The audience, and what each member will press me on: three formal epistemologists who work in a Bayesian, accuracy-first idiom and treat the whole debate as a question about how a rational agent should update on another's assertion; two social epistemologists whose work concerns testimonial injustice and the way credibility is distributed by social position; a developmental psychologist who studies selective trust in young children; an early-modern historian who works on Hume and Reid on testimony; and a virtue epistemologist who will want to know what my anonymous-but-reliable case implies for her framework.

I have forty-five minutes and thirty minutes of discussion. Give me the prep sheet, not a defense. List the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by the damage each would do to my position if I fumble it rather than by the order in which they will be asked, each attributed to the kind of audience member who will raise it and phrased in that person's own vocabulary. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft, question-begging, or concedes more than I should want to concede. Separate the objections that target my thesis from those that target my framing of the debate's history or my invented terminology. Tell me which two or three I should pre-empt in the talk itself and what I should concede up front so that I am not seen to be dodging. Flag any objection that would be fatal if it were right, as opposed to merely irritating. Identify the one reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the session, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
