Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and the strange thing is that we agreed about every step of the argument and still ended up somewhere completely different: she came away convinced that it establishes that no one is ever morally responsible, and I came away convinced that it dismantles only a self-creationist conception of responsibility that hardly anyone who defends responsibility actually holds. I want to write an exam question that forces students through that disagreement rather than letting them rehearse the steps and stop.

Take the role of an experienced examiner for a final-year undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, someone who writes questions designed to separate students who have understood the structure of an argument from students who have memorised a summary of it. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a short, clearly labelled brainstorming list, for your own use, of the candidate cruxes you might build the question around, each with a one-line note on what it would discriminate between; then commit to the one that is hardest to answer by recitation.

Your deliverable is a single essay question for a 90-minute final examination, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the argument, and it must not hint at which responses are the good ones. It should require students to engage, on their own initiative, with at least the following:

(a) why the argument is supposed to go through whether or not determinism is true, and what follows from that independence;
(b) whether indeterminacy in the agent, or a power of agent-causation, could stop the regress or only relocate it;
(c) the case of someone who, over decades, with full information and without coercion, deliberately and repeatedly revises her own deepest values through a procedure she understands, each revision endorsed by her values at the time, so that she is in an ordinary sense the author of the person she has become — whether such a life ever terminates the regress, and why or why not;
(d) whether the argument shows that no one is in fact responsible or that the conception of responsibility it attacks is incoherent, and what turns on the difference; and
(e) what a defender of responsibility must say about the kind of responsibility the argument targets, given that the argument threatens to prove too much, reaching every possible agent and not merely human ones.

For the model answer, write what a strong student would write, not a survey of the literature: no citations, no reading list, and no dependence on the examiner or the reader having read any particular paper. For the marking scheme, give the specific moves a top answer must make, the marks each is worth, and, for each move, the characteristic error that should cost marks — for instance, treating the argument as an argument about determinism, or supposing that an undetermined neural event supplies the authorship the argument demands.
