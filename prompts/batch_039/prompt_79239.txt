I am reconstructing an argument for a graduate seminar and I cannot decide where it breaks, or whether it breaks at all. I would like you to diagnose it.

Take a thousand possible lives, L1 through L1000. L1 is a long life of sustained ecstasy and achievement; L1000 is a long life of unrelieved agony. Adjacent lives differ by a single micro-step: moving from L_i to L_i+1 adds one extra second of mild headache per decade, or shaves one second off a pleasant experience, or lowers the intensity of a good moment by an amount no one could notice. No single step changes the kind of life in question; the person living it could not tell that the step had been taken.

(1) For every i, L_i+1 is not worse than L_i. Adjacent lives differ only imperceptibly, and a difference no one can detect, and that leaves the character of the life intact, cannot make the life worse.

(2) The relation 'not worse than' is transitive: if A is not worse than B, and B is not worse than C, then A is not worse than C.

(3) Therefore, applying (2) to (1) a thousand times over, L1000 is not worse than L1.

(4) But L1000 is unrelieved agony and L1 is ecstasy; L1000 is far worse than L1.

(5) (3) and (4) are incompatible, so something above is false.

Two things keep me from simply naming a culprit. First, I can run the same construction with 'not worse than' replaced by 'is at least as tall as', stepping down from 2.00 m to 1.00 m in increments too small for the eye to catch. Every step of that version looks as innocent as (1), yet its conclusion — that a one-metre person is at least as tall as a two-metre person — is plainly false, and no one thinks tallness is intransitive. So either the shared form is broken somewhere, or the analogy is disanalogous in a way I have not spotted; someone will say the cases differ because lengths can be measured and values cannot, but I do not see how to turn that into a diagnosis without begging the question. Second, a friend tells me the repair is to say that the middling lives are neither better nor worse nor equal but 'on a par', and that parity is not transitive; I cannot tell whether this solves the problem or merely renames it.

I would rather keep (2) than abandon it: I need 'at least as good as' to be transitive, or I cannot rank options, and if the relation cycles then an agent who prefers what is better has preferences that cycle, and cycling preferences can be pumped. But I am equally unwilling to say that some single, undetectable step is the one that ruins the life, since nothing in the lives themselves seems to mark that step out.

Tell me where the argument actually fails. If a step equivocates or shifts its ground, name the step and the shift. If the argument is valid and one of the premises must simply be abandoned, say which, and then price the exits: for each candidate I could reject, state what I must accept in its place and what that costs me elsewhere — in particular, whether I can keep the ranking that choice seems to require. End your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...} giving the number of the step you would give up, or the word form if you take the defect to lie in the inference pattern rather than in any premise.
