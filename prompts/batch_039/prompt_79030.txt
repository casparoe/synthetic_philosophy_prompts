Construct a thought experiment that separates epistemicism from supervaluationism about vagueness without rigging it in either theory's favour. I want it for a seminar exercise, so the aim is a case where the two theories agree on everything an ordinary person inside the case would look at and diverge only underneath. It must be realistic — a committee, a clinic, a workplace, a family — fit in a single paragraph, and turn on one vague predicate applied to three or four borderline items; no sorites series, no grain-counting, and none of the textbook props (heaps, baldness, colour patches). Build it so that the theories agree on whether the claim may be asserted, whether to act on it, and whether the answer could in principle be found out. Two further constraints are the point of the exercise: (i) both theories must hold that, for each borderline item, 'it is F or it is not F' is true, while disagreeing about the disjuncts; (ii) the case must be built so that 'at least one of these is F' is true on every admissible sharpening of the predicate while no single item is. The case may not stipulate either theory's verdicts; the divergence has to be generated by the theories applied to the facts.

Then do the check. Answer as exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion: (1) the scenario, one paragraph; (2) the epistemicist's verdicts on each question above, with the reasoning that produces them; (3) the supervaluationist's verdicts on the same questions; (4) the one feature of the case the divergence depends on, and what becomes of constraint (ii) if the items can simply be lined up on a single scale; (5) how an epistemicist would attack the case as rigged, and what it still shows if that attack fails. Items 2–5 should be a sentence or two each.
