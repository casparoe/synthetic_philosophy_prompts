Not sure if this is a real question or just me being annoyed, but I've asked three people in my department about it and gotten three different non-answers, so here goes.

I teach high school chemistry and I've been reading philosophy of science on my own, so I don't have the words for this. The pattern I keep running into: a lower-level theory is suposed to account for a higher-level one, and the accounting really does work, but only in a limit that nothing real ever reaches. Phase transitions are the clean case. Statistical mechanics explains why water has a sharp boiling point, but the sharpness only shows up if you let the number of molecules go to infinity. Real water in a real pot boils over a range, its not sharp. So the theory explains the sharp version, and the thing on my stove is a smeared-out copy of the sharp version — as if the ideal object were the real one and my actual pot were a defective instance of it. Same shape with the second law coming out of reversible mechanics: you need infinitely many particles or infinite time to get irreversibility, and the finite case only gets 'almost.' Same with the classical world emerging out of quantum mechanics, where you send h to zero and classical behavior appears, except h isn't zero.

Is there a name for that specific shape? Not 'does reduction work,' but 'the reduction works and it lands on an object that doesn't exist,' so that the phenomenon being explained is one we never actually have, and the version we do have is a permanent approximation to it. I know there's a lot of talk about weak vs. strong emergence and about whether higher-level sciences are autonomous, but that seems like a different question — that's about whether we could in principle get the derivation, and I'm asking about the case where we do get it and the place it lands is empty. And I know science idealizes constantly — frictionless planes, ideal gases — so either this is just that, or there's a difference and I need the difference spelled out.

If it has a name, great, but I don't just want the label, I want the edges: what's in, what's out, and which nearby thing people most often mistake it for. If it doesn't have a name, coin one and define it, and give me the boundary cases — including whether the frictionless plane sits on the same side of the line as the boiling point or not, because my gut says no and I can't defend that yet.
