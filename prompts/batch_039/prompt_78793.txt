I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science who got dragooned onto a computational neuroscience consortium's Standards & Replication panel because I was apparently the only person in the building who'd read anything about mechanistic explanation, and now I have to write the actual opinion. The case is this. Two member labs study what everyone in the consortium has casually been calling "the same mechanism": a recurrent cortical circuit that implements a particular form of adaptive gain control. Lab A works at the cellular level, with a perturbation-signature detector they built themselves -- it takes a fresh dataset and asks whether the variables that used to predict the output still do, holding the input distribution fixed, so that it's supposed to flag a change in the mechanism itself rather than just a change in the inputs (one of the postdocs keeps calling this "mechanism shift as opposed to covariate shift," which I think is just a rebranding of an old distinction but whatever). Lab B runs the same preparation but reads out at the population level with calcium imaging, and when they feed their data through A's detector it flags. B says: different mechanism, the conservation claim is dead. A says: same mechanism, you're just measuring it at a coarser grain, and the flag is an artifact of your readout rather than a fact about the circuit. The stakes are concrete: there's a review paper in press that asserts the mechanism is conserved across the two preparations, and our panel has to decide whether to require it be revised, whether to fund a bridging study to settle it, and what guidance we give member labs about describing "the same mechanism" in future papers. We meet Thursday.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate: a clear recommendation with a time frame, the reasoning behind it, a criterion reviewers can actually apply to future cases, and an honest treatment of the strongest thing the side I come down against could say. I want you to commit on the substantive question of how the mechanism should be individuated here -- whether by what you can intervene on to change the outcome and how specific that intervention is, by which parts are constitutively relevant to the whole's behavior, by the causal role relative to a contrast class, or by something else -- rather than laying the four options out and leaving it to me, and I'd like you to say plainly where that criterion gets the case wrong if it does. It would also help if you take a stand on the more general thing I keep going back and forth on: is "same mechanism" ever a two-place relation between two systems, or is it always implicitly three-place, relative to a description and a contrast class someone chose, and if it's the latter, what follows for a detector that claims to tell you when the mechanism changed. Don't worry about citing anyone, I know the literature, I just need the argument.
