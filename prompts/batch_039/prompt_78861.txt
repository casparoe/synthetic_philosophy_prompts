I'm in a reading group that's been working through stuff on sortition — replacing elected legislatures with randomly selected citizens' assemblies — and we've hit a wall I think fiction could get at better than another argument. The thing we keep going around in circles on is whether the point of the lottery is that the assembly looks like the population (a mirror, so every interest is present) or that the assembly doesn't owe anybody anything (a jury, so nobody can be bought or leaned on), and whether those two justifications even support the same institution or quietly pull apart. So I want a short story from you, and here's the setup, which is my own invention, so please don't feel bound by anything you've actually read about real lottocracies: a mid-sized river city somewhere in the upper Midwest has run its government by lot for about fifteen years. Two wrinkles in its charter matter for the story. First, the pool for any given decision isn't the citizenry, it's everyone who'd be materially affected by that decision, so a vote on water rights pulls in farmers outside the city limits, a downstream town, employees of a bottling plant, and the people who fish the river. Second, the assembly alone decides whether sortition continues: at the end of every term the body votes on renewing the system, and no referendum, court, or ordinary legislature can end it — only the lot can end the lot. I want the story set over the night before that renewal vote, after the assembly has already finished the water-allocation fight it was actually drawn to settle and is now stuck deciding whether to keep the thing that gave it power.

I'd like the POV of someone who was a committed skeptic of the whole arrangement when her name came up — she thought it was a cute idea that would fall apart the first time it had to make a hard call — and who now has to vote on it. Give me at least one other member drawn in under that affected-pool rule who doesn't live in the city and doesn't vote in its elections, so the reader feels the oddness of that. I don't want a polemic in either direction: the case for ending it and the case for keeping it should both come out sounding like something a reasonable person would actually say at 2 a.m., and if the story lands on an ending I don't expect, even better.

Keep it to roughly 1,500-2,500 words, in American English (I'm reading it aloud Thursday and I'll trip over British spellings).
