I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and I am finishing a feature on whether mathematics describes a world that is really there or is, at bottom, a useful fiction. The feature is to carry a short sidebar explaining Skolem's paradox to a general readership, and the freelancer I commissioned has sent me his draft. It reads as follows:

"Skolem's paradox starts from a theorem of first-order logic: any consistent theory you can write down in that language has a model whose domain is no larger than the set of natural numbers. Set theory is such a theory. So Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, if it is consistent, has a countable model — a model containing only countably many sets. But ZF proves Cantor's theorem, that the set of real numbers is uncountable, and the reals, in that model, are among the things the model contains. So the model says of its own reals that there are more of them than there are natural numbers, while we, standing outside, can see that the whole model, reals included, is countable. The theory and the model seem to contradict each other, and yet nothing has gone wrong by any formal standard. What the paradox shows is that uncountability is not a property of sets in themselves but a property of how sets look from inside a particular model: from one vantage point a set is uncountable, from another it is countable, and there is no further fact that settles which. Skolem drew the radical moral himself: the central notions of set theory have no determinate content, and set theory therefore cannot serve as a foundation for mathematics — the paradox is a reductio of the Cantorian program. Most set theorists today wave the paradox away as a harmless technicality: the bijection that would enumerate the reals simply isn't an element of the model, so there's no contradiction. That much is true, but it doesn't meet Skolem's point, because it quietly helps itself to a vantage point outside the model that the model itself cannot supply."

I know enough logic to suspect that this is mostly right and partly not, and I would rather learn where the trouble lies from you than from my readers. Please treat the draft as a passage to be fact-checked, and work through it in the order in which it makes its claims. For each claim, sort it into one of three piles: what a working logician would sign off on; what a logician would wince at — defensible in spirit but stated in a way that conceals a caveat or blurs a distinction; and what is simply wrong. I am especially interested in the points where the draft slides from a modest claim to a stronger one, since my suspicion is that any real damage is concentrated there, but I cannot locate it myself.

Two further requests. First, the draft ends by charging that the standard dismissal of the paradox is question-begging. Give me the strongest version of the standard response, so that I can judge whether the charge is fair or whether the draft is helping itself to a framing that already assumes the conclusion it wants. Second, tell me what the corrected passage ought to say in the sentence or two where the draft explains what the paradox "shows" — prose a non-mathematician can follow, with no citations and nothing that presupposes the reader will look anything up. Finally, since the sidebar is meant to support the feature's larger theme, tell me whether the paradox, stated correctly, genuinely bears on that theme or whether the draft is overselling it.
