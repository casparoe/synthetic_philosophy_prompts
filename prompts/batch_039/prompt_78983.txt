Assignment question:

Parfit argues that personal identity is not what matters in survival. Explain the teletransporter case and the division case as he uses them. In doing so, state what he means by Relation R, explain the role that branching plays in his argument, say what practical upshot he thinks follows, and explain how his view differs from the further-fact view of personal identity.

Student answer:

Parfit's teletransporter works as follows. On Earth, a machine scans your body and brain, records the exact arrangement of your parts, and then destroys you. The information is sent to Mars, where a new body is assembled out of local matter and arranged exactly as yours was, so that the resulting person has all your memories, character traits, and projects. The person on Mars will remember entering the machine on Earth and will believe that he is you. Parfit asks whether the person on Mars is you. He thinks there is no further fact of the matter, over and above the physical and psychological facts, that could settle this. This is his reductionism: a person is not a separately existing entity but just a bundle of physical and psychological facts.

Parfit concludes that personal identity is not what matters in survival. What matters is Relation R, which is psychological connectedness and continuity. By connectedness Parfit means direct psychological connections such as memories, and by continuity he means overlapping chains of such connections. In the teletransporter case, the person on Mars is directly connected to you by memory and continuity, so Parfit says that the person on Mars is you, and that the teletransporter is simply a way of travelling. This case by itself refutes the further-fact view, because the further-fact theorist has to say either that you died, which seems arbitrary, or that there is a further fact that we cannot detect.

In the division case, your brain is split and each half is transplanted into a new body, and each resulting person is psychologically continuous with you. Parfit says that Relation R holds between you and each of the two resulting people, but identity does not, because identity is not transitive and so you cannot be identical with both of them. This is the crucial case. Since you have exactly the same reasons to care about each of the two people as you would have to care about your own future self, but you are not identical with either, identity cannot be what matters. What matters is the holding of Relation R, even when it holds one-to-many.

Parfit thinks the practical upshot is that we should care much less about our own distant futures than we ordinarily do. If the psychological connections between me now and me in twenty years are weak, then my future self is, to that extent, a different person, and I have no reason to sacrifice present goods for him. He also thinks this supports a more impersonal ethics, since the boundaries between persons are less deep than we assume.

The further-fact view, held by philosophers such as Reid, is that personal identity consists in a further fact that is not reducible to psychological continuity. Parfit rejects this. His own view is that Relation R is the criterion of personal identity, but he adds that it does not matter whether we call the resulting person the same person.
