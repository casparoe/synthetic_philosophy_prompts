I'm a retired biochemist — thirty-odd years running a lab, only got seriously into philosophy after I stopped working, so apologies in advance for the vocabulary. Next month the philosophy department's reading group here is letting me present a paper, and I'd like to not embarrass myself. My claim is that the hinge certainties from Wittgenstein's On Certainty are best understood as calibration assumptions — the way a lab holds fixed that the balance is calibrated and that the freezer stayed at minus eighty. You can't check the instrument with the instrument, and if the freezer had drifted you wouldn't say your data were wrong, you'd say the experiment never happened. I want to push it further: that these commitments are warranted functionally, because inquiry is impossible without them, not because they're justified, and that this means we should stop asking whether hinges are known at all. I've also got a rule I call the one-plank rule — any single hinge can be pulled out and replaced, but never all of them at once; the boat is never in dry dock.

The group is about ten people and I know them a bit: three or four Wittgenstein people who've read the book many times and are protective of it, two analytic epistemologists who work on closure and peer disagreement, one historian of science who does nineteenth-century weights and measures (I keep worrying she'll bring up the French revolutionaries scrapping every old unit at once), and the rest grad students. Give me my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this room, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest, plus the one question I'm definately hoping nobody asks. No reading lists please — I can't look anything up before then anyway, and I'd rather not have it in the room.
