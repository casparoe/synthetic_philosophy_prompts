Bit of background: I took early retirement after thirty-one years as a geologist (mostly petroleum, then a stretch in enviromental consulting) and I've been trying to make up for lost time by reading philosophy and sitting in on a reading group a friend runs. Last month was the socialist calculation debate and I've been chewing on it ever since.

Here's the arguement I came away with, which I'm fairly sure is the Mises/Hayek line. I've numbered it, because that's what the group does now:

(1) To allocate resources rationally, someone has to be able to rank the alternative uses of any given resource. Someone has to know what you give up by putting this tonne of steel here instead of there.
(2) Opportunity costs aren't physical facts. You can't measure them with an instrument, and they aren't written on the steel. They exist as prices, and prices come out of exchange between people who own the things being exchanged.
(3) Under socialism the means of production are owned by the state, which is to say by one owner. There is no exchange of means of production between separate owners.
(4) So there are no prices for means of production.
(5) So nobody, the planners included, can know the opportunity costs of anything.
(6) So a socialist economy can't allocate rationally at all. Not slower, not uglier — the calculation simply can't be done. Mises said it first, Hayek sharpened it by pointing at all the knowledge that never gets written down anywhere, and Lange's answer (let the central board set prices by trial and error and tell managers to minimize cost and expand output until marginal cost equals price) is a market in disguise, smuggling in exactly the thing the argument says can't exist.

The friend who runs the group says this proves too much and I honestly can't tell whether he's being clever or just difficult. His move: if (2) is right, then a firm can't allocate rationally either, because inside a firm the divisions own nothing and don't trade with each other at arm's length — the transfer prices are set by the firm itself, out of thin air, for its own convenience. Yet firms move steel, labor and capital around every day, and some of them are bigger than most countries. So are armies, hospitals, universities, the Apollo program. If no exchange means no prices means no opportunity costs means no rational allocation, all of those should have collapsed, and they didn't. He adds that Lange's board is doing just what a Walrasian auctioneer does, and that market prices are wrong all the time too, so 'wrong prices' can't be the line between possible and impossible.

So which step do I give up? I'd most like to keep (2) — it's the piece that felt like a genuine discovery to me and I don't want to go back to thinking costs are just lumps of stuff lying around. And I'd like to keep some version of the conclusion, because I do think there's something the planners can't do that the market can. Tell me which premise to drop, why it looked so good in the first place, and what each live option actually costs me: drop (2) and what's left of the argument, drop (3) and what's left of 'socialism', keep everything and just weaken (6) and what have I conceded in the process. And tell me whether the firm analogy is the same point as the market-socialist reply or a different one, because I can't tell if it's an objection or a restatement.
