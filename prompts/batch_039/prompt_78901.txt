I am a retired pharmacologist — forty years in preclinical work on receptor binding, and for the past decade a reader of moral and legal philosophy with more enthusiasm than training — and I sit on the substance-use advisory council of a mid-sized state. At our next meeting the council must adopt a formal position on a bill that the health committee has sent us for comment, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion beforehand so that the discussion has something concrete to argue with. Please draft that opinion for me.

The bill, in brief: it removes criminal penalties for possessing small quantities of any controlled substance and makes possession an administrative matter handled by a three-member review panel — a clinician, a lawyer, and a social worker. When police find a quantity below a threshold set out in a schedule to the bill, which the drafters call a five-day supply, they confiscate the substance and mail the person a summons. The panel may impose an income-scaled fine, order attendance at an assessment, and, in the drafters' words, direct the person to treatment. The possession itself leaves no criminal record. What has split the council is the enforcement provision: a person who fails to appear, or who fails to complete what the panel directs, can be charged with a misdemeanor after a second failure. There is a further wrinkle I should mention because it will matter to the reasoning: the state's treatment system currently has waiting lists of several months in most counties, so in practice a person ordered into treatment may be unable to comply through no fault of her own, while the escalation provision applies regardless.

I want the opinion to do three things. First, take a position — support the bill, oppose it, or support it only with named amendments — and state what we should ask the health committee to do, and by when, given that it reports out in six weeks.

Second, and this is the part I care about most, I want it to be honest about whether the word administrative is doing any real work. I spent a career on the difference between a receptor and a binding assay, and I have come to suspect that when people relabel a practice they often believe they have changed it. Some philosophers of criminal law I have been reading argue that what is distinctive about criminal punishment is not the magnitude of the burden it imposes but the censure it expresses — that it addresses the person as someone who has done wrong and merits public blame, and that this is why burdens imposed without that meaning, such as a tax, a quarantine, or a civil fine, are a different kind of thing. If that is right, then I want to know whether a system that summons you by name, records you, seats you before three officials, and holds a criminal charge over your head for non-compliance expresses the same thing, whatever the statute calls it. The bill's supporters on the council say the criminal sanction attaches only to disobedience of the panel's orders and not to the drug use, and that this is precisely the difference between civil and criminal. Our counsel says the distinction is procedural, and that what matters is that you face the panel with no jury and no right to counsel. I do not think either has stated the strongest version of his own case, and I would like the opinion to state it for them before answering it.

Third, something more in my own line. The schedule defining a five-day supply is a table of numbers, and I can tell you as a pharmacologist that a personal supply is not a quantity that exists in nature: tolerance varies by orders of magnitude between individuals, and a dependent person's five days and a novice's five days are not the same pharmacological object. I do not want the opinion to pretend the schedule can be made scientifically correct. I want it to say what kind of instrument it is instead — a rule that purchases determinacy at the price of accuracy — to judge whether that trade is acceptable here, and to notice that the same trade reappears when we ask whether the panel should apply rules or exercise judgment case by case.

Write it for an audience of clinicians, legislators, and one lawyer, none of whom wants to read a treatise: a clear recommendation up front, the reasoning behind it, and an explicit and fair statement of the best argument against the recommendation. Roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words. Do not cite literature — I cannot check citations at the council table and the committee will not check them either, and I would rather have the argument than the footnote. And do not lean on Portugal as evidence; everyone at that table cites it in support of whatever he already believed, and I have come to distrust it as an argument, though you may use it once, accurately, as an illustration of what a panel system looks like if you need one.
