I have read that Mill argued for freedom of discussion on the grounds that we are fallible, so a suppressed opinion may be the true one, and that even a correct opinion loses its force unless it is kept alive by being contested. What I want to know is how that case would fare against a distinction I have come across and have probably simplified.

The distinction is between two ways a person's beliefs can be insulated from correction. In the first, a person simply never meets the relevant opposing information, because their sources are too narrow; the remedy is exposure, since once they encounter the other side the belief usually weakens or adjusts. In the second, the opposing view is not absent but has been discredited in advance: outsiders are taken to be hostile, bought off, or deceived, so that hearing their disagreement is itself treated as further proof that the insiders are right. Here exposure does not pop the structure; it feeds it, and confidence can rise precisely when the contrary evidence arrives.

Does his fallibility argument still do its work when the mechanism that normally corrects us has been turned against itself? Would he say a person in the second case holds an opinion in his sense at all, or only repeats one? And is there anything in his picture of how opinions are kept alive that would lead him to expect contestation to entrench a belief rather than invigorate it?
