I write novels for a living — four of them now, plus a decade of teaching in a workshop where the currency is craft talk rather than philosophy. My philosophical education amounts to one undergraduate ethics course and a stubborn habit of reading the stuff badly on my own, so I will need the argument explained as we go. What I do have is a working vocabulary: stakes, beats, reversals, the turn at the act break, want set against need, point of view, free indirect discourse, the earned ending, tragedy against comedy. I trust those words the way other people trust their instruments.

There is a dispute in moral philosophy I keep circling, about whether two goods can be genuinely incomparable — so that no amount of either outweighs the other — and whether a real dilemma is possible, meaning a situation in which you are in the wrong whichever way you turn, and the wrong you did not commit is still, in some sense, yours to carry. I would like that dispute restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. Then I want the residue report: which claims come across cleanly, which come across only with distortion, and which cannot be said in my language at all — with your judgment about whether the failures are defects in craft talk or signs that the philosophers were saying less than they thought.

Two words of my own, since I do not have the philosophers' terms and I suspect mine are doing real work. The first is the ledger. It is the running account a reader keeps without being asked: this cost her more than that, this was worth it, this was not. A scene clears the ledger when the reader can rank the losses. I have never written a scene that did not, in the end, depend on the ledger's being clearable, and I have never met a novelist who admitted as much. The second is the shard. It is what a character carries out of a choice that the book never absorbs — not guilt exactly, not regret exactly, the unspent remainder that leaves the next chapter faintly out of true. Craft treats the shard as texture, a matter of tone. I have begun to suspect it is the only place where the incomparability I mentioned actually survives contact with a story.

So: translate the dispute into the ledger, the shard, and the standard craft terms, strand by strand, and tell me what does not survive. Two things I want ruled on. First, does the ledger commit me to commensurability — if every dramatizable choice must be rankable by a reader, is craft quietly assuming the very thing the philosophers deny, and does that make my scenes evidence about how value works or only about how reading works? Second, is the shard the same object as the philosophers' remainder or a different animal — theirs, as I understand it, is a standing unmet obligation rather than a mood, and if mine is merely psychological, I want to be told that the translation has failed and why. I will add a third if you will allow it: when I give each horn of a dilemma its own chapter, its own interiority, and never write the scene in which the two are weighed against each other, am I representing incomparability or evading it?
