For about fifteen years I have read rationalist blogs and forums more or less daily, and the durable effect is a reflex: when a group of people settles on an answer, I want to know whether the group is tracking something or merely aggregating social pressure. I now chair the review committee of a small grantmaking foundation, which has handed me the job of writing our voting procedure, and the justification I keep reaching for — nine of us, most of us better than chance, so the majority is almost certainly right — is carrying more weight than I can defend.

I do not need the textbook version of the Condorcet jury theorem; I have the textbook version. What I need is a procedure the committee can actually run, built on what the theorem and its descendants genuinely license, and candid about where we have been assuming what we would have to argue for. Nine members, uneven and overlapping expertise, monthly meetings, two kinds of question: factual ones (will this program produce the outcome its proposal claims?) and evaluative ones (is this worth funding at all?). Fixed budget each cycle. Three years of minutes, plus each member's record on the questions where we later learned the answer. No statistician; the board must approve whatever I write; and it has to be runnable in the ten minutes before a vote. We tried an internal prediction market for one cycle and it was a mess, which is one reason I would rather the procedure rest on the structure of the vote than on anyone's self-reported confidence.

Write the procedure so that it answers, in the order these things arise:

- how to tell whether a question is one the theorem speaks to at all — a determinate answer, members better than chance, errors independent given the truth — and what to do with the questions that fail that test, including, I suspect, every evaluative question we face, where the majority may have standing to decide and no authority to be right, and where the procedure must stop and leave the call to a named person rather than to a rule;
- what to do about the fact that our errors are plainly correlated: we read the same two sector reports and the same three funders' assessments, so I doubt we are nine independent draws. I want a step that estimates how much independence survives, steps that manufacture more of it (what must happen before anyone speaks, what members must disclose about where their view came from), and a clear statement of how much of the theorem's benefit remains when independence is only partial;
- guidance for questions with more than two options, where I understand majority rule can fail to be even internally consistent;
- a decision about weighting — demonstrated accuracy, stated confidence, or neither — given small samples, a shifting mix of question types, and the likelihood that some members are worse than chance on some kinds of question.

Then run it on three cases: a factual call where our sources overlap heavily, an allocation among three candidate programs, and a plainly evaluative question. At least one should come out in a way I will not like, and I want to be told which steps are not procedure at all but my discretion dressed up as method. No reading list and no citations; I will take the results on trust. What I want is a checklist I could hand to a colleague who has never heard of Condorcet and have her run it in a meeting.
