Ok so me and a friend have been arguing for weeks about fakes and I want an actual answer about what our whole way of treating originals is secretly assuming. Here's the pattern that set it off. A picture that everybody agrees is a good picture loses almost all of its price the day the attribution gets knocked down a notch: same canvas, same brushwork, nothing physical changes, it just turns out the master designed it and assistants painted it, or that it's by a follower. Now put that next to some other things the same market does. Conservators can strip and replace most of a painting's surface and it stays the original, and cleaning often makes it worth more. Partial authorship is fine and it's graded, fully autograph, partly autograph, studio, follower, and those things still sell. But forgery isn't graded at all: a brilliant fake that fooled experts for forty years, and that's arguably a better painting than the dull little sketch the master actually made, is worth about the price of the frame once it's caught, even though the only thing that changed is what we now believe about who made it. Then there's prints and casts, where there are hundreds of originals, and an impression pulled in the artists lifetime and one pulled from the same plate by the estate thirty years after they died are visually identical and priced worlds apart. And museums fill rooms with casts and facsimiles of things they'll never own, and people get something real out of them. And when an artist destroys or defaces their own work, whatever survives is often worth more than the intact thing ever was. My friend says the lesson is that we value the history, not the object, the unbroken causal chain back to one particular pair of hands. I half buy it but it doesn't fit the restoration case, and it doesn't fit the estate print case either, since both objects have just as much chain as their expensive twins. Also there's a shrine in Japan where they tear the buildings down and rebuild them from scratch every twenty years, on the plot next door, and everyone treats the new one as the shrine itself rather than a copy, which seems to break the whole picture.

So what theory of value does this cluster of practices presuppose? Reconstruct it as carefully as you can. Then tell me which bits strictly need it and which bits are just things a market happens to do, scarcity and investment and status signalling, that would survive under any theory of value at all. Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, for instance that the value is simply in the experience of looking at the thing? Is it even internally consistent, given restoration and the estate prints? And if we swapped whatever it presupposes for a straight experience based view, what concretely changes: labelling, prices, insurance, authentication, what museums bother to acquire, and the law, where I'd want to know whether a forger is basically a fraudster or something else entirely, and whether it's ok to destroy a forgery once it's been found out.
