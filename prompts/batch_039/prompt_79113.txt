I have been in a political theory seminar that has spent most of the term on Mill, and a friend in the same seminar keeps pressing a claim I cannot quite rebut: that the Nordic countries are the closest thing in the world to Mill's program actually realized — that Sweden, Denmark and Norway are roughly what On Liberty and the Principles of Political Economy would have produced had Mill been handed a small, rich, unusually homogeneous kingdom and a century of peace in which to work. I suspect the resemblance is superficial and that the parts of the Nordic arrangement I admire are admired for reasons Mill would have rejected, but I cannot say where the divergence lies. What I want from you is a prediction of Mill's views, reasoned from his actual commitments rather than from the label 'liberal' and rather than from a version of Mill quietly updated into a modern social democrat. Take the following description of the Nordic model as given, and do not go looking for further empirical detail: taxation at among the highest levels in the world, raised substantially through broad consumption taxes rather than through unusually high corporate rates; a wage structure compressed from below by strong unions and centralized bargaining between peak federations rather than by statute; universal childcare and parental leave, with near-universal full-time female employment; publicly funded school choice, under which parents may direct state money to independent schools; retail alcohol monopolies with heavy alcohol taxes; established churches retained into the twenty-first century; estate and net-wealth taxes that once existed in some of these countries scaled back or abolished since the 1990s; an informal norm, sometimes called the law of Jante, that treats conspicuous individual distinction as suspect; and a small, historically Lutheran population with high interpersonal trust, now diversifying, governed by the strictest immigration regimes in Western Europe.

Work through these features one at a time and tell me, for each, whether Mill would endorse it as an application of his own principles, endorse it only on grounds he would have to be argued into — in which case give me the argument he would need — or oppose it. Be explicit about where the harm principle is genuinely strained rather than merely extended; I expect the alcohol monopoly and the school-choice arrangement to be the two hard cases, and I want to know which way each cuts and why. Then do three further things. First, identify the places where two of Mill's commitments pull in opposite directions — I am thinking of his defence of individuality against his confidence in the judgment of the educated, and of his suspicion of state power against his willingness to limit bequests and inheritance — and say which commitment he would subordinate, and why that is the better bet rather than a coin flip. Second, tell me what he would say about whether the model can be exported: does it rest on preconditions such as homogeneity, religious habit and pre-existing trust that cannot be legislated, and does his nineteenth-century thinking about which societies were ready for self-government commit him to an answer he would be embarrassed to give today? Third, state the rule by which you take him to be sorting these features, and name one further policy — Nordic or not — that would test that rule, saying what each of two rival readings of him would predict about it.

Please write this as continuous prose organized under headings, not as a list of one-line verdicts. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list or citations; I do not have library access at the moment, and I would rather have your reasoning than your references. Where you are unsure how a particular text of Mill's bears on a point, reason from his settled commitments and mark the place where you are extrapolating, and close by telling me where the largest risk of anachronism in the whole exercise lies.
