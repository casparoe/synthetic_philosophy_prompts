I'm in a weekly reading group on philosophical method, and last week's session on thought experiments got kind of heated, so I want to write up a dialogue version of the fight to bring back on Tuesday. What's at stake is that phrase everyone kept throwing around — "intuition pump" — for a thought experiment that's supposed to make you *see* that something is the case rather than argue you into it. I'm not intersted in a survey or a both-sides summary; I want an actual dialogue between two people in the group, Ines and Theo, who are both sharp, both partly right, and who respond to each others actual points instead of talking past each other.

Ines holds the line that a thought experiment only earns its keep if you can pull an explicit argument out of it — premises you'd accept on their own, a conclusion, and the story doing nothing except make the whole thing vivid. Wherever a case seems to be doing more than that, she says, the extra force is coming from the telling, and that's a warning sign: you're being pumped, not argued with. She's happy to conclude that most of the famous cases in the group's syllabus are really doing rhetoric, and that the ones that survive her test — mostly the ones that kill a proposed definition — are doing something fairly modest. Theo thinks the test is rigged. His line is that every argument bottoms out in something unargued for, and some of those bottom-level things can't be stated in advance at all; they can only be brought into view by walking someone through a case. So the ideal of extracting the argument and keeping the story around as decoration is a fantasy of an argument that argues for everything from nowhere. He'd add that her test is circular, since what counts as an independently acceptable premise is usually a matter of what earlier cases have already gotten you to accept.

The thing they should keep circling back to is a case I made up for the session, because I want to see how the two of them read the same small story so differently. Here it is. In a building with one lift, someone reprograms the lift so that it will never stop on the seventh floor on Tuesdays. Nobody is hurt. The people on seven are mildly inconvenienced — they take the stairs, they're a bit annoyed, that's all. The programmer did it because he finds it funny to picture them pressing the button and nothing happening. Almost everyone in the group had the intuition that he's done something wrong, and nobody could say what the harm was — which one person took as proof that wrongness doesn't have to be about harm at all, and someone else took as proof that the case was doing the work rather than the argument: swap "he finds it funny" for "his boss ordered him to, for some reason or other" and see whether the intuition survives. I want the dialogue to actually run that experiment rather than just mention it, and to end without a tidy resolution — let each of them give up something real and keep something real, and let them part still disagreeing about what the case shows. Roughly 1000 words for the dialogue, in four labelled parts: "The test" (about 300 words, on what Ines's requirement actually amounts to), "The lift" (about 300, the case and the swap), "The remainder" (about 250, on whether the leftover force is rhetoric or evidence), and "Concessions" (about 150, on what each one has to give up). Then a short final section titled "Who got the better of it" (~150 words) where you tell me straight which one you think is ahead and name one specific thing that would change your mind.
