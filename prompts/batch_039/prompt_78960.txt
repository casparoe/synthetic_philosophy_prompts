Ok so I did 35 years as an analytical chemist before I retired, and since then I've been reading philosophy, and something keeps nagging at me about the way the AI safety crowd states its problem. There's the narrow version — the system has to do what the operator actually intends, with intent cashed out in terms of instructions and observable behaviour — and then the separate question of whether the intent was worth having, which seems to get parked somewhere and never picked back up. I want to write a parody of a certification scheme built on the narrow version: a standards body that certifies systems as Intent-Aligned, written deadpan as a numbered-clause standard, where the fine print is scrupulous about the first question and keeps explicitly declining the second — how the operator's intent gets inferred in the first place, what happens when a flawlessly executed request is one nobody would have endorsed, the ethics desk with its limited opening hours, alot of hand-waving about who the operator even is. I spent enough of my life inside ISO documents that I want that flavour, and it should be funny to someone who's read the outer-alignment and specification-gaming material while staying legible to someone who hasn't — so don't invent absurdities, just take the narrow framing seriously and follow it where it goes. 600 words max, numbered clauses, and start with a one-sentence bottom line the way I'd have started an abstract.
