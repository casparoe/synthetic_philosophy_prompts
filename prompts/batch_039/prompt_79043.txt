I am a professor of philosophy who teaches an advanced seminar that spends a fortnight on the thesis usually called origin essentialism — the claim that a human organism could not have developed from a different sperm and a different egg — and every year the discussion dies at the same point, because the intuition is either taken as obvious or dismissed as an artefact of how we describe the case. I want to turn this into a two-page proposal for an internal grant that would buy me a semester of relief from teaching, and I have a half-formed line I want you to build the proposal around: the thesis is almost always stated as one claim and defended with one example, when in fact it decomposes into four claims that come apart as soon as one leaves the textbook case. Call them zygote essentialism (the organism could not have developed from a different fertilised egg), gamete-pair essentialism (it could not have arisen from a different sperm and egg), genome essentialism (it could not have had a different nuclear genome), and lineage essentialism (it could not have had different biological parents). Three cases that the literature has never properly digested pull them apart. Monozygotic twinning, where a single zygote yields two distinct organisms, shows that gamete-pair essentialism does not individuate persons at all: two people can share a single origin. Chimerism, where one organism can carry cell lines descended from two separately fertilised eggs, shows that the presupposition that an organism has exactly one origin is not even an empirical universal, and forces a decision about whether the fusion or one of the contributing zygotes is the origin. Mitochondrial replacement, in which the intending parents' nuclear material is combined with a donor egg's cytoplasm and mitochondria so that the resulting child carries a third person's mitochondrial genome, makes the four claims come apart in every combination, since what counts as the origin now depends on whether we count the donor's cytoplasmic contribution, her mitochondrial genome, or nothing of hers at all. My suspicion — and I want the proposal to test it rather than assume it — is that once these cases are on the table the surviving thesis is considerably weaker than its defenders suppose, and that what looked like a modal fact about individuals is really a norm about how we trace lineages.

Please write the proposal so that the question is stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer, in a sentence a referee could disagree with, and so that the reader can see why the standard defences and the standard objections both leave the issue open: each side argues from the same single example, and neither has had to say what it means by an origin. The approach should be conceptual rather than empirical — I intend to do case analysis and then test candidate principles of origin against the cases, meeting the natural objection that twinning and chimerism are biological curiosities which leave the modal claim untouched, rather than waving it away. Give me a work plan for the semester, week by week or chapter by chapter; a description of what the deliverable looks like (I am imagining a paper of around eight thousand words plus a small workshop, but say what you think is achievable); an account of what would count as a real result as opposed to a merely clarifying restatement; and an honest statement of the failure mode. I would much rather the proposal say plainly that if all three cases can be accommodated by a small patch to the thesis then there is no project here, than overpromise. Please also give a candid assessment of whether the scope fits one semester of partial relief from teaching, and where it would have to be cut if it does not. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations: I shall add the references myself, and I would rather the proposal refer to positions and arguments by description.

Begin directly with the proposal itself: no covering note, no restatement of this brief, no commentary on what you have done. Let the first sentence be the sharpest statement of the question and of the claim I am proposing to defend, and then set the rest out under clear headings.
