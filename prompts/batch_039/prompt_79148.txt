I have recently become the analyst who prepares the risk assessments on which my agency's scientific advisory committee decides whether to adopt a proposed intervention. My colleagues keep handing me a single best-estimate number for the relevant probability, and I no longer think that number is honest: what we actually have is a set of disagreeing estimates, a model identified only within bounds, and sparse data. Once the probability is a set rather than a point, expected-utility maximisation stops telling me what to do, and I know there are rival replacements -- maximin (do as well as possible in the worst case), maximin expected utility (do as well as possible under the worst admissible distribution), Hurwicz (a blend of the best and worst cases), E-admissibility (admit any option that is optimal under some admissible distribution), and maximality (admit any option that no other option beats under every admissible distribution). Write me a procedure my team can run on any proposal, within about a week and recorded on a page: how to elicit, bound and record the set; how to choose among these rules and defend the choice, given that choosing a rule is itself a decision under the uncertainty we are trying to handle; what to do when the rules disagree; how to avoid the trap in which collecting more evidence makes the set wider rather than narrower; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it must hand the decision to a named person because the residual question is how much risk we are willing to accept rather than a question of fact. Then run it on two proposals -- a screening programme whose benefit and harm are both uncertain, and a novel vector-control intervention with an imprecise ecological risk -- including one whose answer I will not like, and close by saying what the procedure cannot settle.
