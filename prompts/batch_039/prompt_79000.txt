I'm trying to turn some notes into a short paper about promises you make to yourself, and I'd like comments on the arguement before I write it up properly.

The line I want to take: a self-promise — made silently, like saying to yourself 'I promise to finish this chapter tonight' — is a real promise and not just a resolution, but it's structurally weaker than a promise to someone else. The usual story is that a promise puts a second party in a position where they have a claim on you, and it's that party who can release you from it. In a self-promise the promisor and the promisee are the same person, so the only person who could release you is you, and that's why we prop these things up with written notes, telling a friend, or turning them into bets. So the weakness isn't a fact about our psychology, it's a fact about the shape of the act.

The bit I'm stuck on: if the promisee is the *future* you, does the whole thing end up hostage to a controversial view about personal identity over time? I want to say no — that the second-party requirement only ever needed the two roles to be occupiable, and one person can occupy them at different times. And I want to bring promises to the dead in under the same heading, since there the promisee also isn't around to release you. The case I keep coming back to: I promise a dying friend I'll finish their book. Do I still have a promise once they're dead? I say yes, and that it's the same shape of case as the self-promise.

Could you comment? Specifically: (1) is the move about what the second-party requirement really needs sound, or am I quietly assuming what I want to prove? (2) do the future-self case and the dead-friend case actually belong together, or am I fudging a real difference between them? (3) is there anything flat-out wrong about how I'm using promises and performatives generally — e.g. whether a silent promise even counts as doing anything at all. No citations needed, I'll sort those out myself. British English, please.
