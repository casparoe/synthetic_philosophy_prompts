I run people development at a mid-sized UK logistics company, and I have to sign off the facilitator's pack for a one-day resilience workshop we have commissioned for our warehouse team leaders. The timing is awkward: it lands three weeks after we announced a pay freeze and a move to rotating shifts, and the consultancy we have engaged, Prosoche Partners, has built the whole day around Stoicism — the tag amor fati, which the facilitator credits to Epictetus, a two-column sorting exercise, a view-from-above segment, and a closing negative-visualisation practice. Write me the facilitator's run-sheet as Prosoche would actually have written it: roughly 700 words, deadpan and entirely in house style, with timings, the leader's scripted lines for each exercise, a short case study lifted from our own operation, and the debrief Q&A he has prepared in advance. It should be funny to someone who has read the Discourses and perfectly innocuous to our finance director, and it must never wink — no narrator stepping outside the document to let us in on the joke.

The humour has to come from the doctrine rather than from garbling it, so please get the philosophy right and let the corporate framing do the damage. Three things in particular. First, the facilitator treats the dichotomy of control as though the ancient distinction were about causal reach — what we can and cannot bring about — when Epictetus's point concerns what is up to us in the sense of our own judgements, impulses, desires and aversions; and he has quietly replaced the ancient two-way split with a three-way one (things wholly within our power, wholly outside it, and partly within it), which he presents as the classical position. Second, he keeps the doctrine of preferred indifferents, which is precisely what stops Stoicism from collapsing into quietism, but arranges matters so that the preferred indifferent he is most exercised about is the pay rise; meanwhile the Stoic account of oikeiōsis — the outward extension of concern from oneself to household, city and the whole of humanity, and with it the insistence that we act for the common good — is missing from the pack except as a slide about teamwork. He may also note that Epictetus was a slave and Marcus Aurelius an emperor, and draw the lesson that the philosophy suits every level of the organisation. Third, since the day has to sit alongside the company's new performance framework, he asserts that everything is fated, and when someone asks the obvious question in the Q&A he reaches for the ancient reply that our own efforts are themselves among the fated things, co-fated with the outcome, and moves the session on. That Q&A should include the question the room will actually ask — 'isn't this just telling us to accept the pay freeze?' — and answer it without conceding a single thing.

Please commit to one reading and see it through rather than hedging: decide what the facilitator genuinely believes, keep him internally consistent, and land the target — the workplace appropriation of Stoicism, and specifically the way the dichotomy of control gets used to convert a legitimate grievance into a failure of assent — without softening either side of the joke. Write in British English throughout (organise, programme, labour, the works). When you have finished the run-sheet, add a few lines clearly separated from it, under a heading of your own, noting where the piece takes liberties with the doctrine or leans on a contested reading — for instance if it has the facilitator state the trichotomy as classical, or treat control as causal influence, say so plainly, rather than letting the satire pass as a summary of the philosophy.
