I'd like a joke, written out as a short comic dialogue of perhaps four to eight exchanges, for an audience of epistemologists and statisticians who work on uncertain evidence. The humor has to come from the mechanics of probability kinematics rather than from the mere presence of Bayesian vocabulary: a punchline that would still land on someone who has never heard of Jeffrey conditionalization is a punchline I don't want, and neither do I want a nerd joke whose only content is that Bayesians are a type of person.

The mechanism I want the joke to exploit is this. Updating by strict conditionalization is order-invariant: conditioning on A and then on B yields exactly the posterior you get by conditioning on B and then on A, since each amounts to conditioning on their conjunction. Jeffrey conditionalization is not like that. It is defined only relative to a partition, the rigidity condition has to be stipulated update by update rather than derived, and two successive updates on different partitions generally fail to commute, so that two agents with identical priors who receive the same two pieces of uncertain evidence in different orders can end up with different posteriors while neither of them has made a mistake. That asymmetry, rather than the mathematics in general, is what the joke should turn on. If a neighboring feature of the rule — its sensitivity to how the space is partitioned, say — gives you a better joke, you may use that instead, but choose one feature and let the punchline depend on it.

The characters should be two colleagues who learn the same things in a different order and then discover that they disagree about something that matters to both of them: a pair of doctors reading the same two inconclusive tests in different sequences, two auditors, two referees, whatever setting makes the disagreement concrete rather than abstract. Neither should be able to locate the other's error, because there isn't one, and the joke should end somewhere a working Bayesian would have to concede is an embarrassment for the rule rather than a confusion about it. The best version, I suspect, lets one of them try to explain the situation in plain language and fail for exactly the right technical reason.

Don't explain the theory back to me afterwards, don't tell me whether the joke works, and don't open with a remark about how difficult it is to write jokes about probability. Just the joke.
