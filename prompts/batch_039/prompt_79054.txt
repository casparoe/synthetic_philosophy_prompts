Last night a friend and I got into a two-hour argument that ended with both of us admitting we weren't sure what we were arguing about, which is probably the most productive outcome either of us has had in a while. The setup: I claimed that the whole family of views on which what you ought to do is whatever some idealized version of you — fully informed, fully rational, thinking as long as it takes, no akrasia, no cognitive slips — would endorse or choose after ideal deliberation is basically the only honest way to be a realist about norms without pretending we already know the answer. My friend said it's a shell game: every version either smuggles the answer in at the start or collapses into "do whatever you'd want if you were better at wanting." Then we started listing sub-problems on a napkin and ran out of napkin, which is why I'm asking you.

What I actually want: I'm a philosophy PhD student (metaethics plus decision theory, no ML, no math past a decent decision theory course) with roughly a year of funded time before I have to commit to a disseration topic, and I want to spend it on the open questions here that are genuinely upstream of the rest. So please don't hand me a survey or a reading list — hand me an ordered agenda and defend it. I want your criteria stated up front (how many other questions depend on a given one, whether it's tractable by armchair-ish work rather than empirical or formal results I'm not going to produce, how much a plausible answer would actually change what the rest of the literature can say), the trade-offs between those criteria, which one you'd tell me to drop, and what over the next year would move something up or down the ranking.

The napkin items were roughly these, and I'm especially curious whether they're really distinct questions or one question wearing five hats: (a) the baseline problem — any idealization has to hold something fixed while improving something else, and depending on whether you hold fixed my present values, my present attachments to particular people, or only my most abstract aims, you get different verdicts, so is there a non-arbitrary way to fix the baseline, or is every version just a substantive ethics in disguise; (b) the authority problem — granting that we could compute what the idealized me would endorse, why does that bind actual me, who is not that agent and might not even recognize him, and is there a story on which the idealized verdict is authoritative rather than merely the preference of a smarter stranger; (c) the aggregation order problem — for a whole society, do you idealize each person first and then aggregate the idealized verdicts, or aggregate actual preferences first and idealize the aggregate, and does the order matter (I suspect it matters enormously and that people quietly assume one of them); (d) the vacuity problem — if the account says only "whatever the ideal procedure outputs," what constraints on the procedure keep it from being formally empty, and can those constraints be motivated without presupposing a substantive theory; (e) the deferral problem — how should we act now given that later, better deliberation might overturn what we currently think, i.e. how much option value does waiting have and is there a principled stopping rule. Tell me which two or three of those deserve the year, which are overrated, and — the part I care most about — where the crux really is: if the indirect approach turns out to be untenable, which of these is the question whose answer is doing the damage?
