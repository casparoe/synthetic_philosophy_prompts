I have spent most of this term arguing about decision theory over lunch with a friend of mine — a condensed-matter physicist with no formal training in philosophy, but with remarkably stable intuitions about cases — and I have begun keeping a list of his verdicts. On Newcomb's problem in its standard form, where the predictor has never yet been wrong, reaches her prediction by working out what you will decide, and has already put a million dollars into the opaque box if and only if she predicted that you would take only that box, he takes only the opaque box. On the hitchhiker case, in which he is stranded, the only driver who passes will stop for someone who will pay on arrival, and the payment cannot afterwards be enforced, he says he would pay, and treats the fact that he could walk away without paying as beside the point. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma played against a perfect duplicate of himself, produced from a scan taken before either of them deliberates, he cooperates. When I run the same Newcomb setup with the predictor replaced by a stage magician who fills the box according to the toss of a fair coin, he takes both boxes at once. He insists in every case that he is assuming no backward causation, and of the predictor in the first case he keeps saying that she is no fortune-teller, only a copy of him running a step ahead.

The case I want to put to him next is this. A large cohort study has found that carriers of a particular allele are far more likely than non-carriers both to take up a certain line of work and to develop, in middle age, an illness that is slow, incurable, and eventually fatal; the work itself neither causes nor protects against the illness; the allele is fixed at conception; and there is no test he could take. He has just been offered a place on a well-paid and genuinely enjoyable graduate programme that leads into exactly that line of work. Accepting the place would be strong evidence that he carries the allele, declining would be evidence that he does not, and his decision would have no other effect on his health. He does not know whether he carries it.

What I want from you is not merely a guess at what he will say, but an account of which rules fit his existing verdicts equally well — causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, the view that one should choose the option one would want every instance of one's own decision procedure to choose, or something else again — what each of those rules predicts about the graduate programme, and which one you would bet on his running, with your reasons. And if his answer to this case leaves the question open, tell me what further case I should put to him in order to force it, and what I should infer about his underlying rule from each of the answers he might give.
