ok so I'm a junior and I run the peer tutoring thing at my school after class, and I'm also on the student committee for our new "mastery based grading" pilot (whole other story lol). Anyway the problem we keep running into is this: someone gives you the right answer and you cant tell if they actually KNOW it or if they just... got it right somehow. My little brother can tell you any times table answer but if you ask him 7x8 a different way he freezes. And in tutoring I've had kids who ace the multiple choice and then fall apart on the one question that asks them to explain or use it somewhere new.

So my teacher lent me this philosophy book and I read the Meno thing, where Socrates asks whether true belief is really worth less than knowledge, and the answer is basically yes it is, because true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus that run away unless you tie them down, and you tie them down by being able to give the reason why. Which is a nice image but its a dialogue, not something I can hand to a tutor.

Here's what I want. Act like an expert on epistemology (theory of knowledge) who also actually knows the learning-science stuff about how people fool themselves into thinking they learned something -- the fluency illusion, re-reading feeling like learning when it isnt, cramming that evaporates, that kind of thing. Think carefully before you write anything, and start with a short brainstorm of what really separates knowing something from having a lucky true belief, then turn that into a procedure I can actually run.

The procedure is for a peer tutor or a teacher with maybe 10 minutes and zero philosophy training. I want it as an ordered list of things to do or ask, with what counts as passing each step, where you stop and say "thats a true belief, not knowledge, dont mark it mastered," and -- this matters -- where the procedure has to get handed to a human because its a judgment call and not a fact. Keep every step doable inside an actual tutoring session (ask them to explain it, make them do it a different way, ask what would change their mind, come back to it in a week, whatever works), and tell me which steps are load bearing and which I could skip if I'm in a rush.

Then run it on three made-up students: (1) one who memorized the steps for solving quadratics and gets every practice problem right, but cant say why you divide by 2a or what completing the square is actually doing; (2) one who can explain photosynthesis beautifully with all the right words but cant apply it to a plant in a closet; (3) one who aced my whole practice test by cramming the night before and will defintely have forgotten all of it by next week. For each one, say what the procedure spits out and whether that answer feels right to you.

Second, what can it just not do? (I'm already worried about the kid who can do the thing perfectly but cant put it into words, and the kid who knows it fine but freezes because I'm staring at them.) I dont want a reading list or citations or names of papers, just give me the idea straight. And if the Meno framing isnt actually the right one for what I'm trying to do, say so and use a better one.
