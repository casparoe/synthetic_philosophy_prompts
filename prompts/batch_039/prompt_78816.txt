Ok, so I've been chewing on this for a while and I want to know what principle, if any, is actually driving my gut. It started with the Darwin and Wallace thing and then I started making up cases. Assume throughout that the result is correct, important, and not fraudulent.

1. Two people on different continents prove the same theorem within a few weeks of each other, neither aware of the other, both proofs complete. Whoever publishes first gets the name on it. My verdict: they discovered it equally, and the name is an accident of the postal service. If I could, I'd put both names on the theorem.

2. Same setup, except the second person publishes 25 years later, having had the proof in a drawer the whole time. My verdict: they still discovered it, and I'd even say they got there first, but they shouldn't get the name, and I don't feel bad about that.

3. Someone publishes the result in an obscure journal in the 50s. It's correct. Nobody reads it, nobody can use it. It gets re-proved in the 80s, becomes famous, and carries the 80s person's name. My verdict: the 80s person is the discoverer. The 50s person gets a paragraph in a history paper and that seems right. And I'd say the same even if the 80s proof was basically a reproduction of the 50s one. (When it finally gets noticed, by the way, three separate people rediscover it in the same year.)

4. Someone builds a big framework and never states the corollary that everyone else sees immediately. A student of theirs states it the next year and it's named after the student. My verdict: the theorem is the framework-builder's and the naming is an injustice -- even though they never said it, and even though the step from framework to theorem is trivial.

5. Four groups get the same result within a year, and everyone at the time was saying it was coming, people were giving conference talks about how all the pieces were sitting there. My verdict: the first one still deserves full credit, no discount.

6. Somebody notices something totally unexpected while doing something else, in a field where nobody was even asking the question. Verdict: this is the paradigm of discovery, maximum credit.

7. Somebody announces the same true thing years earlier on the basis of a completely bogus argument -- reasoning is nonsense, conclusion is right. Verdict: no credit as a discoverer. Right by accident doesn't count.

8. A program brute-forces a search and finds a theorem no human had. Real, correct, important. Verdict: I want to say it was discovered, but "who" is genuinely strange, and I don't think the person who ran the program discovered it. The credit feels thin, but I can't call it zero.

9. The result comes out of a forum thread over six years -- hundreds of people, nobody ever holds the whole thing, it just slowly assembles, and there's no moment where any single person has it. My verdict: it was discovered. Here's the one I keep going back and forth on: whether "the thread" deserves credit in the same way a person does, or whether credit is the kind of thing that only attaches to someone who could have been wrong on their own.

What I can't square: 3 and 4 seem to want opposite rules about whether you have to actually say it out loud. And 5 and 6 seem to want opposite rules about whether it matters that someone else would have gotten there. The crude principle I keep reaching for is: credit goes to whoever turned the discovery from possible into actual. But 3 and 4 both break it -- the 50s person made it actual and gets nothing, the framework person never made it actual and gets everything.

So is there a principle that gets all nine, or close? I'm fine with a two-part answer if the parts are principled rather than ad hoc. If something has to go, tell me which verdict and why. And say what your principle predicts for a case I haven't given: a sealed, dated, notarized envelope containing the result, never opened, found in the person's effects 40 years later, with everything independently re-derived in the meantime. Also, if the answer is "discoveries being multiple means none of these people deserve anything special," say so and tell me what that does to case 6.

Write it in American English please.
