Hi — I'm a third-year philosophy PhD, my area is formal epistemology / Bayesian confirmation, and I've got a question that's been bugging me at the seams of my work.

Quick setup so we're on the same page (I'd rather state it than assume you've got it loaded): the "old evidence" problem is roughly this — if you already know E, your credence in E is 1, and conditionalizing on E leaves everything where it was, so a fact you already knew can never confirm a hypothesis H, no matter how well H predicts E and how badly H's rivals do. That feels wrong, since a lot of good science looks like a theory explaining some anomaly everyone already knew about. There are fixes floating around (update on the newly-noticed entailment from H to E rather than on E itself; or ask what your credence in H would have been had you not already known E), but that's not really what I want to ask about.

Put on your philosopher-of-science hat. Here's the practice I want you to interrogate. In stats/ML there's an iron rule: you never evaluate a model on data it was trained on — you hold out a test set, and a pattern that only shows up in data the model already saw doesn't count as a real discovery. The same norm runs through empirical science: preregistration, the demand that a hypothesis make a novel prediction, reviewers who get suspicious when the data were already sitting in the drawer when the hypothesis was dreamt up. Call it the novelty norm: evidence only confirms a hypothesis if the evidence is new — held out, or not already known at the time the hypothesis was fixed.

What I want: treat the novelty norm as the object of study, and reconstruct the theory of confirmation it presupposes. Specifically —

(a) Does the practice strictly need that theory, or is it merely compatible with it? I can see at least two very seperate stories: one is the Bayesian probability-1 point (known evidence literally can't move credences); the other is that a hypothesis fitted to data is chosen post hoc, so the "confirmation" is a selection artifact rather than a probability fact. Those strike me as different diagnoses with different consequences. Which one does the practice actually commit you to, and could it get by on some third story instead?

(b) I suspect the norm quietly runs together three senses of "old" — old to the individual agent, old to the hypothesis (it was formulated with an eye on the evidence), and old to the relevant community. Do these come apart in cases that matter? Does the practice's implicit theory need them to line up, or does it fall apart where they split?

(c) Suppose we adopt one of the Bayesian fixes so old evidence can confirm. Does the novelty norm collapse, or survive in modified form — and if it survives, what is it now tracking, if not confirmation?

Don't hand me a menu and let me pick. I want your verdict: which theory the norm presupposes, whether that theory is defensible, and where you think I've got it wrong. Take your time — start by brainstorming what "confirmation" would have to mean for the novelty norm to be more than a useful heuristic, then commit. If you think the norm is philosophically confused, just say so and defend it. Flag anything you're unsure about, and tell me if I've misstated the old evidence problem itself. No citations needed — I'm not asking for a reading list.
