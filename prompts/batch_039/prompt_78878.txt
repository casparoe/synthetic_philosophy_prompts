For a reading group on graded theories of mind, I want a story that dramatizes a four-grade taxonomy we have been working with, in which minds are sorted not by intelligence but by where the trial and error happens. A Darwinian creature has its unsuccessful variants eliminated in the lineage: it cannot learn from its own mistakes, because those mistakes are paid for by never being born. A Skinnerian creature pays for its mistakes within its own lifetime, in behavior, under reinforcement and punishment. A Popperian creature pays for almost nothing: it runs the trial internally and discards the failed hypothesis before acting, so the error costs only a wasted rehearsal. A Gregorian creature runs some of its trials outside any single head, in a public system of symbols — tallies, diagrams, notation, recipes, words — which preserves the results of trials performed by minds it will never meet. The grades are cumulative rather than exclusive, and rising a grade is no guarantee of doing better: an internal model can be out of date, and a public notation can encode a mistake for centuries.

Please write the story as exactly five numbered scenes, each a single short paragraph, with no title, no preface, and no closing paragraph. None of the four grade names may appear anywhere in the story, and no scene may use the vocabulary of selection, conditioning, simulation, or cultural transmission; a reader should be able to infer each creature's grade from what a mistake costs it and who ends up paying. Each scene is told from inside the creature's own point of view, in a register its grade can actually support — the earliest creatures must have no metacognitive words available to them. A single recurring thing, an object or a sound, must appear in all five scenes and mean something different each time.

The first four scenes each center on one grade, in ascending order. The third must contain a moment in which the creature's inner rehearsal returns a confident answer that the world then contradicts, and the scene must not settle whether a lifetime of ordinary reinforcement would have served it better. The fifth scene introduces a creature that operates fluently at the highest grade while relying on a symbol system it did not build and cannot state the rules of; it must end without deciding whether this creature belongs to the fourth grade or to none of them.
