Next month I have to run a single seventy-five-minute session of an interdisciplinary graduate reading group — philosophers and economists, all of whom will have read Hume's 1752 essays on money and interest beforehand, and none of whom has worked on the history of monetary thought. I need a teaching plan, not an explanation of the material: the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, the one example that should carry the session, the misconceptions to expect and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, the closing question that would tell me whether the idea has landed, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.

The session has one job: to stop the group from leaving with the tidy proto-monetarism they will arrive holding. The components I think have to be in play — tell me if the dependencies actually run the other way — are: the distinction between money as an instrument of exchange and money as wealth; the closed-economy claim that a larger stock of money, once prices have settled, alters nothing real; the open-economy mechanism by which money redistributes itself among trading nations; the transition, the interval during which an inflow is supposed to raise industry before prices catch up; and Hume's separate argument that the rate of interest is fixed by the demand for borrowing and the profits of commerce rather than by the quantity of precious metal. The plan must make the group feel the pull between the second and the fourth of these: the same writer who describes money as the oil that smooths the motion of trade rather than one of its wheels also holds that during the interval an influx of money has real effects on industry. Whether that is a genuine exception to neutrality, a claim about transition only, or a tension Hume never resolves is the thing I want staged, not settled.

For the carrying example: I want one case that can be run twice, once with the money already in place and once mid-inflow, so the group can see the difference between a level and a transition. It has to involve at least two regions, so the distribution mechanism is visible; it must not import banking or paper credit, since Hume's views there are a separate tangle; and it must not deliver the money as a lump sum to everyone simultaneously, because that presupposes the very neutrality we are interrogating. Tell me what the example must have and what it must lack, and whether the obvious candidate — a shipwreck, a newly opened mine — will do or will quietly beg the question.

The misconceptions I expect, and want placed in the sequence: that Hume is a quantity theorist tout court and these essays are a way station on the road to the equation of exchange; that the interval amounts to Hume recommending debasement as a stimulus; that the balance-of-trade mechanism requires gold to be physically carried from country to country; that the interest argument is a piece of naive empiricism rather than a causal claim about profits and borrowing; and that because money is not a wheel of trade, Hume thinks money does not matter. I may be wrong about which of these the group forms and in what order; correct me, and add the ones I have missed — especially any that will form only after they have understood the earlier material, since those are the ones I will not see coming.

For the closing check, I want a single question whose answers separate a group that has memorized the neutrality claim from one that has understood the transition argument — something they cannot answer by reciting the essay back at me.

Finally, tell me what to leave out and to say so out loud. I am inclined to omit the later bullionist and currency-school controversies, Hume's essays on banks and paper credit, and anything requiring a written equation; tell me whether that leaves the session incoherent, and if so, what the minimum addition is.
