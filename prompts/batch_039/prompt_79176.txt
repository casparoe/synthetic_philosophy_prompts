Write a short story set in a country whose entire public insurance system rests on a single principle: a person is owed repair for the misfortune she did not choose, and owes the cost of the misfortune she did choose. The principle is luck egalitarianism, and its central line is the one between option luck — the luck of the gamble you knowingly took — and brute luck, the luck of what simply befell you. Your story lives on that line.

The setting is the claims office where the line is drawn. Claimants must give an account of how their misfortune came about, and an officer must sort each account into what was circumstance and what was choice. Follow one officer through a single day, and give her a caseload that pulls the line from several directions at once.

- A woman whose injury came from a risk she took deliberately, but whose appetite for that risk is itself traceable to a childhood she did not choose. If the appetite is part of her, she is being charged for being who she is; if it is part of her circumstances, it is unclear that anything she ever does will count as a choice.
- A man whose misfortune looks like pure brute luck until the record turns up one small, ordinary decision made years earlier that hindsight has made load-bearing. Everyone in the office can see the decision; no one can agree whether it was a gamble or just living.
- A claimant whose case is easy — a plain bad break with no decision anywhere in the chain — and who shows the reader what the scheme costs even when it works: what it is to have to prove that you did not deserve what happened to you, and to be helped as someone blameless rather than simply helped.
- A claimant whose trouble is that the life she wants is more expensive than the lives of those around her, and who must be told, in the vocabulary of the office, that an ambition is not a circumstance.

Then let the principle produce the consequence it really does produce: someone is refused, and the refusal is right by the scheme's own lights. Show what the officer does with that, and what the country has decided it can live with.

Do not let the story argue for or against the view. No character should deliver a verdict on it, and the narration should not adjudicate. Let the office's procedures, the claimants' accounts, and the officer's reasoning carry the philosophy, so that a reader who has never heard the term could reconstruct the principle, its appeal, and its cost from the story alone. It should read as fiction — scene, character, dialogue — rather than as a case study with a narrator's commentary. Keep it short, keep it inside the office if you can, and do not reach for a redemptive ending.
