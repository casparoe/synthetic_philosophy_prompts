Our epistemology reading group has spent two years circling G. E. Moore's two-hands argument — the one from his 1939 British Academy lecture 'Proof of an External World', in which he holds up one hand, then the other, and concludes that at least two external things exist — and the discussion has hardened into four positions that no longer touch one another.

The Moorean says the argument is a proof in the strict sense: the premises are distinct from the conclusion, he knew them, and the conclusion follows. The sceptic who denies the premise shows only that he knows less than the rest of us, and no further defence is owed to him. The Wittgensteinian says the argument has no force whatever, because a doubt that reached the premise would undercut the framework within which the words in the premise have any use at all — a doubt of everything is not a doubt but a gesture. A third member insists that a proof must be able to move someone who does not already accept its premises, and that by this standard Moore has given us nothing; a fourth replies that this standard is a confusion about what proofs are for, since we do not require a proof in arithmetic to persuade someone who rejects the axioms.

I have to circulate a short written diagnosis before Thursday's meeting, and I want it to be usable rather than a fifth round of the same four speeches.

Set out what each position is actually committed to, as stated here rather than as you would find them in the literature, and then tell me what kind of disagreement this is: verbal; substantive, about the standard a proof must meet; or two parties answering different questions under one heading — one about what may be claimed in ordinary life, the other about what can be vindicated from no assumptions at all. Say whether the deadlock is itself evidence that the question is ill-posed.

One member has offered a hypothesis I would like tested rather than restated: that the impasse is a clash between a dialectical conception of proof, on which its function is to move an interlocutor, and an alethic one, on which its function is to establish a truth; that Moore's argument plainly succeeds on the second and plainly fails on the first; and that both sides are therefore right and the dispute dissolves. Tell me whether that is a dissolution or a relocation — in particular, whether the dialectical demand can be defended without begging the question in its own favour.

A diagnosis worth the name should travel: say whether the same shape of standoff — one party refusing to grant the framework on which the other's evidence depends — recurs in non-philosophical settings, or whether the resemblance is superficial.

Close with what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or an argument that none should be expected. I don't need a literature survey or a reading list.
