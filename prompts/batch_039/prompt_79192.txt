I would like a name for a pattern I keep meeting in arguments, or, failing a name, a careful account of what the pattern is and whether the argument at its centre is sound.

The pattern concerns acts of a certain shape: worth doing if a few people do them, harmful if many do. Suppose several people are each in a position to do such an act. None can observe what the others choose; all have the same information; each knows this about the others. The argument I keep encountering runs roughly as follows: if it were right for me to do this, it would be equally right for everyone else here to do it; if everyone did it, the result would be bad; therefore it is not right for me to do it. Two settings in which I have watched this happen:

- A group of seminar participants, every one of whom wants the visiting speaker to slow down and explain a step, and not one of whom asks, each reasoning that if the request were in order for them it would be in order for the other twenty, and that twenty interruptions would destroy the session. Nobody asks, and nobody follows the argument.
- Members of a committee, several of whom think a decision should be reopened. Each declines to raise it, on the ground that if the grounds for reopening were good they would be good for every member, and that a committee which reopens its decisions whenever any member objects never finishes its business. The decision stands.

What strikes me is that this is not a case of overlooking the aggregate; the agents are reasoning from it. Nor is anyone hoping to be spared an effort: each would act if they were the only person in the position. Yet the upshot is that the situation in which the act ought to be done by a few is precisely the situation in which it is done by nobody, and no one can make themselves the exception without either exempting themselves from a test they apply to others or breaking a symmetry nobody is entitled to break. There seems to be no individual mistake anywhere in the structure: each agent's conclusion is defensible given the others' conclusions, and theirs given his.

Does this have an established name? The nearest things I know are the bystander effect, the free-rider problem, and what is sometimes called the unilateralist's curse, but none of them seems to be the same thing. The first two concern a good that somebody must supply, and the agent's expectation that somebody else will supply it; here nobody expects to be spared, and what is at issue is a level of activity rather than a shared good. The unilateralist's curse runs in the opposite direction: it concerns many agents acting on private and noisy estimates of whether an act is worth doing, and it predicts too much action rather than too little.

I am also unsure how far the central argument is valid, since it appears to treat a permission as though it had to hold universally, in a case whose whole point is that the permission is exercised by a few. If there is an established term, please say what exactly it names — the type of situation, the move in argument, or the outcome — and how it differs from the concepts above. If there is none, please coin one, define it, and mark its edges: whether it covers cases in which the harm comes from a sum of tiny contributions rather than from a threshold; whether it survives if the participants can take turns or draw lots; and whether the same structure appears wherever a permission must be stated in general terms rather than case by case.
