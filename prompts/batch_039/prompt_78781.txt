I have drafted the essay below and would like substantive comments on it before I revise. Please read it as a piece of philosophy rather than as an exercise in exposition: I want to know where it goes wrong, which of its moves are unoriginal in ways I have not noticed, and whether its central claim is a genuine thesis or a redescription of a familiar one.

Essay: Replacement Without a Moment of Death

Suppose that a person's neurons are replaced, one at a time, by silicon units that at every stage replicate the functional profile of the tissue they displace, so that the subject's behaviour, memory reports and dispositions are indistinguishable from what they would have been had the procedure never been performed. Call this the gradual case. No one can say at which replacement the person ceased to exist, if they ceased to exist at all, and I take it that this inability is not merely epistemic. The natural conclusion, and the one I want to defend, is that the person survives.

I want the argument for that conclusion made more precise than it usually is, because the usual version — there is no moment of death, therefore there is no death — infers the absence of a distinction from the absence of a boundary, and that inference is not in general sound. A process can be such that no single part of it is decisive while the process as a whole is destructive; a slow poison administered in imperceptible doses has exactly this shape. The gradual case therefore needs a principle that the poisoning case does not satisfy.

The principle I propose is this. A process is step-independent when no proper part of it is such that the fate of identity is settled by that part alone. Identity is preserved by any process that is both step-independent and organisation-preserving, where organisation-preserving means that each stage of the process realises the same functional organisation as its predecessor. Gradual replacement satisfies both conditions. Poisoning satisfies the first and fails the second, which is why it destroys where replacement does not. Call the steps that would settle identity, if there were any, the ledger; the claim is then that identity is preserved exactly when the ledger is empty.

Two consequences follow, and they are why I think the principle is worth stating. First, identity is a matter of the shape of a process in time rather than of its endpoints: two processes beginning and ending in the same states can differ over whether they preserve a person, because they can differ over whether their ledgers are empty. Second, since the ledger is a feature of the process as a whole and not of any of its stages, identity can be settled by facts that no observer at any stage, the subject included, is in a position to detect. I regard the second consequence as a feature rather than an embarrassment: facts about identity need not be facts anyone could come to know from the inside.

The obvious objection is that whether a process is step-independent depends on how it is carved into steps, and that the carving is our choice; divide finely enough and every process has an empty ledger. My answer is that the grain of the steps is fixed by the physical grain of the causal interactions constituting the process, not by our descriptions of it. Whether that answer is adequate is, I think, the weakest point in the essay.

I would like comments under the following headings, in this order, and I would rather have four objections that hurt than twenty that do not.

1. Accuracy. Is any claim I attribute to the standard debate misstated? Have I described the gradual case in a way that quietly begs a question, for instance by building functional equivalence into the setup rather than arguing for it? Are there well-known objections I have simply not anticipated?

2. Validity. The central inference runs from step-independence plus organisation-preservation to the preservation of identity. Is the step sound? In particular, are the two clauses doing independent work, or does organisation-preservation carry the whole argument while step-independence is idle decoration — and if the latter, what is lost by dropping it?

3. The distinctions I lean on. I separate identity from survival and the shape of a process from its endpoints. Do I hold these apart consistently, or do I slide between them — for instance, when I claim that identity can be settled by facts no one could detect, am I still talking about identity rather than about something else that deserves another name?

4. The strongest objection. Give me the single most damaging objection, stated in its strongest form, and then tell me whether I can answer it without abandoning the essay's conclusion. If I cannot, say what the conclusion would have to become.

5. Novelty. Which parts of this are already in circulation in some form, even if under other names? I would rather be told that a move is standard than believe I have invented it. Where I have coined terms, tell me whether the coinage marks a real distinction or merely renames one.

6. Revision. Tell me what to cut, what to develop, and what to concede outright. If the essay is confused at a level deeper than my individual claims — if, say, I am pressing an identity question in a form that presupposes an answer — say so, and say what the better question would be. Where I present something as a decisive answer that is in fact a restatement of the conclusion, identify it.
