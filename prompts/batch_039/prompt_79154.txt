I retired from atmospheric chemistry about three years ago and, in a fit of what my wife calls late-onset humanities, I've been reading moral philosophy ever since. One thing has been bugging me. I spent my whole career doing ensemble forecasts under model uncertainty — running forty climate models, weighting them, refusing to pretend I knew which one was right — and it turns out philosophers have a whole literature on what to do when you don't know which *moral* theory is right, and it looks alot like my old job, except nobody can agree on the units.

So write me a mock quarterly report from a "moral portfolio manager": a guy who runs his moral life the way I used to run a pension fund. The theories are asset classes. His credences in them are position sizes. He rebalances when he reads something convincing. He sends his trustees a letter every quarter explaining how it went. Call it Q3 Report to the Trustees of the Moral Portfolio, about 800 words, first person, deadpan, in the real register of a fund letter — sober, faintly defensive, with a paragraph about "risk."

The jokes have to come from getting the actual problems right, so please build the piece around things like:

- The units problem. Two theories can agree that an action is the best one available and still have no common scale for how much better it is, so "the expected choiceworthiness of an action" — his phrase, meaning roughly the average across theories of how good each one thinks the action is — can just be undefined. I want him to wave this off in a parenthetical and then quietly contradict himself twenty lines later.
- The favorite-theory option. One trustee keeps writing in to say you should simply do what your best-supported theory says and stop pretending the average of theories is a thing that exists. The manager should have a bad answer to this. Not a stupid one — a bad one, the kind you give when you haven't thought it through.
- Fanaticism. One small holding — say 2% in a theory that says a certain outcome is worth infinitely much — keeps trying to take over the entire portfolio. The manager treats this as a position-sizing question rather than a crisis of faith.
- Theory individuation. If you can always split a theory into two variants and give them separate credences, his portfolio becomes gerrymanderable, and he should be dimly, uncomfortably aware that he's doing this.
- Hedging vs. not hedging. At least one theory in the portfolio says that compromising with other theories is itself wrong, which means the hedge loses by construction under that theory. He should notice this and file it under "diversification benefits."
- The paralysis problem, or whatever he calls it: when his credences are close to uniform, the ranking of actions stops meaning much, and he starts making decisions on the basis of what he calls "operational considerations." Don't spell out what those are.

End with a short appended memo from a compliance officer who thinks the whole exercise is a category error — moral theories aren't independent bets, and a "return" isn't a thing — and let him be annoying and maybe a little bit right.

Keep it legible to someone who's never heard of any of this. No names, no citations, no footnotes to real papers, no jargon that only works if you already know the term. And definately don't have the manager announce the joke — he should be sincerely trying to run a portfolio. The satire is aimed at the habit of treating moral uncertainty as a diversification problem, not at any particular philosopher.
