The question:

3. Sartre holds both that the person in bad faith is not simply lying to himself or mistaken about the facts, and that he remains free and answerable for what he does. (a) Explain what the difference between bad faith and ordinary self-deception is supposed to amount to, using the café waiter and the woman on the first date. (b) Explain how bad faith is possible on Sartre's own terms — in particular, what he means when he says the belief involved is unstable and undermines itself, given his rejection of the Freudian unconscious. (c) Can a sincere person be in bad faith? Defend your answer.

The answer:

Student #14

Sartre begins from the claim that existence precedes essence. A paperknife is designed before it is made, so its purpose is fixed in advance by whoever made it; a human being is not like this. There is no human nature that tells us what we will do, and so each of us has to make himself out of nothing. Sartre's way of putting the consequence is that we are responsible for what we are, and this responsibility shows up as anguish — not fear of any particular thing, but the recognition that nothing outside us can decide for us. Bad faith is the flight from that recognition. The person in bad faith tries to convince himself that he is a thing with a fixed nature, so that his behaviour follows from what he is rather than from a choice he is making.

The café waiter is the clearest case. His movements are a little too quick and a little too precise, his attentiveness a little too eager; his whole manner is a game of being a waiter. The point is that he cannot be a waiter the way an inkwell is an inkwell — he can only play at it — and yet by playing at it so earnestly he treats the job as an essence and hides from himself that he has chosen this life and could choose otherwise. The woman on the first date has the same structure. Her companion takes her hand; she leaves it there, neither returning the gesture nor pulling away, and keeps the conversation on a level of generality at which nothing is being asked of her. By refusing to read the gesture as a sexual approach, she postpones the moment at which she would have to decide. In both cases a free being tries to occupy the place of a thing.

For this to work, though, the person in bad faith has to keep the truth out of his own awareness, and that seems to require a division inside the self — some part of the subject that knows what is going on and holds it back from consciousness. Sartre cannot say that the waiter simply does not know he is free, because then there would be no flight, only ignorance; and he cannot say that the waiter does know, because then the deception would collapse as soon as it was formed. The account therefore needs something like Freud's censorship, an agency that does the hiding, and Sartre's originality is that he takes over this Freudian machinery while refusing the name. What results is a belief that has to be held and undermined at the same time. The cost is that the bad-faith subject now looks like someone in the grip of a mechanism rather than someone choosing, and to that extent Sartre's insistence that the waiter is fully to blame for his bad faith looks excessive.

On the last part: if bad faith is self-concealment, then the cure is honesty. The man who admits he is a coward, instead of explaining that his circumstances made him one, has stopped fleeing, and he is no longer in bad faith. Sincerity and bad faith are therefore opposites rather than the same thing under two names.
