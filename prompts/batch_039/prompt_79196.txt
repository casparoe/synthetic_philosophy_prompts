I recently committed to giving a fixed share of my income to whatever I judge to be the most effective charities, and the arguments I have had since then keep sliding between three different kinds of complaint without anyone noticing: that my picture of the good is wrong, that my picture of what I owe others is wrong, and that my picture of what I can actually know is wrong. I would like a decision tree for my reading group that sorts the critiques of effective altruism along those lines, so that we stop talking past each other. Every branch node should be a question we can answer without already knowing which critique it leads to, and the first fork should be the one I just described. Underneath it, split as the arguments actually require: on the epistemic side, separate the complaint that we measure the wrong things from the complaint that only measurable things get funded in the first place; on the practical side, separate the attack on earning to give from the attack on the movement's professionalised institutions, and within the first of those separate the objection that a donor's labour is replaceable from the objection about what the donated money ends up funding; on the political side, separate the claim that private giving displaces public provision from the claim that the movement's funding structure quietly decides which questions get researched. Build the tree from the space of answers rather than from a list of critics, and locate the named positions in it afterwards. Leaves should include at least the measurability-bias critique, the institutional critique, the earning-to-give critique, and the displacement critique, each with one sentence on the commitment its defender will find hardest to live with. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, note any combination of answers that nobody occupies and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and say which single answer, if someone gave it, would make the rest of the tree moot. Aim for six to eight leaves. My group can follow the arguments but has not read the primary literature carefully, so keep the nodes in plain language and do not lean on the names of authors or works.
