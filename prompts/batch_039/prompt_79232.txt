I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, working on animal cognition and the moral status of invertebrates, and in three weeks I am presenting my work at a seminar hosted by a large entomology department. The audience will be roughly a dozen people: several insect neurobiologists who work directly on nociception, learning, and motivational trade-offs in insects; a couple of researchers in agricultural and pest management; a behavioural ecologist; and one philosopher of biology who is broadly sympathetic to my project but firmly empirical in temperament. I need a preparation sheet for the discussion period, not a summary of my talk.

The position I will defend is a qualified version of the scale argument for taking insect welfare seriously. Its core move is to set aside the binary question of whether insects are sentient and to reframe the issue as a question about the expected moral weight per insect, which I analyse as the product of two terms: a credence that the insect has morally relevant experience, and a scale term fixed by how many insects there are and how they live and die — pesticides, parasitism, starvation, and the like. My central claim is that the scale term is robust across essentially every plausible empirical scenario and is enormous, while nearly all of the genuine scientific disagreement is disagreement about the credence term; consequently, the productive move is to stop treating sentience as a verdict to be delivered and instead to specify the neurobiological and behavioural markers that ought to raise or lower the credence — centralised integration of nociceptive signals, motivational trade-offs between avoiding harm and pursuing other goals, opioid-mediated modulation of avoidance, self-administration of analgesics, and so on — and then to ask what credence the present evidence actually licenses. I argue that the credence is low but far from negligible, and that a low credence multiplied by a scale many orders of magnitude larger than the vertebrate one still yields an expected moral weight that dominates that of, say, farmed mammals. I concede at the outset that I cannot measure insect suffering; my claim is only that our uncertainty does not license indifference.

Please prepare me for the questions this particular audience will actually ask — not the ones I would get from an effective-altruism group or a philosophy-of-mind reading group. I want the objections ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrased the way these people would phrase them, with a two-minute reply to each and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than wait to be pushed on, which objection I ought to pre-empt in the talk itself, and which single reply has to land for the whole argument to survive the hour. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Assume the audience is allergic to a priori moralising, protective of the legitimacy of their own research, and quick to notice when a philosopher has quietly assumed the conclusion; make sure the sheet engages my probability/scale decomposition and the marker list rather than retreating to generic doubts about whether insects feel pain.
