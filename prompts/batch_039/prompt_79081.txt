I'm applying for a small internal grant — three months of part-time work, ending in a paper of roughly seven thousand words — and I need a formal research proposal written up. My half-formed idea sits in the supertask literature, in the Thomson's-lamp family of puzzles, where an infinite sequence of state changes is packed into a finite interval and we then ask about the state of the system once the interval has closed. As far as I can tell, the standard exchange settles into two replies: a deflationary one, on which the description simply fails to determine an endpoint state, so we may stipulate whatever answer we like or none at all; and a limiting one, on which some continuity or convergence requirement on the relevant quantity does fix an answer. My impression is that both replies are answering a question I have not seen cleanly separated from a second question, and that the separation is what makes the puzzle tractable.

The distinction I want to develop is between a semantic question — does this description determine a unique endpoint state? — and a modal one — is the process described a physically possible process in the first place? The deflationary reply, I suspect, treats the first question's answer as settling the second, when it does not; and the limiting reply treats the mathematical limit as a physical fact when it may be only a convention of extension. The angle I want to propose as the missing link is a criterion of robustness. Take a lamp that dims rather than switches, with brightness given by a convergent series, so that its endpoint brightness is fixed and stable under small perturbations of the schedule; contrast the on/off case, where the outcome is not stable in that way. My hypothesis is that this difference is the right model for the boundary question generally: an endpoint value is fixed just when it is the robust limit of the family of nearby admissible schedules, so the on/off lamp fails the test and the dimmer lamp passes it — which would make the lamp case physically impossible rather than indeterminate, and would give the limiting reply a principled foundation instead of a stipulated one. Whether robustness can be made precise enough to do this work, or whether it merely relocates the arbitrariness it was meant to remove, is the thing I actually want to find out.

Please write the proposal. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions leave it open, and why the robustness criterion is not simply the continuity reply in different words; lay out what I would actually do, in stages, over three months; say what a successful result looks like and what would count as the project failing — including the real possibility that the criterion is vacuous or circular; and be honest about scope, in particular whether this is too thin to sustain the time or too large for it. If my distinction or my criterion turns out to be confused, say so and reframe rather than defending my formulation. No bibliography or list of readings; I will supply the references myself.
