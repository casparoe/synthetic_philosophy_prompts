ok so for a thing I'm writing I need to figure out what a theory of echo chambers is even supposed to do. Every time I read something about echo chambers or epistemic bubbles it uses the word for a slightly different thing and then argues past everyone else, and I keep wanting to ask "does your theory even have to explain that?" before we start. So before I read any more takes I want the spec first: what does any decent theory of echo chambers / epistemic bubbles have to be able to do?

Here's what I think it has to handle, but tell me where I'm wrong and what I'm leaving out, and for each one say which case is doing the work, cause a requirement with no case behind it is just a vibe:

1. The difference between a group that never happens to hear the other side (my uncle only watches one channel, but nobody told him to) and a group that's been actively taught that the other side is lying on purpose, or is paid to lie, or is evil. Those feel different, and the second feels worse, but I cant say why, and if a theory can't separate them it isn't doing much.

2. It can't just mean "any tight community." A math department ignoring a guy who keeps emailing them a proof that 1=2 is being right, not closed minded. But my aunt got told for years that her illness was anxiety, and the facebook group of people with the same thing turned out to be right and the doctors wrong. So "closed off from the mainstream" can't be the definition of the bad thing.

3. Whether the problem is the false beliefs or the broken process. I can imagine a group that reads one source, checks it badly, and happens to be right about the thing. I'd still say something's off there. A theory should be able to say what.

4. Keeping apart the two things everyone calls "polarization": disagreeing more about the actual issues, versus just disliking the other side more. I'm pretty sure those don't move together, and alot of the doom talk is about the second one.

5. Some findings it has to not contradict, and tell me which of these I've got wrong, because I'm going off half-remembered stuff: (a) the famous "correcting someone makes them dig in deeper" thing mostly fell apart when bigger studies redid it, and corrections usually just work a little; (b) there was an experiment where they paid people to follow a bunch of accounts from the other side and the people ended up more polarized, not less; (c) people on both sides hugely overestimate how much the other side hates them and how extreme they are. A theory that says "just show them the other side" or "they're all brainwashed" has to survive all three.

6. It should say what the actual harm is. If someone in a bubble is happy, has friends, and is wrong about one thing, what's the damage? I want the harm named in a way that doesn't sneak the answer in.

Then two things I really want: which of these can't all be had at once? My gut says 1 and 2 (don't pathologize tight groups) and 6 (say what's actually wrong) are pulling in opposite directions, so tell me if that's a real tension or if I'm being dumb. And which single requirement does the most work, like which one knocks out the most theories.

Also say where your own answer is most likely to be wrong, and what would change your mind -- like which finding or case, if it turned out differently, would flip which requirements you think are non-negotiable. No reading list or citations please, I can't look stuff up and I don't want names and dates, just the spec.
