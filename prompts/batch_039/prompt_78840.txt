I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy assembling a two-page proposal for a summer research grant from my university's ethics institute; the sum is small but the review committee is interdisciplinary, so the document has to be legible to a political theorist and an economist as well as to philosophers. Please take the role of an expert in intergenerational ethics and the theory of rational choice, and think carefully about framing before you draft anything, because the hard part of this genre is finding a question that is genuinely open rather than merely unresolved-looking, and stating it so that it has a determinate answer. Before you write the proposal proper, spend a short paragraph generating two or three rival statements of the question and choosing among them, telling me why the losers lose.

My half-formed idea is this. Three quite different things get run together under the heading of pure time preference, and I suspect the conflation is doing real work in the debates: first, a descriptive claim about how we in fact weigh near against far goods; second, a prudential claim about whether my own future good counts for less than my own present good merely because it is future; and third, a moral claim about whether the interests of people who do not yet exist count for less merely because they are future. The arguments I have encountered treat the second and third as independent — the prudential side gets defended by appeal to its being my life to spend, the moral side by appeal to the absence of reciprocity, of any determinate claimant, of anyone who could be said to be wronged. But there is a structural parallel I cannot shake, and I want the proposal built on it: on the reductionist picture of personal identity, my future self stands to me roughly as a future stranger stands to me, the relation differing in the degree of psychological connectedness rather than in kind. If that is right, any principle permissive enough to license discounting my own future welfare should be permissive enough to license discounting theirs, unless the two cases differ in some further respect. I have been collecting candidate disanalogies and would like the proposal to take a position on which survive: that I can bind my future self by promise or resolution in a way I cannot bind posterity; that the causal route from present sacrifice to future benefit runs through my own agency in the one case and through collective, uncertain institutions in the other; that the non-identity problem makes future people's membership in the moral community depend on present choices, whereas my future self is a fixed referent; and that psychological connectedness is a graded relation which could ground a graded prudential concern, while moral concern is not obviously gradable at all. I want to know whether the coupling claim survives these, and if it does, what it implies about how much we owe the future — and whether it makes the familiar standoff in climate economics between the near-zero and the few-percent camps look like a disagreement about the wrong parameter.

So write the proposal. It should open with the question stated precisely enough that a reader could tell what would count as answering it; explain why the existing positions leave it open (no literature review — refer to positions and arguments as needed and I will add the citations myself); set out what I would actually do across a ten-week summer in terms that make it recognisably philosophical rather than a survey, and say why that method beats the obvious alternative of cataloguing intuitions about future generations — my own inclination is to reconstruct the strongest version of each disanalogy as a formal constraint on the discount function and then test whether any of them survives the parallel cases, but tell me if that is the wrong instrument; say what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing (I am serious about this part, and would rather have a proposal that names the way it could turn out empty than one that promises a deliverable it cannot produce); and be honest about whether the whole thing is too large for ten weeks, with a fallback version if it is. Close with a short paragraph on what the payoff would be for someone outside philosophy. Two pages, headings, no bibliography.
