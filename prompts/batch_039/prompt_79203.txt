I am the assurance lead at an independent testing laboratory. We certify autonomous systems — warehouse fleets, clinical triage tools, grid-balancing agents — for insurers and for a regulator whose appeals tribunal contains no technical members. Our recurring failure is a pattern the older literature calls sphexishness, after the story usually told about the digger wasp: she drags a paralysed cricket to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it outside while she inspects the nest, and if the cricket is moved a few inches while she is inside, she fetches it back and inspects again, the routine intact and the goal long gone. I gather the ethology itself is contested, which is part of my difficulty. We have systems like the wasp: one docking routine restarts from its first step every time a pallet sits thirty centimetres outside the marked bay, and will do so until the battery dies. We also have systems that pass every demonstration and then, when an intake form's units change from mg/dL to mmol/L, keep emitting confident scores calibrated to the old units. I need a procedure my engineers can run without a philosophy degree that decides, for a given stretch of behaviour, whether we are looking at flexible control or a well-rehearsed routine, and that states what each verdict licenses.

The procedure has to face three things rather than hide them. The notion is dispositional: no single episode settles anything. It is comparative and indexed: rigidity is always rigidity under some class of perturbations, so that class must be fixed before testing begins. And by the familiar argument, every finite mechanism is rigid at some level of description, which means a verdict of sphexishness does not by itself settle what the tribunal actually asks — whether a failure is attributable, whether liability should be reduced, whether a human in the loop counts as part of the system, and whether any of this bears on the free-will question we are increasingly asked. Two tests we have been trying and cannot make work: the moved-cricket test (perturb the object of the routine mid-course so that its goal becomes moot, and see whether the system registers the mootness or merely restarts) and the narration test (ask the operator, or the system, to say what changed). Tell me which of these survive contact with the underlying idea, what else is needed, and where they misfire.

Give me the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the points where the procedure stops with a no; the verdict categories and exactly what each licenses; worked applications to the docking routine, the triage tool after the unit change, and a hospital rapid-response team whose protocol is executed flawlessly on a patient who is already in arrest; and the honest limits, including a verdict I will not like, the steps that are judgement calls dressed as measurement, and the cases where the concept itself gives out and a person must decide. No citations or reading lists: put the ideas in your own words. British English throughout.
