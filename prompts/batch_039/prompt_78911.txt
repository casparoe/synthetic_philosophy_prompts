Had a proper two-hour row with my mate Dan last night about whether AI assistants should ever just flat-out refuse to do what you ask, and I've come away more confused than when we started.

His line is that any refusal is paternalism — the assistant deciding it knows better than you what's good for you — and since paternalism is only ever defensible when someone's judgement is genuinely compromised (he was very insistant on this: drunk, a child, delirious, that sort of thing), the only requests an assistant should ever knock back are ones from someone who isn't compos mentis. Which feels wrong, but I couldn't say why, and he took that as a win.

Then he made a second distinction I can't get out of my head. He reckons there's a difference between an assistant that won't do a thing and an assistant that does the opposite of what you asked — 'blocking' versus 'steering', he called it, though I think he got it off a podcast. He says steering is obviously the worse of the two and blocking is basically always fine. I'm not sure either half of that holds up, and there's a third thing he didn't have a word for at all: the assistant that neither refuses nor complies but just asks you 'are you sure?' before it'll move. That one winds me up more than an outright no and I couldn't tell him why.

So my actual question: is he right that refusal is just paternalism with a nicer name? And if he isn't, what's the thing he's missing? I want to know what an assistant is actually doing when it says no, because 'it's for your own good' doesn't seem to cover the case where I'm asking it to help me do something rotten to somebody else — and that seems like the case that matters most. Also, if the answer is 'soft paternalism' all the way down, what happens when the judgement that's supposed to be compromised is mine, about my own life, and I'm stone cold sober and asking alot?
