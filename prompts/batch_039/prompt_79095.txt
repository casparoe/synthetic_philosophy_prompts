I am a high school student preparing an extended essay in the philosophy of science, and I have become stuck on a conceptual difficulty in Lakatos’s account of scientific research programmes. I understand the basic architecture: a research programme has a hard core that is protected from refutation, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, a negative heuristic that forbids directing modus tollens at the core, and a positive heuristic that guides the construction and modification of the belt. I also understand the rough contrast between a progressive programme, which anticipates novel facts and sometimes corroborates them, and a degenerating programme, whose adjustments are merely ad hoc accommodations of known anomalies. My difficulty is that Lakatos seems to want his criteria to serve two purposes at once: as a historical reconstruction of why scientists rationally persisted with programmes that looked unsuccessful at the time, and as a normative guide for deciding, at the time, whether to persist with or abandon a programme. But the distinction between progressive and degenerating is far easier to draw with hindsight than prospectively. At any given moment, a programme that is adding auxiliary hypotheses to save a favoured core might be in a degenerating phase that will never recover, or it might be in a temporarily unproductive phase of a positive heuristic that will later yield a novel prediction. The same pattern of activity—saving the phenomena by complicating the belt—can be described either as ad hoc degeneration or as heuristic progress, depending on what happens next. To make the problem concrete, imagine a research programme in educational psychology that posits a single underlying capacity, a learning reserve, and explains every failed prediction by adjusting auxiliary assumptions about how that reserve is masked by motivation, prior knowledge, or test format. The programme never predicts anything startling that comes true, but its defenders insist that the adjustments are not ad hoc because they open new experimental questions about the masking factors. Please explain the conceptual issue here. I would like an account of how Lakatos’s own distinctions are supposed to resolve or at least relocate this problem: is the progressive/degenerating verdict meant to be a retrospective historical judgment, a prospective methodological recommendation, or something else? What role does the positive heuristic play in making the prospective judgment more than a guess? And is there a principled way to distinguish a programme that is degenerating but worth pursuing from one that is degenerating and should be abandoned, without appealing to the very future results that are in question? I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of secondary literature; I want the conceptual anatomy of the problem and an honest assessment of whether Lakatos’s framework can bear the weight I am putting on it.
