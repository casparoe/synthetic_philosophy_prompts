Write a mock arbitral award of about 700 words, in the flat, numbered register of a commercial tribunal, resolving a contract dispute that turns entirely on what one symbol in a pricing schedule means. The buyer reads the plus sign as addition, as everyone does. The seller's accountant, who has never in his working life added numbers above 57, argues that nothing in his past usage settles the matter: every sum he has ever computed is equally consistent with addition and with a divergent function that agrees with addition below 57 and differs above it, and he cannot be shown to have meant one rather than the other. Have the tribunal resolve the dispute along the lines of the communal-practice response to the rule-following paradox — since no fact about anyone's past usage determines the answer, the meaning is whatever the relevant community's practice licenses, and where the parties' practices diverge the tribunal must decide which practice is to count — while insisting throughout that it is merely finding the ordinary meaning of the term. The award should enact that theory rather than announce it: it is binding because everyone in the jurisdiction will go on treating it as binding, and the opinion should remark in passing that its own words will have to be read by the same rules. Include a short dissent arguing that the majority has confused what the parties meant with what the tribunal will make them mean. The jokes should come from the tribunal getting the paradox exactly right and not noticing what that does to the law: a specialist should find the satire fair, and a reader who has never heard of the paradox should still be able to follow the ruling.
