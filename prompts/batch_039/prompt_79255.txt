I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm closing a long feature on the physics and metaphysics of backward time travel. I'd rather end the piece with a poem than with a summary paragraph, and I'd like you to write it for me.

Approach this as a poet who happens to know the time-travel literature cold — someone who understands why the grandfather paradox is usually handled as a constraint on which histories are possible rather than as a flat contradiction, and who can tell apart a fix that keeps a single fixed history internally consistent from one that replaces it with branching histories. But approach it as a craftsman first: this has to work as a poem, not as an argument in verse.

The form should be the argument.

- Fourteen lines, no fixed meter or rhyme, but one hard constraint: the last line must be word-for-word identical to the first, and the poem has to keep making sense when you run past the end and start again. The seam between the last line and the first needs to be a place where the sense keeps going rather than a place where it stops. If the loop is working, a reader who goes around twice should find that the second pass is the first pass.

- Somewhere in the poem the speaker has to try to break the loop — that's the grandfather material — and the poem's own shape should be what quietly refuses her. Don't let her announce the lesson; let the return of the first line do that work.

- She has gone back to stop the first working time machine from being built, and has brought with her, without meaning to, the one thing that makes it possible. I want that object or piece of information to have no origin anywhere in the poem: the poem should describe it completely and still leave it unanswerable where it came from. That's the real subject of the piece for me — not "could you kill your grandfather" but "could something come from nowhere."

- Give the other escape route a line or two at most — the thought that instead of one self-consistent history you get a branching of histories, so that the traveler's arrival makes a new past rather than altering the old one — and then set it aside, because this poem is going to take the first horn. A reader who knows the debate should be able to see that the poem knows about the second option and has chosen against it.

Before you draft, spend a few lines of prose working out where the seam falls, what the un-originated thing is, and what the first line would have to carry in order to be both an opening and a destination. Then write the poem. Then read it through twice as a loop, check that the last line hands off to the first without a stumble, and tell me in a single sentence whether it does and where you are still unsure.

Please skip epigraphs, footnotes, and the names of physicists or philosophers. If a term of art is useful, use it exactly or leave it out. Someone who has never heard of the paradox should still think it's a good poem; someone who has studied it shouldn't wince.
