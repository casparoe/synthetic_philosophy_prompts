I am trying to get clear on a family of arguments in combinatorial game theory that establish that the first player has a winning strategy in a symmetric game without ever exhibiting one, and I would like the full specification before I examine any more of them. Take the setting I have in mind: two players alternate moves in a finite game with perfect information and no chance, the winning condition treats the two players' pieces identically, and the argument usually runs as follows. Suppose the second player had a winning strategy; then the first player could make an arbitrary opening move and thereafter follow that strategy as though she were the second player, choosing some arbitrary move whenever the stolen strategy calls for a move that is no longer available to her, on the thought that an extra piece of her own can only help. Write the desiderata: everything an argument of this kind must deliver to count as having established that the first player wins. I want the requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it — the games it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the results it must not contradict — along with your judgment about which of them are genuine requirements and which are artifacts of the way the argument is customarily told. Flag any pair of them that I should not expect to be jointly satisfiable; that is the part I care about most. In particular, I want to know what such an argument must say about the gap between showing that the second player cannot win and showing that the first player does win, what it must say about the assumption that a spare move is never a liability, and whether a requirement that the argument identify an opening move is compatible with its working uniformly across the whole class of games. Tell me which single requirement does the most to narrow the class of games the argument covers, what becomes of the argument in a game that can end in a draw or in which a player would sometimes rather not move at all, and how the requirements differ for the mirror-image arguments that establish a second-player win. I also want to know what the argument can and cannot be asked to deliver about the strategy itself, given that it is an existence proof and not a construction. I mean to use the list as a checklist for separating real arguments of this kind from hand-waving, so keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them rather than on scoring any particular argument. Set it out as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would explain it in a letter.
