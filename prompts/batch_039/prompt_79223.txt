Next month I am presenting at a small workshop on decision theory and formal epistemology, and I need a question-period prep sheet rather than a rehearsal of my own argument. The thesis I am defending: Pascal's mugging is not a problem about enormous or infinite utilities; it is a problem about evaluating an act that has no place in any state space, and it dissolves once we require that every act be assessed against a partition of states fixed in advance of, and independently of, the act being assessed. On that view the standard remedies — capping utilities, discounting minuscule probabilities, lexicographic or non-Archimedean preferences — are either ad hoc or restatements of the same requirement. My audience is mostly expected-utility theorists working on imprecise probability and bounded rationality, plus two longtermists who treat the mugging as a live practical threat and will want to know what my requirement does to cause prioritization, and one statistician who will ask what it implies for ordinary model selection. Tell me the objections this audience will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary, ordered by how likely and how damaging each one is; for each, the reply I can give in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Take a definite position throughout: rank the objections, say which ones I should concede rather than contest, and do not hedge by laying out options for me to choose between.
