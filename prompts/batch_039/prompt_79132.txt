I'm a philosophy graduate student putting together a seminar paper on the temporal structure of consent — the fact that consent is always given by a self at one moment and is supposed to bind, or fail to bind, a self at a later one — and I've decided to make Michel Gondry's Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind (Kaufman's screenplay) my central case, partly because the usual medical-ethics examples (surgical consent forms, advance directives, research waivers) have been worked to death in the literature and partly because the film seems to me to put a much harder case on the table. I'd like you to read it through the question of what makes consent valid. The standard triad of deception, coercion and competence should be in play and treated seriously, but I don't want the answer to stop there. My hunch is that the film's real subject is not whether Joel's consent was free — he signs Lacuna's paperwork while grieving and barely attending to it — but what it means for a consent to be given by a self that will not survive to be bound by it. The procedure is, in effect, an inverted Ulysses contract: ordinary self-binding ties a future self to a decision it will later regret, whereas Lacuna ties the future self to a forgetting it cannot even regret, because the grounds for regret have been removed. It is also one-directional, since the clinic takes only the consent of the person being erased; hence Clementine can have Joel removed from her memory without his knowledge, and hence Patrick can court her afterwards with endearments and mementos he lifted from Joel's memories while he was helping to delete them. Joel's attempt, inside the procedure, to hide Clementine in memories where she does not belong, and the fact that the machinery cannot hear this change of mind because the consent is already on file and the technicians have a schedule, strike me as the film's central consent scene.

Please give me a reading rather than a summary, and one that is faithful in both directions: tell me which scenes the standard theory genuinely illuminates (is Mary's erased affair with the doctor a coercion case, a deception case, or a case in which a wrong is converted into a non-event, so that the person wronged is left with no standing to complain? does Patrick's courtship vitiate whatever consent Clementine gives him, and if so, is the defect in the consent or in the knowledge on which it rests?), where the film resists the theory, and where I may be forcing the film to say something it doesn't. I especially want a position on whether the Joel who changes his mind in the dream is the same agent who signed the form — if he is not, then the mid-procedure revocation is not a revocation at all and the clinic has done nothing wrong, a conclusion I find intolerable and cannot yet argue against — and on whether the ending is a repair or a fresh instance of the same problem: the two of them listen to the recording of their own earlier selves and choose each other with full knowledge, but the selves doing the choosing are precisely the selves who will do it again. No citations or literature survey needed; I want the argument. And please don't let this collapse into the familiar memory-and-personal-identity reading — I know that reading, and my whole point is that the identity question is downstream of the consent question rather than the other way round. Finish by saying what the film contributes to the debate that a paper on advance directives cannot.
