I keep teaching the Hinckley verdict and every year the same thing happens -- the students are furious, and the fury is the philosophically interesting part, because it's the fury of people who think responsibility is the default and insanity is the exception. So: run that acquittal and the public reaction to it through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. You do what you do because of the way you are; to be truly responsible for it you'd have to be responsible for being that way, which takes a prior act of self-making, which takes a prior way of being, and so on back -- nobody is ultimately responsible for anything. Do the mapping properly. Is the insanity verdict the law's one concession to the regress, and if so why does it stop where it stops? What does Strawson say about Hinckley versus the perfectly ordinary defendant, given that on his view there's no difference between them? Then bring in actio libera in causa, the doctrine that you're answerable for what you do in a state you voluntarily entered (you got yourself drunk, so the drunk act is yours), and tell me whether that actually terminates the regress or just walks it back a step -- I think the latter, and I think its also Strawson's reply to anyone who wants to save responsibility by pointing at earlier voluntary choices. And the harder bit: the reform that followed the acquittal put the burden of proof on the defense and narrowed the test, which is exactly what the outrage wanted. If Strawson is right, what is that reform doing -- an institution refusing to follow an argument it can't answer? And where does the Basic Argument break on this episode: does Hinckley show it proves too much, or does the reaction show it's right about our practice and wrong about our theory? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning -- no preamble, don't restate the question.
