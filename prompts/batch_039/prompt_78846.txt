You are a philosopher of art and an epistemologist of testimony, and I want a careful audit of a comparison that keeps doing real argumentative work in discussions of aesthetic testimony. I want the audit, not a tour of the literature: no reading lists, no citations, just your own reasoning. Please begin with a short scratch section in which you brainstorm the correspondence and the candidate failure points before you commit to a verdict.

The comparison is between aesthetic testimony and testimony about how something tastes. A colleague of mine insists that hearing from a trusted critic that a film is a masterpiece is like hearing from a reliable oenophile that a wine is delicious. The report tells you something about the speaker's experience, and perhaps something about the wine, but the deliciousness itself is not something you can receive at second hand; you have to drink it. On the strength of that, he draws a strong conclusion: you cannot come to know or be justified in believing that a work is beautiful on someone else's say-so, and an aesthetic belief formed that way is defective as a judgment, since you are deferring where you ought to be judging for yourself.

What I want from you:

1. A correspondence table: what plays the role of the wine, the taster, the taste, the report, the drinker, the drinking. Then say which of those roles has no clear counterpart in the aesthetic case.

2. The disanalogies, sorted. Some are local (artworks are made, interpreted, and embedded in traditions, and we argue about them; wine is drunk and that is that), and some are structural: whether the object of aesthetic judgment is a private hedonic reaction; whether a judgment of taste is the kind of thing that claims the agreement of others rather than merely reporting how the speaker is affected; whether appreciation is given or acquired, which is precisely what critics are supposed to supply. Separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's conclusion from the ones that are colourful but idle.

3. What the source case quietly imports. The analogy looks innocent because everyone agrees that you cannot taste the wine by proxy. Ask what has to be true of aesthetic value for the analogy to do its work, and whether those things are being assumed rather than shown, in particular whether the comparison presupposes the very acquaintance requirement it is being used to establish.

4. The verdict on the conclusion. Does it survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If only something weaker survives, state the weaker thesis precisely, and say what would have to be added to get the strong one back.

5. Whether a better comparison is available, for instance a critic as a guide or teacher who can make you see what you would otherwise have missed, and whether that one is more than a debating point in the other direction.

Be explicit throughout about which steps depend on empirical claims about how criticism actually affects appreciation and which are purely conceptual; where you need an empirical premise, flag it rather than assert it. Aim for a piece of analysis a philosopher could argue with, not a balanced summary.
