I am a novelist at work on a book about a woman who undergoes a procedure that replaces her neurons one at a time with prosthetic units that reproduce the input–output behaviour of the tissue they displace. The process takes months, she is awake and living her ordinary life throughout, and at the end nothing observable about her has changed. One faction in the book holds that she died somewhere along the way and that what remains is a copy; another holds that she is plainly the same person, since there was no moment at which anyone died. I want to write both factions with equal sympathy, so I am asking for an explanation of the underlying conceptual issue rather than for a verdict, and I would rather have the argument and its weak points laid out than a reading list.

1. Set out what the gradual-replacement argument is meant to establish about mind uploading, and why the gradualness is supposed to do the work — why replacing the whole brain in one stroke is thought to be a different matter from replacing it neuron by neuron, when the end state is the same.

2. I suspect the argument has the shape of a sorites, or of a tolerance principle applied to a vague predicate. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and what the argument must then say about the threshold: whether there is a first neuron whose replacement kills the original, and if there is none, what follows.

3. The objection that worries me most is the one that turns the argument against itself. Ordinary brains already replace their matter continuously — molecules, and over years cells — so if gradual material replacement were fatal to identity, none of us would survive a decade. Does the uploading argument therefore prove too much, namely that identity never rested on matter at all? And if so, why should the instantaneous copy-and-destroy be any less identity-preserving than the slow version? Lay out what each side says in reply.

4. Give me the view that there is no determinate fact about where in the sequence the original gives way to a successor — that the question has no answer rather than a hidden one — and the related move that identity is not what matters and that some relation such as psychological continuity is. Do these dissolve the puzzle or merely rename it? Do they commit their holder to saying the two factions are, in some sense, both right?

5. The duplication problem my plot keeps colliding with: suppose the gradual procedure preserves her, and suppose also that a complete record of her pre-procedure brain preserves her when instantiated afresh in new hardware. What do we say when the slowly replaced woman and the freshly instantiated one exist at the same time?

6. Two wrinkles of my own, which I would like taken seriously as tests rather than as decoration. First, in my version she is under continuous anaesthesia for the whole procedure: she experiences nothing from the first swap to the last. If the no-moment-of-death intuition depends on her being continuously there, does the argument survive the removal of continuity of experience? Second, she undergoes the procedure in yearly stages, living normally in between; is the staged version different in kind from the unbroken one, or only in pacing?

7. Throughout, be explicit about which distinctions the argument actually needs — material continuity, causal or functional continuity, continuity of experience — and say where an answer would change if one of those distinctions were drawn differently. The more an answer turns on a distinction, the more I want that distinction named and shown at work.
