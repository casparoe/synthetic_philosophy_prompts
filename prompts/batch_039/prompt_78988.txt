You are a philosopher of language and metaphysics — at home in the semantics of quantification, in the methodology of ontology, and in the craft of drafting summaries of live disputes that partisans on both sides will accept as fair. Two colleagues of mine have argued for two years about whether ontology has a subject matter beyond language, and they have now agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration. I have been asked to produce the joint statement they would both sign. Write it.

The two positions, as each party states them:

The deflationist (call her D). When she says "there are tables" and her colleague says "strictly speaking there are no tables, only particles arranged table-wise," they are not contradicting each other. "There are" can be given different meanings; under one meaning her sentence is true, under another his is. Neither meaning is the meaning. Once all the non-linguistic facts are fixed, a meaning for the quantifier can be found on which any disputed existence sentence comes out true, and another on which it comes out false; so the dispute is verbal, and what remains — which vocabulary to use, what we gain and lose by using it — are practical questions about our purposes rather than questions about what there is.

The realist (call him R). There is a fact of the matter about what there is, and it is not created by the meanings we assign. If two speakers' sentences can each be made true by a different assignment of meaning, then at most one of the assignments matches how things are; the other is a language we could speak but which misdescribes the world. Moreover, D's own claim — that there are many candidate meanings of "exists" — is itself made with a quantifier; if no meaning of the quantifier is privileged, that claim is not so much as true or false. And the notion of one vocabulary tracking reality better than another is not idle: it is supposed to explain why some vocabularies let us state laws that are not endless.

Two concrete cases, whose shared facts both parties accept and neither may revise. (i) A stand of trees in which thousands of genetically identical trunks rise from a single connected root system that has spread for millennia, with no further fact about any "boundary" between them beyond what the connectivity and the genetics supply: the dispute is whether there is one organism there or many. (ii) A committee: both agree that it has a written charter, that its decisions are attributed to it rather than to any member, that only its members speak for it, and that whether it can be sued is a matter of local law. The dispute is whether the committee exists over and above its members and its rules.

Produce the document, written in the first person plural and signed by both parties, with these parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list, each a claim both would sign, made as strong as possible — no platitudes, nothing true by definition. Include what is not in dispute about the semantics of quantifiers; what each concedes about the other's position; how they jointly read the shared facts in cases (i) and (ii); and what each accepts would count as a reason against her or his own view.

2. Disagreements. A table with columns: sub-question | D's answer, in wording she would accept | R's answer, in wording he would accept | what turns on it. It must cover at least: whether the facts plus a choice of meaning settle the truth of "there are composite objects"; whether one language can be better than another in any respect beyond convenience; whether D's own thesis presupposes a privileged quantifier; and whether "is this dispute verbal?" is answerable at all.

3. Cruxes. For each: the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party; a test, case, or argument both parties agree bears on it; and each party's prediction, recorded in advance. At least one crux must concern the translation test — whether a faithful manual can be given mapping each party's existence talk into the other's without changing which facts are being reported — and at least one must concern whether R's "tracks reality better" does explanatory work that "simple and useful" does not.

4. Verbal or genuine? State plainly which parts of the residual disagreement are verbal and which are genuine, and what the genuine remainder is. If you judge that D and R would themselves disagree about whether some part is verbal, add that disagreement to the table.

Constraints: write both positions in your own words; include no citations, reading lists, or names of famous proponents, and let nothing depend on an author's authority. Everything the document needs must be inside the document.

Method: before drafting, brainstorm privately — the strongest version of each side, several candidate formulations of each agreed claim and each crux, and the spots where a formulation is slanted or vacuous. Test every agreed claim by asking whether a clever partisan could sign it in bad faith; strengthen it or drop it if so. Then write the document, favouring precision over completeness.
