I teach a course on philosophy of religion and we just wrapped the omnipotence paradox unit, and I keep being struck by how much of the literature goes into the stone-so-heavy thing and how little goes into the fact that actual institutions seem to have settled the paradox in practice without ever arguing about it. The case I've been chewing on: the Vatican's procedure for certifying a miracle for canonization. As I understand it — happy to be told the fine print is different, just assume the rough shape is right — a claimed cure gets put forward, a panel of doctors goes through the medical records, and the question they're asked is whether the recovery can be explained by ordinary medicine. If they say it can't, and if the cure was sudden, complete and lasting, and it came after people had been praying to the candidate, then it can count as a miracle and be credited to that candidate's intercession. What gets me is that the whole apparatus is built to detect an intervention: it needs a stable, law-governed nature as the backdrop, and it treats "medicine has no explanation for this" as the fingerprint of God. And notice the paradox just silently falls out the back of it: nobody ever proposes a logically incoherent cure — a lesion that was and wasn't a lesion, a cure that both happened and didn't — the tests are all for surprising but perfectly describable events. So the procedure seems to be running on one particular resolution of the paradox (omnipotence ranges over possible deeds, not over nonsense, so "can God make a contradiction" isn't a task but a phrase with no content) while never once arguing for it, and quietly ruling out the voluntarist picture where power is sheer will unconstrained even by describability.

So: I want the implicit-theology writeup. What view of divine power does this certification practice actually presuppose, and which rival pictures of omnipotence does it quietly rule out? Which ones could license it just as well — because my suspicion is that it's alot more underdetermined than it looks, and that a Thomist, a Molinist and something closer to occasionalism could all sign off on the same paperwork. And the part I'm really after: is the practice internally consistent? The same institution's official theology says God is the primary cause of everything that happens, that natural causes are themselves sustained by God at every moment — and if that's right, there is no such thing as a "medicine cant explain this" zone, the gap isn't a gap, and the fingerprint is on everything. The procedure needs the gap to be real, and it needs God's power to show up precisely as the exception. Is that a straight contradiction, or can the practice get by on something weaker — say that these cures are signs rather than interventions, or that the intervention talk is just the way a tribunal has to talk? And then take the last step: if you decided the practice had to be rebuilt around one of the rival views, what would actually change on the ground — could you still use the doctors, could you still credit the intercession, or does it collapse into "we find it striking that this happened"?

Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'll do the references myself; I want the reconstruction and the argument. And if the honest answer is that the practice doesn't presuppose any single view of divine power, then say so and show me which parts of it are doing which work. One more thing — please don't lean on whatever you happen to remember about particular saints or particular cures, keep it at the level of the procedure itself.
