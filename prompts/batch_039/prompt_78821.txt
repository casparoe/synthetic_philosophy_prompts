I teach a seminar on experimental philosophy, and this year I also got drafted onto our college's academic-integrity board, and the two things have started bleeding into each other in a way that is honestly making me a little unhinged. The relevant background, briefly: I've been rereading the work on the Knobe effect and everything it spawned — the chairman case, where a guy who announces he doesn't care about the environment gets asked whether he brought about an environmental side effect on purpose, and people say yes when the side effect is bad and no when the identical side effect is good — plus all the follow-ups showing the same moral-valence flip turning up in causal judgments, knowledge ascriptions, and a pile of other places, and the long-running fight over whether this is a performance error or a sign that the folk concept of intentional action is itself moralized. What I want from you is not a lit review. I want the most important implications of that whole research program for how we actually run these hearings.

Concretely, here's our procedure, which I've come to suspect is a Knobe experiment we run on ourselves every month. A student uses a chatbot to produce an essay; we see the essay and the grade first, then the student's file, then finally we get to the question our rulebook actually turns on, which is whether the misuse was intentional — that finding is what unlocks the harshest sanction, while a finding of neglegence caps it. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: we read intent backwards off how bad the case looked, and the moral-valence literature predicts exactly this. My grad student wants to test it by running a variant where the AI-assisted paper is actually better than what the student would have written alone and the student disclosed it herself — same act, good or at least neutral upshot — and before she burns a semester on it I'd like help seeing what hangs on the answer.

So: what are the most important implications? I care about things like whether the order in which we see evidence should be fixed by rule, whether a hearing can be debiased at all or whether the fix has to be structural (seperate the intent finding from the sanctioning decision, blind reading, rewrite the rulebook to drop intentional in favor of something closer to knowledge or recklessness), whether the effect is the same size in expert fact-finders or mostly a folk phenomenon, and whether the same mechanism predicts that our outcomes will track how sympathetic the student looks. Please be explicit about the fork: if the effect is a bias, the implications go one way; if it reflects something real about how intentional action is ordinarily understood, then maybe the rulebook is the artifact and the folk are fine. Tell me which implications are robust either way, which are hostage to how the vignettes get built, and where the literature is genuinely divided rather than settled. And rank them — if I can only push one change through the board this year, I want to know which.
