In two weeks I am to give the welcome speech at my sister's house for my new niece: a small secular naming ceremony in the garden, forty people, almost all of them family. I have been asked to do it because I teach bioethics and because her parents wanted something more than a raised glass. I have been putting it off for a month.

The complication is that my niece was chosen. Her parents had three embryos screened, and for each one the clinic supplied a figure for the life that embryo was expected to have; they chose the highest. I helped them think it through, so I cannot pretend to stand outside it. One grandmother used the phrase playing God at Easter and meant it. Two of the guests are geneticists for whom the whole procedure is no more remarkable than choosing a paint colour. The parents have asked me not to let the selection define their daughter.

What I cannot resolve, and what I am hoping the speech will resolve for me, is this. A principle known as procreative beneficence holds that parents ought to select, among the children they could have, the one expected to have the best life — that this is a requirement rather than a liberty. If it is right, the ordinary language of welcome is compromised: telling this child that she would have been loved however she came is either false or an admission that her parents were prepared to do wrong. But the other register is worse. Telling her that she was chosen because her life promised best is to hand a newborn a ranking of lives, and to say something about the people who are living with the conditions that were screened against — even though no one was made worse off by not being born. I also distrust the clinic's figure, since the life an embryo is expected to have has to be expected relative to some comparison class, and nobody could tell me which one.

Write the speech. Five minutes, no jargon, but do not flatten it: there will be a philosopher, a paediatrician, and both grandmothers in the room. The philosophical difficulty should shape what the speech can and cannot say, rather than being mentioned and then moved past. If a welcome speech is the wrong instrument for this — if anything honest would wound someone who is listening — say so plainly rather than writing something smooth; and if I have the philosophy wrong, correct me instead of accommodating me. End with a single line beginning Verdict: stating in one sentence what the speech ultimately commits me to.
