I'm in a fortnightly reading group that has spent the past two months on the metaphysics of time, and last week we reached backwards time travel and the grandfather paradox. One of the members — a competent philosopher, not a crank — ended the discussion by saying, flatly, that you can't change the past, as though that settled the matter: a traveller who killed his own grandfather would be changing the past, changing the past is impossible, so backwards time travel is impossible. I don't think that's right, but I also couldn't say which of the things he might have meant I actually disagree with, and I'd rather have the disambiguation than a verdict on time travel. So treat 'you can't change the past' as a slogan whose precise content is up for grabs and lay out the distinct claims it could be making, ordered from the ones that are true by definition and cost nothing, through the substantive metaphysical theses, to the ones that are simply false. At a minimum I want the menu to separate: (a) the reading on which it is a trivial consequence of what the words mean, a 'changed past' being a contradiction in terms; (b) the causal reading, that nothing later can be a cause of anything earlier; (c) the modal reading, that past events are fixed in the sense that no later intervention could make them other than they in fact were; (d) the self-consistency constraint that some physicists have floated, on which any journey into the past must already be part of the single history that obtains, so that the would-be grandfather-killer's gun jams or he misses; and (e) the epistemic reading, that even if the past were altered we could never tell, because our records would alter along with it. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and — the part I care most about — which of them my friend's argument actually needs. If it needs a reading on which the slogan is false, or needs a reading so trivial that it can't do the work, say so plainly rather than softening it.

I'd also like you to test a distinction I've been trying out: between changing the past, understood as making it have been other than it was, and merely affecting the past, understood as being a cause of something earlier, which the past then always contained. Is that a real distinction, does it rescue any of the readings, and does it leave anything of the grandfather paradox intact, or does it dissolve the puzzle by fiat?
