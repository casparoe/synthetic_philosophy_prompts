I'm a third-year student in a course on decision theory and the ethics of the long term, and next week I'm leading a sixty-minute discussion section for about fifteen students — one prior philosophy course each, comfortable with expected value and not much beyond it. My assigned topic is whether the simulation argument dampens the case for fanaticism about the far future, and I don't want you to lay the argument out for me. I want a plan for teaching it.

Here is the shape of the question as I'll have to present it. The charge of fanaticism, as it's usually pressed, is that a theory which tells us to trade near-certain modest goods — a life saved now, a stable century — for a minuscule chance of an astronomically large far-future payoff is fanatical, however the arithmetic comes out. The simulation argument is the familiar disjunction: either almost no civilization survives to the point of being able to run ancestor simulations, or almost none of those that do choose to run them, or we are almost certainly in one. The suggestion I'm meant to teach is that a substantial probability of being simulated blunts the fanatic's case, because the far future we would be purchasing with that minuscule chance is not straightforwardly ours to spend — the simulators may terminate it, may be running countless instances of us, and may be the ones whose future actually carries the value. I suspect the suggestion is at best half right, but I would rather have your plan than my own opinion.

So: what has to be in place before the students can even hear the question, what depends on what, and why that order rather than the obvious one; the single case that should carry the whole session, what it must have and what it must not have, and whether a mugging-style case — a stranger promising an astronomical reward against a vanishingly small chance that he can deliver it — is a mistake here, since I suspect it teaches suspicion of large numbers rather than the structure of the nesting, though I can't tell whether that's a genuine constraint or merely my taste; the misconceptions they'll bring and the ones they'll form, in the order they'll surface, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each off — I expect "fanaticism means caring too much about the future" and "the simulation argument is a skeptical claim about whether reality exists, so it cannot bear on a decision"; what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so; and the one question I should put to them at the close to see whether the structure landed rather than just the vocabulary.

Give me your verdict too, not a survey of positions: say whether the simulation argument does dampen far-future fanaticism, with your reasons, and let the session's arc commit to that answer rather than hedging around it.
