I'm a retired atmospheric chemist — thirty-odd years on aerosol and radiative-forcing models, now happily unemployable — and since I stopped going to the office I've been teaching myself epistemology and decision theory out of textbooks, which is going about as well as you'd expect. A thing keeps coming up that I can't get straight, and it's Aumann's agreement theorem. My nephew, who reads the rationalist blogs, keeps telling me it proves that two people who are equally well informed and equally rational simply can't disagree, and that therefore whenever I think a competent colleague is wrong about something, one of us has screwed up. I can follow the proof well enough — same prior, common knowledge of the posteriors, and the posteriors have to be equal. But it seems to me that the whole interesting content is buried in the assumptions, and I want someone to tell me whether I'm right about that or whether I'm just too rusty on measure theory to see that the thing has teeth. Two specific things bother me. First, my impression is that common knowledge is doing all the heavy lifting: if you only assume that each knows the other's opinion, and each knows that the other knows his, and so on for some fixed finite number of levels and then you stop, the theorem just fails — you can cook up two agents with the same prior who keep disagreeing forever, quite rationally, even after they've announced their posteriors to each other. Is that actually right, and if so doesn't it mean that the theorem only bites in cases where the disagreement has been aired to an infinite depth, which never happens between two humans with finite patience? Second, and this is the one that really bugs me, the setup seems to require that both agents are carving up the same space of possible states of the world, so that what they disagree about is a well-defined event in that shared space with a number attached to it. But my actual disagreements with colleagues were almost never like that. We didn't disagree about the probability of an event in a shared space; we disagreed about which variables belonged in the model, about what counted as a possibility at all, about whether the question was even well posed. And I have a suspicion that that kind of disagreement isn't merely hard for the framework, it's invisible to it — there's no common event for the two posteriors to be posteriors *of*, so the theorem says nothing, and the conciliationist literature I've dipped into seems to slide right over this.

So what I'd like from you is an essay that actually decides something. Take the slogan that rational people who share their evidence can't agree to disagree, and tell me whether it's a genuine constraint on how a reasonable person should react to a competent dissenter — i.e., whether the fact that a well-informed peer disagrees should by itself move me a lot, or a little, or not at all — or whether it's a bit of idealization that does no work outside the toy cases and shouldn't be cited at anyone in an argument. Give me the strongest case on both sides: the conciliationist who says my disagreement with an equally competent peer is itself evidence that I've slipped somewhere, and the defender of the theorem who says it's a conditional and all the real action is in the antecedent. Then commit. I want a verdict, not a survey with a shrug at the end, and I especially want to know which of the assumptions you think is doing the real work — the common prior, the common knowledge, the shared state space — and whether the framework can be patched to handle disagreements about the model rather than disagreements inside it, or whether that's asking for something the formalism can't give by construction. (If there are ideas out there that bear on this, just describe them in plain terms; I'm not asking for a reading list, I don't have library access and I'd rather have the argument than the references.) And tell me honestly how confident you are in the verdict you reach, and name the single step where you're least sure of yourself — I'd rather have the soft spot flagged than papered over.
