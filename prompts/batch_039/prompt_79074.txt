You're a philosopher of science who knows the realism/instrumentalism literature and the philosophy of modelling cold. Think carefully before you answer. Start by brainstorming the four or five nearest concepts before you commit to any of them, and please don't just hand me the closest -ism and stop -- the carving is the whole point.

Context: I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science, so I live in this stuff, but from the modelling side rather than the philosophy side.

Here's the thing I can't name. In my field people fight constantly about whether some posited entity is *real*, and the fight has a shape I've started to recognise. Two people agree on every number, every fit statistic, every out-of-sample prediction -- and still disagree about whether the entity exists. Press the one who says yes and the justification is always the same: it earns its keep, it compresses the data, it predicts better than the alternative. Press the one who says no and the justification is also always the same: the entity is *defined* by the model, so of course the model recovers it; you've told me the model is internally consistent, not that anything is out there. And then that's it. Nobody produces a fact that would move the other side. The argument ends not because it's been settled but because both parties have run out of things to say. It's not that they misunderstand each other either -- they understand each other fine.

Two instances. (1) In my own modelling work I have a latent variable that does genuine explanatory work, and a reviewer asked me to justify that it isn't just a summary of the data. I couldn't tell whether that was a request for evidence or a request for a different kind of sentence. (2) There's a long-running fight in my subfield about whether you can read a system's representation of something off a linear probe: one camp says the probe shows the representation is there, the other says the probe is doing the work and all you've shown is decodability. Same shape, and again nobody's data is in dispute.

And the part I actually care about is that the same person flips. In the intro of a paper the construct is a cause -- it drives the effect -- and in the discussion it's a convenient label for a heterogeneous family of processes. Nothing happened in between. The realism seems indexed to which section of the paper you're in, or to whether you're attacking someone else's construct or defending your own. I don't think it's just dishonesty or confusion; I think something structural is going on and I don't have the vocabulary for it.

I know the neighbourhood: realism vs instrumentalism, the idea that a pattern can be real because it supports compression, the idea that you can take different stances toward the same system (physical, design, whatever) and they aren't rivals, deflationism about these questions, and the merely-verbal-dispute stuff. My problem is that none of them quite covers the specific combination I keep seeing: (a) a dispute guaranteed to survive complete agreement on all observations, (b) a criterion of reality -- predictive success -- that anything inside a working model satisfies automatically, and (c) one person sliding between the two positions according to what they need the construct to do in that paragraph.

So: is there a name for this? If there is, tell me which of those three parts it covers and which it leaves over. If there isn't, coin one, define it, and give me the boundary cases -- what it is not, and where it shades into ordinary inconsistency on one side or a genuinely defensible pluralism on the other. And tell me what would count as resolving such a dispute, if anything would.
