I am a philosophy professor preparing a proposal for a small internal grant that would support one semester of work and a workshop. I have a half-formed idea about Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. Discussions of that essay usually treat monumental, antiquarian, and critical history as three genres or temperaments and then ask which of them best serves life. I am less interested in ranking them than in a tension internal to critical history. Critical history is supposed to serve life by putting the past on trial and breaking the grip of a past that has become a burden. Yet the act of judging the past seems to require a standpoint outside the very historical conditions being judged, and it may also destroy the horizon that, on Nietzsche's own account, life needs in order to flourish. My tentative hypothesis is that the tension can be resolved only by distinguishing two functions that are ordinarily run together: a therapeutic function, in which critical history loosens the hold of a dead past and returns the agent to action, and a juridical function, in which critical history assigns guilt, blame, or debt. I want to argue that the therapeutic function can serve life while the juridical function tends to undermine it, and that this distinction illuminates why contemporary critical uses of history, for example in disputes over monuments, reparations, or curriculum reform, so often slide into a self-defeating moralism. I also suspect that the three modes are better understood not as three genres but as three responses to different pathologies of historical consciousness: too much memory, too little, and memory of the wrong kind. I want to use Nietzsche's notions of plastic power and horizon as the operative ideas, but without turning the proposal into a commentary.
