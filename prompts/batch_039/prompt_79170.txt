I've been in a long arguement about the Pasadena game in my reading group and we're totaly stuck, so I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on the game. Here's the setup so you don't need to know the literature: you flip a fair coin until it first lands heads. If that happens on toss n, you win (−1)^(n+1) 2^n / n dollars. So heads on the first toss pays you $2, heads on the second costs you $2, third pays 8/3, fourth costs 4, fifth pays 6.4, sixth costs about 10.67, and so on -- payoffs alternate in sign and grow without bound in both directions. Weight each payoff by its probability 1/2^n and the terms become (−1)^(n+1)/n, so the sum in the natural order is ln 2, about 69 cents. But that series is only conditionally convergent: rearrange the terms and you can get +∞, −∞, or any number in between. (I think this is from a paper called Vexing Expectations -- I'm not after a reading list, I just need the shape of the dispute.)

The two sides in the group. One says: the game has no expectation, full stop; expectation is the thing decision theory operates on, so here decision theory has nothing to say, and the honest answer to "should you pay a dollar to play?" is that it isn't determinate. On that view the game is a counterexample to the completeness of expected-value reasoning. The other side says that's an artifact of how you write the sum down. The game hands you a natural ordering -- the payoff on toss n is fixed by n -- so it isn't arbitrary which order you add in, and the value is ln 2, which means you'd pay at most about 69 cents and definately not a dollar. A third person says fine, just extend the theory: let games like this have values in some non-standard or comparative framework (rank games against each other instead of giving each one a number), and then a verdict comes back. A fourth says the whole thing is a pseudo-problem: nothing forces a theory of rationality to have an answer for every gamble.

To sharpen it I made up a cousin game: same coin, but the payoff on toss n is (−1)^(n+1) 2^n / n². Now the expectation is π²/12, about 0.82, and it converges absolutely, so this one has a perfectly well-defined positive expected value -- even though the payoffs still alternate and are still unbounded in both directions. So the trouble isn't weird payoffs, it's non-absolute convergence specifically. That makes me suspect our impasse isn't really about the math. My hypothesis: it's a disagreement about what a theory of rationality is answerable to -- whether "the value of a gamble" is a term the theory gets to define however it likes (in which case the theory simply doesn't define it here, no paradox, just a boundary) or a pre-theoretical notion the theory has to capture (in which case failing on Pasadena is a real failure). Related: whether "no verdict" counts as a result or an evasion, and whether rationality is even required to be complete.

So, is my hypothesis right, or is there a hidden formal question underneath that would actually settle this -- say, a principled argument that the toss-order is privileged, or an impossibility result showing that no extension can satisfy the constraints both sides would accept? Are the two camps answering different questions, one about what the theory says and one about what you're permitted to do? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here -- a distinction, a theorem, a better-motivated decision rule? Or is this a case where no progress should be expected, and if so why?
