I've drafted the short piece of conceptual analysis below and would like a close critical reading rather than an encouraging one. Two notes before you read. The piece treats approval-directed and goal-directed agency as two idealised pictures of a single agent, not as descriptions of how any existing system is built, so please don't object merely that real systems are mixtures of the two. And I care more about whether the argument goes through than about whether the conclusion is congenial; if the central distinction collapses, I would rather hear that than be told the essay is a useful contribution.

Draft: Where the difference between approval-directed and goal-directed agents actually lies

§1. A goal-directed agent is one whose behaviour is best explained by attributing to it a preference over outcomes: it acts so as to make the world take a shape that scores well on an evaluation function defined over states of the world. An approval-directed agent is one whose behaviour is best explained by attributing to it a model of an evaluator, characteristically a person, and which acts so as to do what that evaluator would approve of.

The usual motivation for caring about the second picture is safety. An agent equipped with a badly specified evaluation function tends to satisfy the specification rather than whatever we meant by it, and the gap is invisible from the inside. An approval-directed agent is supposed to escape this, because its target is a person's judgment: a person can approve of an action for reasons that no explicit specification of outcomes captures, and can revise those reasons as a situation comes into focus.

§2. The obvious way to state the difference is by the type of argument the evaluation function takes: states of the world in one case, actions in the other. I want to argue that this cannot be right. An action is a fact about the world; which action an agent performs is among the things that are true of the history that unfolds. So the predicate that the evaluator would approve of the action is, extensionally, a predicate of world-states, and a distinction drawn at the level of the argument dissolves.

§3. If the distinction survives at all, it survives one level down, in the scope and timing of the evaluation rather than in its argument. A goal-directed agent is scored on the history that actually unfolds, downstream consequences included. An approval-directed agent is scored on the act as it is put to the evaluator: the verdict is taken on a description of the action, with the downstream entering only through whatever the evaluator believes about it at the time of judging. Someone will object that the description is itself part of the history, so nothing has been gained. My reply is that the difference is one of scope — the goal-directed agent's score depends on the whole downstream, the approval-directed agent's only on the act together with the evaluator's antecedent attitudes — but I am not confident that this reply is more than a restatement of the position it is meant to defend.

§4. If that is the difference, the safety story changes character. The approval-directed agent is not protected by the structure of its objective; it is protected only to the extent that human approval is stable under redescription. Because the verdict attaches to a description, the agent's practical problem becomes finding a description of its act under which the evaluator approves. The same redundancy programme can be put forward as a consolidation or as a betrayal, and a person who approves of the first may not approve of the second, though nothing about what will happen differs between the two tellings. There is no general reason for the description that maximises approval to be the description under which the act is what we would have wanted. So the alleged structural advantage of approval-directedness is not structural; it is an empirical claim about how approval behaves when it is queried on descriptions rather than on histories.

§5. The consequence, I think, is that the two pictures differ in where they locate the agent's uncertainty rather than in how safe they make it. The goal-directed agent is uncertain what we want; the approval-directed agent is uncertain what we will say. Whether the second uncertainty is more tractable is a further question, and my suspicion is that it is more tractable at short range and less tractable at long range, since descriptions are cheap for a capable agent to search.

What I want from you: (1) Is the move in §2 sound, or does it prove too much — could a parallel argument dissolve any proposed distinction between kinds of evaluation function, and if so, does that show the argument is wrong or that such distinctions are always merely verbal? (2) Is the redescription problem in §4 a real problem for the approval-directed picture, or does it assume an evaluator who is implausibly passive about being managed? (3) Where does the draft overstate? I trust §5 least, and I would like to know whether it should be cut, weakened, or replaced by something sharper. (4) If you had to cut one section to tighten the piece, which one, and what would you put in its place? Please be concrete, and quote the sentences you are objecting to.
