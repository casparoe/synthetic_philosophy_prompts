Two friends and I have been arguing for weeks about the toppling of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol, and we have reached the point where swapping intuitions is useless and what we need is a framework applied honestly to the case. The facts, briefly, so that we are at least arguing about the same thing: the bronze went up in 1895, on a plinth bearing a plaque that presented Colston as among the most virtuous and wise sons of the city; a 2018 proposal to add a second plaque saying plainly where his money had come from stalled over the wording; on 7 June 2020, during the protests that followed the killing of George Floyd, demonstrators pulled the statue down, defaced it, and rolled it into Bristol Harbour; the council recovered it a few days later and put it in storage; it was subsequently displayed in a museum, still graffitied and still lying on its back; four of the people who helped pull it down were tried for criminal damage and acquitted by a jury in January 2022; and the empty plinth stood in place for years afterwards, until a replacement plaque was finally installed on it in 2025.

I have been trying to build a framework for cases like this one, and I would like you to run the episode through it and then tell me what the episode does to the framework. The framework has four claims. First, an act/object distinction: a monument is not a fixed inscription but a standing claim, so that a generation which leaves it up renews it — on this view there is no neutral option of mere persistence, only re-dedication or removal. Second, a distinction among three modes of display: honorific (the object is shown as worthy), documentary (shown as evidence of a past attitude), and admonitory (shown as a warning), with the museum as the device that is supposed to convert the first into one of the others. Third, a question of standing: who is entitled to alter a public commemorative settlement — present residents, the descendants of those wronged, the donors' heirs, the council, or the crowd? Fourth, a distinction between iconoclasm from below and official removal, which may produce the same outcome on quite different grounds.

Take the framework seriously enough to say where it bites. Does the first claim really hold, or is leaving a statue up merely tolerating rather than affirming it — and if so, does the framework's strongest claim collapse? Does the museum conversion actually defuse the honorific force, or is musealisation a way of having it both ways, and did the graffitied, reclining display settle that question or keep it open? What does the criminal trial do to the standing question, given that twelve jurors rather than theorists answered it? Test the framework on one contrast case of your choosing, where removal ran through official channels rather than through a crowd — the Rhodes statue at the University of Cape Town, or a Confederate monument taken down by a city council — to see whether the framework generalises or was built for Bristol. Then reverse the direction and be severe: what does the Colston episode show that the framework cannot express? Finally, say whether the memory literature helps here or only muddies things — Margalit's case for ethical duties of remembrance, especially toward those with whom we stand in thick relations, set against Rieff's case that the cult of memory does more harm than good and that forgetting is underrated. Would either of them say that the statue's fate matters much at all?
