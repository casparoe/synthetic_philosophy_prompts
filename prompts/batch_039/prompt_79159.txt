I'm in a joint seminar between the philosophy department and the economics department on self-control, and I have been arguing for weeks with a friend about whether the two literatures we keep reading are even about the same thing. I want to settle it, or at least locate the disagreement precisely. The proposition I want your credence in is this: the episodes philosophers classify as akrasia and the episodes behavioral economists model as present bias are one phenomenon rather than two — not merely overlapping in their stock examples, but such that every central case of either is a central case of the other, and such that a complete explanation of one could be translated, without explanatory loss, into the vocabulary of the other. To make that truth-apt, fix the terms as follows. Take akrasia strictly: the agent acts contrary to the all-things-considered judgment she herself holds at the moment of acting about what it is best for her to do now — so the judgment must be contemporaneous with the act, not merely a plan formed earlier and since abandoned. Take present bias as the empirical pattern in which an agent's relative valuation of a sooner, smaller reward against a later, larger one shifts systematically with the delay of both, producing choices that reverse as time passes, and in which the agent's earlier self would not have endorsed the choice her later self makes. Give me your own credence in the proposition, and note that the number is the least interesting part of your answer.

What I want is the decomposition, and I want it exhaustive enough that I can see where my friend and I part company. Break the proposition into the sub-questions it actually depends on — candidates include: whether one concept is essentially about a judgment and the other essentially about a preference, and whether a judgment and a preference of the relevant kinds can be identified with one another or merely correlated; whether present bias is a structural feature of how value is represented or an artifact of the models we happen to use, and correspondingly whether akrasia is a genuine failure of motivation or a misdescription of agents who never held the relevant judgment in the first place; whether the two classes of central cases in fact coincide, given that much of the akrasia literature concerns non-temporal lapses (betraying a friend's confidence for the pleasure of telling the story, caving to social pressure, remaining in a relationship one judges one should leave) while the discounting literature concerns intertemporal consumption — and whether those non-temporal cases can be redescribed as discounting without making the redescription vacuous; whether the mechanisms plausibly implicated on each side (cue-triggered wanting as distinct from liking, hot-to-cold shifts in how one represents one's own future states, the depletion or strategic allocation of executive control) are shared, partly shared, or disjoint; and whether the standing empirical disputes about any of those mechanisms, if resolved one way or the other, would move the number at all. For each sub-question give me a rough probability and one line of justification, then show how the terms combine into your final figure, then tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to. Beyond that: which single sub-question is most likely to be the one my friend and I actually disagree about, given that my credence in the proposition is around 0.75 and hers is around 0.25; what specific argument, experiment, or result would move me most; and, if you think part of my question is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and give me a number for the residue that survives once the verbal part is set aside.

Finally, test the identity claim against a short battery of cases rather than only in the abstract, and use each case to say which direction it cuts. (a) An agent who reverses her preferences over time but never forms any all-things-considered judgment that her earlier choice was better — so present bias, but arguably no akrasia. (b) An agent who binds herself in advance and then, at the moment of action, judges that the binding is now not worth honoring and deliberately pays a penalty to escape it — arguably no failure of will at all, since she acts on her current judgment. (c) An agent who sticks resolutely to an earlier resolution while judging, at the moment of acting, that the resolution is no longer what she has most reason to follow — by the strict definition akratic, though we would ordinarily call her admirable, and I want to know whether you think that shows the definition is wrong or only that akrasia is not always a defect. (d) An agent whose lapse is entirely non-intertemporal, so that no sooner-versus-later tradeoff is even on the table. Write it as a numbered set of terms with their probabilities, then the combination, then a short sensitivity note; no need to cite anything or name the literature, since I know it, and I would rather have the argument than the apparatus.
