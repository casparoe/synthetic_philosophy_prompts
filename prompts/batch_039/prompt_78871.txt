I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and next month I'm doing a long sit-down with a philosopher who's spent her career defending disjunctivism about perception — the idea that a genuine perception and a hallucination aren't two flavours of one underlying mental state but states of different kinds, with no shared inner core. Shes not famous outside the field, but she's smooth in interviews, and my last two attempts at this kind of piece came out sounding like: so you deny the common factor — yes — great, thanks. This time I want to come at her through the argument from illusion rather than through hallucination, because my impression is that illusions are where the view starts to creak.

So: write me nine or ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. She'll probably reach for the line that an illusion is just a misperception of a real object, that the stick in the water is still a stick, and I don't want the follow-up to let her leave it there. Please make sure I get something on each of these:

- the classic illusion cases (bent stick, the two lines with the arrowheads that look different lenghts, the white wall that goes yellow under a streetlight) and why she thinks those are easier to handle than hallucinations — and whether saying they're easier costs her anything;
- the one I keep chewing on: somebody in a dark hallway sees a figure standing there and it turns out to be a coat on a hook. Illusion or hallucination? Where exactly is the line, and is it a real distinction or just a labelling choice?
- the arrowheads case specifically: you can measure the lines, know they're equal, and they still look unequal. If the perceiver is genuinely seeing those lines, what does she say the experience is doing?
- the rubber hand demo — you stroke a fake hand in the person's view while stroking their real hand out of sight, and after a minute they'll report the fake one as theirs. Is she willing to say the subject is perceiving a rubber hand as her own hand? If not, what's gone wrong?
- the epistemic side: she presumably wants perceivers to be able to know things that hallucinators can't, but a hallucinator is still being reasonable. How does she dodge the old demon-world problem, where the demon's victim seems just as justified as you or me?
- the circularity worry — that the argument from illusion only gets going if you already assume veridical and non-veridical experience share a kind, and that denying the assumption starts to look like it's motivated by wanting to save the view. What independent reason does she have for the denial?
- the neuroscience angle: if somebody eventually pins down a brain signature common to seeing and hallucinating, is that fatal, and if not, what would make her give the view up?
- whether she's a disjunctivist about anything other than perception — memory, imagining, testimony — or whether perception is the special case.

Also give me one closing question that gets at whether this is a discovery about how the mind works or a decision about how to use the word perception. And keep it plain enough that my editor, who has no idea what disjunctivism is, can follow every question.
