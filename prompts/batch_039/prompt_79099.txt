I have been helping draft a collections policy for the small archive at the college where I work, and the more I look at what we already do, the less I understand what we think we are doing. Concretely: we hold the papers of a few dozen administrators, trustees, and prominent alumni, a number of which arrived with donor agreements sealing portions of them for twenty or twenty-five years; we work from a records-retention schedule under which most routine administrative files are destroyed after a set period and only a small remainder is flagged for permanent retention, with the destruction itself logged only as a note at the level of the whole series, so that no one can later tell what was in the files; our mission statement says we preserve the documentary record of the institution and make it available without taking sides in the disputes the records document; and we have a standing rule that we do not create records, we only acquire them — even though we also run an oral history program interviewing residents of the neighborhoods the college displaced in the 1960s and 1970s, and have begun digitizing the records of a few community organizations that keep the originals and let us host the scans. Meanwhile the same policy document complains that the collection is silent about the people most affected by the institution's decisions, and proposes outreach as the remedy.

What I want from you is not a defense or an indictment of any of this, but the theory it presupposes. Taking these practices together, what must we believe about what makes a record worth keeping — is significance a property of the document itself, or of some relation to possible future users, or a byproduct of who happened to hold power and who happened to be written down? What must we believe about the archivist — a neutral conduit through which material passes, or a participant in the events being documented? What does the retention schedule assume about the value of the ordinary and the bureaucratic relative to the exceptional and the prominent? And what picture of the public, and of its claim on the record, is built into a mandate to preserve the record for accountability while sealing a donor's papers for a quarter century?

Then separate what is genuinely forced on us from what is merely compatible. Which parts of this policy would be equally licensed by a rival account of what archives are for — say, a privacy-based account of donor restrictions, a property-based one, or a purely pragmatic "sealed is better than lost" one — and where do the pieces actually conflict rather than merely sit uneasily together? Finally, suppose we replaced the presupposed view with something like its opposite: that the archive's job is to document the exercise of power over people rather than to keep the papers of the powerful, and that a gap in the record is itself something to be recorded, dated, and explained. Tell me what would have to change in our acquisitions, our retention schedule, our access rules, and our finding aids, and which of those changes would be genuine implications of the new view rather than just things that would look nicer.
