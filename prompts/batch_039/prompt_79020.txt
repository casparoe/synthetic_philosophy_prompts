I have been reading Anscombe's protest against Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary degree, and I find her central claim compelling: choosing to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is always murder, and Truman did precisely that when he authorized the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. But when I try to extend her reasoning, it seems to prove far too much. I have laid out the argument below, and I cannot see where to stop it.

(1) Choosing to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is always murder.
(2) Truman chose to kill the innocent civilians of Hiroshima and Nagasaki as a means to ending the war and saving lives.
(3) So Truman murdered.
(4) If Truman murdered, then anyone who intentionally participates in a plan to kill the innocent as a means to an end is also a murderer.
(5) The pilots who dropped the bombs intentionally participated in that plan.
(6) The scientists who designed and built the bomb knew it would be used to kill civilians and intended their work to contribute to that end.
(7) The citizens who voted for the government that ordered the bombings, or who supported the war effort, intentionally contributed to the policy that killed the innocent.
(8) Therefore the pilots, the scientists, and the voters are all murderers.
(9) But it is absurd to call all of them murderers; many were merely doing their jobs, or were one among millions, or did not know the specific plans.
(10) Therefore something is wrong with the argument, and I need to know which premise to reject.

I would most like to keep (1) and (3). I am willing to give up (8), but I want to know what giving it up costs me elsewhere. Some people tell me the problem is in (4): that participating in a plan is not the same as choosing to kill, but I do not see how to draw that line without letting Truman off the hook too, since he also did not personally drop the bombs. Others say the problem is in (6) or (7), because foresight is not intention, but Anscombe seems to think that if you knowingly bring about an evil as a means, you intend it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to give up (1), what do I replace it with? If I keep (1), must I accept (8)? I would rather not, but I want to know what I am paying.
