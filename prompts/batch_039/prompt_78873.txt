I am assembling a small anthology of fiction that illustrates hard problems in jurisprudence, and I would like you to write a story for it.

The problem I want the story to body forth is the self-reference of legal rules: the fact that a legal order must contain, somewhere, the rule that says how its own rules are changed, and that this rule can be changed by the very procedure it defines. I do not want an essay on this, and I do not want a story in which a character explains it. I want a story in which the machinery of a fictional constitution generates a live dilemma that ordinary people have to act inside of.

**The setting.** A small republic — invent its name, its geography, its recent history — whose constitution contains the following provisions and, apart from the Act described below, nothing else that bears on amendment:

- Any provision of this constitution may be amended by the procedure set out in Article II, and no provision may be entrenched against amendment. Any clause that purports to make a provision unamendable is void from the moment it would otherwise take effect. (Article I)
- An amendment is valid if and only if it is adopted by a two-thirds majority of the Assembly and adopted in conformity with the procedure in force at the moment of its adoption. This Article may itself be amended, and the amendment of this Article is governed by this Article. (Article II)
- An amendment takes effect upon its enrolment in the Register of the Constitution, and no amendment has retroactive effect. (Article III)

**One piece of history.** Some sixty years ago the Assembly adopted a short Act of Clarification, which is enrolled in the Register and has been treated as part of the constitution ever since. Its operative sentence resolves an ambiguity in Article II: it provides that the procedure in force at the moment of an amendment's adoption means the procedure in force immediately before the vote is taken, not the procedure that the amendment itself would bring into force. Nothing about the Act's own adoption was irregular on its face. Whether it was validly adopted depends on how Article II is to be read, and how Article II is to be read is precisely what the Act settles.

**The present.** An Assembly, or a faction within one, has just done something that could only be done if the Act of Clarification is good: amended the constitution by a bare majority, or entrenched something against future amendment, or both. The story is the day or so in which the question of that amendment's validity stops being academic and becomes something a particular person must decide or suffer.

**What I want from the story.**

- The pivot should be an act of enrolment. Give me a Registrar — or clerk, or archivist — whose duty is to enter the amendment in the Register with its date and hour, and who understands that the wording of the entry (whether the amendment is recorded as validly adopted, or enrolled under protest, or left formally open) will fix, for every court and office downstream, which procedure was in force at the moment of adoption. Let the pressure concentrate on the pen.
- Give the constitutional question a stake that is not itself constitutional: a title to land, a citizenship, a pension, a marriage, a prison sentence, a seat, a debt. At least one character should be someone who will be ruined by one answer and saved by the other, and who cannot follow more than the first step of the reasoning.
- Voice at least two incompatible readings of Article II, each in the mouth of someone with a real case for it, and make it plain that neither is stupid. One holds that the Act of Clarification is valid because a rule for changing rules can only be applied to the very act that changes it under the rule that act asserts for itself, so the Act bootstraps itself into force and everything since is valid. The other holds that this cannot be so, because it would let any majority at any time validate its own act by declaring which rule governed it — and that the alternative conclusion, that the Act and sixty years of law made under it are void, is one somebody has to be willing to accept.
- Let the pragmatic consideration have its say without being the villain: the argument that a constitution whose entire recent history is void is not a constitution but a fact, and that a legal order is held together by what people will treat as law as much as by what the text says. Someone should make this argument and someone should refuse it, and neither should be obviously right.
- End on a concrete act rather than a summary of positions: a signature, a date, an hour, an entry, a refusal, a hand on a ledger. It may remain ambiguous what the act settles, but the reader should be able to say exactly what was decided and what was left open. Do not append an explanation, a moral, or a note on what the story illustrates.

**Constraints.**

- Roughly 2,500 to 3,500 words.
- Invent everything. Do not quote, paraphrase, or allude to any real constitution, statute, case, or person, and do not use epigraphs. The republic, its history, its officials and its documents are yours. Do not introduce any further provision, hidden document, or discovered fact that would settle the question.
- Keep the legal reasoning in dialogue, in the Registrar's deliberation, and in the shape of events. No stretch of constitutional theory should run longer than a few sentences, and the narration should avoid the vocabulary of paradox, self-reference, or validity except where a character would use it.
- Let the ordinary texture of the place come through — weather, work, food, money, gossip — so that it reads as a story about people in a country rather than a thought experiment with stick figures.
