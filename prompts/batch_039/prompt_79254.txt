I'm a second-year PhD student in a philosophy of mind reading group, and we've spent three weeks on the family of views that try to earn mental content from biology rather than from the head — the teleosemantic tradition associated with Dretske and Millikan, on which a state counts as being about something because it is the biological function of that state, or of the mechanism that produces it, to indicate that thing, where "biological function" is cashed out in terms of what the state and its mechanism were selected for. As I understand the view, error is supposed to be possible on this picture because a state can be tokened in conditions other than the conditions that figure in the normal explanation of why it is produced — the conditions under which it was selected to indicate what it indicates — and that is what it is for the state to be false. I came in sympathetic to all of this. Then a friend in the group, who reads far more carefully than I do, put the following on the whiteboard and said it shows the project quietly eats itself, and that the only way its defenders escape is by letting "normal conditions" mean whatever they need it to mean. I wrote it down more or less as he had it:

(1) A state represents a condition X just in case it is the biological function of that state, or of the mechanism that produces it, to indicate X; and what fixes a state's biological function is the history of selection that explains why the state, and the mechanism that produces it, are there at all.
(2) The view can allow for error, and this is its main selling point: a state can be tokened when the conditions that figure in the normal explanation of its production — the conditions under which it was selected to indicate X — fail to obtain, and that is what it is for the state to be false.
(3) Consider a beetle whose chief predator is a hunting wasp. The beetle has a detector, D, that fires when a wasp's wingbeat pattern reaches its antennae, and firing triggers a dive into the leaf litter that usually saves the beetle's life. D exists because it did this.
(4) But D also fires for the wingbeats of other beetles, and for raindrops striking the antennae. Every population in the lineage has been like this; there never was an ancestral population whose D fired only for wasps.
(5) Selection can favour a state only for what that state in fact did. Across the lineage, D's firings were followed by a successful dive on wasp occasions, by a harmless but wasted dive on beetle occasions, and by the same on raindrop occasions. Firing was never worse for the beetle than not firing.
(6) So all three sorts of occasion are among the conditions in which D was selected to fire: the normal conditions of D's firing include other beetles and raindrops.
(7) So the function of D is to indicate the disjunction wasp-or-beetle-or-rain, and by (1) that disjunction is what D represents.
(8) But then D cannot be wrong: whenever it fires, the disjunction obtains. And the same reasoning applies to any detector, however crude, in any organism whatsoever.
(9) So teleosemantics cannot explain error after all, contrary to (2); it must give up either (1) or (2), and either way the project fails.

I can't find the step I'd want to deny, and I don't want to rescue the view by fiat, since my friend's complaint is precisely that its defenders do exactly that. The two things I'd most like to keep are the core claim in (1) and the promise of a real account of error in (2); if one of those has to go, I want to know which and what it costs.
