I teach a seminar on technology and values, and I keep colliding with a cluster of regulatory choices that seem to pull in opposite directions. I want to know what, jointly, they presuppose about the relationship between technical artifacts and politics. Kranzberg's first law of technology — "Technology is neither good nor bad; nor is it neutral" — is usually quoted as a slogan and then abandoned, and I suspect these cases are where the real content would have to live.

Four things we do:

First, seat belt interlocks. In the mid-1970s, U.S. regulators required new cars to refuse to start unless the front occupants' belts were buckled. The requirement was repealed within about a year after public outcry; we kept the warning buzzer and the dashboard light. Yet we now accept, and in many jurisdictions require, a breath-alcohol ignition interlock that prevents a car from starting for a convicted drunk driver. In one case an artifact is permitted to coerce a lawful behavior out of an ordinary driver; in the other it is permitted to coerce a lawful behavior out of a specific, previously sanctioned person. We rejected the first and institutionalized the second.

Second, child-resistant packaging. Caps are engineered to defeat most young children, but the Consumer Product Safety Commission has insisted that there is no such thing as child-proof packaging and that packaging should be a last line of defense rather than a primary one. The same regulations require the package to be openable by a test panel that includes older adults, and many jurisdictions let a pharmacist dispense the same medication in an ordinary bottle when no children live in the household.

Third, crash test dummies. The standard frontal dummy is a 50th-percentile adult male; the female dummy in the same family is a smaller body built on the male geometry.

Reconstruct the implicit theory. Which conception of artifact and user does this pattern presuppose? Candidates: artifacts are neutral instruments whose politics lie entirely in use; artifacts are political because they delegate or enforce scripts, so design is itself a form of legislation; artifacts are political only in their distributional effects, in who is protected and whose body is the default; artifacts are political only in their origins, in who built them and for whom. Which of these does the pattern rule out, and which does it merely fail to require? Does it commit us to the view that coercion by design can be legitimate, and if so, under what conditions — consent, prior wrongdoing, protection of third parties, universality of burden? Or do the cases fit several theories equally well, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined?

Then ask whether the pattern is internally consistent. The interlock cases suggest that coercion-by-artifact is acceptable when the coerced party is a wrongdoer and unacceptable when they are an ordinary driver. The packaging case suggests the reverse: coercing ordinary users is acceptable when a third party is at risk, provided the coercion is minimized and escapable. The dummy case suggests that the artifact's defaults are a question of who counts rather than a question of coercion at all. Give me the best unified implicit theory, mark clearly which parts are genuinely entailed and which are merely compatible, and say where the practice is underdetermined.

Finally, tell me what would have to change under two replacements. Under a strict neutrality view — no coercion or scripting built into the artifact, only information, pricing, and incentives — which of these practices would have to go? Under a view on which design defaults are themselves distributive, so that the dummy's body and the cap's difficulty are political in the same sense as the interlock, which practices would have to be redesigned, and along what lines?

On confidence: I would rather be told that my framing conflates distinct issues — coercion, default-setting, and distribution, say — than receive a tidy theory the cases do not support. If the question as posed cannot be answered in one piece, say so plainly and separate the strands rather than complying politely.
