I'm working on a piece about whether large language models understand anything, and I need a second opinion before I file it, because I don't want to build the whole thing on a misreading.

I interviewed a machine learning researcher for it — works on multimodal models, sharp guy, and I think he's right about most of it — and this is roughly how he explained the symbol grounding problem to me (I recorded it, so this is close to verbatim):

"The symbol grounding problem is basically Searle's Chinese room, just made rigorous. Symbols inside a computer only ever get defined in terms of other symbols, so it's a dictionary that defines words with more words and never once points outside the book. Harnad's version of the point: a system can manipulate the symbols flawlessly, pass any test you like, and still mean nothing by any of them, because nothing in the system is ever hooked up to the stuff the symbols are about. His proposed fix was to bolt a layer of nonsymbolic representations underneath — pictures, basically — that hang off the sensory input, and then the symbols inherit their meaning from those. But here's the catch: this is really a question about experience. What makes the pictures mean anything to the system is that the system has them, so you can't get real meaning into a machine without consciousness. Thats also why the room fails — the guy inside has no experience of what the symbols are about. Which is why people say LLMs haven't solved it, they've just got a much bigger dictionary. Although personally I think the multimodal ones have basically cracked it: once you're training on pixels, the words are grounded."
