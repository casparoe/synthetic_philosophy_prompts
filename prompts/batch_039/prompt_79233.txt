Alright, I keep going back and forth on Dutch book arguments and I want someone to tell me if theres a principled line here or if I'm just picking and choosing. Heres my gut on six cases.

1. Someone is 60% sure it'll rain tomorrow and 60% sure it won't. A bookie offers two bets at those odds, they take both, they lose whatever happens. Genuine defect. Their opinions don't hang together and the book is what proves it.

2. Same credences, but this person would never bet on anything, ever, for any stake. Nothing the bookie offers gets taken. I don't think there's any defect here. The book only shows something if you'd actually buy it.

3. A bookie offers an infinite sequence of bets, each of which the person will take at their own stated odds, and if you add the whole infinite sequence together they're guaranteed to come out behind -- but every finite chunk of the sequence leaves them even or ahead. There's no stage where they've actually lost anything; the loss only exists at infinity. I say that's a trick. Nothing they ever actually do loses them money.

4. I'm 50/50 on whether the gadget works. I'm also completely certain that after the salesman gets a crack at me tomorrow I'll be 90/10. A bookie exploits the gap. I don't think that's a defect. I'm allowed to think my future self is going to be a sucker, and the scheme only works because the bookie knows exactly how I'll fold.

5. This is the one that trips me up. Someone whose credences are fine at every single moment -- everything adds up, nothing to book synchronically -- but whose updating is just bad. Give them evidence and their new credences aren't what conditioning on that evidence would give. A bookie who knows the rule can take them for everything over a series of offers, and every individual bet looks fair at the moment it's made. I want to say there's a real defect here, and that the sequence shows it, even though at no single time is there a one-shot sure loss and their credences are never incoherent.

6. Someone with a cycle of preferences -- they'll trade A for B, B for C, and C for A, paying a little each time -- can be walked around the loop until they're broke. That feels like a defect too, but it's about tastes rather than opinions, so I don't know if it's the same species of thing or a different one that just happens to have the same shape.

So I want to call 1 and 5 genuine and 2, 3 and 4 tricks, and I have no idea what to do with 6. The obvious principle -- a sure loss shows a real defect only when there's a finite bunch of bets, all acceptable to the agent at one single time, that guarantees a loss -- nails 1, 2, 3 and 4 and then tells me 5 is fine, which I don't buy. But if I patch it to something like "it's about the opinions being exploitable, not about whether the bets get taken," it starts calling 2 and 3 defects as well, and 3 definately isn't.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely enough that I could check a new case against it. What does it say about someone with imprecise credences -- a set of probability functions rather than a single one, who'll only take a bet when every function in the set says the odds are good -- is that person Dutch-bookable, and does your principle say it should count as a defect if they are? And be honest about which of my six verdicts the principle can't keep; if I have to give one up, say which and why, and if you think the standard answer to any of this is right, tell me why rather than just that it's standard.
