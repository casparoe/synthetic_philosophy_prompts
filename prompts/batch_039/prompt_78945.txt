I have drafted a short essay for an interdisciplinary volume aimed at educated non-specialists — a chapter that will sit between a piece on moral luck and a piece on the ethics of attention. I would like comments on the draft rather than a rewrite. The draft follows.

The Uneven Tightness of Cultures

Every society has rules, but societies differ in how much they demand and how hard they punish. The tight–loose framework names that difference and offers an explanation: cultures grow tighter where life has been dangerous, unpredictable, or crowded, because one person's mistake can cost everyone. Where threat recedes, cultures loosen.

That is a decent first pass, but I think the framework treats as background something that deserves to be a variable: who does the demanding. Let me distinguish vertical from horizontal tightness. Vertical tightness runs through institutions — written rules, designated enforcers, penalties that arrive from above. Compliance is a response to the threat of sanction by an authority. Horizontal tightness runs through peers. There is no codified rule and no official, but everyone knows the standard and everyone's compliance is everyone else's business. The sanction is not a fine; it is the slow withdrawal of regard.

Once the two are separated, they turn out not to move together. Small egalitarian face-to-face communities are frequently ferocious about conformity while being almost entirely unhierarchical. Conversely, states that legislate in enormous detail can be places where nobody expects the rules to be applied to anyone in particular. And the standard instruments blur the two: self-report ratings of how appropriate various behaviors are cannot distinguish a respondent who fears a fine from one who fears her neighbours, while indices built from statutes and penalties on the books measure vertical capacity rather than how much a culture actually demands.

The distinction matters because the two forms fail differently. Vertical tightness is brittle: it holds while the enforcement apparatus holds and evaporates when it does not, which is why formal order so often gives way to something quite loose after a state collapses. Horizontal tightness survives the collapse of the state, survives regime change, and survives emigration; but it is bad at absorbing outsiders, who cannot read the rules and are not forgiven for failing to, and bad at permitting innovation from insiders, because innovation looks like deviance to exactly the people whose regard you need.

A second worry: threat is the wrong master variable. What tightens a group is the ratio of the cost of an unpredicted deviation to the cost of suppressing improvisation. Threats raise the numerator, but so do interdependence and irreversibility. A submarine crew, a surgical team, a monastery, an air-traffic control room, a fishing crew working a single net: none of these faces the ecological pressures the framework emphasises, all of them are tight, and their tightness tracks how badly a single improvisation would go. The reframing also predicts looseness wherever errors are cheap and recoverable, not merely wherever threats are absent — and it flags a case that embarrasses both stories, namely prosperous and secure communities that are nonetheless tightly normed, about lawns, school admissions, recycling, and the correct attitude toward the local football team. Nobody is starving and nobody is invading, yet deviation is expensive.

Finally, the two forms impose different unfreedoms. Vertical tightness can be escaped by exit: you leave the jurisdiction and the rule no longer reaches you. Horizontal tightness cannot be escaped by moving, because it travels with you as your reputation among the people who know you; leaving it requires severing every relationship you have. If that holds, the interesting moral question is not how tight a society is but which kind of tightness it runs on, since the two cost different people different things and cost different things to leave.
