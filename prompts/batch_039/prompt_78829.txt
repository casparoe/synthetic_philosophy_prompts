I've come to hold a view about religion and the state that I'd like tested rather than confirmed, and I have no academic background, so I'll state it plainly. I think the French arrangement is the right one, but not for the usual reason that religion is dangerous or divisive. My reason is that a state cannot treat citizens as equals while it is sorting them by what they believe, so the rule has to be identical for everyone, applied to the majority as much as to anyone else. Two consequences follow that I'm prepared to defend: the rule should not be relaxed or carved out for anybody, and the places where France plainly does not keep it — the state pays the teachers in most private religious schools, it owns and pays for the upkeep of churches and cathedrals built before the separation, and in part of the country it still pays the salaries of the clergy — are not counterexamples to the principle but evidence that the country should apply it more thoroughly, not less.

What I want from you is the honest list of what would count against this, sorted by how decisive each item would be, and with a clear line drawn between what tells against the principle itself and what tells only against French policy or French history. Three things interest me especially. First, whether "the same rule for everyone" is actually neutral in effect, or whether the rules as written quietly take one side; give me the sharpest form of that objection, and tell me which parts of it the view's own defenders would concede and which they would simply wave away as not really about the principle. Second, whether the view risks anything: if every group that feels singled out can be told it is the group's problem, and every success can be credited to the principle, then I want to know what it predicts that a rival arrangement does not, and whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind. Third, whether it collapses into its rival: the same principle seems satisfiable both by banning headscarves and turbans in state schools and by letting everyone wear whatever they like, so I'd like to know whether the principle is doing any real work or whether something unstated is doing it for it. The thing that would move me most is a case in which the principle, applied even-handedly to everyone including the majority, produces precisely the unfairness it was supposed to cure.

End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in a short phrase, the single defeater that damages the position most.
