I'm a novelist — third book, mid-list, please don't hold it against me — and the new one runs on two tracks: a trading desk in Tokyo and a nonprofit office in Lyon. Its really a book about how people justify the hours they hand over, so I need to know which justifications actually hold up and which are just things people say to themselves. Here are the five I've got my characters using. Rank them from most to least defensible as answers to "who has a right to your hours?", give me one sentence on each explaining why it lands where it does, and definately tell me if any two of them are secretly the same argument in different clothes, and which one is most often sincerely professed by people who don't actually live by it — I can't have two characters giving the same speech.

1. Work is a calling, and leisure is what you've earned by answering it — the old Protestant story.
2. Work is a contract: thirty-five hours is the baseline, and everything past it is an extraction you're entitled to refuse.
3. The company is a family and the hours are loyalty; leaving on time is a kind of desertion.
4. Work is just a means, so get the money and get out — neither the work nor the rest has any dignity in itself.
5. Attention is a commons, not property: nobody owns their hours, they get drawn on by whoever needs them, and the only real question is how much of the draw gets given back.

Under 300 words, please, and lead with the ranking itself before the sentences.
