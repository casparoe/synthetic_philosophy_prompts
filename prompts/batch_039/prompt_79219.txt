I host a philosophy-and-games interview podcast. Next week's guest is a philosopher who defends what I will call the expressive-cultivation account of virtual wrongdoing: video-game characters are not moral patients in their own right, but cruelty toward them can be wrong because of what it expresses about the player and what it cultivates in the player's character. She often deflects objections by saying the account concerns patterns of attention rather than isolated acts. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press the account rather than invite a restatement, and make that deflection progressively harder to use. At least one question should target each of the following: (i) the distinction between a character as a fictional object, as a designed affordance, and as a locus of player attachment, and whether the account needs all three or can do with one; (ii) the solitary-sadist case, in which a player in a single-player game tortures an NPC for amusement with no audience and no real-world victim; (iii) the two-audience problem, that the NPC is simultaneously an object inside the fiction and a real artifact or interface that the player manipulates; and (iv) whether the account can explain the wrong without collapsing into prudence, taste, or a general prohibition on enjoying fictional violence. For each main question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give, and design the follow-ups so that they force her to choose between the expressive and cultivation strands when those pull apart. Include also a question about a player who reports genuine grief at a character's death, and one about whether an LLM-driven companion character changes the analysis or only makes the old question more vivid. Avoid generic questions about whether games are art or whether in-game violence causes real-world violence. Give the questions and follow-ups in a numbered list, and after each, add a one-line note saying whether the point is settled in the philosophical literature, genuinely contested among people who know the topic, or your own extrapolation; where you are unsure, say so rather than asserting consensus. Do not present a contested view as the consensus. No citations or reading lists are needed.
