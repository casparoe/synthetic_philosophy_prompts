I'm a philosopher of language, and in three weeks I'm officiating at my oldest friend's wedding — he and his fiancee know I've spent twenty years on Austin and performatives and they think it's hilarious, so the officiant is me. Sixty guests: half are academics, a few of them people who know this literature cold, and the other half are her family, including kids and a grandmother who will not sit through a lecture. What I need from you is the short set of remarks I give before the declaration that actually marries them. Take the role of a philosopher of language who also has an ear for how theory sounds out loud in a room, and think carefully before you draft; start by brainstorming for yourself what a speech of this kind is and isn't allowed to do, and where the philosophy can do real structural work without turning into a talk.

Here's what I keep getting stuck on, and its why I'm not just writing it myself. Almost every sentence in a normal toast is a description or a prediction in disguise — you two are perfect for each other, you'll be happy — and I'd be up there asserting things I have no standing to assert. The sentences I can stand behind aren't descriptions at all; they're the other kind, the ones that do something: a promise, a blessing, a thank you, an avowal. An avowal, as I'm using it, is the first-person case that isn't a report on an inner state and so isn't true or false the way a report is — its point is that the speaker takes responsibility for something rather than describes it. So I want the whole speech leaning that way, and I want one line in it that is unmistakably an avowal and not a description.

Austin's misfires and abuses can help if they earn their place — the act that never comes off at all (wrong words, no standing) versus the one that comes off but is hollow — I think there's a warm joke in there that the philosophers will catch and everyone else will just find charming. Dont explain the theory, dont cite anyone, no jargon in the actual speech. Give me the remarks, then a short note on the two or three places where you made a choice because of the philosophy and what you'd have written otherwise.
