Please explain the problem of the unity of the proposition, focusing on the regress that arises when we ask what unifies its constituents—the regress often associated with F. H. Bradley. I do not want a survey, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the argument itself reconstructed and assessed. Begin with a minimal case: two objects and a dyadic relation. Explain what is supposed to be missing when we merely list those items rather than assert something, and why the relation cannot simply be added as a third item without raising the same question again. Then distinguish three readings of what the regress is supposed to show: that relations cannot be real, that propositions cannot be structured, and that propositional unity cannot be explained by appeal to any further item. For each reading, say what would have to be true for it to succeed and what an opponent would say. I am especially interested in a distinction that is often blurred: between a regress that is merely infinite and one that is vicious. Does each step generate a new explanandum, or only a new explanans? If the former, what exactly is the new thing that needs explaining? If the latter, is the regress harmless? Consider the response that the unity of a proposition is primitive, and say whether that is a solution, a refusal to answer, or a substantive thesis, and what its costs are. Then consider one or two other responses, such as the idea that a relation is not another constituent but is unsaturated or operative in a different way, and assess how much of the regress they actually avoid. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
