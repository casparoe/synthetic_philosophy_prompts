I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about a decision problem, and we got nowhere: each of us could see exactly where the other was going wrong, and neither could make the other see it. I would like you to put the two positions into a dialogue, each stated as strongly as it can be.

The problem, so that nothing rests on prior familiarity with it. A driver must pass two intersections in sequence and cannot double back. Turning off at the first is worth 0; turning off at the second is worth 4; driving past both ends in a crash is worth 1. The driver's amnesia is total and known in advance, so the information available at the two intersections is identical: whatever rule the driver uses, they use it at both. Since no rule can distinguish the two intersections, every available policy amounts to a single probability of turning off, applied at both; the best such policy turns off with probability one-third, for an expected value of four-thirds, which beats always continuing (1) and always turning off (0). Yet at any given intersection, reasoning about that intersection alone seems to point elsewhere: continuing is better if this is the first intersection, turning off is better if this is the second, and the driver cannot tell which one they are at. Our argument was over what that gap shows.

Write the exchange between the two readings of the case, each held by someone competent in this area.

On the first reading, the gap shows that the proper unit of rational assessment is the whole plan, not the isolated act. The driver is one agent occupying two states it cannot tell apart, so what look like two decisions are one decision applied twice; a theory that prices the two acts separately is not merely awkward but incoherent, and the one-third randomization is not a trick but the plain expression of the driver's ignorance.

On the second reading, the gap shows only that the driver's information is coarse, which is a fact about the driver and not about rationality. A theory of rational choice says what to do given what one knows, and when what one knows fails to separate two situations, the verdict is correspondingly coarse. Nothing here forces us to abandon act-by-act evaluation; it only reminds us that where information is imperfect, the objects of choice are functions from information states to acts. The talk of one decision applied twice is, on this reading, a verbal trick, since there really are two episodes of deliberation and each is a genuine choice.

Have them press each other on at least these points, and on anything else the exchange brings to life:

- whether the particular numbers matter, and whether the case changes character if the amnesia is merely probable rather than certain;
- whether it is intelligible for a rational agent to do, some of the time, the thing that is worthless at the place where they might actually be, and if so, what it could mean to do that deliberately;
- what to make of a driver who, before setting out, installs a device that will force the one-third randomization: whether that settles matters, or merely relocates the difficulty, or reveals that the position needs a crutch in order to guide action at all;
- whether the two intersections should be counted as one agent or as two agents who share a payoff, and what turns on the answer.

Let each answer the other's actual move rather than alternating monologues; let each concede what they must; and do not force a verdict at the end, since I would rather see the disagreement sharpened than papered over. I don't need names, dates, or sources — it is the argument itself I am after.
