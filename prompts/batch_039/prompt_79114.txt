I am a retired natural scientist — thirty-five years in experimental chemistry — who has spent the past several years reading philosophy of science in earnest for the first time. I have just finished a short book on scientific method by an author whose competence I have no reason to doubt, and before I take anything from its account of Lakatos I would like that account audited. What follows is his summary of Lakatos's methodology as I have it in my notes; the sentences are his in substance and, so far as I recall, close to his wording.

'A research programme is a series of theories that share a hard core of central assumptions, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses; when a prediction fails it is the belt that is revised, never the core. A programme is progressive when it predicts novel facts and degenerating when it merely accommodates facts already known, and Lakatos's advance over Popper is to appraise whole series of theories rather than isolated ones. The negative heuristic forbids criticism aimed at the hard core, while the positive heuristic sketches the problems the programme should pursue. A programme is refuted as soon as an experiment contradicts the hard core, and rational scientists thereupon abandon it — that, the author says, is what happened to phlogiston chemistry and to Newtonian mechanics. And because Lakatos concedes that a degenerating programme may recover and that we cannot tell at the time which programmes will succeed, his verdict is that there is no rational standard of theory choice: he is halfway to Feyerabend.'

I want to know, sentence by sentence, what someone who works on Lakatos would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong — and whether the closing verdict follows from whatever survives the corrections. I am not asking for an exposition of the methodology; I am asking where this gloss departs from it. My own hunch, which may be an artefact of a laboratory career, is that the conclusion is doing rather more work than the premises will bear. I own the book, so I need no citations and no reading list.

Be direct with me if the request is ill-posed. If the gloss turns out to be essentially accurate and I am hunting for errors that are not there, tell me so plainly rather than manufacturing five tidy criticisms to fit the format; if my hunch about which sentence carries the weight is misplaced, say that too.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one for each sentence of the summary, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
