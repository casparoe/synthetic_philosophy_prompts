Last Friday my flatmate Roz and her brother Tom ended up having it out in our kitchen about our mate Dan, who's supposedly finishing his thesis and keeps not finishing it. Dan's installed one of those apps that blocks his socials until he's written 500 words, and he's also got a savings account he can't touch for a year because he kept dipping into the rent money. Roz reckons Dan's just being sensible about himself. Tom reckons the whole business is abit pathetic and slightly dishonest. It escalated from there and I stood there making tea for about forty minutes. Roughly how it went:

(1) Roz: what Dan does is textbook hyperbolic discounting — everyone overweights the next hour against next year, and the grown-up response is to notice that and price the future properly, at a steady rate, like an actuary. Tom: why is the actuary the gold standard? A steady rate means caring almost nothing about anything past a few decades, and never revising it — consistent, sure, but consistency isn't sense. A consistent miser is still a miser.

(2) Roz: I'm not bothered about the level, I'm bothered about the flip. Dan wants, tonight, to work in the morning; in the morning he wants to scroll. Same man, same bit of work, and the only thing that's changed is how near the boring part is. Both of those wants can't be right, so one of the two Dans is making a mistake. Tom: maybe they're not the same man in the sense that matters. I care more about myself next year than about a stranger next year, and nobody calls that a bias, so why is it automatically a bias to care more about the me who's here now than the one who turns up later? And anyway, if you're not even sure what rate is right — you don't know how long you'll last or whether any of it will still matter to you — then averaging over all the rates you might have gives you one that starts steep and flattens off. Which is exactly the shape they keep finding in the lab. So maybe the shape needs no bias to explain it.

(3) Roz: fine, but the flip still turns Dan into a mug. He'll pay to stop himself doing something he'd pay to be allowed to do an hour later; his own preferences are a market anyone could arbitrage. That's not a different taste, that's incoherence. So the app and the locked account are just self-defence, and it's mad to call it weakness of will when he's actually doing something about it. Tom: self-defence against who? He's the one who'll want the money. Calling tonight's want his "considered" preference and tomorrow's his "impulse" is rigged — tomorrow's version will have reasons too, and you've just decided in advance which of them is the real Dan. And here's the bit I can't get past: if he really judged that working in the morning was best, all things considered, he'd do it. So either he didn't really judge that, or there's no weakness at all, just two wants shoving each other, and "weakness of will" is a moralising label we slap on the loser.

I couldn't referee it. Every time I thought one of them had it, the other came back with something that seemed definately just as good. Could you go through it move by move and rate each of the six, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each one? I'd also like to know who's more right overall, and what both of them are missing — especially on that last point about whether weakness of will is even a real thing. Please don't just give me the names of positions or who's supposed to have said what; I want the actual reasoning, not a reading list. Write it in British English.
