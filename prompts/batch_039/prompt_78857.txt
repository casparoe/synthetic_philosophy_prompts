I'm writing a term paper for a social epistemology seminar on the peer disagreement literature — equal weight, total evidence, steadfastness, the whole thing — and my seminar leader suggested I pair it with a film night, so I got three freinds to watch Lumet's 12 Angry Men with me and now I have to turn it into something. I know the standard positions well enough that you don't need to rehearse them for me; what I want is the reading. The obvious take is that Juror 8 is the steadfast dissenter and the film vindicates him: eleven men tell him the boy did it, he refuses to fold, and he turns out to be right, so there's your counterexample to conciliationism. I think that's too quick, partly because of a distinction a friend floated at the film night that I can't tell whether it's doing real work or is just a fancy way of saying the film is about deliberation. The distinction: there's disagreement where we agree on what the evidence is and disagree about what it supports — the equal-weight machinery is built for that, you downgrade, you split the difference — and there's disagreement about whether the evidence is even in yet, whether the process that produced the majority's view was clean, whether the pool of considerations has been exhausted. In the second kind there's nothing to average, because you can't average your credence with someone whose position is that we haven't finished looking when your position is that we have. My friend claims 12 Angry Men is almost entirely the second kind, and that Juror 8 never actually asserts the boy is innocent — his stated position is that he doesn't know and the case deserved more than five minutes. If that's right, the film isn't testing conciliationism at all, it's testing the inquiry norms that the disagreement debate quietly presupposes.

So: give me the reading, and be honest about where the film resists it. Is Juror 8 a case of disagreement at all, or is he just refusing to close an inquiry, and does that distinction survive scrutiny or collapse once you press on it? Note the beats that make it messy — the juror who keeps checking his watch and wants to get to the ballgame votes not guilty for no epistemic reason whatsoever; the last two holdouts break differently, one on the glasses and one who tears up a photograph of his own son and comes apart, which is a conversion by something that isn't evidence; the room is hot, the fan doesn't work, and the majority's confidence is soaked in haste and bigotry and one man's private history, which means the peer condition never held in the first place. Does that let a conciliationist off the hook — the disagreement had no authority to begin with — and if so, what does the film show about the fact that whether someone counts as your peer is usually only visible in retrospect, when the vote is already taken? Then tell me what the film adds that the argument alone doesn't, if anything, and say plainly which scenes a reader should watch again for it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, no restating what I asked. And no citations or reading list, I just want the reading.
