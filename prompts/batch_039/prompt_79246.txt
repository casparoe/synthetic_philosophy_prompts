Context. In a reading group on skepticism, a colleague has twice used a single line of G. E. Moore's as a conversation-stopper. Both times he raised his hand and said "here is one hand." The first time, it came at the end of an argument: he took himself to have refuted the skeptic on the spot, insisting that the gesture was a proof that there is an external world. The second time, it came at the beginning of an exchange: when I asked what justified the premise, he repeated the line, this time as a way of declining to argue further, saying that some things are more certain than the premises of any philosophical argument to the contrary. My suspicion is that these two uses cannot both be right, and that the second is not even the same kind of move as the first.

Task. Lay out, as a menu rather than a defense of any one option, the distinct precise claims that the one-liner could be making. Order the readings from thinnest to thickest: from what the line says on its most literal, uncontroversial construal, through the readings on which it is an epistemological thesis, up to the readings on which it is not a claim to knowledge at all. For each reading, state the thesis in a sentence or two, say what it commits its holder to, assign a verdict — trivial platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but dubious, or false — and say what argumentative work it could and could not do. Be explicit about which readings would actually license the first use (refuting the skeptic on the spot) and which would license the second (declining to argue), and about which pairs of readings are mutually exclusive, so that my colleague cannot help himself to all of them at once. Do not simply rehearse the familiar verdict that the proof begs the question; I want the space of readings mapped.

Two further things I want from you. First, address whether the gesture matters: is the claim the general proposition that hands exist, or something that only a demonstrative identification of this object here could deliver, and what follows for how the line should be evaluated? Second, end with the two or three questions I should put to my colleague to force him to commit to one reading, together with a brief statement of which reading you think he most likely meant on each of the two occasions.
