I am preparing a short position statement on ambiguity for a reading group, and I would like you to audit it before I circulate it. Here is what I hold.

1. I am a Savage-style expected-utility theorist. I take rational preference to be maximisation of subjective expected utility with respect to a single probability measure, and I regard the sure-thing principle as a substantive constraint on rational preference rather than a modelling convenience.

2. I think the Ellsberg choices are rationally permissible. Someone who prefers a bet on the urn of known 60/40 composition to the corresponding bet on the urn of unknown composition is not making a mistake, and in the three-colour case, where the urn holds a known quantity of red balls and the remainder black or yellow in unknown proportion, someone who prefers betting on red over black while preferring to bet on black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow is likewise not making a mistake. I do not think these people are exhibiting a bias that we should debias them out of; I think they are tracking something real.

3. I think the risk/ambiguity distinction is epistemic rather than psychological. The difference between the two urns is a difference in what the agent knows about the generating process, not in the agent's attitude toward the unknown. So ambiguity aversion ought to be analysable as ordinary risk aversion directed at something — the agent's uncertainty about the composition, say — rather than treated as a primitive extra ingredient in the utility function.

4. I am suspicious of non-additive-probability and multiple-prior models of the Choquet and maxmin families. They accommodate the Ellsberg data, but I cannot see a general normative principle behind them, and I think departures from expected utility should be licensed by a principle rather than by the fact that they fit a pattern.

5. On the two-urn version specifically, I have always thought that preferring the known urn to the unknown one is not a violation of the sure-thing principle at all; the genuine violation appears only in the three-colour case, where the two pairwise preferences have the sure-thing structure.

6. Here is a verdict I would defend. Suppose the composition of the unknown urn had been fixed by the toss of a fair coin that I did not see: heads, the urn holds one hundred red balls; tails, one hundred black. Then I would not pay anything to avoid a bet on that urn, and I would say that preferring a known fifty-fifty urn to it would be a mistake. The second-order uncertainty there is fully specified, and there is nothing left for ambiguity to do.

I am aware that some of these look as though they cannot all be right together, but I have never been able to tell which ones, and I would rather find out now than in front of the group.

Go through the set and tell me which commitments pull against each other, and how badly. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, distinguish the ones that are merely apparent from the ones that are real, and for each real tension say which commitment would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. Finally, tell me which single revision would clear up the most.
