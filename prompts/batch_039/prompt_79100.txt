I teach a graduate seminar in metaphysics and philosophy of action, and for the past two terms a colleague from the law faculty and I have co-convened a working group on causation by omission. Two of the regulars have now spent months arguing past each other, and I have concluded that the fault is with the form of the debate rather than with either of them. The first camp — I have been calling them the inclusionists — holds that omissions, absences, and failures are causes in whatever sense ordinary positive events are: that the lifeguard's not swimming, the builder's not installing the guard, the inspector's not opening the file are the causes of what followed, and that the counterfactual dependence we cite in such cases is not a second-best substitute for the real relation but an instance of it. The second camp — the restrictionists — holds that nothing happens when someone fails to do something, that the relata of causation must be occurrent particulars, and that "the omission caused it" is either false as stated or a compressed way of saying something about what a person is answerable for, a distinct and derivative relation whose logic still needs spelling out. I want the document these two should have produced at the end of the first term: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, written so that both could put their names to it without either feeling that the other's vocabulary had been smuggled in.

The statement should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what is uncontroversially true across a fixed set of cases; what each camp concedes to the other; and where the two of them agree that the *moral* and *legal* asymmetry between acting and omitting comes apart from the metaphysical question, if they do agree about that. Second, a disagreements table, one row per crux, with each side's position written in wording that side would accept — the relata question, the question whether counterfactual dependence suffices for causation or merely marks it, the question whether omission-causation talk is context-sensitive pragmatics rather than semantics, and the question whether there is a determinate fact of the matter at all. Third, for each crux, the case, argument, or comparison that both camps agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. To keep this from turning into an endless parade of hypotheticals I have asked them to work from one template: an infant drowns in a bath while a competent adult in the next room reads; a second adult is asleep and could not have heard; a third hears, forms the intention to go, and is struck by a car in the hallway; and a fourth walks in, sees the child, and walks out again. I want the statement to say what distinguishes the second from the first, and whether the fourth differs from the first in kind or only in the quality of our evidence. I have also proposed two pieces of machinery and want them used or rejected explicitly: a substitution test, under which any claim that an omission caused an outcome must survive restatement with the omission replaced by the positive event whose occurrence the omission excluded, in the same direction and with the same strength; and a term of my own coinage, a "shadow event," for the occurrence that would have occupied the omission's place had things gone otherwise. If either camp can accept the notion of a shadow event, or if the substitution test begs the question in favour of one side, I want that said. And I have a suspicion I would like tested rather than confirmed: that at least one of their four cruxes is verbal, turning on whether "cause" is being used univocally across the two camps. If so, say which one and why.

Write as a philosopher of causation who is also a careful drafter — someone used to producing formulations that parties in dispute can actually sign. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you list the candidate cruxes and set aside the ones that are merely terminological, then produce the statement itself. Do not include a bibliography, and do not attribute any position to a named author; I want the arguments in their own right, and I do not want the document resting on your recollection or mine of who said what. Finally, I would rather have pushback than compliance. If the two positions as I have described them turn out on inspection to be compatible, or if the joint statement can only be written by quietly adopting one side's vocabulary, or if the case template is rigged so that one side wins by construction, say so plainly and tell me what the real disagreement is, rather than producing a document that sounds even-handed and that neither camp would in fact sign. A short honest statement is worth more to me than an impressive long one.
