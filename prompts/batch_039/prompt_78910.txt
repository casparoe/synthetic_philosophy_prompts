I am a novelist, and the book I am finishing turns on a character — a répétiteur with a ruined career and an unreasonably good ear — who hears music the way Schopenhauer says it should be heard. The festival where I am appearing this autumn has asked me to give a session on those pages: fifty minutes, a whiteboard, an upright piano in the corner that I can play serviceably if not well, and an audience of twenty who read novels and go to concerts and have never opened a book of philosophy. One of them is a retired piano teacher who will have views about the bass line. I am not asking you to explain Schopenhauer on music to me; I can deliver the explanation myself. I am asking you to plan it.

Here is the material as I have it, in my own words, so that you need not reconstruct it and I need not vouch for a translator. Music, for Schopenhauer, is the exception among the arts: the others copy the Ideas, the grades in which the will objectifies itself, and so give us the will at one remove, whereas music is a copy of the will itself. He therefore does not put music at the top of the series of the arts; he puts it outside the series. He maps its structure onto his metaphysics: the bass is the lowest grade, the inorganic, the unyielding mass; the middle voices are the intermediate grades; the melody in the upper voice is the highest grade, the life of the will, and melody is the history of that will — striving, momentary satisfaction, renewed striving — with dissonance and its resolution standing for the will's discord and its passing appeasement, and a suspended chord for longing. What music expresses is the affects themselves, joy and grief and longing as such, not my joy or this particular person's grief. The composer, on this account, states the profoundest wisdom in a language his reason does not understand; he does not know what he is saying. Words are a foreign addition, and it is absurd to compose music for a libretto. He goes so far as to say that we could as well call the world embodied music as embodied will. And there is one pressure point I want handled rather than hidden: if music is the immediate expression of the will's strivings, what becomes of his larger claim that art quiets the will?

What I need is the teaching plan, in these parts.

First, the order. Which pieces must be in place before the next can land, and why each dependency runs the way it does — what breaks if I explain the Ideas before the will, or the melody before the bass. Tell me which steps to cut if the room is slow, and where I am most likely to lose them.

Second, the one example that carries the whole thing. It must be wordless; short enough to survive being played badly on an upright; familiar enough that they hear it in their heads; with an unmistakable bass under a melody, and a dissonance that resolves. Tell me what it must have and what it must lack, why the obvious candidates will not do — the opening of the Fifth, a Chopin nocturne, a Wagner leitmotif, a film score, a song with a text — and how I should use it: what I play first, what I say over it, and at what point in the sequence.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface. My guesses: that music expresses the composer's feelings; that the will means desire or appetite; that music is merely ranked first in a league table of the arts; that if music does not copy the Ideas it must be about nothing; that he is a mystic and there is nothing to explain. For each, the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off, and the sentence that does it.

Fourth, the question to put to the room at the end that would show whether the idea has landed rather than whether they were moved. Not a quiz on terminology, and not a request for agreement.

Fifth, what I should refuse to explain at this level and say so out loud — I assume the Kantian machinery, the thing in itself, and the later history of the idea.

Finally, one sentence I can offer honestly when someone asks whether any of it is true.
