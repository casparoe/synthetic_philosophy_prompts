I've talked myself into disjunctivism about perception and I'm starting to worry I've talked myself into something I couldn't be argued out of. The core of it: a genuine perception of the tree and a hallucination that seems just like it aren't two species of one genus — they're not the same fundamental kind of mental event, even though they're subjectivly indistinguisable from the inside, and in the good case the tree itself is a constituent of my experience. Some versions of the view say the hallucination is characterized purely negatively, as an experience that just isn't a perception of anything, rather than as having some inner content of its own. That's the part I like least and can't obviously do without.

So: what would count against it? I want the honest map, not the stock objections I already know how to wave off. A few things I'm genuinely unsure about, and I'd rather you work them through than just name them:

- The argument from illusion in its older form (things look other than they are, so what we're directly aware of has to be something mental) — does disjunctivism actually answer that, or does it just refuse the premise by stipulation? And does the answer get worse when the case is an illusion rather than a hallucination, where there really does seem to be something appearing-as-such that I'm aware of?

- A case I keep chewing on: a single experience that's only partly veridical. I get the shape right and the colour wrong; or I see the object through a medium that distorts one property and leaves the rest alone. If one token experience can be veridical with respect to one property and illusory with respect to another, does the veridical/hallucinatory split still carve experience into fundamental kinds, or does every perception come out as a mixture?

- Causal deviance: a neurosurgeon reads the object's properties off a scanner and stimulates my visual system to match, so my experience is caused by the object and covaries with it, but nothing perceptual is going on. I want to say that's a hallucination. If so, the entire difference between the good case and the bad case lives in the causal history rather than in the experience — so how much of the view is left, and does the epistemic version (the one about what my perceptual justification rests on) survive if the metaphysical version doesn't? I'm also not sure which of those two I'm actually holding, so tell me whether they can come apart.

- Does any of it predict something a rival doesn't? Is not-the-same-fundamental-kind the sort of claim that could ever turn out false in a way anyone could detect, given that the two cases are indistinguishable from the inside by construction? If not, is that a defect, or is it just what a claim about the structure of experience has to look like?

Rank what you give me from fatal to merely awkward, flag which ones a committed disjunctivist would concede themselves, and say plainly whether you think the view is just insulated. For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is a case where I have to admit the good and bad cases share something that does explanatory work — if I need a common factor to explain why the hallucination seems the way it does, I think I'm done. No citations or reading lists, just the arguments.
