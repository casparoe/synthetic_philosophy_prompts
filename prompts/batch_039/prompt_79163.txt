I teach the second half of a graduate seminar in philosophy of social science, and my unit on causal inference from historical data keeps going flat. My students can recite the debate over why some nations are rich — institutions, geography, culture — and they can list the standard objections, but they treat the whole thing as a spectator sport, as though the difficulty were a matter of insufficient cleverness on the part of the researchers rather than something structural about the evidence available. I want a ninety-minute activity for sixteen students that puts them against the wall instead of telling them about it. What I have in mind is a simulated research conference on a fictional archipelago: ten islands, a shared colonial past, a common census, a common currency, and a handful of well-documented crises in the twentieth century. The participants split into three research teams, each handed only one family of variables for the same ten islands (institutional, geographical, cultural), plus a fourth group acting as a replication and referee panel and one or two individuals playing regional historians with archival material about a single island. Each team must eventually put a causal claim on the board, stated so that it has a definite falsifier and a definite policy implication, and there is a point scheme, no real money, that rewards claims surviving the others' challenges and penalizes claims that get withdrawn. I will prepare the underlying data-generating story in advance and reveal it only in the debrief; I want it arranged so that all three families of variables correlate with prosperity in the ten-island sample, so that those correlations have different provenance (one variable genuinely upstream of prosperity, one downstream of it, one serving as a proxy for a fourth thing that no team can observe directly), and so that no additional data of the same observational kind can break the tie. The point is for them to arrive at the standoff the real literature is stuck in without being able to tell themselves that a better dataset would settle it.

Give me the full runnable design: the materials I need to prepare, including the hidden structure and the three data booklets, with what the numbers should look like and why; the roles and how to assign them; the rules and payoff scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; and the debrief. The activity should work with students who have read nothing for it, and nobody should hear the standard labels for the three positions until the debrief begins. The debrief should map particular moments in the session onto the concepts — I would like them to be able to point at the exact minute when their confidence became unearned — and should leave them with the resources the field actually has for living with this, not just the vertigo. Numbers should be simple enough that they can be worked by hand or in a spreadsheet during the session; no real money, and nothing requiring internet access in the room. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I already expect one team to try to win by merging its data with another team's and declaring victory, and I expect the referee panel to turn prosecutor — and how I should handle each failure mode without deflating the exercise. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. I am aware that real research on these questions sometimes has non-observational leverage, such as a border drawn for reasons unconnected to the people living on either side of it, and I do not want to send students away thinking the whole question is undecidable; tell me exactly which piece of the real epistemic situation I cannot stage and how I should supply it in the final ten minutes.
