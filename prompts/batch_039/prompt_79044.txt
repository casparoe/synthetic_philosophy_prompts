Ok so I need a satire peice for this dataset I'm putting together, and I've landed on care ethics. Here's the shape of it.

An advice column — the classic agony-aunt setup. Short letter from a reader, then the columnist's reply. But the columnist is a card-carrying care ethicist, the relational kind rather than the "everyone should just be nicer to each other" kind, and the whole thing is deadpan. She never winks. She never has a moment where she realises she's out of her depth. She's warm and serene and completely confident from the first line to the last. Around 650 words total, letter included; keep the letter under 150 words.

Make the letter a real tangle: her mother has moved in and needs more and more, her brother does nothing at all, her employer wants her back in the office three days a week, and the man next door keeps asking her to sit with his father so he can get to his own medical appointments. So: who does she owe what.

The jokes have to come out of the theory rather than out of the aunt being daft — someone who actually teaches this stuff should laugh, not wince. So build her answer out of the real moving parts:

- She opens with attention. Caring, for her, means becoming engrossed in the other's reality, so that your own projects stop being the organising centre of your attention and their needs slide into that slot. She'll describe this as a trainable skill and be quietly proud of being able to teach it.
- She draws the line between caring about someone in general and caring for someone in particular, and she gently notes that the letter-writer is doing a lot of the first (the neighbour's father, in the abstract) and not much of the second.
- The one she can't get around: caring is a relation, not a virtue, and a caring relation is only finished when the person cared for takes it up as care. The carer can't unilaterally declare it done. She should use this in a way that quietly makes her advice unfollowable — if the mother doesn't recieve it as care, no amount of work counts, and the aunt has no way of telling the reader how much work is enough.
- She gets to the regress (who cares for the carer) and handles it sincerely, in a way that still leaves the reader holding the bag.
- She has no way of saying how much is owed to the mother versus the neighbour versus the reader's own kid, and she shouldn't pretend otherwise: care isn't the sort of thing you put on a scale, and the weighing is itself a failure of attention. But the letter demands an answer, so she starts reaching for fairness — the brother's share, what the employer owes her, an actual rota — and the reader should watch the whole thing turn into justice-and-rules thinking at exactly the moment it ran out of road. She must never notice this, and no line in the piece should point at it.
- Her one resource for the reader's exhaustion is the memory of having been cared for, which is supposed to be enough to summon care when the spontaneous kind isn't there. Play that completely straight.
- Let the reader see what she doesn't: she never once asks why the brother is absent, or why it's the women in this letter who are doing the caring.

End it structurally useless. She can't complete the caring on the reader's behalf — only the cared-for can — so the reply has to hand the whole question back. But do it warmly, the way a competent professional closes a column, not like a dodge.

The target is the claim that care ethics can be a complete ethic: that attending to relationships can do all the work principles, rights and fair distribution are normally asked to do. Keep it fair — the point is to be recognisable, not to score.

No citations, no reading list, no quotes, and don't put words in any real theorist's mouth, I don't want invented quotations. Don't name-drop actual philosophers either, just use the ideas. Someone who's never read a word of care ethics should still enjoy it.
