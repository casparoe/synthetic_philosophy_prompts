I'm a junior at a mid-sized public high school, and I sit on the editorial board of our philosophy club's year-end zine, which this year is a satire issue: each of us takes one argument off the syllabus and does something funny with it, and I've drawn the antinatalist asymmetry. Our faculty adviser spent two Thursday meetings on it and it has been living in my head ever since. Stated flatly, as I understand it: pain that is present is bad; pleasure that is present is good; pain that is absent is good even when there is no one there to be spared it; and pleasure that is absent is bad only when there is someone there to be deprived of it. Put those four together and not being brought into existence has the good of no pain and none of the bad of missing pleasure, so coming into existence comes out behind never coming into existence at all. There are two satellite claims I'd like to be able to draw on as well: that we are systematically too generous in our assessments of our own lives, and that there is no duty to bring a happy person into existence but there is a duty not to bring an unhappy one.

What I want from you is a mock advice column of roughly 700 words, with an invented agony aunt who is a committed antinatalist — invented by you, not standing in for anyone real, and answering in the deadpan register of a syndicated school-newspaper page, with no winks to the reader and no signals that she is a joke. Give it a masthead line, three letters, three replies, and a sign-off. The letters are: first, a couple writing in to announce a pregnancy and to ask, half-seriously, whether she will be sending a card; second, a reader who has just understood the asymmetry and is furious at their parents for having had them, asking whether to say something to them and whether anyone has actually wronged them; third, a reader who asks, politely, why the aunt is still here if she believes all this. Please make each reply trade on a different clause of the asymmetry, keep the three replies mutually consistent — the third is where the whole thing could collapse, and I would rather the aunt sound worse than turn out to be a hypocrite — and include at least one joke that only lands for someone who has grasped that a benefit can be credited to a party that does not exist. At least one reply should reach for a comparison from ordinary life rather than from philosophy, since that is where good advice columns do their work, and the aunt's name and her sign-off should carry part of the joke rather than being decoration. The piece should be genuinely funny to our adviser, who knows the argument, and still legible to the classmates who have never heard of it; the aunt should be the strongest version of the position rather than a straw man, so that any unfairness in the satire is deliberate and defensible.

Format it as follows. First the column itself: the masthead line, then each letter kept under 90 words and each reply under 130, ending with the sign-off, roughly 700 words in total. Then, on a new line, a section titled "Notes on the choices," about 200 words, in which you tell me which clause each of the three replies is trading on, where the column came closest to undercutting itself and how you kept it from doing so, which of the three replies a real antinatalist would most want rewritten and why, and any place where a reader could fairly accuse the satire of cheating.
