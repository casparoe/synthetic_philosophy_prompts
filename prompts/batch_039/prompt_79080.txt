I'm in a small reading group that has spent a few weeks on the ethics of wild animal suffering, and last session one of us produced an argument that I have not been able to shake. It goes like this. (1) The value of a state of affairs is the sum, across all the sentient beings in it, of their lifetime welfare, where a being's lifetime welfare is the balance of its pleasures over its pains. (2) The life of a typical wild animal — small-bodied, short-lived, and subject to enormous juvenile mortality, starvation, exposure, and predation — contains more pain than pleasure, so its lifetime welfare is negative. (3) Bringing a life whose lifetime welfare is negative into existence is bad, and refraining from bringing it into existence is good, since no one is harmed by never having been born. (4) So, other things equal, a world containing fewer wild animals contains less total suffering than a world containing more of them. (5) Habitat destruction reduces the number of wild animals, because it lowers the carrying capacity of the land for them. (6) Therefore, other things equal, destroying habitat reduces the total suffering of the wild and is on that count good, while protecting or restoring habitat is on that count bad. (7) And if fewer is better, then the best outcome of all is a world with no wild animals in it — the elimination of wild animal suffering by the elimination of the animals. (8) So clearing rainforest, draining marshes, and otherwise shrinking the wild population is a moral improvement, and conservation is a moral error. (9) But (7) and (8) are monstrous: the same reasoning, applied consistently, would license the deliberate extinction of every wild species, and applied to human beings would license killing anyone whose life is on balance miserable.

I can see that the argument is meant to be a reductio of something, and I cannot find the step that deserves it. I am attached to (3) — I think the asymmetry between coming into existence and never existing is real and I do not want to surrender it — and I am also inclined to think that the sheer number of suffering beings has to count for something, which is what makes (4) tempting. My question is this: is the argument valid, in which case which single premise is least costly to abandon, and what does abandoning it cost me elsewhere? Or is there a step that only looks valid under a reading the earlier premises do not actually support? I am especially unsure about the move from (3), which is a claim about not creating lives, to (6), which is a claim about destroying lives that already exist, and about whether treating fewer animals as a proxy for less suffering survives contact with how populations actually respond to habitat loss — the mortality pulse during the destruction, the rebound afterward, and the shift in species composition toward the small, fast-breeding animals that seem worst off. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (3) and (4) than (1), but I want to know what I am buying and what the alternatives cost.
