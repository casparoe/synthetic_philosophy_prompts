ok, probably a dumb question, but this has been bugging me for about a year and I can't find the name for it. 3rd year PhD in ecology, I model populations, and I've been reading philosophy of science on the side because something about how we talk about our models doesn't add up to me.

Here's the pattern. Take a model everyone agrees is wrong in ways everyone can list — well-mixed population, identical individuals, no evolution on the timescale, constant environment. It works. People cite it, build on it, it predicts fine. Then someone builds the realistic version: spatial structure, individual variation, the mechanism we all know is actually operating. And it's worse. Not just harder to solve, I'd understand that, that's just maths. It fits the data worse, and it's less illuminating to read — the extra machinery is true and it somehow buries the thing you could see before. So the extra truth cost us prediction and understanding both, wich isn't the usual simplicity/tractability tradeoff I keep reading about.

Second half, this is what actually bothers me. The same assumption gets opposite verdicts for no reason I can point at. Assuming individuals are identical is a harmless simplification in one paper and the first thing a reviewer kills you for in another. Two models of the same system, one assuming individuals know nothing about each other, one assuming perfect information, both fit fine, both published, nobody troubled. And the same false assumption gets used as a defence and as an objection in the same conversation — "the mechanism is right even if the assumptions are false" versus "the mechanism can't be the one operating, because the assumptions are false." When I ask what makes the difference the answer is always "it depends what the model is for," but nobody can tell me what about the purpose makes one distortion fine and the other fatal, and the same person gives me both verdicts depending on whose paper it is.

I know the words idealization, abstraction, toy model. I don't think those are it. What I'm after is the relational bit: the same omission being innocent in one model and a howler in another, where the difference tracks the job the model is doing rather than any property of the assumption itself. Is there a name for that? Is it just the how-possibly / how-actually distinction? Is there a term for the failure mode where adding a true mechanism makes the model worse? I've also been told it's all old news because of that famous economics line about unrealistic assumptions — the one where only predictions matter and assumptions can be as unrealistic as you like — but that can't be the whole story, since we obviously do treat some assumptions as fatal.

If there's no name, coin me one, with a definition and boundary cases. Does it matter whether the realism was left out for tractability or because the modeller never thought of it? And is it the same phenomenon when two accepted models rest on assumptions that flatly contradict each other?
