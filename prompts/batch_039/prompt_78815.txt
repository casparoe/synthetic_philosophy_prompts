A seminar argument I have been carrying around seems to prove something I cannot accept, and I cannot find the step that lets me out.

(1) A state is conscious just in case its content is broadcast to a global workspace — made available to a wide range of consumer systems that can use it to guide report, memory, and action.
(2) Availability is a functional property: a state is available just in case the system's organization routes it to those consumers.
(3) Organization is substrate-neutral. If two systems compute the same mapping from inputs to the states that guide report, memory, and action, then the same states are available in both — and anything computed with feedback loops can also be computed without them, by a purely feedforward system with enough layers.
(4) A large enough lookup table, or a nation of people passing notes according to fixed rules, can compute that mapping.
(5) So there is something it is like to be the lookup table, and something it is like to be the nation.
(6) There is nothing it is like to be a lookup table.

I would rather keep (6) than (5), and I would rather keep the global-workspace picture as an account of access than keep (5). Tell me which premise or step to give up, why it looked fine, and what giving it up costs. If the answer is that (1) was never about phenomenal consciousness at all, say what remains of the theory as an account of experience; if the answer is that (3) fails, say what more than function is required and how we could ever tell that it is present.
