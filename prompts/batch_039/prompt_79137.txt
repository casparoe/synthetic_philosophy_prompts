ok so I'm in this metaphysics reading group and next week it's material constitution — the statue and the lump of clay, the old one where a sculptor takes a lump that was already sat there on the table, shapes it into a statue, and now you've got two things in the exact same place made of the exact same clay. The lump was there before the statue existed and it'll still be there after; squash the statue and the lump survives, just uglier; and yet there's only one lot of clay. I said I'd open the session with a poem, so: give me a pantoum. Sixteen lines, four quatrains, the usual rule where lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the closing quatrain folds back so two lines from the opening stanza come round again at the end.

Two speakers, the lump and the statue, and I want them sharing lines. Every repeated line has to be something the lump can say and the statue can say, meaning something different in each mouth, without changing a single word when it comes round again — if you have to tweak the wording so it fits the second speaker, thats cheating and the whole point is gone. So the form is doing the work: same stuff, said twice, two things.

It needs to be right about the case, not just evocative. The lump predates the statue and outlives it, the statue is essentially that shape while the lump only happens to be it, squashing the statue leaves the lump standing, and the two of them have every particle in common so nothing physical can tell them apart. Then land on the bit that actually keeps me up: if they're physically identical in every respect, what makes it true that one of them is essentially a statue and the other isn't? I want that left as the open question at the end — don't answer it, don't name any philosophers, no citations, no Latin, no jargon my potter mate wouldn't use.

British English please. And don't let it drift off into a poem about change in general or being and becoming — it should stay on the coincidence. If it isn't obvious which line is carrying the weight, add one sentence after the poem saying which one shifts meaning between the two mouths.
