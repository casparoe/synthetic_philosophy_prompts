I am preparing a talk for a two-day workshop on superrationality and cooperation, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the session that follows it. The audience is unusually mixed, and the mix is the point. Roughly a third are decision theorists who work on causal and evidential decision theory and know the Newcomb and smoking-lesion cases inside out. A third are experimental game theorists who doubt that any of this predicts how people actually behave in a laboratory. The remaining third are rationalists for whom the two puzzles below are the cleanest demonstration that ordinary expected-utility reasoning fails and that one should cooperate with one's own mirror images; they tend to reason in terms of choosing as though one were choosing for every agent in one's epistemic situation, a view sometimes labelled updateless or functional decision theory. My talk states its thesis in the first five minutes and spends the rest of its length defending it, so the questions will be aimed at the thesis.

The puzzles, briefly, so that you pitch the replies at the right level of detail. In the Platonia dilemma a trillionaire gathers twenty people and promises a billion dollars to whoever sends him a telegram before noon the next day, on condition that exactly one of the twenty does so; if two or more send, or none does, nobody receives anything, and the players are forbidden to coordinate. The superrational recommendation is usually given as: each player sends with probability one in twenty, that being the choice which maximises the chance that exactly one telegram arrives. In the Luring Lottery, which was reportedly run as a real contest for readers of a science magazine in the 1980s, entrants sent in a postcard bearing a number that counted as that many entries; the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, and it went to the holder of a randomly chosen entry. The superrational recommendation was that each entrant submit exactly one entry; in the event, many entrants submitted astronomically large numbers, the prize was diluted to nothing, and the contest collapsed.

My thesis is that these two puzzles are a poor showcase for superrationality, and that their apparent success depends on sliding between two different things that might be maximised. In the Platonia dilemma the recommended prescription is obtained by maximising the group's expected payoff, and it is not stable under unilateral deviation: if the other nineteen really are sending with probability one in twenty, then sending with certainty is worth close to 380 million dollars to me while not sending is worth nothing, so the prescription directs me to do the one thing my own situation gives me overwhelming reason not to do. The only symmetric equilibrium of that game, incidentally, is that all twenty send and nobody wins. The Luring Lottery opens the same gap in a different shape: one entry each is nobody's best response to anybody, and best-response reasoning runs away without limit, which is precisely what the entrants discovered. My conclusion is not that superrationality is incoherent, but that these puzzles establish something far narrower than they are advertised to establish: that some situations admit no individually stable cooperative solution, which is a fact about those situations rather than a discovery about the nature of rationality.

Prepare the questions I will actually face from these three groups, ordered by the damage they would do if I fumbled them, each phrased the way that part of the audience would genuinely phrase it, with a two-minute spoken reply, a frank note on where the reply is thin, and a note on what I should concede outright rather than defend. Do not pad the list with objections any audience would raise; I want the ones that are specific to these commitments. End with the single question I am hoping nobody asks. There is no need for citations or reading suggestions, since the audience knows the literature and I will supply the references myself.
