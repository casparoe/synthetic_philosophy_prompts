I am currently enrolled in an advanced undergraduate seminar in epistemology, and I have been asked to design a diagnostic instrument for our unit on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. By higher-order evidence I mean evidence about the reliability of one's own belief-forming processes, and by epistemic akrasia I mean the condition of believing something while believing that one's belief in it is epistemically irrational or unsupported. I would like a quiz that sorts my classmates by the positions they actually hold on the relation between first-order belief and higher-order evidence about their own reliability, rather than by the labels they would choose for themselves. Please write the quiz.

It should consist of ten concrete cases. Each case should describe an agent who has formed a first-order belief on some basis and then receives higher-order evidence—for example, a calibration test showing systematic overconfidence in that domain, a peer's disagreement, a diagnosis of cognitive fatigue, a drug that the agent learns impairs reasoning, or a track record of errors in a particular kind of inference. Each question should offer two or three answers that differ over what the agent may or must believe, how much confidence the agent may retain, whether the agent is criticizable for retaining the belief, and whether the agent's overall doxastic state can be rational. Phrase every question so that a respondent can answer it without knowing which position the answer is supposed to support, and do not let any question simply ask whether the respondent is a conciliationist, a steadfast theorist, or an epistemic akratic.

Then provide a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions. The key should include at least the following, characterized by their commitments rather than only by name: the conciliationist view that higher-order evidence is genuine evidence and must be given weight alongside the first-order evidence; the steadfast view that a peer's disagreement need not reduce the force of one's original evidence; the right reasons view that the first-order evidence alone fixes what one ought to believe, so higher-order evidence bears only on blameworthiness or on whether one is justified in holding the belief; the total evidence view, on which both orders of evidence count but neither simply replaces the other; the level-splitting view that one can rationally believe p while believing that one's belief in p is not rationally supported; and the no-level-splitting view that such a combination is necessarily irrational. For each position, add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. If any of these positions come apart only under a distinction I have not built into the cases—for instance, between evidence about the reliability of a faculty and evidence about the reliability of a particular inference, or between propositional and doxastic justification—flag that explicitly rather than collapsing them.

Include also a short section on combinations of answers that no actual position occupies, and for each combination say whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, identify which positions the quiz cannot separate from one another and why, and propose one or two additional cases that would separate them if I wanted to expand the quiz. The quiz is for advanced undergraduates in a seminar, so it should be challenging but should not depend on knowledge of any particular published article; I care about the inferential structure of the positions, not about who said what.
