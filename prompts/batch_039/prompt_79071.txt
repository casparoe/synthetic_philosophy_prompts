So me and my mate had a two-hour argument last night about whether nature is this beautiful balanced whole or basically a meat grinder, and he kept citing Princess Mononoke at me like it settles it -- the forest gives and takes, the Forest Spirit kills and revives, everything eats everything, so who are we to interfere. I love the film, but I think that reading lets it off the hook, and what I actually want is for you to read it against the philosophy of wild animal suffering, not the usual "Ghibli = environmentalism" thing.

Here's my problem. In that whole literature -- the stuff arguing that the suffering of animals in nature, predation and starvation and disease and exposure, is an enormous amount of disvalue that we mostly don't even register -- the hard question is what, if anything, follows from that, and the film seems to answer it with a mood. The boar god at the start isn't suffering because of nature: he's got an iron ball Eboshi put in him, and the pain has literally turned him into a demon. Okkoto and the boars march on Irontown because they'd rather die fighting than dwindle. San and Moro are magnificent and they're also predators. The Forest Spirit is life and death in one animal. So does the film actually take a position on whether any of this suffering is bad in itself and whether anyone ought to do anything about it, or does it just make it beautiful and sad and call that balance? And where the film does seem to have a view -- pain turning into curses, hatred as a wound that spreads -- is that view closer to the animal-suffering position than the film's human characters are? (Ashitaka's arm is almost a little argument about how suffering propagates. I keep thinking about that and I dont know if I'm reading too much in.)

I don't want the film flattened into an illustration of the thesis, so be honest about where it resists the reading. It's genuinely sympathetic to Eboshi and Irontown, and it refuses to make the animals into victims -- that might be a real ethical stance or it might be exactly what stops the film from saying anything.

Finish by telling me whether the film adds something to the debate that a philosophy paper doesn't, or whether it mostly muddles it. I'd honestly rather get "it muddles it" than a fake resolution.
