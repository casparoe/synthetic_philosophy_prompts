I'm supposed to be writing a reply to a commenter for a grad seminar on epistemic rationality, and in drafting it I've realised that the position I've been defending all year might be a demolition site. So before I embarrass myself in public, here's my actual view set, as honestly as I can state it: (1) accuracy is the only thing that's good in itself epistemically — being closer to the truth with my credences is the whole of epistemic value, and coherence, calibration, understanding and explanatory power are at best useful because they tend to get me closer to the truth. (2) Accuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule, and I genuinely don't think it matters which one; if a norm only falls out with the Brier score and not with the log score then it isn't a norm of rationality, it's an artifact of a bookkeeping choice, so I want the whole theory to be invariant across strictly proper rules — I've been calling that rule-invariance and I'd give up a lot to keep it. (3) Probabilism is a real requirement and it has to be justified accuracy-first: Dutch book arguments are pragmatic and I want nothing to do with them, and I don't believe coherence is somehow constitutive of credence. (4) Conditionalization is also a real requirement, and the reason is the expected-accuracy argument — the update rule that maximises expected accuracy is conditionalizing on your evidence, so that's what rationality demands of you. (5) The engine of that argument is immodesty, i.e. you can't rationally be in a state that by its own lights expects some alternative state to be more accurate than it is, and I think immodesty is independently obvious — more obvious than anything it's used to justify. (6) I don't think rationality has any irreducably diachronic or plan-level component: a time-slice is rational just in case its credence function maximises expected accuracy under a strictly proper rule, full stop, and the idea that there's a further question about how a time-slice should defer to or constrain later time-slices strikes me as either reducible to (1)-(5) or as smuggling in pragmatic considerations through the back door. (7) I also think there are full beliefs and not just credences, and that a binary belief state is every bit as scoreable for accuracy as a credence function is, so accuracy-first has no trouble accommodating belief at all, whatever my friend keeps saying. (8) And I hold the threshold view: believing just is being confident enough, around 0.9, and I think that threshold is a requirement of rationality rather than a convenient fiction, so the preface "paradox" isn't a paradox, it's just what you should expect once you stop conflating belief with certainty.
