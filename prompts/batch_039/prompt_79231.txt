I am a novelist at work on a book about a prime minister who authorizes a missile strike on a house where the man she believes is planning a massacre is sleeping. The strike kills him and thirty-eight other people, nearly all of them guests at a wedding, and it very probably prevents an attack that would kill several thousand. The plotting I can manage. What I cannot settle is what she is meant to feel in the months afterward — and two friends who read the manuscript have taken positions so opposed that I have given up trying to referee them myself.

The first, a former foreign-service officer, holds that she must understand she has done wrong. The strike may well have been justified, he says — perhaps it was the least wrongful thing available to her — but justified and right are not the same, and the distance between them is the whole of what she has to live with. The residue is the point: the moral order is supposed to leave a mark on the person who overrides it, and the mark is guilt. A leader who orders such a thing and sleeps soundly is not sturdier than one who is haunted; he is worse. He adds that a soldier broken by what the mission required is not malfunctioning — the wound is the truth of the act — and that the guilt ought to be public as well as private, because a country that lets its leader bury the dead quietly has let itself off too.

The second, a lawyer who has spent her career on accountability cases, calls this a confusion dressed up as seriousness. If the strike was the best thing available, then it was not wrong, and one cannot owe guilt for having done right. What the prime minister feels, on her account, is horror and grief, which a terrible act properly provokes; but guilt is the fitting response to wrongdoing, so to demand it of her is to demand that she believe something false about what she did. Worse, she says, the residue view is a gift to every torturer and assassin — it hands them a story in which they are at once criminals and saviors — and it turns a question about what a state may do into a private drama about one official's soul. The real questions are whether the act was justified and whether the institutions that permitted it are answerable.

The first friend answers that without residue, morality is a ledger of consequences with extra steps; the prohibition on killing the innocent must keep standing even when it is overridden, or it was never a prohibition, only a steep price. The second replies that this proves too much: the prohibition does keep standing — that is precisely why the choice is tragic and why she calls the strike the least wrongful option — but standing is not the same as violated-and-still-owed. And she adds a parting shot: if residue attaches to acts but not to omissions, then the prime minister who cancels the strike, keeps her hands clean, and lets the thousands die carries no residue at all on his account — so his view quietly licenses the clean-handed catastrophe.

I need to write the character's interior life, and I cannot adjudicate this alone. Please work through the exchange move by move: who is right about what, where each of them overreaches, and what each is missing. I do not want a verdict alone — I want to know which of the particular claims I should accept and which I should let go, and why.
