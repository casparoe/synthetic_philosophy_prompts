Two weeks from now I'm up at our decision theory reading group and I'm already dreading the Q&A, so I want a prep sheet. The thesis I'm defending, roughly: the usual way of stating superrationality — in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between two identical reasoners you should cooperate because your reasoning and theirs is the same reasoning — is either trivially true or plainly false depending on what "identical" is doing, and the fix is to drop the talk about identity and model the two players as having decision procedures drawn independently from a shared prior over procedures. Once you set it up that way, cooperating in a one-shot PD is just the expected-value-maximizing move, the spooky "logical correlation" between the two choices turns into an ordinary conditional probability, and the real work becomes saying what licenses the shared prior. If the prior is just a fact about the world, I've reinvented evidential decision theory with extra steps. If it's a constraint on which decision procedures count as rational, then I owe an account of why rationality has that constraint — and that's the part I'm least sure of.

The room is eight people and I know alot of what's coming. Three are economists who do game theory and think "rational" just means "no profitable unilateral deviation"; any refinement that needs a story about the players' algorithms looks to them like either an empty relabeling or repeated-game logic smuggled into a one-shot game. Two are committed causal decision theorists who've both worked on Newcomb's problem and think conditioning on your own action is a mistake, period. One works on team reasoning / we-mode and thinks the whole "what should I do" framing is the error — the question should be what we should do. One is a logician who will ask how a finite agent can condition on facts about its own decision procedure without self-reference trouble. And one does bounded rationality and thinks the setup quietly assumes players with perfect introspective access to their own algorithms.

Flag any two of them that are really one objection wearing different hats.
