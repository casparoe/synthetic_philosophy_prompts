I am to give a short speech at my parents' fortieth wedding anniversary. The room divides, and has divided for a decade, over one disputed question about my aunt's death: my mother's relatives have simply never encountered the evidence that tells against their view and would most likely revise it if they did, while my father's relatives have been taught that anyone who contradicts them is protecting the hospital, so any evidence I produce will be taken as proof that I too have been bought. Write the speech for me. I am not willing to settle the question — that is not what the evening is for — but nor am I willing to stand up and declare that nobody can know, which sounds like modesty only to people who are not in an echo chamber and like a fresh insult to those who are. The speech must be honest, warm and deliverable in about three minutes; it must not turn the trust either half of the room places in me into a lever for the conclusions I happen to hold; and, while nobody present has heard of the distinction between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber, the speech should still show why the remedy that would suit one half of the room is useless, or worse, for the other. Write in British English, and add two sentences afterwards identifying the move you are relying on.
