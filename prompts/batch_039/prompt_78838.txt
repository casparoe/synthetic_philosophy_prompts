Two weeks from now our school's exchange programme holds its end-of-year dinner, and I have been asked to give the closing speech: five minutes, to next year's outgoing students, their parents, a few of our teachers, and — because they flew over for it — the host families from the town in northern Italy where I spent last year, including the family I lived with. The difficulty is that the speech is expected to do something I have gradually come to think is nearly impossible, namely prepare the new students for the place by telling them what the people there are like. My philosophy teacher gave us a handout that sorted claims of that sort into three kinds: claims about how some trait is distributed across a population, claims about what a country's institutions and everyday rules reward or punish, and claims about what the people are like, as she put it, in their hearts. Her argument was that only the first two can be checked against anything, and that only the third is what people normally mean by national character. She also warned me that in a speech the third kind creeps back in through tone even when you never state it, and that the people who notice this most sharply are the ones being described — which, at this particular dinner, is a table of Italians who have been extremely kind to me. My friends tell me I am overthinking it and that I should simply be nice.

Please write the speech. It needs to give next year's students something genuinely useful and concrete — I think that has to come from the first two kinds of claim, and probably from saying what to do when a prior meets an actual person — while never ascribing a character to a people, and without turning into a lecture about the distinction: the whole point is that the speech should enact the discipline rather than announce it. It has to work as a warm, funny, grateful speech for the parents and teachers and especially for the host families, who will be listening for how they are being summarised, and it should leave me able to answer honestly when, afterwards, my host father asks what I really thought of them. Please write the speech itself rather than an outline, and please put no quotations in it — I would rather it contain none than one I get wrong in front of my teachers.
