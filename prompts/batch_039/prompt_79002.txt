Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about whether "the alignment problem" names one problem or several, and the argument ended the way such arguments do: we discovered we were using the same phrase for different things, and then argued about whether that mattered.

His claim was that the phrase is a bundle of at least four separable targets — getting a system to do what its principal actually wants rather than what was literally specified; keeping it doing that as capability grows and training selects for whatever scores well; getting its internal representation of the objective to survive distributional shift; and keeping multiple systems with divergent objectives from eroding one another's corrigibility. He thinks the last is not even the same kind of problem as the first three, and that a lot of apparent disagreement in the field is people answering different questions under one label. I think the bundle is not accidental: all four share a structure, in that the difficulty in each case comes from the gap between the space in which we can state what we want and the space in which the system optimizes. That is why they feel like one problem even when they are not.

I have been asked to help a funder who can support roughly eight to ten researchers for three years on conceptual alignment work — philosophy, decision theory, formal epistemology, some theory-adjacent mathematics, but no empirical ML engineering. So I need to turn this argument into a funding agenda.

Give me a prioritized list of the open conceptual questions that most deserve those researcher-years, with the reasoning in front of the list rather than behind it. For each: why it is upstream of other work rather than downstream of it; how tractable it is for people whose tools are arguments and toy models rather than GPUs; what it would mean for it to be answered at all; and what developments over the next two or three years should move it up or down the list. I care about the dependency structure more than the list itself — which questions, if settled, would settle or dissolve others, and which are load-bearing for the whole agenda even though nobody enjoys working on them.

Then address the question we were arguing about, as a component of the agenda rather than a preliminary to it. Should anyone be paid to work on whether alignment is one problem or several? Is that taxonomy work upstream — a genuine prerequisite for dividing labor sensibly — or is it the kind of question that feels foundational and mostly functions as displacement activity? If it is upstream, say what a good decomposition would look like and what would count as a criterion for one being correct rather than merely convenient. My own proposal is that solving every part must entail solving the whole, and that each part must be independently attackable, but I suspect that criterion is either too weak or too strong, and I want to know which.

Finally, tell me what would change your ranking. If the ordering depends on an empirical bet — about how training scales, about whether deceptive behavior emerges, about whether interpretability matures — name the bet, and say what the ranking looks like under the alternative.

I do not want a reading list or a survey of who believes what. I want an argument about where the effort should go, and what would make you revise it.
