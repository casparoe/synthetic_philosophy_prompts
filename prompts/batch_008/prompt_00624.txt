A friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing over whether "weakness of will" names anything beyond what behavioral economists call hyperbolic discounting. Her view, following Ainslie and Laibson, was that the smoker who resolves at breakfast to quit and lights up after dinner is not exhibiting some special rational failure — she is simply discounting the future more steeply as the reward nears, exactly as a hyperbolic discount curve predicts, so there is nothing further for philosophers to explain. I resisted this, drawing on Richard Holton's account: weakness of will is specifically a failure to abide by a resolution one still endorses, and that notion does not obviously reduce to any pattern of time-inconsistent preference. Neither of us gave ground.

Could you help settle the matter by constructing two short cases: (1) an unambiguous instance of hyperbolic discounting that should not count as weakness of will (akrasia) in the traditional philosophical sense, and (2) an unambiguous case of weakness of will that involves no hyperbolic discounting, or indeed any intertemporal trade-off, at all. For each, specify exactly which feature of the rival definitions does the work of separating it from the other phenomenon, and close with your own verdict on whether akrasia and hyperbolic discounting pick out genuinely distinct phenomena or are ultimately just two vocabularies for the same underlying pattern.
