For my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy, I'm investigating whether transitivity is really a requirement of rational preference, and I've hit a wrinkle in the reading that I can't fully untangle on my own. In one of our early meetings, my EE supervisor walked me through the standard money-pump argument for why cyclic preferences are irrational: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and yet C to A, then starting from A you would pay a small fee to trade up to C (since you prefer C to A), then another small fee to trade up to B (since you prefer B to C), and then, since you prefer A to B after all, a further small fee to trade back to A — leaving you exactly where you started, only poorer. She presented this as more or less settling the question, but when I did some further reading over break, I found that the philosophical literature treats the argument as considerably shakier than that, and I don't think I understand the disagreement well enough yet to write about it responsibly.

In particular, I kept running into a distinction between agents who choose "myopically" (getting pumped because they only ever look one trade ahead), agents who choose in a "sophisticated" way (who see the whole sequence coming and simply refuse, partway through, the trade that would set them up for the eventual loss, even though that trade looks attractive taken in isolation), and agents who choose in a "resolute" way — a term I gather traces back to a philosopher named McClennen, who apparently argued that an agent can commit at the outset to an entire plan and simply stick to it, so that they never have to make an in-the-moment choice that goes against their standing preferences. If either of the latter two strategies actually works, it seems like the money pump only ever catches naive choosers, not cyclic preferences as such. I also came across a passing mention of later theorists trying to construct a cleverer version of the pump specifically to defeat sophisticated and resolute agents too, but I couldn't find anything that explained how that is supposed to work in terms I could actually follow.

Could you help me get a real handle on this? Specifically, I would like you to (1) explain clearly what distinguishes sophisticated from resolute choice as responses to the pump, and tell me whether you think either one is quietly cheating — for instance, by smuggling in something other than the agent's own preferences to decide what happens at each step; and (2) explain whether there is a legitimate way to redesign the money pump so that it still threatens agents who choose with full foresight, rather than just naive ones. I would rather you work through the actual mechanics of the arguments than give me an even-handed survey of "some philosophers think X, others think Y," since I need to take an actual position in my essay.
