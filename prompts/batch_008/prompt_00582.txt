I've been trying to work through Bayesian approaches to rational belief on my own, and I keep running into something called the problem of logical omniscience. As best I can tell, any agent whose degrees of belief obey the standard probability axioms must assign credence 1 to every logical and mathematical truth, and must count as already "knowing" every logical consequence of what it believes, with no actual reasoning required to get there. I understand why no real person believes things this way, but I don't understand why this is treated as a genuinely deep problem for the theory of rationality, rather than just a harmless idealization we accept for convenience, the way physicists accept frictionless planes. Could you explain what's really at stake in the problem of logical omniscience, and give me some intuition for why it has proven so hard to build a formal model of rational belief that avoids it without sacrificing whatever made probability theory attractive as an account of rationality in the first place?
