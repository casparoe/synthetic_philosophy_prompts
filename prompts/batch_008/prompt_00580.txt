A few of us on my team have a running Slack channel where we share whatever we've been reading about AI safety, mostly for fun, and last week someone linked an old forum post about something called the "commitment races problem." The basic idea, as I understood it, is that sufficiently strategic agents (the post was framed around future AI systems, but the logic seemed to apply just as well to people, companies, or governments) have a strong incentive to lock in aggressive, irreversible commitments as early as possible, even before they've fully thought through the situation or settled on their own values, because whoever credibly commits first can force everyone else to bend around them. The unsettling part is what happens when two such agents reason this way at the same time, in ignorance of each other: each can rush into a commitment that is perfectly sensible on its own terms but jointly incompatible with the other's, so that they lock themselves into a disaster that neither would have chosen if they'd simply waited and talked first. It reminded me a little of two drivers in a game of chicken each welding their own steering wheel in place before either one has even seen the other car coming.

I'd like to understand this dynamic through a story rather than more decision theory, so could you write me a short piece of fiction that dramatizes a commitment race along these lines? I'll leave the setting entirely up to you — two AI systems encountering each other for the first time, a tense labor negotiation, two nations, two colony ships haggling over the same resource, or something else entirely — but I'd like the underlying mechanics to come through in the plot and characters' choices rather than in explanation: why the pressure to commit early and half-blindly arises, what actually makes a given commitment "credible" rather than a threat that can quietly be walked back, and how the race to get there first can trap even intelligent, basically well-meaning parties into a catastrophe that each of them recognizes only slightly too late to stop. A few pages is plenty; I'm not after anything novel-length, just something with enough narrative weight that the logic of the trap actually lands.
