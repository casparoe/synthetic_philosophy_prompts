I've been slowly working through Quine's *Theories and Things* this year, partly prompted by a LessWrong-adjacent reading group I'm part of that tends to treat naturalized epistemology as more or less settled background for how to think about everything else, ethics included. Tucked into the middle of the collection is "On the Nature of Moral Values" (1978), apparently the only essay Quine ever devoted entirely to ethics. It's a strange, thin piece next to the rest of his corpus: he spends most of it comparing the relative "uniformity" of moral values, aesthetic taste, and language across cultures (his verdict is that morality is far more convergent than aesthetics or language, though still less convergent than science), and then argues that ethics is methodologically much shakier than science, because moral disagreements can only be adjudicated by appeal to other moral standards in a closed coherentist loop, whereas scientific disagreements can eventually be forced to confront a recalcitrant experience at the periphery of the web of belief. He ends up more or less signing on to Stevenson-style emotivism: even where disagreement about ultimate ends is irreducible, all we can do is keep calling the good "good" and hope the word does its emotive work.

What's been bugging me is that this sits badly with the rest of Quine's system, and I don't think the tension gets discussed much outside fairly specialized corners of the secondary literature. As I understand it secondhand, via a summary of a reply Morton White apparently wrote, White's objection was that Quine had already spent his career dismantling exactly this kind of dualism: no sharp analytic/synthetic line, no theory-neutral observation language, nothing tested in isolation from the "corporate body" of the whole web. If moral sentiment can play a role in that web analogous to the role sensory irritation plays for empirical belief — a peripheral contact point that beliefs are answerable to, even if only imperfectly and holistically — then the fact/value split should arguably have gone the way of the analytic/synthetic split, leaving Quine some sort of naturalized moral cognitivist rather than an emotivist. Quine, as far as I can tell, never accepted this move, and I can't tell whether that's because the disanalogy he points to (no moral observation sentences, no mechanism forcing convergence) is actually principled, or whether he just declined to follow his own holism where it led.

Could you write an essay working through this tension for me? I'd like you to reconstruct both sides as strongly as you can: the case that a consistent Quinean naturalist, given everything else Quine believed about holistic theory-testing, should have naturalized ethics right along with the rest of the web; and the case that Quine's disanalogy is principled, so that ethics really is stuck being methodologically second-class in just the way he says. Then I'd like you to actually take a side rather than concluding that "it depends" — if your answer needs qualification, make the qualification specific (something like "consistent Quinean naturalism supports cognitivism about X but not about Y"), rather than leaving the question open.
