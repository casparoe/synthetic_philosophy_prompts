I've been reading about the replication crisis for a research methods class, and there's a story that keeps coming up which seems like it should definately settle the whole frequentist vs Bayesian debate, except apparently people still argue about it, so I feel like I'm missing something. The case is Daryl Bem's 2011 precognition study, where he got p < .05 in eight out of nine experiments for something that's basically impossible (people supposedly "feeling" random future events before they happen). Wagenmakers and some co-authors redid the analysis using a Bayes factor instead of p-values and found the evidence was weak to nonexistent, even though by normal significance-testing standards Bem's results looked like a slam dunk. To me that seems like pretty solid proof that p < .05 is just a bad, gameable bar and Bayesian stats are the actual fix — like if journals had required Bayes factors instead of p-values from the start, wouldn't we have avoided basically the whole replication crisis (power pose, ego depletion, all the social priming stuff)?

I brought this up with a friend who's doing her PhD in stats, expecting her to agree, but she got kind of cagey and just said something like "it's not that simple, priors are doing a lot of work there too" without really explaining what she meant. Is she just being difficult, or is there an actual reason why "switch everyone to Bayesian analysis" isn't the obvious slam-dunk solution to the replication crisis that the Bem case makes it look like?
