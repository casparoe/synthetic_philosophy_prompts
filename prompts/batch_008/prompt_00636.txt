I'm researching a feature on the intellectual origins of our current fights over data privacy and surveillance, and I've been re-reading Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis's 1890 Harvard Law Review article "The Right to Privacy" (4 Harv. L. Rev. 193) — the piece often credited with introducing the legal concept of privacy into American law. After building their case through a long survey of copyright and trade-secret precedents concerning unpublished letters, diaries, and the like, Warren and Brandeis arrive at this passage, meant to state the underlying principle:

"These considerations lead to the conclusion that the protection afforded to thoughts, sentiments, and emotions, expressed through the medium of writing or of the arts, so far as it consists in preventing publication, is merely an instance of the enforcement of the more general right of the individual to be let alone. It is like the right not to be assaulted or beaten, the right not to be imprisoned, the right not to be maliciously prosecuted, the right not to be defamed. In each of these rights, as indeed in all other rights recognized by the law, there inheres the quality of being owned or possessed—and (as that is the distinguishing attribute of property) there may be some propriety in speaking of those rights as property. But, obviously, they bear little resemblance to what is ordinarily comprehended under that term. The principle which protects personal writings and all other personal productions, not against theft and physical appropriation, but against publication in any form, is in reality not the principle of private property, but that of an inviolate personality."

I have not been able to pin down what philosophical work "inviolate personality" is actually doing here. Is it (a) an independent concept — something like dignity or personhood — that genuinely explains why these disparate protections belong together and why they aren't really property, or is it (b) little more than a label retroactively attached to a pattern the authors had already noticed in the case law, in which case the argument risks circularity: it names the family of interests it needs to identify rather than giving independent criteria for what counts as a violation of it. Could you help me work through which reading the passage actually supports, and explain how that distinction bears on the later, quite different, philosophical debate over whether privacy is a basic, freestanding interest or — as Judith Jarvis Thomson would later argue — something reducible to more familiar rights over property and person?
