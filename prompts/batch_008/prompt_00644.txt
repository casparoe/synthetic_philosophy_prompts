Kant held that we have no direct duties to animals: lacking the rational self-consciousness that grounds moral personhood, they exist only as means and cannot, strictly speaking, be wronged. As he argues in the Metaphysics of Morals and in his student-transcribed lectures on ethics, any duty "regarding" animals is really an indirect duty to ourselves, since cruelty toward them erodes the sympathetic dispositions on which our direct duties to other human beings depend. His example is a dog or horse that has served faithfully: a master who discards it once it can no longer work does the animal no wrong, but reveals, and thereby cultivates in himself, a callousness that is morally objectionable.

Marine biologists have since amassed strong evidence that octopuses are cognitively sophisticated — using tools, solving novel problems, and displaying stable individual temperaments — despite an evolutionary lineage with no real counterpart among the mammals and birds whose minds have long been familiar to us. This evidence helped move the UK to grant octopuses legal recognition as sentient beings in 2022, and has fueled fierce scientific and public opposition to proposed industrial octopus farms, including to slaughter methods such as chilling live animals in an ice slurry, which critics argue may not produce a swift loss of consciousness.

Reasoning strictly from Kant's own principles, rather than from later, more animal-friendly reconstructions of Kantian ethics, what verdict would he most plausibly reach on the ethics of industrial octopus farming? Address whether his indirect-duty argument depends on a felt analogy between the animal and familiar human conduct, of the kind his dog example trades on, and, if so, whether the octopus's evolutionary and psychological remoteness from human life is enough to place such farming outside the reach of his framework altogether.
