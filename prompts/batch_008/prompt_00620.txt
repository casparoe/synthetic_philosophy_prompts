Hey, I've been down a rabbit hole reading old Society for Psychical Research material for a piece on the history of "extraordinary claims" debates, and I keep getting stuck on a bit from Sidgwick's opening address as the SPR's first president, back in 1882. Earlier in the speech he argues that society's blanket incredulity toward these phenomena is itself the real scandal, not the phenomena themselves. Then a bit later he lays out the society's actual investigative strategy like this (and yes, this gap is how the quote is usually reproduced, so its not just me messing up a copy-paste):

"We have done all that we can when the critic has nothing left to allege except that the investigator is in the trick. But when he has nothing else left he will allege that.... We must drive the objector into the position of being forced either to admit the phenomena as inexplicable, at least by him, or to accuse the investigators either of lying or cheating or of a blindness or forgetfulness incompatible with any intellectual condition except absolute idiocy."

I can't quite pin down whats doing the logical work here. Is this basically the same move as the old "liar, lunatic, or lord" trilemma for the resurrection — list a few options for the skeptic, make each one sound humiliating or ad hoc, then declare victory by elimination? Or is there something more defensible going on, given this is coming from a guy who was otherwise a pretty careful epistemologist? Could you break down the actual argument structure here, what hidden premises it needs in order to work, and whether "exhausting the skeptic's outs" like this really shifts the burden of proof or just smuggles in an unfalsifiability problem on Sidgwick's own side?
