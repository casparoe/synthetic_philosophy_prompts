I spent about thirty years as a radio astronomer before I retired, mostly cataloguing galaxy clusters and thinking in terms of billions of light years, so I like to think I'm not easily rattled by big numbers. But something I stumbled across recently in my new philosophy hobby has genuinely kept me up at night. A former student of mine, who's now deep into the effective altruism / rationalist online scene, pointed me toward an idea called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (it apparently used to go by the clunkier name "multiverse-wide superrationality," which I sort of prefer, it sounds more dramatic). As far as I can piece it together, the argument runs: if the universe (or multiverse) is big enough, it almost certainly contains vast numbers of agents who reason the way we do but who we can never contact or causally affect in any way whatsoever; and yet, because our decision-making is correlated with theirs, choosing to act cooperatively toward the values we share with that whole scattered population is, in some strange evidential sense, choosing it on their behalf too. You end up with this picture of countless civilisations, forever unable to signal one another, nevertheless coordinating their behaviour purely by each reasoning about how the others reason - a handshake with no hands, across a gap no signal could ever cross. I don't know if I'm explaining it right, but it definately rattled me more then most of what I've read this year.

I can't stop thinking this would make a wonderful premise for short fiction, the more idea-driven end of the genre, the kind of thing Greg Egan does, or the colder, more cosmic stretches of Liu Cixin's Three-Body books. Would you write me a short story (a few pages is plenty) built around this concept? I don't want a lecture wrapped in dialogue, I'd rather the ECL logic just be the load-bearing structure of the plot itself, maybe a character, or a whole civilisation, forced to decide, with everything at stake, whether to commit to a cooperative strategy whose payoff they can never confirm. I'm curious what happens to an idea that looks a bit bloodless on the page of a decision-theory paper once it's actually got a character standing inside it, having to live with the choice.
