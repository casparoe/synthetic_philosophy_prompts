I teach a philosophy of physics seminar, and every single year when we hit Everett's many-worlds interpretation someone brings up quantum suicide and the whole session dissolves into people arguing about whether they'd personally take a specific bet instead of actually thinking it through. So I want to outsource this to you and see if you can do better than my students usually manage. You know the setup, probably - Moravec and Marchal had versions of it back in the 80s but Tegmark's the one everyone actually cites: you sit in front of a device that performs a genuinely quantum measurement each trial and kills you instantly, no lingering, if it comes up the "wrong" way. The argument for quantum immortality is that since you're never around to have the experience of being in a branch where you died, subjectively you should expect, with certainty, to keep waking up alive no matter how many trials you run. Some people push this into an actual wager: rig the machine so a "win" pays a fortune and a "loss" just erases you painlessly, and ask whether a rational many-worlder should take that bet at arbitrarily terrible odds, since "you" will only ever be around afterward to spend the winnings.

Here's the part that never seems to survive contact with the reddit-tier version of this thought experiment, and it's what I actually want you to engage with. First, the whole argument is quietly doing naive branch-counting - treating "a branch where I survive" and "a branch where I don't" as symmetric possibilities to be indifferent between. But the Deutsch-Wallace decision-theoretic case for the Born rule is precisely the claim that rational credence in branching scenarios has to track amplitude-squared weight, not a head-count of branches, so it's not remotely obvious naive branch-counting is even a coherent notion of "my credence" to begin with. Second, David Lewis, in one of the last things he wrote (published in 2004, a few years after he actually died), pointed out that even granting the argument, conditionalizing on survival across many repeated trials should make you expect to end up increasingly crippled and decrepit rather then unscathed, since the "clean" surviving branches get squeezed out relative to the ones where you barely limped through as the trials pile up. So the immortality reading might be less "great news" and more a curse.

What I want is an actual essay that weighs these two complications against the naive case for the bet and reaches a real verdict, not a survey that ends with "there are considerations on both sides." Is it rational for a committed Everettian to take the quantum suicide wager, and if so, under what account of self-locating credence does that survive the amplitude-weighting objection? And seperately: if some version of quantum immortality is just true regardless of what's "rational" to bet on, is that actually good news given the Lewis point, or does it just mean death specifically is off the table while everything short of it stays fully in play? Give me an actual position and defend it - "yes, but only under X notion of credence" is a fine kind of answer, "who knows" is not.
