I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a good deal of time thinking about the puzzle that arises when a self-driving car or an autonomous weapon causes a death that no single person actually chose: the manufacturer could not have foreseen that exact failure, the machine is not the kind of thing we can praise or blame, and the operator had no real control over what happened in the moment. Writers on the subject often call this a "responsibility gap." In reading Aristotle's account of voluntary and involuntary action in the Nicomachean Ethics, I was struck by his discussion of so-called "mixed" acts, such as a sea captain who jettisons cargo during a storm: not something he would choose in itself, yet still voluntary in Aristotle's eyes, because the origin of the act lies in him and he understands exactly what he is doing. Working strictly from Aristotle's own criteria of compulsion, ignorance, and the origin of an action, how do you think he would judge a case in which a fully autonomous system causes a fatal harm that no human directly decided on? Would he treat the harm as simply involuntary and therefore no one's fault, or does his framework point to someone who bears responsibility in a way that today's debates tend to overlook?
