I've been slowly working through Augustine's early treatise "On Lying" (De Mendacio) after a long-running thread on a rationalist forum about honesty norms — specifically, about whether the wrongness of lying is best cashed out in terms of intent to deceive, or in terms of a stricter notion like "asserting what you yourself believe to be false," independent of intent. Going in, I had the standard picture of Augustine as an uncompromising rigorist on this topic: the theologian who, in the later Contra Mendacium, is usually read as holding that one may not lie even to protect an innocent person from a murderer, and may only fall back on silence or evasive non-answers. So I was thrown by the following passage from Chapter 4 of De Mendacio (translated by H. Browne, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series), which seems to take a far more permissive possibility seriously, and which I've found genuinely difficult to untangle even at the level of "who is doing what to whom":

"The one, who knows or thinks he says a false thing, and says it on purpose that he may not deceive: as, if he knows a certain road to be beset by robbers, and fearing lest some person for whose safety he is anxious should go by that road, which person he knows does not trust him, should tell him that that road has no robbers, on purpose that he may not go by it, as he will think there are robbers there precisely because the other has told him there are none, and he is resolved not to believe him, accounting him a liar. The other, who knowing or thinking that to be true which he says, says it on purpose that he may deceive: for instance, if he tells a person who does not believe him, that there are robbers in that road where he really knows them to be, that he to whom he tells it may the rather go by that road and so fall among robbers, because he thinks that to be false, which the other told him. Which then of these lies? The one who has chosen to say a false thing that he may not deceive? Or the other who has chosen to say a true thing that he may deceive? […] For the question is not now which of them sinned, but which of them lied: as indeed it is presently seen that the latter sinned, because by speaking a truth he brought it about that a person should fall among robbers, and that the former has not sinned, or even has done good, because by speaking a false thing he has been the means of a person's avoiding destruction."

Augustine then brackets the good or ill that each speaker intended, and reframes the question purely in terms of truth, falsehood, and will, working through four rival definitions of "lie" in turn:

"For if a lie is an utterance with will of uttering a false thing, that man has rather lied who willed to say a false thing, and said what he willed, albeit he said it of set purpose not to deceive. But if a lie is any utterance whatever with will to deceive; then not the former has lied, but the latter, who even in speaking truth willed to deceive. And if a lie is an utterance with will of any falsity, both have lied; because both the former willed his utterance to be false, and the latter willed a false thing to be believed concerning his utterance which was true. Further, if a lie is an utterance of a person wishing to utter a false thing that he may deceive, neither has lied; because both the former in saying a false thing had the will to make a true thing believed, and the latter to say a true thing in order that he might make a false thing believed."

Two things about this have me stuck, and I'd appreciate a careful reconstruction rather than the one-paragraph gloss on "Augustine and lying" you get from encyclopedia entries. First, purely as a matter of logic: could you spell out why Augustine treats it as a genuinely open question which of the two travelers lied, rather than this being an easy case of "the second one obviously lied and the first one obviously didn't"? I think I'm missing why his four candidate definitions actually come apart on this example rather than converging on the same verdict. Second, and more interesting to me: Augustine seems to concede outright that the man who told the beneficial falsehood "has not sinned, or even has done good." How is that remotely consistent with the absolutist position he's famous for? Is this passage best read as him trying on, and later rejecting, a more permissive view he associates with Old Testament casuists like Sarah and the Hebrew midwives, before he tightens his own definition of lying — or is there a stable distinction available to him, maybe between "lying" and "sinning," or between the material and formal aspects of a falsehood, that would actually let him consistently hold both that this particular liar did no wrong and that lying as such is always wrong?
