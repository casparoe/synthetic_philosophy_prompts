I'm working on my AP Research paper this year on theories of consciousness, and I've gone fairly deep into Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory (IIT). The core idea, as far as I can tell, is that a physical system counts as conscious to the extent that it has a lot of "integrated information," a quantity Tononi calls Φ, which roughly measures how much a system's behavior depends on it working as an irreducible whole rather than as a bundle of separate parts loosely wired together. The bigger a system's maximal Φ, the more conscious IIT says it is, and Tononi openly accepts that this commits him to a mild panpsychism, since even something as simple as a thermostat or a photodiode ends up with a tiny nonzero Φ. My AP Research teacher mentioned that 124 consciousness researchers signed an open letter in 2023 publicly calling IIT "pseudoscience," which struck me as an unusually harsh thing for scientists to do to a rival theory, so I started reading around to understand exactly what the critics found so objectionable.

The objection I keep getting stuck on is one raised by the computer scientist Scott Aaronson. He shows that you can build absurdly simple systems with enormous Φ values: for instance, a system that does nothing but multiply an input vector by a certain kind of matrix (the same mathematical trick behind the error-correcting codes used on CDs and DVDs), or, more physically, a network of basic logic gates wired up according to what mathematicians call an expander graph. Just by scaling these systems up, you can push their Φ far beyond any reasonable estimate of the human brain's, with no ceiling in sight. Since nobody seriously thinks a glorified error-correction circuit is having experiences, and since IIT explicitly ties degree of consciousness to the size of Φ, Aaronson treats this as a clean reductio: apply IIT's own formula and it tells you a piece of CD-player circuitry is more conscious than you are. He argues that this is exactly the kind of "common-sense" test a theory of consciousness ought to pass on simple, familiar cases before we trust it on the hard ones, the way a new physical theory is expected to reduce to ordinary Newtonian mechanics at everyday speeds and sizes.

What I'd like from you is not an explanation of why this objection is compelling, since I think I already follow that part, but the strongest case you can build for the opposite conclusion: that Aaronson's counterexample does not actually refute IIT. I'd like you to directly confront the "common-sense test" move rather than argue around it: is a theory of consciousness really obligated to match untrained intuitions about exotic edge cases like matrix multipliers and expander graphs, given that those intuitions were shaped exclusively by and for creatures with brains? And can IIT's defenders treat the panpsychism-adjacent conclusion as a genuine discovery to be accepted, rather than an absurdity to be avoided, in the way physicists eventually accepted that simultaneity is relative or that distant particles can be entangled, even though both once seemed absurd? If, after building this case as forcefully as you can, you still think it ultimately fails, I'd like to hear that too, but only once you've genuinely tried to make it succeed.
