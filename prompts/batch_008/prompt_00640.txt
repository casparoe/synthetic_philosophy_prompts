A friend and I spent a good chunk of last night arguing about epistemic akrasia after our seminar on formal epistemology spent a week on higher-order evidence, and neither of us really managed to convince the other, so I'd like a more careful outside take than we could produce ourselves.

The case that got us stuck was a variant of one from the reading. Picture a medical resident near the end of a thirty-hour shift, working out a weight-based drug dosage by hand. She's done this exact calculation hundreds of times and is genuinely excellent at it. Tonight nothing about the process feels any different from usual: she goes through the steps, gets a number, checks it once, and can't find an error. But she also knows, from her own training, that residents who've been awake this long get precisely this kind of calculation wrong roughly half the time, all while feeling just as confident and clear-headed as when they're rested. There's no calculator to double-check against and no colleague around, and re-deriving the number from scratch just reproduces the same confident feeling. My friend's position, following what she called the "Enkratic Principle" (she claimed Titelbaum defends a strong version of it, and that it's basically what Sophie Horowitz calls the "Non-Akrasia Constraint"), is that it's simply irrational for the resident to end up trusting the number while also believing her own reasoning is probably unreliable tonight, so rationality requires her to actually lower her confidence in the dosage, full stop. I pushed back that this throws away perfectly good first-order evidence: nothing about the fatigue changes what the arithmetic itself shows, so revising the belief looks like ignoring evidence sitting right in front of her, which seems just as irrational as ignoring the base-rate information about tired residents. We went back and forth on "Level-Splitting" views (I gather this is associated with people like Lasonen-Aarnio) that let her stay confident in the dosage while also believing her evidence probably doesn't support that confidence, but neither of us could really explain why that isn't just a cheap way of having it both ways.

Could you lay out, more rigorously than we managed: (1) why cases like this are thought to generate a genuine dilemma rather than dissolving once you "just update on the total evidence"; (2) what exactly the Level-Splitting response is offering as a way out, and in what sense it's supposed to respect all of the resident's evidence; and (3) why that response is usually taken to come with a serious cost via a "bootstrapping" problem — that is, why permitting this kind of split between first-order and higher-order belief, even in a single case, seems to commit the resident, if she keeps finding herself in similar situations, to reasoning her way back to full confidence that she's never actually impaired. If there's a good reply available to a Level-Splitter against that bootstrapping charge, I'd be curious to hear it too.
