For my philosophy of mind seminar we read Ned Block on the Turing test next to Scott Aaronsons essay on why complexity theory should matter to philosophers, and there's a disagreement in there I want to see fought out properly. Aaronson notes that a giant, merely computable lookup table (basically Block's "Blockhead") can pass a Turing test of any fixed length, so an argument that passing it is impossible has to invoke complexity (exponential vs polynomial resource growth), not mere computability -- and some people push further, saying the lookup table wouldn't be a mind while a compact, efficient program with the exact same input-output behavior would be, because of the efficiency. Write me a dialogue between two functionalists arguing about this: one says bringing efficiency in like that is a category error (functionalism was always about the abstract computation; efficiency's just engineering), the other says you have to build efficiency into "sameness of computation" or functionalism can't tell a mind from a lookup table. Make them actually engage each others points -- and have someone get stuck on the fact that complexity classes are technically about resource scaling across a whole family of growing problems, not one fixed table for one fixed conversation length, so it's not even clear what "exponential" means here. Neither should clearly win.
