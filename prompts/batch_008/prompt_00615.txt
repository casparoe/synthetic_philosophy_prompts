In my philosophy of language seminar this term, we've spent a couple of weeks on Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following, and the part of the argument I keep returning to isn't the "quus" paradox itself, which I feel I have a solid grip on, but Kripke's specific rejection of the dispositional response to it — a response that seemed obviously right to me on first read, until our professor spent a whole session on why it fails. The natural thought is that what makes it true that I mean addition rather than some deviant quus-like function is just that I have a disposition to answer "125" rather than, say, "5," when asked about "68+57," and that such a disposition, being an ordinary physical fact about me, needs no further metaphysical backing. Kripke rejects this on two connected grounds: my actual dispositions are finite, bounded by things like the size of the numbers I can handle or my own lifespan, whereas meaning addition is supposed to fix a determinate correct answer for infinitely many cases I will never in fact confront; and even among the cases I would respond to, my dispositions include dispositions to err, so at best they tell us how I would respond, not how I ought to respond if I am to accord with what I mean by the symbol.

What I'd like help with is working out the most important implications of this specific pair of objections — finitude and normativity, not the rule-following paradox in general — for the question of whether something like a large language model can be said to follow a rule, or mean anything by the tokens it outputs, given that such a model is itself, at bottom, an enormously elaborate statistical disposition to produce certain outputs given certain inputs. If Kripke's argument is sound, does it bite at least as hard against such a system as against a human speaker, since a model, too, is finite and disposed to occasional error? Or are there disanalogies that change the picture — for instance, whether error-correction during training plays anything like the role Wittgenstein's skeptical solution assigns to a correcting community, or whether it matters that a model's dispositions are the product of explicit optimization rather than mere accumulation? I already know the shape of the underlying paradox, so I'd rather you focus on the two or three implications you find most significant, and on whether, in your view, this is a case where the analogy between minds and models genuinely illuminates the philosophy, or one where it mostly breaks down.
