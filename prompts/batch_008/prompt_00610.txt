I write a science and ideas column, and I'm currently reporting a feature on how forecasters, doctors, and intelligence analysts revise their confidence when new evidence arrives as an impression rather than a fact — think of a radiologist who comes away from a scan "70 percent" convinced a shadow is a tumor, rather than someone holding a definitive biopsy result. Chasing that idea down sent me into the philosophy of probability, and specifically into Richard Jeffrey's generalization of Bayesian updating, which he illustrated with his own example of an observer examining a piece of cloth by dim candlelight: she can never become fully certain whether the cloth is green, blue, or violet, yet the sighting still rationally shifts her confidence, not just about the cloth's color but about all sorts of things that hang on it.

For the piece, I want to characterize the relationship between this procedure — usually called Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics — and the textbook rule of conditionalizing on evidence one comes to know outright. My understanding is that strict conditionalization is really just a limiting case of Jeffrey's more general rule, but I'd like you to compare the two properly and spell out exactly in which ways strict conditionalization is the special, more constrained member of the pair. Could you also unpack what Jeffrey's so-called "rigidity" condition actually requires, and why ordinary conditionalization satisfies some version of it almost automatically, in a way a genuinely uncertain observation does not? There's one wrinkle I keep seeing invoked but never explained in plain terms: that, unlike strict conditionalization, successive rounds of Jeffrey updating need not commute, so that absorbing two uncertain observations in one order can leave you with different final credences than absorbing them in the other order. Is that really a strike against the rule's claim to rationality, as some critics apparently think, or is there a principled reason a Bayesian shouldn't lose sleep over it?
