I've been preparing a graduate seminar on the history of utilitarianism, and this week I had my students read Bentham's essay "Offences Against One's Self" — written around 1785, but suppressed by Bentham himself and not published until the twentieth century — in which he mounts what historians of the subject generally treat as the earliest sustained argument for homosexual law reform written in England. (Bentham's own word for the topic, "paederasty," meant for him simply male homosexual acts in general, not the narrower and darker sense the word has since acquired.) The essay makes a nice test case for his official jurisprudence, since the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation explicitly disqualifies what Bentham calls the "principle of sympathy and antipathy" — treating one's own approval or disapproval of an act as a sufficient reason in itself for punishing it, quite apart from whether the act actually makes anyone worse off — as a legitimate basis for law. Mere disgust is officially not supposed to count.

What puzzled several of my students, and me on rereading it, is that Bentham does not handle his contemporaries' disgust at these acts by simply declaring it unreal or beside the point. He concedes outright that the antipathy produces a genuine, particular pain in everyone who feels it whenever the act comes to mind, and he even grants that watching the offender punished affords real pleasure to those who hate the practice — both of which look like perfectly legitimate entries on the pro-punishment side of the ledger for a thinker who insists that all pains and pleasures must be weighed impartially, whatever their source. Yet he still concludes that none of this warrants punishment, essentially on the grounds that antipathy not already justified by the independent harmfulness of the act is "grounded only in prejudice," and that criminalizing the act would not even reduce the suffering in question anyway, since informal social disapproval already punishes the offender in proportion to the antipathy regardless of what the law does.

I'd like help thinking through whether this is a genuinely principled move on Bentham's part or a piece of sleight of hand. His hedonism is supposed to count every actual pain and pleasure impartially, so on what non-circular basis can he discount a pain merely because its causal history runs through "prejudice" rather than accurate belief, given that the pain itself is, by his own admission, just as real either way? Could you reconstruct the underlying logic of his argument as charitably as possible — including whether it actually needs that "prejudice" premise to do the work he wants, given the separate point he makes about punishment being instrumentally futile here — and then assess whether a strict hedonistic utilitarian genuinely has the resources to make this move, or whether it can only be rescued by quietly importing some non-hedonic standard of which pains and preferences are allowed to count?
