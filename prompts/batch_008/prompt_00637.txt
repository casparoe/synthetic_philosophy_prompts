I'm a novelist, and something in Nelson Goodman's Languages of Art has been nagging at me. He calls painting "autographic": even an undetectable copy of a painting is a forgery, because what makes a painting genuine is its history of production. He calls literature "allographic": since a text is fully specified by its sequence of words, a letter-for-letter copy is just another authentic instance of the work, not a forgery, whatever its provenance. That seems basically right, yet literary history keeps handing us cases we call forgeries without hesitation — William Henry Ireland forging Shakespeare's handwriting and passing off his own play, Vortigern and Rowena, as a newly discovered Shakespeare work that collapsed into farce within months of its 1796 premiere once the hoax broke, or James Macpherson passing off his own verse as translations of a bard, Ossian, who probably never existed. The words on the page never changed; only our beliefs about their origin did, and that was enough to destroy both works' standing. If a text can't be forged the way a painting can, what was actually being faked in these cases, and does Goodman's framework survive contact with them or need real revision? I'd like you to work through the actual mechanics, not just restate the general theory.
