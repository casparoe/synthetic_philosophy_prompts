Quick heads up — so I'm chipping away at a piece on the weirder corners of 17th-century intellectual history, and I've fallen into a bit of a rabbit hole on Thomas Hobbes and geometry. Everyone remembers him for Leviathan, but apparently he spent something like two decades insisting he'd squared the circle, getting absolutely torn apart by John Wallis and the Oxford math crowd the whole time, and never once backed down. A philosopher friend sent me a passage from Hobbes's "Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematics" (1656) that's supposed to be the key to why he cared so much about geometry in the first place, but the 17th-century syntax is kicking my ass and I want to make sure I've actually got the argument straight before it goes anywhere near my editor.

Here's the bit I'm stuck on: "Of arts, some are demonstrable, others indemonstrable; and demonstrable are those the construction of the subject whereof is in the power of the artist himself, who, in his demonstration, does no more but deduce the consequences of his own operation. The reason whereof is this, that the science of every subject is derived from a precognition of the causes, generation, and construction of the same; and consequently where the causes are known, there is place for demonstration, but not where the causes are to seek for. Geometry therefore is demonstrable, for the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves; and civil philosophy is demonstrable, because we make the commonwealth ourselves. But because of natural bodies we know not the construction, but seek it from the effects, there lies no demonstration of what the causes be we seek for, but only of what they may be."

Could you walk me through what's actually being argued, line by line, in plain terms? I get the headline idea ("we only really know what we build ourselves") but I'm fuzzy on why that lands geometry and civil philosophy together as the only two real sciences, while physics — which Hobbes very much wanted a seat at the table for — gets shut out. Is "construction" carrying some technical weight tied to his materialist idea that points and lines are literally generated by motion rather than existing as abstract objects, sort of a nominalist thing? And is this the same reasoning that made him so weirdly unshakeable about his bogus quadrature of the circle, like being the "artist" of his own diagrams gave him some special claim on whether the proof actually worked, even after real geometers showed it didn't? I'd love a proper breakdown I can use for readers who aren't philosophy nerds, not just a "here's the standard gloss" but an actual assessment of whether the argument holds up on its own terms.
