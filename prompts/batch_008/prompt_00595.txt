As a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, I spend my working life picking apart other people's arguments. Lately, though, I've been stuck on a dispute that broke out much closer to home, in my own office, and for once I can't work out how to adjudicate it myself.

Our team's expense policy simply states that employees should book "reasonably priced" hotels when traveling for work. In the three years I've been here, this was never a live issue: nobody had ever spent more than about $150 a night, and everyone assumed we all meant the same thing by "reasonable." Then a colleague traveled to a city that happened to be hosting a massive trade convention the same week; hotel prices roughly tripled, and he booked a room for $450 a night. When our manager objected, he argued, quite sincerely, that he hadn't broken the rule at all: on his reading, "reasonable" had always meant something like "reasonable relative to prevailing market conditions," and $450 was in fact the cheapest defensible option available that week. Everyone else is adamant that "reasonable" obviously meant something like a fixed, situation-independent ceiling, and that he is straightforwardly in the wrong.

What's been nagging at me is this: when I checked, I found that every booking anyone had made before this one is equally consistent with both readings of the policy, since we had simply never hit a price spike before, so the two interpretations happened to agree on every past case and only diverged on this new one. I even tracked down the manager who wrote the original policy, hoping she could just tell us what she'd meant, but she admitted she had honestly never considered how it should apply to a price spike like this. So there is no past decision, no email, and no recollected intention that settles which rule we were actually following all along. That has started to feel like more than an HR technicality to me. Is there some fact of the matter here about which interpretation is genuinely correct, one we are simply failing to uncover? Or were we never collectively committed to one determinate rule in the first place, in which case what, if anything, makes my colleague's reading a mistake? I would like your honest, carefully reasoned view on which of these is true, what you think it implies about how a dispute like this should actually be resolved, and whether you think this is just a quirk of fuzzy policy language or points to something more general about what it means to follow a rule at all.
