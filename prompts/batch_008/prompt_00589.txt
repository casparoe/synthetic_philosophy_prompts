I've been chewing on a puzzle that I suspect already has a name in philosophy, though I've never studied it formally. Picture two urns of 100 balls each. Urn A holds exactly 50 red and 50 black balls, and I know this for certain; Urn B holds red and black balls in some ratio I know nothing about. Asked my probability that a ball drawn from Urn A is red, I'll happily say 50%. Asked the same about Urn B, saying "50%" feels dishonest, as if it's papering over a real difference in my state of mind that no single number can capture.

When I've put this to people who know more about Bayesian reasoning than I do, I get two opposite answers. Some say rationality demands one exact number for Urn B too, since I'll act some particular way once a real bet is on the table, and my action will betray an implicit number whether I name one or not — so refusing to choose is either incoherent or evasive. Others say my hesitation is tracking something real, and that a whole range of probabilities, not a single value, is the honest way to represent my state of mind here.

Could you write a dialogue between two characters holding these positions, standing in front of the urns just as they are about to place a bet on the colour of a ball drawn from Urn B? Give the single-number character their strongest case for why the other can't place any actual bet without their "range" quietly collapsing into one number, and give the range-defending character a genuine answer to that challenge rather than a quiet concession. Neither should be a pushover for the other — I want to see them really engage with each other's strongest move, not talk past one another.
