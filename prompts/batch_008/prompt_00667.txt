Okay this is a bit of a crossover question but bear with me. In my paradoxes and epistemic logic seminar we covered the surprise exam paradox: a teacher tells the class there'll be a surprise test sometime next week and that they won't be able to figure out the day in advance, the students "prove" via backward induction that it can't be Friday (since if it hasn't happened by Thursday they'd know it has to be Friday), then by the same logic it can't be Thursday either, and so on until no day is left - and yet the exam does happen, say on Wednesday, and everyone really is surprised. Seperately, in a decision theory elective I'm taking we went over the backward induction paradox you get in the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma (the centipede game is the same basic idea), where common knowledge of rationality is supposed to force both players to defect, or stop, right at the very first move, even though that's obviously not how actual people play and arguably isn't even what fully rational players "should" do. I mentioned to my logic professor that these felt like the same move wearing different clothes, and she just said something like "people have tried that, be careful" and moved on to the next slide, so naturally I haven't been able to drop it since. I even dug up a paper (Bovens, I think, on the finite iterated prisoner's dilemma and the surprise exam paradox) that literally puts the two side by side, so at least I know I'm not imagining the resemblance.

What I actually want from you is an argument for the more careful, skeptical position my professor seemed to be gesturing at: that despite the surface-level similarity (both look like "it can't be the last possible point, and by the same reasoning not the second-to-last either, etc." regresses), a diagnosis that actually dissolves the surprise exam paradox - like Sorensen's "blindspot" idea, where the teacher's announcement generates a proposition that's true but structurally unknowable in advance by the student - doesnt automatically carry over to dissolve the backward induction paradox in the finite game case, and that lumping the two together as "basically the same paradox" hides an important difference in what kind of epistemic state is doing the work: one student's knowledge of a single external contingent fact, versus common knowledge of rationality distributed across multiple strategic agents who are each reasoning about each other's reasoning. Please don't just lay both paradoxes out side by side and call it a day - I want an actual argument for why the parallel breaks down, ideally one that could survive someone pushing back with "come on, they're structurally identical, you're just being pedantic."
