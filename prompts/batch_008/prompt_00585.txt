Hey — I'm putting together a collection of short stories that each riff on one specific idea from philosophy of science, and I want to try one built around Ian Hacking's "looping effect" work (the Mad Travelers and Rewriting the Soul stuff, plus that "Making Up People" essay). The idea that keeps grabbing me is that human categories aren't like rocks or quarks — they don't just sit there once you've discovered them. Diagnose someone with a psychiatric condition and they find out about it, they react to it, they start performing or resisting the category, and that reaction loops back and reshapes the diagnosis itself for whoever gets classified next. I brought this up at my writers' group last week and everyone immediately jumped to "oh so labels effect people," which is true but feels like the watered-down version of what Hacking's actually arguing.

What I keep coming back to is the fugue epidemic Hacking writes about — compulsive, amnesiac walking that showed up as a real diagnosis in 1890s France (the Albert Dadas case) and then basically disappeared within a generation once the cultural "niche" for it closed — alongside the multiple personality boom of the 80s and 90s, which he treats the same way. Could you write me a short story, a couple pages, that actually dramatizes the loop instead of just gesturing at it? I'd like it to follow either a patient, a clinician, or both across two eras, closely enough that we see the actual mechanism doing work in the plot: the classification changing someone's behavior, the behavior forcing a revision of the category, maybe the niche closing by the end so the "illness" quietly stops being available to have. Invent the specific disorder and setting if that's easier, I just want the loop itself, not just a moral about labels being powerful.
