I'm putting together a small collection of short fiction in which each piece reimagines a classic philosophical thought experiment as an actual story rather than a dressed-up syllogism, and the one I keep returning to, without ever drafting it to my satisfaction, is "Death in Damascus." I know the tale in its oldest clothes: Somerset Maugham's retelling of the old Mesopotamian fable, in which a servant begs his master's horse and gallops off to Samarra, only to discover that Samarra was precisely where his appointment with Death lay all along. That version, though, is really just a meditation on fatalism, and a fairly well-worn one. What interests me far more is the version decision theorists later built on top of it (Gibbard and Harper, if I'm remembering the paper correctly), in which Death is not merely an emblem of an inescapable future but an explicit, near-perfect predictor: he keeps an appointment book, filled in well ahead of time on the strength of very reliable forecasts of where a person will be, and both he and his quarry know this about him. In that version, the trouble isn't that you can't outrun your fate; it's that once you start reasoning about what your own choice would be evidence of, neither staying in Damascus nor fleeing to Aleppo can be made to sit still under you. Deciding to run is itself grounds for suspecting the book says Aleppo, and deciding to stay delivers you right back to the city the book most likely names.

Could you write me a short story, somewhere in the range of 1,000 to 1,500 words, built around that second and colder puzzle rather than the first? I'm happy for you to keep the old caravan-city setting or move the premise elsewhere entirely, so long as the underlying arrangement survives the move: an agent who is very rarely wrong, who commits to a place before the protagonist chooses, and whose reliability is common knowledge between them. I'd like the protagonist to be someone clear-headed enough to actually notice the trap for what it is, turning the reasoning over, perhaps even reaching for a coin to flip out of sheer desperation, so that the story's dread comes from the impossibility of ever landing on a decision the character wouldn't immediately want to reverse, rather than from the gentler, more familiar irony of a fate nobody can escape. If you can find room for a second character who reasons in a more coldly causal register and so refuses to spiral the way the protagonist does, that would be a welcome complication, though I'd rather the theory stayed buried in the plotting than surfaced as a lecture; and I'd like the ending to leave the reader inside that irresolution rather than tidy it away.
