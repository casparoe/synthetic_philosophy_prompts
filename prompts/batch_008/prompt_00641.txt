ok so this is a weird mashup but here goes — in my art elective we've been talking about the Notre Dame restoration (it reopened back in Dec 2024 after that huge fire in 2019) and my teacher brought up this whole debate about whether rebuilding a burned/damaged old building "exactly like it was" actually saves it, or if it just turns it into a really elaborate fake. she said people have been arguing about this since way before Notre Dame, and gave us this excerpt from a Victorian art critic named John Ruskin (from his 1849 book The Seven Lamps of Architecture) who apparently thought restoration was basically a crime. i've read it a bunch of times and the vibe is clear (he really does NOT like restoration) but i can't fully untangle his actual reasoning. this is the part that's tripping me up:

"That which I have above insisted upon as the life of the whole, that spirit which is given only by the hand and eye of the workman, never can be recalled. Another spirit may be given by another time, and it is then a new building; but the spirit of the dead workman cannot be summoned up, and commanded to direct other hands, and other thoughts. And as for direct and simple copying, it is palpably impossible. What copying can there be of surfaces that have been worn half an inch down? The whole finish of the work was in the half inch that is gone; if you attempt to restore that finish, you do it conjecturally; if you copy what is left, granting fidelity to be possible (and what care, or watchfulness, or cost can secure it?), how is the new work better than the old? There was yet in the old some life, some mysterious suggestion of what it had been, and of what it had lost; some sweetness in the gentle lines which rain and sun had wrought. There can be none in the brute hardness of the new carving. Do not let us talk then of restoration. The thing is a Lie from beginning to end. You may make a model of a building as you may of a corpse, and your model may have the shell of the old walls within it as your cast might have the skeleton, with what advantage I neither see nor care; but the old building is destroyed, and that more totally and mercilessly than if it had sunk into a heap of dust, or melted into a mass of clay."

can someone help me figure out what he's actually claiming here? like is "the spirit given by the hand and eye of the workman" a real argument or just a poetic way of saying "old stuff is special because its old"? and why bring up the worn-down half inch of stone specifically — what's that supposed to prove? my friend read this too and said Ruskin's being dramatic, that a stone-for-stone perfect rebuild basically just IS the building, so theres nothing actually lost except maybe some "feeling." i kind of disagreed but couldn't explain why when she pushed back. is there an actual logical argument buried in here for why a copy (even a flawless one) isn't the same as the original, and would it also apply to something like an art forgery, or a perfect replica of some famous object?
