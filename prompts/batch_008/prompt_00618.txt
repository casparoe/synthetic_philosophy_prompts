I've been putting together a lecture on Kepler for my history-and-philosophy-of-science seminar, and I got into a whole argument about him with a colleague from the history department at lunch that I still haven't let go of. Everyone knows the cartoon version: brilliant guy figures out elliptical orbits and then, sort of embarrassingly, also casts horoscopes on the side. But it's wierder than that once you actually look at what he did. In Tertius Interveniens (1610), Kepler explicitly sets himself up as the "third man" between traditional astrologers and a critic named Feselius who wanted the whole discipline banned, and he junks most of the Ptolemaic apparatus -- houses, dignities, sign-based reasoning -- as baseless. What he keeps is the aspects (conjunction, trine, square, etc), which he re-grounds in a theory of harmonic/geometric ratios -- the idea that the soul directly resonates with certain ratios -- that shows up roughly a decade later in Harmonice Mundi and underwrites his actual astronomy too. He also kept a paying astrological clientele his whole life, including Wallenstein, whose horoscope he cast in 1608 and got dragged back to update in 1625.

My colleague thinks this is basically economically-motivated compartmentalization dressed up after the fact in fancy harmonic language -- Kepler needed the money and the patronage, full stop (there's that great line of his about astrology being astronomy's "foolish daughter," who nonetheless keeps her wise mother fed). I keep wanting to insist that the harmonic theory of aspects is doing real metaphysical work for him, continuous with, rather than opposed to, the same geometrical mysticism that got him the three laws -- so writing it off as pure hackery gets the intellectual history backwards. We're clearly both a little anchored on our own priors at this point and neither of us is budging, so I want a genuinely outside verdict.

Could you write me an essay on this? Lay out the strongest version of each reading -- sincere and integrated vs. opportunistic and compartmentalized (or, I suppose, some third option if you think we're both wrong) -- and then actually commit to one, with reasons, rather then just telling me "it's complicated." I get enough of that from journal referees.
