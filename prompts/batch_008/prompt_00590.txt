Hey, so I'm about a third of the way into a novel where a secondary character is basically a stand-in for a certain kind of 19th century public intellectual -- the type who picks a fight with a tabloid and then spends the rest of his career insisting he's the innocent victim of "the mob." I finally sat down and read up properly on the actual history I'd been riffing on without really knowing it, which is Kierkegaard and the Corsair affair. Short version: Møller publishes a snide little review of Stages on Life's Way in the annual Gæa, and Kierkegaard, writing as Frater Taciturnus, hits back not by defending the book but by outing Møller as a secret contributor to The Corsair -- a gossip paper he considered so far beneath him that being praised by it (which it had been doing) was actually more insulting to him than being attacked by it would've been. Goldschmidt and co. take the bait, and for weeks Copenhagen gets treated to cartoons of Kierkegaards uneven trouser legs and his hunched, funny walk, until he's referring to himself as "laughter's martyr" in his journals. And then, almost the moment it dies down, he publishes Two Ages, with that whole second half everyone quotes about "leveling" and the public as a phantom that flattens the individual through idle talk and anonymity.

Here's the thing I can't settle for myself, and what my character's eventually gonna have to reckon with: does this make Kierkegaard a hypocrite -- a guy who manufactures the exact mob dynamic he then builds a whole philosophy out of denouncing, using a pseudonym no less, while writing as though he were merely its victim -- or does his own account of what's required to become "the single individual," someone willing to stand apart from the crowd and be mocked for it, actually make provoking that mockery the logical, maybe even necessary, working-out of his own thought, so that "hypocrisy" just isn't the right charge? Don't give me an essay that shrugs and says both readings have something going for them. Actually weigh the real considerations here -- not the two-sentence "leveling" summary you'd get off a syllabus -- and commit to a verdict, then defend it.
