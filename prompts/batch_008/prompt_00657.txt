I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on the ethics of migration. While working through material that sits just outside the usual Carens–Walzer–Miller axis of the field, I got stuck on an argument in Michael Dummett's short 2001 book "On Immigration and Refugees" - a text I only came across because it turned up, almost as an aside, in a footnote of a paper on refugee rights. It's a strange, underdiscussed book: Dummett spent his career on the philosophy of logic and language and is best known as the world's leading interpreter of Frege, but late in life he drew on decades as an anti-racism campaigner to write his only sustained treatment of immigration. I think there's a real gap in its central argument, but before I commit to that claim in writing, I want to see the strongest possible case made for the move I'm suspicious of.

Dummett takes the 1951 Geneva Convention as his starting point and argues that its persecution-based definition of "refugee" is too narrow. What actually grounds a right to asylum, on his view, is something more basic: every adult has a right to live somewhere as a full, self-governing "first-class citizen," under at least the minimal conditions for a decent human life, and any circumstance that denies someone this - not persecution alone, but equally severe and inescapable poverty, or the absence of any real self-government - ought to count as grounds for claiming refuge elsewhere. A state that fails to help secure this for someone thereby becomes complicit in the injustice that denies it to them. Dummett draws the further conclusion that many people currently dismissed as "mere" economic migrants ought properly to be reclassified as refugees on this basis. And the book is generally read - by reviewers at the time, and in how it gets invoked since - as going further still, as underwriting something like a general presumption in favor of admitting immigrants as such, defeated only in narrow circumstances (chiefly, a serious risk of rapidly overwhelming the receiving population), with the burden of justification falling on the excluding state rather than on the person seeking entry.

This last step is exactly what I doubt survives scrutiny. The foundational right, as Dummett states it, is triggered by a deficiency: it switches on when someone's minimal conditions for a decent, self-governing life are unmet, and it is hard to see what it would still have to say about a person for whom those conditions are already satisfied - someone who wants to migrate not to escape destitution, persecution, or disenfranchisement, but to advance a career, join a partner, or simply try living somewhere else. Please argue for the position that Dummett's stated premises, however charitably reconstructed, license only the narrower conclusion - an expanded, need-triggered right of asylum - and cannot by themselves support the more general presumption in favor of free entry the book is taken to endorse, so that bridging the gap would require a genuinely independent argument that Dummett never actually supplies. I'd also like you to confront, rather than sidestep, the obvious rejoinder: given the scale of global inequality, the class of people whose conditions for a decent life genuinely are unmet at home may be so large that the practical difference between the two conclusions nearly disappears - so address whether, and why, the conceptual distinction should still matter even if its practical bite turns out to be small.
