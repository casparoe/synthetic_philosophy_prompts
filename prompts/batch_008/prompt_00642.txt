I write about science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I'm currently reporting a longer piece on the philosophical arguments that AI safety researchers borrow from decision theory to defend some of their more dramatic claims about advanced AI. The one I keep getting stuck on is what specialists call a "coherence argument" for expected utility maximization, better known by its nickname, the "money pump": the claim that an agent whose preferences are cyclic — who prefers A to B, B to C, but also C to A — can be exploited through a sequence of trades that each look sensible in isolation but leave it strictly worse off, with nothing gained, which is supposed to show that any sufficiently rational agent, biological or artificial, must end up with preferences that behave like a utility function.

At a workshop I sat in on last month, a philosopher ran a live version of this during the coffee break, using three pastries from the snack table and a volunteer who cheerfully admitted to preferring the croissant to the muffin, the muffin to the scone, and the scone to the croissant. A few dimes changed hands at each swap, and within a minute the volunteer was out sixty cents and back to the pastry she'd started with. It made for good theater, but the argument that broke out at the bar afterward struck me as the real story. One philosopher insisted the trick generalizes much further: even an agent with no preference at all between two options, rather than a cyclic one, can supposedly still be forced into a version of the same trap unless it gives up having any gaps in its preferences whatsoever. The other shot back that an agent can dodge the whole apparatus simply by committing in advance to a plan and sticking to it even when a later trade looks tempting when considered in isolation. I gather this is more than an academic squabble: it bears directly on proposals to deliberately build advanced AI systems with a strategic gap in their preferences — say, no preference between being shut down sooner or later — specifically so that they won't resist being switched off, a design that only works if such gaps can actually survive this kind of exploitation.

Could you write the dialogue I wish I'd been able to record at that bar: a real, technical back-and-forth between the two philosophers, with each pressing the other on their strongest point rather than restating their own? I'd like the one defending the money-pump argument to engage seriously with the resolute-choice escape route rather than dismiss it out of hand, and the one skeptical of the argument to grapple honestly with whatever maneuver the defender uses to try to force the incomplete-preference case, rather than retreating to a quick line like "but that's not really a preference." Please don't let either side win too easily, and feel free to end things unresolved — my strong impression from the workshop is that the specialists themselves haven't settled it either.
