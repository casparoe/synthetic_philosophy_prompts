I'm taking a seminar on classical Islamic philosophy this term, and this week we worked through the Seventeenth Discussion of al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* (we're using Marmura's parallel Arabic–English edition), together with Ibn Rushd's rebuttal in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* (Van den Bergh's translation). Al-Ghazālī's target there is the Avicennan idea that a cause necessitates its effect; against this, he argues via the famous case of fire and cotton that experience only ever shows us one event following another, never the effect actually being produced by the cause, so that what we call a "natural law" is really nothing more than a habit (*ʿāda*) God has adopted of regularly conjoining certain events with others — a habit he can and occasionally does suspend, as when Abraham is thrown into the fire and it does not burn him. Ibn Rushd answers that this is disastrous for the very possibility of demonstrative knowledge: if fire's burning cotton is merely customary rather than necessary, he argues, then nothing about the natures of things can be relied on, and we lose any principled basis for calling anything "known" rather than merely "so far observed." A few of us stayed after arguing about this, and split roughly down the middle: half thought Ibn Rushd's objection is basically decisive, the other half thought al-Ghazālī can simply reply that reliable regularity is all knowledge ever required in the first place, so the objection doesn't actually land.

For my term paper, I want to work through this properly rather than just summarize both sides, so could you write me an essay that does the following: reconstructs al-Ghazālī's argument against necessary causal connection and for occasionalism, lays out Ibn Rushd's strongest objection to it (plus any further difficulties you think the view faces, or resources al-Ghazālī has for answering back), and then actually settles the question instead of leaving it a tie — on balance, does al-Ghazālī's occasionalism survive as a defensible account of natural causation, or does Ibn Rushd (or some other consideration you think is stronger) show that it fails? I'd like a real verdict defended with argument, not a "both sides have a point" ending. As a smaller side question, I'd also value your take on whether the standard comparison between al-Ghazālī and Hume on causation actually illuminates what's at stake here, or whether it mostly obscures the fact that al-Ghazālī's regularities are underwritten by a willing, personal God in a way Hume's are not.
