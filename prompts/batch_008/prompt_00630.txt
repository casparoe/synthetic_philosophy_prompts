I'm building a syllabus for a graduate seminar on eighteenth-century moral psychology, and this time around I've finally worked in a week on Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness (Discours sur le bonheur) alongside the more familiar Hume and La Mettrie readings. There is one move she makes in it that I keep coming back to without feeling I've properly understood, and I would welcome some help thinking it through before I try to lead a seminar discussion on it.

Du Châtelet holds that happiness depends on several things at once: freedom from prejudice, virtue, good health, strong tastes and passions, and, more surprisingly, susceptibility to illusion. In Isabelle Bour's translation (Judith Zinsser's edition of her selected writings), she puts the point starkly: "we owe most of our pleasures to illusions, and unhappy is the one who has lost them." Her examples are vivid: she says we could take no pleasure in a play or a puppet show unless we let ourselves forget, for the moment, that the figures before us are not who they seem to be, and she describes, with disarming candor, sustaining herself for a long time on the illusion that she was still loved by Voltaire after his feelings for her had visibly cooled. Yet only a few pages later she insists, just as emphatically, that error is always harmful and is never to be indulged. Her way of squaring these two claims is compressed almost to the point of opacity: illusion, she writes, does not "make us see objects entirely as they are ... it adjusts them to our nature," and she immediately compares this to optics, which she says likewise never shows us objects exactly as they are without on that account deceiving us.

What I can't decide is whether there is a principled distinction doing real work in that analogy, or whether "adjustment" is simply a flattering name for exactly the kind of self-deception that her own rationalism ought to condemn — a rationalism rigorous enough, after all, to produce an annotated French translation of Newton's Principia. Could you help me reconstruct the strongest version of the illusion/error distinction her optics comparison is meant to secure, and tell me whether, on reflection, you think it actually does the philosophical work she needs it to do?
