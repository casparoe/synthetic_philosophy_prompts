I spent my whole career as an experimental physicist (mostly optics, then some condensed matter work toward the end) and only really started reading philosophy after I retired, mostly through a little reading group that meets every couple weeks at the library. A while back we got on the subject of women in the history of science, which is how I properly discovered Emilie du Chatelet -- I'd known her name from the vis viva dispute and the Newton translation, but had no idea she'd also written a short essay on happiness that apparently sat in a drawer for decades before it got published after her death. Ive seen it titled "Discourse on Happiness" in one place and "Reflections on Happiness" in another, which is a little confusing on its own, but that's not my actual question.

There's a passage near the start that's been rattling around in my head for a week now. My French is rusty but serviceable, so rather than lean on one translation I worked through the original myself, and as best I can render it, it says: "To be happy, one must have rid oneself of prejudices; be virtuous; enjoy good health; have tastes and passions; be susceptible to illusion -- for we owe most of our pleasures to illusion, and unhappy is the one who loses it. Far from seeking to dispel it with the torch of reason, try instead to thicken the varnish it lays over most objects; that varnish is even more necessary to them than grooming and adornment are to our bodies."

What I can't get past is the whiplash in that single stretch of text. She opens by saying happiness requires freeing yourself of "prejudices" (préjugés), which sounds like exactly the clear-eyed, reason-governed attitude you'd expect from someone who spent years doing serious mathematical physics. Then almost in the same breath she says don't turn the torch of reason on illusion, thicken it instead. Is "prejudice" doing different work here than "illusion" -- some real distinction about which errors serve us and which ones just distort -- or is this a spot where her ethics and her epistemology genuinely pull against each other? I'd like to actually understand the couple of sentences rather than just wave my hand at them, since I'm supposed to be leading the discussion at the next reading group meeting.
