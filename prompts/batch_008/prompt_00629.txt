I've been implementing some distributed-consensus code at work, which sent me down a rabbit hole into epistemic logic — the "Reasoning About Knowledge" style formalism people use to analyze things like the Byzantine generals problem or the muddy children puzzle. In that framework, an agent's knowledge is modeled as indistinguishability between possible global states, and it falls straight out of the semantics that if an agent knows P, it knows that it knows P (the positive introspection axiom, which I gather philosophers call the KK principle). Nobody in the distributed-systems literature treats this as remotely controversial — it's just baked into the model as soon as you use equivalence relations for indistinguishability.

So I was surprised to find that in mainstream epistemology this is a live and contested question, and that Timothy Williamson has an actual argument against it, in a book called Knowledge and Its Limits, built around a "margin for error" principle and a case of gradually warming up after standing outside in the cold. The conclusion is supposed to be that essentially no non-trivial mental state is "luminous," where being luminous means that whenever you're in the state, you're automatically in a position to know you're in it. My understanding is he then runs a related but distinct argument aimed specifically at the KK principle, using a different setup involving a precise measurement rather than a temperature sensation — though I could be garbling that part. I tried reading a summary of the reasoning and got stuck on the case-by-case bookkeeping without ever really feeling why the argument is supposed to be convincing.

I don't have any formal philosophy training, so I'd appreciate an explanation geared more toward intuition than formal apparatus: what's actually doing the work in Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, and why does the same style of reasoning supposedly sink the KK principle even though we just get it for free in formal models of knowledge like the one above? I'd also value your take on how seriously to treat the objection that this whole argument is essentially a sorites paradox in another guise, and on Williamson's reply that "sharpening" the relevant predicate (fixing an exact cutoff for something like "feels cold") blocks that comparison — I don't really follow why sharpening is supposed to help.
