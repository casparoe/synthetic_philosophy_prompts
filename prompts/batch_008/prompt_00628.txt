A friend and I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about the final, unfinished pages of Spinoza's Political Treatise. We have been working through the political writings together in a reading group, and had finally reached the point in Chapter 11 where the manuscript breaks off, mid-argument, because Spinoza died before he could continue. He had just arrived at the question of who counts as sui juris (independent, and therefore entitled to a voice in the democratic council), and had already excluded foreigners, servants, and children on those grounds before turning, in the last surviving paragraphs he wrote, to women, whom he excludes as well -- not merely by convention, he insists, but because experience shows that no society anywhere has ever had the sexes rule jointly, because the one apparent counterexample, the Amazons, simply replaced one exclusive rule with another, and because in any case the ordinary affects of sexual jealousy would make joint rule unworkable.

My friend's reaction was the one I think most people have: that this is an embarrassing lapse, refuted by Spinoza's own resources, especially his account of the imagination and the affects, which he uses everywhere else to explain away apparently fixed traits as artifacts of upbringing and custom rather than nature. On her view, a "consistent Spinozist" should simply have applied that same debunking move to the case of women and concluded the opposite of what he actually did. I found myself resisting this without being able to fully articulate why, and I would like your help doing that. My suspicion is that the rescue is too quick, because it quietly smuggles back in a nature/convention distinction that Spinoza's political naturalism exists to dissolve. He derives natural right directly from actual power -- a doctrine he sets out in Chapter 2 ("Of Natural Law"), the very same chapter in which he rejects the idea that human beings are a "dominion within a dominion" and insists that even the most confused, passion-driven behavior follows the common laws of nature exactly as much as anything else does. If that is right, then a power differential produced and sustained by custom, upbringing, and the imagination is not thereby some merely "conventional," normatively idle fact for Spinoza -- it is just as much a fact about actual, currently obtaining potentia, and so just as right-conferring, as a power differential produced in any other way. Pointing out that women's subordination is a product of custom rather than of some deeper metaphysical necessity would not, then, by itself give a Spinozist any grounds for resisting his argument.

Could you make this case as rigorously as you can, engaging with the actual structure of the Chapter 11 argument rather than a generic summary of it, and with the strongest form of my friend's objection -- including, if you think it strengthens her side, the sort of evidence commentators like Hasana Sharp have drawn from Spinoza's far more egalitarian treatment of Adam and Eve in the Ethics? I am less interested in a verdict on whether Spinoza was, all things considered, simply a man of his time than in whether the specific inferential move my friend wants to make -- from "it's custom, not nature" to "a Spinozist has no reason to endorse it" -- actually goes through, given his other commitments. If it turns out there is no way to salvage consistency here, I would like to understand exactly where my argument breaks down.
