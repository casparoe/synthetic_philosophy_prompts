I've been reading about historical thought experiments on the mind, and came across Avicenna's "flying man" argument, which predates Descartes' cogito by roughly six centuries. As I understand it: imagine a person created instantaneously as a fully grown adult, suspended in empty air, blindfolded, with his limbs spread apart so that no part of his body touches any other part or anything else, and with no sensory input of any kind — no sight, no sound, no touch, not even the feeling of air on his skin. Avicenna claims this person would still affirm his own existence, despite affirming nothing at all about having a body, and concludes that the self is aware of itself without being aware of the body — and from there, that the soul must be an immaterial substance distinct from the body.

Looking further into this, I found that scholars are divided on what the argument actually shows. Some, following Michael Marmura's well-known reading, take it as a genuine proof of mind-body dualism. Others, such as Jari Kaukua, argue that its real job is more modest: to show only that self-awareness is immediate and doesn't depend on being aware of any object at all, without thereby proving that the soul could actually exist apart from the body. This distinction matters to me because Descartes' own move from "I can doubt that I have a body but not that I have a mind" to "mind and body are distinct substances" is a textbook case of what's sometimes called the masked-man fallacy — mistaking "not known to be the same thing" for "not the same thing." I would like to know whether Avicenna's argument commits the same mistake, or whether the more modest reading lets it escape that charge while still telling us something philosophically important.

Write an essay that reaches a real verdict on this: does the flying man argument succeed in establishing that the self is an immaterial substance separate from the body, or does it only establish the weaker claim that self-awareness doesn't depend on awareness of the body? Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but don't leave the question open — defend a specific conclusion.
