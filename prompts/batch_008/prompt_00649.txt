ok so I've been slowly working through Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, mostly bc he keeps getting namedropped as like the OG utilitarian every time EA people cite Parfit or that "point of view of the universe" stuff. finally got to the concluding chapter, which is famous for being a total downer ending. right before the bit that's confusing me, he's just spent a page arguing that we have no actual self-evident intuition that there's a Supreme Being who rewards virtue and punishes vice - no proof of what he calls "this connexion of Virtue and self-interest." then he writes:

"I also judge that in a certain sense this result ought to be realised: in this judgment, however, 'ought' is not used in a strictly ethical meaning; it only expresses the vital need that our Practical Reason feels of proving or postulating this connexion of Virtue and self-interest, if it is to be made consistent with itself. For the negation of the connexion must force us to admit an ultimate and fundamental contradiction in our apparent intuitions of what is Reasonable in conduct; and from this admission it would seem to follow that the apparently intuitive operation of the Practical Reason, manifested in these contradictory judgments, is after all illusory."

and that's basically how the whole book ends. I get the broad shape of it (egoism and utilitarianism both feel self-evident to him and he can't derive one from the other) but I don't get the actual logical move in that last sentence - why does failing to prove one connexion force the conclusion that the intuitions themselves are illusory, rather than just leaving him with two solid axioms that happen to occasionally conflict? feels like a way stronger conclusion than the premises earn.
