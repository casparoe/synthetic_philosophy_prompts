I have no formal training in philosophy or computer science, but for the past year or so I have been teaching myself algorithmic information theory in my spare time, mostly through Wikipedia, a handful of lecture notes I found online, and as much of Li and Vitányi's textbook on Kolmogorov complexity as I could get through without much of a mathematical background to lean on. What drew me in was a claim I have now seen repeated in dozens of places: that Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity finally turn Occam's razor into something rigorous. Instead of vaguely preferring "simpler" theories, you assign each hypothesis a prior weight roughly proportional to 2^-K(x), where K(x) is the length of the shortest program that outputs x on some fixed universal Turing machine. The pitch, as I understand it, is that this is not merely inspired by Occam's razor but is an actual formalization of it.

The longer I sit with this, the less sure I am that the two are really the same principle, and I have not found a discussion that addresses this head-on. I raised it with a friend who works in machine learning, and he said the two notions of simplicity "obviously" amount to the same thing once you think it through properly, which did not settle anything for me. The traditional razor, as it is usually glossed, is a claim about ontology: not multiplying kinds of entities beyond what a theory needs. (Digging around, I learned that the tidy Latin motto everyone attaches to Ockham was actually coined by a seventeenth-century commentator and never appears anywhere in Ockham's own writings, which made me even less sure how settled the "traditional" version of the razor really is.) Kolmogorov complexity, by contrast, is entirely about the length of a description, with no obvious relation to how many things, or kinds of things, that description picks out. A few lines of code implementing something like a cellular automaton can unfold into a "universe" containing an unbounded variety of structures, while a much longer program could describe a sparse world with almost nothing in it. So I would like you to compare the two notions directly: is Solomonoff induction's version of Occam's razor really capturing the old idea of ontological economy, or is it a different principle, economy of description, that just happens to coincide with the traditional razor in the easy, textbook cases?

There is a second, related question I would appreciate your view on. Kolmogorov complexity is only ever defined relative to a choice of universal machine, and the invariance theorem merely guarantees that any two choices of machine will disagree by no more than some fixed additive constant, not that the notion becomes language-independent in any absolute sense. It seems to me that this quietly reintroduces, in more technical dress, the old objection that "simplicity" is always relative to a vocabulary or a way of carving up the world, an objection raised against Occam's razor long before anyone had heard of a Turing machine. Does the formal apparatus actually dissolve that worry, or does it just relocate it inside the choice of universal machine? I would like a fairly detailed answer that works through where these two senses of simplicity genuinely part ways, and whether anyone has seriously tried to reconcile them.
