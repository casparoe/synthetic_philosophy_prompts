Random rabbit hole, but I can't shake it and want a sharper take than my group chat gave me. I'm drafting a short story that's basically a modern-dress version of that old fable Maugham recycles at the end of Sheppey — the one where Death is out in the market in Baghdad, gives a servant a bad fright without meaning to, the servant bolts on a borrowed horse to Samarra to dodge her, and it turns out Death was only startled to see him there that morning because she'd already got him marked down for an appointment in Samarra that same night. Cute, spooky, five lines long, done.

While I was digging around for how other writers had reused it, I found out that decision theorists apparently stole the exact same setup, relocated it to Damascus and Aleppo, and turned it into an actual technical thought experiment called "Death in Damascus": a man knows Death is coming for him at midnight, he has to be in one of the two cities when the clock strikes, and Death is a genuinely good predicter of which one he'll choose. The part that hooked me is that this is supposedly a real problem for something called causal decision theory, because whichever city he picks, the mere fact that he'd pick it is supposed to count as evidence Death guessed right — so neither option ever settles down into looking like the safe one, they just keep flipping on each other the longer he thinks about it.

I want my protagonist's final decision, and her reasoning for it, to actually hold up rather than read as a spooky non-answer, so here's the ask: write me an essay that settles this instead of shrugging at it. Walk through why ordinary expected-utility reasoning seems to chase its own tail in this setup, explain what philosophers mean by a choice needing to be "self-ratifying," and say whether leaning on that idea genuinely fixes the problem or just relocates it — and weigh in on whether deliberately randomizing the decision (an actual coin flip) counts as the rational move here or as giving up on rationality altogether. Then commit to an answer: tell me what the rational thing to do actually is, and whether it changes depending on whether Death is a flawless predictor or merely right most of the time. My housemate, whos finishing a philosophy PhD and is the one who ruined my evening with this, swears there's no settled answer anywhere in the literature, but I'd definately like to think a genuinely careful argument can still land somewhere.
