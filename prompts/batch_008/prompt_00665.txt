I've spent the past few weeks reporting a piece on how philosophers have argued over the ethics of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and I keep returning to Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet opposing Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree. What strikes me, reading it alongside her later essays on intention and double effect, is that her charge of murder was never a simple utilitarian tally of lives lost against lives saved; she treats that kind of defense as beside the point. The verdict instead rests entirely on the doctrine of double effect: Truman is guilty of murder, on her view, because he chose the deaths of Hiroshima's and Nagasaki's civilians as the very means of forcing Japan's surrender, which she treats as categorically different from a case like bombing a munitions factory embedded in a city, where the civilian deaths are foreseen but not intended as the means to victory.

For the piece, I want to include a serious philosophical challenge to her verdict, one that argues entirely from within her own framework rather than retreating to the "it saved more lives overall" reasoning she anticipated and dismissed. Could you make the strongest case you can that Anscombe's own double-effect apparatus doesn't clearly deliver the murder verdict she wants? One route worth pressing: Hiroshima, whatever else it was, genuinely housed the headquarters of Japan's Second General Army and served as a major embarkation port for troops, so the bombing might be redescribed as an attack on a legitimate military objective, with the deaths of most of the city's civilian population a foreseen but unintended side effect -- structurally closer to her munitions-factory case than to deliberate terror bombing. You'll need to reckon honestly with the objection Anscombe herself raises in "War and Murder," where she dismisses this sort of redescription as sophistical evasion and reserves particular scorn for the idea that the civilian deaths at Hiroshima and Nagasaki were merely an accident incidental to ending the war -- and with the fact that the target committee that chose Hiroshima was candid about wanting to maximize the bomb's psychological and demonstrative effect on the population, not just its military effect. Don't hedge: tell me how far this line of argument actually gets a defender of Truman, and whether it survives Anscombe's own test for a principled, rather than self-serving, redescription of intention.
