So I've been falling down a rationalist-blog rabbit hole lately reading old posts about acausal trade -- the idea that two agents with absolutely no way to causally affect each other (the classic example is two civilizations on opposite sides of the universe, far enough apart that nothing -- not light, not radio, not anything -- could ever pass between them) can still strike a "deal" and both keep their end of it, purely because each one reasoned out what the other would probably do and acted accordingly. Everyone in these threads just kinda treats it as something legit decision theorists actually take seriously, but the more I sit with it the more it feels like it might just be a fancy sci-fi costume for "imagine if everyone were nice."

The part that really gets me is the compliance step. Say you somehow become confident that some causally disconnected agent would do X for you if you commited to doing Y for them. Fine, whatever -- but once it's actually "your turn" to do Y, nothing and no one can ever check whether you followed through, reward you for doing it, or punish you for skipping it -- the other party is unreachable, forever, full stop. Under plain old causal decision theory that seems like an obvious "why bother," just take the benefit and never pay up. And yet it nags at me that this has basically the same shape as Newcomb's problem, where there's no causal channel running from your choice back to what's already sitting in the box, and plenty of smart people still insist one-boxing is the rational move there.

So here's what I want from you: write me an essay that actually grapples with this instead of just gesturing at "decision theory is weird." Lay out the strongest case that acausal-trade compliance can be genuinely rational, the strongest case that it's confused (or at least smuggles in some assumption that doesn't hold for real, bounded agents), and then actually land somewhere instead of ending on "reasonable people disagree." A conditional verdict is totally fine, like "only when such-and-such holds" -- I just don't want a cop-out non-answer.
