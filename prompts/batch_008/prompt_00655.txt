I've been going down a rabbit hole of AI safety posts (LessWrong, Alignment Forum type stuff) and keep running into "coherence arguments" as the reason to expect advanced AI to end up as expected utility maximizers -- basically, if your preferences can't be described as maximizing some utility function, someone can Dutch-book / money-pump you out of all your stuff, so any sufficiently capable, self-improving agent will get squeezed into being coherent whether it wants to or not.

Someone in my study group used this to argue that the whole corrigibility research agenda (building AI that will actually let you shut it down or correct it) is basically a waste of time: if coherence is mathematically forced onto any smart enough system, it'll always resist having its goals edited or being switched off, since thats instrumentally bad for whatever its maximizing. So on his view alignment just reduces to picking the exactly correct utility function from the start, full stop, and all the corrigibility stuff is basically pointless. It sounded pretty convincing when he said it but also kind of too clean and fatalistic. Is that actually what the coherence theorems show, or is he overselling them?
