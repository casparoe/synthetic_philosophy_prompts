As a retired materials scientist, I spent my career characterizing dispositional properties—solubility, brittleness, conductivity—without ever asking how philosophers analyze the concept of a disposition itself. Having since taken up philosophy, I have become interested in a specific dispute: whether the Simple Conditional Analysis of dispositions can be repaired to survive its counterexamples.

Write a dialogue between two equally skilled philosophers who disagree on this. One is a conditional analyst who defends a Lewis-style Reformed Conditional Analysis, patched to survive C. B. Martin's finkish counterexamples (a dead wire connected to a hidden device that makes it live only when touched). The other is a dispositional essentialist who holds that Mark Johnston's masking cases, sharpened by Alexander Bird's 'antidote' examples (a fragile glass cushioned in packaging; a poison neutralized by a co-administered antidote), show that no further repair will work, and that dispositions must instead be treated as primitive, unanalyzable properties.

Have the two engage each other's strongest moves directly rather than talk past one another: the essentialist should explain precisely why masking leaves the causal basis intact yet still blocks the manifestation Lewis's analysis requires it to produce, while the conditional analyst should press the essentialist on whether invoking primitive powers actually explains a disposition's manifestation or simply relabels it. End with the disagreement sharpened, not resolved.
