I'm working on a short piece (probably for the newsletter, maybe I'll pitch it wider) about the private metaphysical beliefs of 20th century scientists and mathematicians that never made it into their published work. Right now I'm stuck on Gödel, specifically the run of letters he wrote his mother Marianne in 1961 where she flat-out asks him if he believes they'll see each other again after she's gone, and instead of just saying "I hope so" he spends four separate letters building her an actual argument for why the answer is yes. I mentioned this to a friend who did a philosophy PhD and even she'd never heard of it, which makes me think its a good "did you know" hook, but it also makes me nervous I'm going to botch the philosophy.

Here's my rough reconstruction of the argument as it stands right now, not polished writing yet, just the bones of it: the world is rationally organized, not chaotic or arbitrary — that's basically the working assumption behind science itself, and Gödel leans on "everything has a cause" as the model for "everything has meaning," very Leibniz. A genuinely rational world doesn't build something and then waste it. Human beings show up with an enormous capacity for growth — intellectual, moral, relational — and then die having cashed out something like a thousandth of it, in his own phrase. If the world really is rational all the way down, that's an intolerable waste, on the order of pouring a foundation and then abandoning the house. So there has to be a further existence where the rest of that potential gets used. And he's oddly careful to specify this isn't reincarnation — he doesnt think you get a new body and relearn arithmetic. It's more that this life just supplies the raw, half-digested material, and the next existence is where you finally understand it, with total clarity, for the first time.

Could you poke holes in this for me? Specifically: does the argument actually hold together, or is "the world is rational" doing way more work than it's entitled to, basically smuggling in the conclusion? Is there a legitimate way to connect this to his incompleteness theorems and his mathematical Platonism (he was fiercely anti-materialist, insisted the mind can't just be a "finite machine"), or would that be a forced connection I shouldn't make? And separately, just as a piece of writing — is this an interesting hook or a cute dead end? I know you can't go pull up the actual letters, but the reconstruction above should be enough to work with.
