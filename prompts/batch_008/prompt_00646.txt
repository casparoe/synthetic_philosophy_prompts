I'm preparing a graduate seminar on Hobbes's philosophy of mathematics, and there's a short passage I'd like help thinking through. It comes from the epistle dedicatory to his *Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematiques* (1656), written in the middle of his dispute with the mathematician John Wallis:

"Of arts, some are demonstrable, others indemonstrable; and demonstrable are those the construction of the subject whereof is in the power of the artist himself, who, in his demonstration, does no more but deduce the consequences of his own operation. The reason whereof is this, that the science of every subject is derived from a precognition of the causes, generation, and construction of the same; and consequently where the causes are known, there is place for demonstration, but not where the causes are to seek for. Geometry therefore is demonstrable, for the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves; and civil philosophy is demonstrable, because we make the commonwealth ourselves. But because of natural bodies we know not the construction, but seek it from the effects, there lies no demonstration of what the causes be we seek for, but only of what they may be." (EW VII, 184)

What I can't quite settle is how the criterion Hobbes states here is supposed to keep civil philosophy on the demonstrable side of the line rather than sliding it over to join physics on the hypothetical side. The argument seems to depend on treating the covenant by which subjects erect a commonwealth as strictly analogous to a geometer's construction of a line or figure: in both cases the reasoner is supposed to already possess the causes of the object because he made it himself. But the premises Hobbes actually needs to get his civil science moving -- claims about the passions, about natural equality, about what drives men to quarrel -- read like generalizations about how human beings in fact behave, arrived at from observation and introspection, not stipulated by construction. That looks structurally identical to how he treats the causes of sensation, which he elsewhere denies we can know with more than hypothetical certainty. So does "we make the commonwealth ourselves" really secure a different epistemic status for civil philosophy than for natural philosophy, or is Hobbes trading on an equivocation between two different senses of "making"? I'd like your best reconstruction of how the argument is meant to work, and whether you think it succeeds.
