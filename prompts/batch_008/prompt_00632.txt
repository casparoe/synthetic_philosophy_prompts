I'm several drafts into a historical novel centered on a fictionalized Russian émigré poet, and researching it has left me stuck on a question that has nothing to do with plot. While working through the rival English translations of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin — including Nabokov's famously literal, deliberately unrhymed and unmetered version, which he defended at length in his own combative commentary — I keep running into the same claim from translators, scholars, and native Russian speakers alike: that the original does things with sound, rhythm, and the intricacies of the "Onegin stanza" that no English version has ever managed to preserve. I have no particular reason to doubt them. But I don't read Russian, and nothing available to me in English would let me perceive what they're describing for myself. So when I catch myself agreeing that "Pushkin's verse is untranslatably beautiful," I'm not sure I'm doing anything more respectable than repeating a verdict I have no independent way of checking — which is an unsettling thing to notice, given that my whole profession trades on judgments of literary quality.

It turns out this isn't just a private anxiety but a live dispute in aesthetics, usually discussed under the heading of aesthetic testimony. "Optimists" hold that aesthetic testimony works much like ordinary testimony: if a source is reliable, their telling me a poem is beautiful can give me real aesthetic knowledge, full stop. "Pessimists" deny this, though not all for the same reason. Some hold that testimony simply cannot transmit aesthetic knowledge in the first place, because a judgment of beauty must be grounded in one's own encounter with the object — a view in the spirit of Richard Wollheim's "acquaintance principle." Others concede that testimony might in principle hand you the knowledge, yet insist some further, non-epistemic norm still makes it improper to rest your belief on someone else's say-so rather than your own experience — a distinction Robert Hopkins presses in arguing that pessimists are better off holding this second, more concessive view.

I'd like you to write an essay that actually adjudicates this dispute rather than just narrating it. Please use the situation of a reader permanently screened off from the very properties everyone agrees make a work great — by a language barrier, in this case, though the specific mechanism doesn't much matter — as a running test case rather than a passing illustration, and let it genuinely pressure-test the positions. Weigh the strongest versions of optimism and pessimism, take a view on whether the split between "testimony can't give you the knowledge at all" and "it could, but you still shouldn't rely on it" is doing real philosophical work or collapses under scrutiny, and end by stating plainly what, if anything, a reader in my position is entitled to believe about the beauty of a poem they will never read in the original.
