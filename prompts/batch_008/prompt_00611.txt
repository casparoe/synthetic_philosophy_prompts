I spent about thirty-five years running a lab on protein synthesis before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen down a pretty deep rabbit hole of semiotics -- specifically Charles Sanders Peirce. My daughter thinks I've completely lost it, going from gel electrophoresis to reading nineteenth-century philosophy, but here we are. I keep getting stuck on his icon/index/symbol business and this whole notion of the "interpretant."

Heres what's been bugging me on my walks. Molecular biologists (myself included, back in the day) throw around "the genetic code" like its obviously a code in the same sense as Morse code or a cipher, with codons "standing for" amino acids. My first instinct was that this couldn't hold up under Peirce's definitions, since a genuine symbol for him is a sign connected to its object by convention or habit, mediated by an interpretant, and I assumed an interpretant basically meant a mind doing the interpreting. A ribosome obviously isn't a mind, so I figured Peirce would call the codon-to-amino-acid relation at best an index, like smoke to fire, not a real symbol. But then I read further and ran into his "quasi-mind" business, where he apparently extends semiosis to things like bees and even purely mechanical sign-processes, which just muddies the water for me all over again. And then there's Crick's old "frozen accident" idea -- that the codon assignments are basically arbitrary and got fixed early in evolutionary history rather than being chemically forced -- which sounds an awful lot like the kind of arbitrariness Peirce thought was the hallmark of a genuine symbol, as opposed to an icon or index.

Obviously I can't just ask the man, seeing as he died in 1914, decades before anyone had heard of a codon. So here's what I'm hoping you can do: reconstruct, as carefully as you can from his actual semiotic theory and not the cocktail-party version of it, what Peirce himself would most likely conclude if some time traveler sat him down and explained the genetic code to him in full. Would he count it as a genuine symbol in his technical sense, or an index, an icon, or none of the above? I want you to actually commit to a specific verdict on his behalf, not just list considerations on both sides, and defend it by working through whichever bits of his apparatus are actually load-bearing here -- firstness, secondness, thirdness, the interpretant and quasi-mind, unlimited semiosis -- and tying that back to the real biological details, like wobble pairing and the fact that the code is nearly but not quite universal across life, rather than hand-waving.
