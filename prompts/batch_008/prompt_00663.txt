I've been falling down a bit of a rabbit hole for a piece I'm working on about the history of arguments for the immateriality of the mind, and I keep getting stuck on Avicenna's "flying man" thought experiment. Every popular summary gives the same headline version: a fully-formed person created out of nowhere, suspended in mid-air, blindfolded, weightless, unable to feel anything -- and they always throw in this oddly specific detail that his limbs are spread out so they can't even touch each other -- and yet he still knows, with total certainty, that he exists. Therefore the soul must be seperate from the body. Sounds like a medieval warm-up act for Descartes, and everyone just leaves it there.

But when I actually dug into some of the secondary literature, it turns out specialists don't fully agree on what the thought experiment is even supposed to be doing logically. Some read it as a genuine standalone demonstration that the soul is an immaterial substance, full stop. Others argue Avicenna himself treats it more like a "pointer" meant to help you notice something -- that awareness of yourself doesn't run through the senses or through perceiving your body -- rather than a rigorous, self-contained proof, and that it was actually the Latin scholastics a couple centuries later who hardened it into a much stronger, freestanding epistemological argument. I mentioned this to a philosopher friend and she just said "depends who you ask," which was not exactly a satisfying answer.

So could you help me actually untangle the logic here? What would the argument need to establish to count as a real proof of mind-body dualism, as opposed to the much more modest claim that self-awareness is just immediate and non-inferential without telling us anything about what the self is actually made of? And is that detail about the non-touching limbs doing real argumentative work -- closing off some specific objection -- or is it more of a narrative flourish? I'm not looking for the Wikipedia version, I want the actual mechanics of the argument and where its real weak points are.
