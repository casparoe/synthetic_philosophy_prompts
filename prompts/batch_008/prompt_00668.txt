I'm preparing a graduate seminar on moral luck, and I keep getting tangled in an interpretive puzzle in one of the classic ancient sources for the debate. In Nicomachean Ethics I.10 (1100a–1101a; trans. W. D. Ross), Aristotle writes:

"If activities are, as we said, what gives life its character, no happy man can become miserable; for he will never do the acts that are hateful and mean. For the man who is truly good and wise, we think, bears all the chances life becomingly and always makes the best of circumstances, as a good general makes the best military use of the army at his command and a good shoemaker makes the best shoes out of the hides that are given him; and so with all other craftsmen. And if this is the case, the happy man can never become miserable; though he will not reach blessedness, if he meet with fortunes like those of Priam. Nor, again, is he many-coloured and changeable; for neither will he be moved from his happy state easily or by any ordinary misadventures, but only by many great ones, nor, if he has had many great misadventures, will he recover his happiness in a short time, but if at all, only in a long and complete one in which he has attained many splendid successes."

Within the space of a few lines, Aristotle seems to assert both that the happy man "can never become miserable" and that he can, given enough great misadventures, be "moved from his happy state" — which sounds a great deal like becoming unhappy, if not outright miserable. Explain whether the happiness/blessedness distinction he introduces genuinely dissolves this tension or merely relabels it, and say what would have to be true of eudaimonia for both claims to be jointly consistent. Then, using Nagel's fourfold taxonomy of moral luck (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal), tell me precisely which variety of luck this passage denies any purchase over virtuous activity itself, and which variety it still concedes a foothold over eudaimonia as a whole. Finally, does that division give Aristotle principled grounds for blocking Nagel's paradox about the encroachment of luck on moral assessment, or does it merely reproduce that paradox one level up?
