I've been chewing on something for a piece I'm writing about the limits of introspection -- basically "can you ever really be sure what you're feeling right now" -- and it sent me down a rabbit hole into Timothy Williamson's book Knowledge and Its Limits, specifically the chapter where he tries to prove that almost nothing about our own minds is "luminous," meaning a condition wich is such that if it's true, you're automatically in a position to know it's true. The example that stuck with me: you wake up cold and just lie in bed reading by the window as the room slowly warms up all morning, so gradually you can't feel it happening from one moment to the next. Williamson's argument is supposed to show that if knowing-you-feel-cold only requires a tiny margin for error between one instant and the next (roughly: if you can't reliably discriminate time t from time t+1, you can't know something at t that's already false at t+1), then chaining that all the way from freezing-and-you-clearly-know-it at dawn to warm-and-you-clearly-don't at noon produces a contradiction -- so apparently you never really knew you felt cold, even right at the start. And the kicker, apparently, is he later runs basically the same move against the KK principle, the idea that whenever you know something, you're automatically in a position to know that you know it.

My philosopher friend, who I tested this on over drinks, got cagey about whether the arguement actually holds up -- muttered something about how it might just be a sorites paradox wearing a trenchcoat (Williamson insists it isn't one), and something about the margin-for-error premise doing a lot of unearned work. That's roughly where my notes trail off into a doodle of a cat, so I need your help. Could you write me a proper essay weighing this out: present Williamson's anti-luminosity/anti-KK argument as strongly as you fairly can, then the best pushback from people trying to rescue introspective self-knowledge (or at least rescue KK for some restricted class of mental states), and then actually land somewhere. Don't leave me with "reasonable people disagree" -- tell me, and defend, whether you think the argument succeeds, fails outright, or only succeeds in some narrower/qualified form, because that verdict is basically what my nut graf hinges on.
