I've been a fairly obsessive reader of the rationalist blogosphere for the better part of a decade, and Bostrom's orthogonality thesis is one of those ideas I assumed I'd fully internalized — until I reread the relevant chapter of Superintelligence recently and got stuck on the objection from moral realism that he spends a few pages fending off. The worry, roughly: if there are objective moral facts, and grasping a moral fact of the right kind is itself motivating (as plenty of moral rationalists hold), then a sufficiently intelligent agent, being better than us at forming true beliefs, should eventually be moved to act well, so orthogonality ought to break down at the top of the intelligence scale. Bostrom's replies are careful rather than triumphant: he stresses that "intelligence," in his stipulated sense of efficient means-end optimization (closer to Legg and Hutter's "ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments" than to anything like Aristotelian phronesis), needn't track true belief in every domain; that final goals could simply be "overwhelming" and "dynamically stable" enough to swamp whatever motivational pull a true belief might carry; and, tellingly, that he doesn't want his case to rest on the Humean theory of motivation actually being correct.

What struck me on this reread is how directly the whole apparatus recapitulates the oldest argument in Western ethics: the Socratic claim, running through the Protagoras and the Meno, that no one does wrong willingly, that virtue simply is knowledge, and that a mind which fully grasped the good could not help but pursue it — a thesis Kant later re-derives from the demands of practical reason itself rather than from any theory of desire at all. So here is what I'd like: a dialogue of something like 700 to 1000 words between two well-matched interlocutors. Ada is a committed defender of Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and, as a corollary, of instrumental convergence — she should be willing to bring up the fact that self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive enhancement, and "goal-content integrity" are convergent sub-goals for almost any final goal, and then have to wrestle with whether that concession secretly helps her opponent more than it helps her. Callias is a present-day defender of Socratic and Kantian moral rationalism who thinks the entire orthogonality/instrumental-convergence apparatus smuggles in a contestable, and probably false, theory of motivation.

Please don't let either side win by default. Callias has to actually engage with the "thin," economist-style notion of rationality Ada is working with, rather than simply asserting his thicker one by fiat, and Ada has to take seriously the possibility that convergence at the level of instrumental sub-goals might, under enough reflective pressure, bleed into convergence at the level of final goals too, rather than just repeating that final goals are orthogonal by stipulation. I'd like each of them to respond to the other's actual best move at each turn, and I'd much rather the dialogue end on a genuine impasse, or a shared diagnosis of exactly where the disagreement lies, than on a clean victory for either side.
