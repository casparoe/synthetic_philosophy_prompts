I am currently teaching a graduate seminar on the epistemic challenges facing consequentialism, and this week we worked through Hilary Greaves's treatment of the cluelessness problem, in particular her distinction between "simple" cases of cluelessness (where the unforeseeable effects of the options under comparison are symmetric in some relevant sense, so that expected-value reasoning restricted to salient, foreseeable consequences remains legitimate) and "complex" cases (where the unforeseeable effects are asymmetric but not tractably estimable, as with the long-run ripple effects of donating to a economic-development charity versus buying oneself a sandwich). Several students in the seminar suspected that this distinction is really a matter of degree dressed up as a matter of kind: either the apparatus Greaves uses to rescue simple cases from the cluelessness worry could, on reflection, be redeployed for the supposedly complex ones, or else the trouble she diagnoses in complex cases infects the simple ones too, once you look closely at how "symmetry" of unforeseeable effects is actually established.

Please write an argument, addressed to an audience already familiar with Greaves's paper "Cluelessness," for the position that the simple/complex distinction cannot be sustained as a principled one -- that is, that there is no stable epistemic or metaphysical criterion sorting cases into one category rather than the other without either trivializing the distinction or collapsing it entirely. Engage directly with the specific examples Greaves uses to motivate the distinction (e.g., choosing a red versus a blue sweater to wear to a party, and donating to a charity aimed at economic development), explain precisely where the alleged symmetry-breaking criterion fails to do the work she needs it to do, consider at least one serious response available to Greaves, and conclude by defending the objection against that response.
