I am currently teaching a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of divine providence, and we have spent the last two weeks wrestling with Molina's account of scientia media—the idea that God knows, prior to any creative decree, the truth of every counterfactual of creaturely freedom (what any possible free agent would do in any possible circumstance), and uses this knowledge to select which world to actualize while leaving creaturely freedom fully intact. My students find the doctrine elegant in principle but deeply puzzling once you try to imagine what it would be like "from the inside," both for the knower and for the agent being known.

I'd like you to write a short story that dramatizes this puzzle rather than merely explaining it. Consider setting it in something like a celestial archive or a pre-creation council chamber, where a Molinist-style intelligence pores over an infinite library of "feasible worlds," each keyed to a single human life and the counterfactuals of what that person would freely do under every conceivable circumstance—tempted here, uncorrupted there, tested by loss in one variant and by fortune in another. Perhaps the tension in the narrative can turn on a moment where the archivist must decide whether to actualize a circumstance for a particular soul, fully certain of the outcome via middle knowledge, and yet must reckon with whether that certainty is compatible with the soul's freedom in any sense worth wanting. Feel free to invent your own narrative voice and imagery, but I'd like the story to leave the reader genuinely unsettled about whether libertarian freedom survives contact with this kind of foreknowledge, rather than resolving the tension too neatly.
