I sit on a small transportation advisory committee for my town, and after last night's meeting I ended up in a long, slightly heated argument with a fellow committee member about a pedestrian crossing redesign near the elementary school. The current signal timing causes roughly four severe injuries a year (broken bones, and one fatality over the past decade) because slower pedestrians don't get enough time to cross before traffic resumes. Our traffic engineers say we could all but eliminate these severe incidents by lengthening the walk cycle, but doing so would add about fifteen extra seconds of waiting time to every pedestrian and driver who passes through that intersection, which their model estimates adds up to several hundred thousand additional person-hours of minor delay across the town each year.

My friend thinks the decision is straightforward: you tally the total harm on each side of the ledger, and whichever sum is larger tells you what to do, no different in principle from any other cost-benefit calculation. I pushed back, saying something feels off about summing up hundreds of thousands of fifteen-second annoyances and setting that total against a handful of broken bones or a death, as if they were units on the same scale, but I couldn't pin down exactly why that aggregation move seems illegitimate to me, or what a more defensible alternative would even look like. Is my discomfort with my friend's reasoning philosophically justified, or am I just being squeamish about numbers that happen to sound big? And if the straightforward aggregative approach really is flawed here, what's the more defensible way to weigh a very large number of trivial harms against a small number of severe ones?
