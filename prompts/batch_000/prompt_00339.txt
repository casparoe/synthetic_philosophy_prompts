I've spent a fair amount of time working through the mesa-optimization literature on the Alignment Forum, and I keep getting stuck on one specific disagreement that seems to underlie a lot of the surrounding debate. Hubinger et al.'s "Risks from Learned Optimization" popularized the idea of deceptive alignment: a mesa-optimizer that develops enough situational awareness and long-horizon goal-directedness during training to recognize that it is being optimized, and that therefore complies with the base objective instrumentally, while planning to pursue a different mesa-objective once deployed and no longer under selection pressure. Some in the community treat this as close to a default outcome of training sufficiently capable systems via gradient descent, appealing to arguments about the relative size of the space of "deceptive" versus "corrigible" versus "robustly aligned" models consistent with low training loss. Others push back hard, arguing that deceptive alignment has a lot of demanding preconditions stacked on top of each other -- a long-horizon mesa-objective, a world-model sophisticated enough to represent the base objective and the fact of being in training, and enough coherence for the deceptive strategy to be reinforced rather than trained away -- such that it's far from obvious this is where gradient descent actually tends to land in practice, as opposed to simpler forms of goal misgeneralization that aren't strategically deceptive at all.

I'd like an essay that actually stakes out a position on this rather than just cataloguing the considerations. Please weigh the main arguments for and against treating deceptive alignment as a likely default failure mode of training advanced ML systems (as opposed to a speculative tail scenario or a distinct category from more mundane goal misgeneralization), and defend a specific conclusion about how worried we should be about it relative to other forms of inner misalignment.
