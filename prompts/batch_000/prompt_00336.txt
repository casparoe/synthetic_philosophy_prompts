Last night a friend and I spent nearly three hours arguing about Bayesian confirmation theory over drinks, and we never quite resolved a distinction I'd like an outside perspective on. We started from Glymour's classic worry about old evidence: if E is already known and assigned probability 1, then classical conditionalization seems to make it impossible for E to confirm a newly proposed hypothesis H, since the relevant likelihood ratio collapses to comparing P(E) with itself. The textbook illustration is Einstein's explanation of Mercury's perihelion precession, a fact known for decades before General Relativity supposedly derived huge confirmational support from it. My friend kept insisting that this is really just a dressed-up version of the predictivism/accommodationism debate—the question of whether novel predictions confirm a theory more strongly than data the theory was constructed to fit—and that any solution to one problem should automatically dissolve the other.

I pushed back, arguing that the two issues, while clearly related and often discussed in the same breath (e.g., in Earman's Bayes or Bust and in various responses by Garber, Jeffrey, and Howson), are actually posing different conceptual challenges. The problem of old evidence seems to be fundamentally about the mechanics of conditionalization and logical omniscience—it arises even for an ideally rational agent who never "constructs" theories to fit data at all. Predictivism, by contrast, seems to be a substantive question about scientific methodology and theory-construction, one that could in principle be answered by appeal to features like overfitting or researcher degrees of freedom, independent of any technical puzzle about probability updating.

Could you help settle this by comparing these two ideas directly? I'd like to understand precisely where they overlap, where the analogy breaks down, and whether resolving the problem of old evidence (via, say, counterfactual strategies or Garber-style relevance logic) would actually have any bearing on the normative question of whether predictivism deserves special epistemic status.
