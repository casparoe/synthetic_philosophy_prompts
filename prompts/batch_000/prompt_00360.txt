I teach a graduate seminar on philosophy of physics, and this week we're covering anthropic reasoning and the Boltzmann brain problem. My students generally follow the technical argument well enough -- that in a sufficiently old, large, or high-entropy universe, thermodynamically fluctuated observers with false memories should vastly outnumber genuine evolved ones, which threatens to undercut our grounds for trusting our own memories and cosmological inferences in the first place -- but they tend to treat it as a curious paradox rather than something with real existential bite. To help them feel that bite, I'd like to assign a short story that dramatizes the predicament from the inside: a character who, for good in-universe reasons, is forced to confront the live possibility that their entire remembered life and the coherent world around them are a momentary statistical fluctuation rather than the product of a genuine causal history, and who has to decide how (or whether) this should change their beliefs and actions in the time they have left. I'd rather avoid a cheap "it was all a dream" reveal; I want the piece to take the underlying probabilistic and cosmological reasoning seriously and let the philosophical unease emerge from it directly, whether the setting is a research lab, a dying far-future universe, or something as mundane as a kitchen table on an ordinary morning.
