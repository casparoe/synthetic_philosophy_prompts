I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and something happened in my research group last week that's been bugging me. Me and another postdoc (we're both pretty equally trained statisticians, have looked at the exact same regression outputs, robustness checks, everything) came to totally opposite conclusions about whether our main effect is actually driven by a confound or not. We've gone back and forth for hours, neither of us has any special extra info the other lacks, and neither of us can point to a flaw in the other's reasoning that would explain the gap. She's just as smart and careful as I am, maybe more so honestly. My gut says I should just... update toward her view a lot, like meet her halfway, since I have no real reason to think I'm more likely to be right than she is. But some other people in the lab said that's basically giving up on having independent judgment, and that if you always split the difference with anyone who disagrees with you, you'll never stand for anything and will just get pulled around by whoever argues loudest or most confidently. So now I'm stuck: when a genuine "peer" (equally competent, same evidence) disagrees with me and we can't find the crux, how much should I actually budge? Is caving toward the average always the rational move, or is there a real case for just... holding my ground even without new arguments?
