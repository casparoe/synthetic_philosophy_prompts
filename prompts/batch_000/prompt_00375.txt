I'm putting together lecture notes for a graduate seminar on decision theory, and I want to make sure I've got the Death in Damascus case straight before I present it to my students. As I understand the scenario, it's structurally just a dressed-up version of Newcomb's Problem: Death has already fixed his prediction about where the traveler will be tomorrow, so nothing the traveler decides now can causally influence that prediction one way or the other. Given that, it seems like causal decision theory should deliver the same clean verdict it gives in the Newcomb case—something like "stay put in Damascus," since the traveler's present choice can't retroactively alter Death's already-settled forecast. On this reading, Death in Damascus would just be another demonstration of CDT's superiority over evidential decision theory, arriving at a single stable recommendation the way two-boxing does for Newcomb.

I'd planned to present it exactly this way, but a colleague who works on formal epistemology raised an eyebrow and said the case is actually supposed to cause trouble *for* causal decision theorists specifically, not just vindicate them against EDT again. I can't figure out what she has in mind, since the causal structure (fixed prediction, no backward causation) looks the same to me as in Newcomb's Problem. Is my gloss on the case basically right and she's overstating things, or is there some feature of Death in Damascus—maybe something about the symmetry between the two locations, or about what CDT recommends once you factor in how confident the traveler should be about the prediction—that actually makes it a genuinely different, and harder, case than Newcomb's?
