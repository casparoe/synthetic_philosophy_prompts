I recently switched teams at the quantitative fund where I work, and the new desk uses an internal "predictive allocation" system that assigns bonus multipliers based on a model's forecast of which trade a portfolio manager will place before the trade is placed. Reading the fine print of how the multiplier schedule is constructed, I realized the incentive structure is structurally identical to the Death in Damascus case from decision theory: whichever trade I choose, the model has (with high but imperfect accuracy) already priced in that exact choice, so the expected payoff from "switching" collapses right back to the payoff of the option I switched away from. I've read the Gibbard-Harper treatment of this puzzle before in a decision theory seminar, but it always felt like a cute abstract counterexample; now that I'm staring at an actual payoff table with this property, I find I don't really understand why the problem is as deep as it's made out to be.

Could you walk me through, at a fairly granular level, why causal decision theory fails to deliver a stable recommendation in Death-in-Damascus-type cases — specifically, why no available action turns out to be "ratifiable" in Gibbard and Harper's sense, and why an agent following CDT seems to cycle between options rather than settle on one? I'd also like to understand whether evidential decision theory genuinely avoids this instability or merely relocates it (e.g., via something like the tickle defense), and whether appealing to mixed strategies or some other patch actually resolves the underlying issue rather than just papering over it. An intuitive, mechanism-level explanation would help me more than another restatement of the formalism at this point.
