I've recently come across the idea of "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) in some effective altruism and rationalist writing on acausal decision theory, and I'm struggling to assess how seriously it should be taken as a guide to actual conduct. The rough argument, as I understand it, is that if the universe (or multiverse) is sufficiently large, there likely exist many agents elsewhere whose decision procedures are correlated with our own even without any causal link between us, and that this correlation gives us evidential reason to act as if we had struck a cooperative bargain with agents holding different values, thereby broadening the range of considerations that should factor into what any given agent does.

Write an essay that lays out the core argument for ECL as clearly as possible, including the role played by something like superrationality, the assumption of psychological or decision-theoretic similarity across causally disconnected agents, and the motivation for thinking this could matter even for agents who are not antecedently committed to any exotic multiverse cosmology. Then weigh the strongest objections: worries about whether we can ever have justified credences about the values and decision procedures of agents we cannot observe or interact with, concerns about demandingness and exploitability, the risk that ECL functions as an unfalsifiable rationalization for whatever an agent already wants to do, and the question of whether the relevant notion of "correlation without causation" is coherent or just a category error imported from Newcomb-style thought experiments. Conclude by taking a clear stance on whether ECL should actually inform real-world moral or practical decision-making, and if so, to what extent and under what conditions, rather than leaving the matter open.
