I'm currently reporting a longform piece on how longtermist and effective-altruist-adjacent researchers think about cooperation that stretches beyond the usual boundaries of bargaining and trade. In interviewing decision theorists at a couple of x-risk research institutes, I kept running into two ideas that initially struck me as nearly identical but that my sources insisted are conceptually distinct: "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL), and what's sometimes called "moral trade" -- the notion that people with different values can each do better by their own lights by dividing moral labor, even without any causal mechanism enforcing the deal. Both seem to rest on the same basic intuition that you can benefit from cooperating with agents you will never meet, but when I pressed further, one researcher told me that ECL only works because of specific assumptions about a large or infinite universe and correlated decision-making, while ordinary moral trade doesn't need any of that machinery.

For the article, I'd like to get this distinction right rather than gloss over it for the sake of a tidy paragraph. Could you walk me through how evidential cooperation in large worlds differs from moral trade as a mechanism for cross-value cooperation? I'm especially interested in three things: first, whether the cooperation in each case is causal or acausal, and what work the size or structure of the universe is doing in ECL that it isn't doing in ordinary moral trade; second, what kind of epistemic or decision-theoretic commitments -- something like superrationality, or beliefs about the existence of correlated agents elsewhere in the universe -- ECL requires that moral trade simply doesn't; and third, whether moral trade could be understood as a special, causally-mediated case of the more general phenomenon ECL is pointing at, or whether the two are doing genuinely different conceptual work that shouldn't be collapsed into one another. A worked example of each, set side by side, would be especially useful for illustrating the difference to readers who aren't steeped in decision theory.
