I've been working through the literature on causal decision theory and keep getting stuck on the Death in Damascus case (Gibbard and Harper's original version, and Andy Egan's later discussion of it). The setup is familiar: Death tells you he will meet you tomorrow, and whatever city you flee to, he will be there, since he is a perfect predictor of your choice. The puzzle is that no matter which city you causally evaluate as "safer," the very act of settling on that choice seems to make the other city look safer instead, so causal decision theory appears to generate a kind of practical instability rather than a stable recommendation.

Some theorists, like Gibbard and Harper, think this instability can be tamed by adding a ratifiability requirement -- you should only choose an option that remains optimal once you fully expect yourself to choose it. Others, most notably Egan, argue that Death in Damascus is actually a counterexample to causal decision theory itself, since it shows CDT can recommend an action that CDT's own reasoning tells you to regret as soon as you've settled on it, and that no ratifiability patch can fully rescue the view without generating new problems elsewhere (e.g. in cases like his "Psychopath Button").

Write an essay assessing this controversy: does supplementing causal decision theory with a ratifiability constraint adequately handle Death in Damascus, or does the case reveal a deeper flaw in CDT that such constraints cannot repair? Engage with the main arguments on both sides, but don't end on the fence -- defend a specific verdict about what Death in Damascus shows us about the prospects of causal decision theory.
