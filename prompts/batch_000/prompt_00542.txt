I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, and we've moved past the standard restaurant-bill and thermometer-reading cases into a thornier issue: whether conciliationism can be applied consistently to itself. As one of my students pointed out, if the Equal Weight View is correct, and a substantial number of one's epistemic peers in metaepistemology reject it, then the conciliationist seems rationally obligated to reduce her confidence in conciliationism itself. But if she does that, she thereby undercuts her own reason for conciliating on the original, object-level dispute that prompted the worry in the first place. This bootstrapping problem has generated a small but sharp literature (Elga's remarks on the "extra weight" a conciliationist might need to escape self-undermining, and various rejoinders about distinguishing levels of disagreement).

I'd like you to write a dialogue between a committed conciliationist and a steadfaster who run into each other at the hotel bar after a conference panel, where an informal straw poll revealed the room split almost evenly on whether the Equal Weight View is even true. The steadfaster should press the self-undermining worry as a kind of reductio, while the conciliationist tries to construct a stable formulation of the view that doesn't collapse into paralysis or arbitrary self-exemption -- perhaps by distinguishing first-order from higher-order disagreements, or by arguing that some asymmetry in the parties' evidence blocks full symmetry. Have the two characters really engage each other's strongest moves rather than talking past one another, and let the conciliationist openly acknowledge, without fully resolving, her own discomfort with the regress before offering her best response to it.
