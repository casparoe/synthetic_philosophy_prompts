So I've been stuck on van Inwagen's special composition question for my metaphysics seminar this week (the "under what conditions do some xs compose a y" framing from Material Beings), and a few of us in the reading group got derailed last night wondering what Leibniz would've made of it if he'd had our contemporary menu of answers on the table. He obviously draws a hard distinction between genuine unities (monads, or composites with a substantial form, unum per se) and mere aggregates like a heap of stones or an army, which sounds like it's gesturing toward something like restricted composition, but he never actually had to confront van Inwagen's specific options: nihilism, universalism, contact, fastenation, "life," etc. He also thought aggregates only get their unity parasitically, from a perceiving mind imposing structure on them, which seems to push toward eliminativism about ordinary inanimate objects.

Given that background, how do you think Leibniz would answer the SCQ if you actually forced him to engage with van Inwagen's own test cases, like the trout-turkey (the scattered fusion of a trout and a turkey) or a swarm of self-repairing nanobots that functionally behaves like a single organism? Would he land on something like organicism, where real composition only happens when there's a genuine substantial form/entelechy unifying the parts, making him basically a nihilist about rocks and turkeys-fused-with-fish but not about living things? Or is there a subtler position buried in the Monadology and Discourse on Metaphysics that I'm not seeing, maybe something that lets artifacts count as well-founded phenomena without full metaphysical unity? I'd love a reconstruction that sticks strictly to concepts he'd have actually endorsed rather than just retrofitting modern mereology onto him.
