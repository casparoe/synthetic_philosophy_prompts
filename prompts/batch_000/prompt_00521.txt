I've been working through the literature on naturalizing mental content, and I keep getting tangled in how different theories handle the disjunction problem. Two views that seem to converge on similar solutions but diverge sharply in their explanatory resources are Ruth Millikan's teleosemantics and Fred Dretske's informational/indicator semantics from *Knowledge and the Flow of Information*. Both want to explain how a mental state can represent, say, "frog food" rather than "small dark moving thing," but Millikan grounds content in the selection history of the consumer systems that use the representation, while Dretske leans on a privileged "learning period" during which information is fixed, plus a later functional/causal role that inherits that content.

Could you compare these two approaches in some detail? I'm especially interested in: (a) whether Millikan's appeal to "proper function" actually does more explanatory work than Dretske's appeal to a privileged learning period, or whether the two are just relabeling the same underlying move; (b) how each theory handles misrepresentation that occurs during the very period that's supposed to fix content in the first place, i.e., whether either view has a principled, non-circular way of specifying the boundaries of that fixing period or selection history; and (c) which theory has an easier time accounting for representations that were never actually "selected for" in any robust evolutionary sense, such as novel concepts formed within a single organism's lifetime through learning or reasoning.
