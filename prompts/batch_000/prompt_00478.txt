I'm a retired physical chemist who, in retirement, has fallen down the rabbit hole of decision theory -- largely through reading about AI alignment, where these questions keep resurfacing in discussions of whether advanced agents must behave like expected utility maximizers. I've worked through the classic money-pump and Dutch-book arguments purporting to show that any agent with intransitive or incomplete preferences can be exploited into a guaranteed loss, and hence that rational agency requires something close to expected utility maximization. What I can't quite get my head around is a more recent objection I've come across, associated with writers like Johan Gustafsson and Andrew Sepielli, to the effect that an agent with genuinely incomplete preferences (as opposed to merely intransitive ones) can dodge the money pump entirely by adopting a simple policy -- for instance, refusing any trade away from its current holding unless the alternative is strictly better, even when the alternative is 'merely' incomparable rather than worse. Some commentators seem to think this policy rescues incompleteness from the coherence theorems, while others insist it's a kind of cheat that smuggles in extra structure (a memory of one's starting point, essentially) that the original argument never assumed. Could you walk me through, at an intuitive rather than fully formalized level, why the money-pump argument seems to push so hard toward completeness in the first place, and then where exactly this "resolute choice" style objection is supposed to land its blow? I'd like to understand what's really at stake in the disagreement, not just see the axioms restated.
