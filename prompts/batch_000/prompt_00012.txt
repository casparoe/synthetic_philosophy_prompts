I was rereading Sartre's account of bad faith last week for a seminar, and I got stuck on something that seems important but that most secondary literature glosses over quickly. Sartre treats bad faith as a distinctive kind of self-deception—the waiter who plays at being a waiter, the woman on the date who refuses to acknowledge the sexual character of her companion's attention—where consciousness flees its own freedom by pretending to be a fixed thing (an in-itself) rather than the perpetually self-surpassing nothingness that it is. But ordinary self-deception, as philosophers like Donald Davidson or Alfred Mele describe it, seems to involve something structurally similar: a subject holding contradictory beliefs, or motivated to avoid unwelcome truths about themselves, while somehow remaining unified enough to pull off the deception.

What I can't pin down is exactly what makes bad faith more than a special case of garden-variety self-deception applied to the topic of one's own freedom. Is the difference merely thematic—bad faith is self-deception specifically about facticity and transcendence—or is Sartre pointing to a distinct psychological or ontological structure that ordinary self-deception doesn't share? Sartre himself seems to think the classic "deceiver/deceived" model of self-deception is incoherent for a unified consciousness, which is partly why he invents bad faith as an alternative. So does bad faith actually solve a paradox that standard theories of self-deception can't, or does it just relocate the paradox somewhere else (e.g., into the strange dual structure of being both facticity and transcendence at once)? I'd like a comparison that takes seriously both the surface similarities and where Sartre thought his account had to diverge, and that evaluates whether that divergence is philosophically earned.
