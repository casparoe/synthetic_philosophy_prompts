I've been prepping a seminar on longtermist decision theory and keep getting tripped up on one piece of evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) that I don't think I can explain well to my students. The whole framework leans on the idea that if the universe (or multiverse) is big enough, there are far-away agents whose dispositions correlate with mine even though we can't causally interact -- fine, that's basically just Newcomb's problem writ large. But what I can't get straight is how ECL proponents justify picking out *which* distant agents count as correlated enough to cooperate with, given that correlation isn't binary and we have basically zero emprical access to the relevant base rates. Like, how do you avoid this collapsing into either (a) an ad hoc stipulation that "value-aligned-but-different" agents are correlated, or (b) a wildly promiscuous view where literally every possible agent is "correlated" to some infinitesimal degree, making the whole cooperative calculus intractable? Can you walk me through how the more careful defenders of ECL (Oesterheld and that crowd) handle this problem, and give me some gut-level intuition for why the view doesn't just dissolve into hand-waving about "acausal" similarity?
