My advisor keeps pushing back on the population axiology embedded in these models, pointing me toward Parfit's repugnant conclusion and Arrhenius's impossibility theorems, which show that seemingly any principled way of aggregating welfare across variable populations runs into some deeply counterintuitive commitment (whether that's the repugnant conclusion itself, the sadistic conclusion, or a violation of something like the mere addition principle). I've been reading around critical-level utilitarianism, variable-value views, and Parfit's own later attempts to find a "theory X" that escapes these results, but I'm having trouble translating the philosophical stakes into concrete modeling advice.

Could you lay out what you take to be the most important implications of taking the repugnant conclusion (and the broader impossibility results surrounding it) seriously, both for someone who has to actually pick a social welfare function for use in a quantitative model like an IAM, and more generally for how economists and policymakers should reason about tradeoffs between population size and average well-being in climate and population policy? I'm less interested in a general survey of the population ethics literature than in the practical upshots: e.g., whether there's a defensible way to sidestep the impossibility results for modeling purposes, or whether we should instead just be explicit about which counterintuitive conclusion we're willing to bite the bullet on and why.
