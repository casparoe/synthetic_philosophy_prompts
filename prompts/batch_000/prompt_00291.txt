I'm a PhD student working on decision theory and the foundations of AI safety, currently drafting a chapter on defenses against acausal threats in multipolar scenarios involving advanced artificial agents. I've been working through the Center on Long-Term Risk's proposal that an agent vulnerable to blackmail or threats from other acausally-interacting agents (or from agents that can simulate it) might adopt a "surrogate goal" -- a comparatively low-stakes objective that it commits to treating as if it mattered greatly, so that a rational threatener is incentivized to target the surrogate rather than the agent's actual terminal values. The hope is that this reduces expected disvalue from realized threats without requiring the agent to abandon a general policy of refusing to capitulate to threats (since, on this proposal, the agent isn't "giving in" on anything it truly cares about). In my reading group this has split people rather sharply: some think surrogate goals are a genuinely stable and elegant way to blunt threats while preserving decision-theoretic integrity, while others think the maneuver is self-undermining -- either because a sufficiently sophisticated threatener can model the act of surrogate-adoption itself and simply threaten both the real and surrogate goal (or threaten to punish the surrogate more severely once it detects the maneuver), or because a commitment that doesn't cash out in real behavioral stakes isn't credible as a target in the first place, or because the whole strategy just relocates the underlying vulnerability rather than removing it.

Please write a dialogue between a proponent who defends surrogate goals as a real solution to the problem of threat-vulnerability, and a skeptic who thinks the strategy is unstable or fails to address the underlying issue. Have them engage with concrete cases rather than trading slogans -- for instance, a threatener who threatens both the real goal and the surrogate simultaneously, a threatener who conditionalizes its behavior on whether the surrogate was adopted specifically for defensive purposes, and the question of what it would even mean for the surrogate commitment to be "credible" without collapsing into a second real goal. Both interlocutors should be equally sharp, actually responding to each other's objections in turn, and neither should simply concede the point by the end.
