I'm currently drafting a literary novel whose protagonist, Elena, is a vision scientist who was raised from infancy inside a strictly monochrome research facility, permitted to absorb every conceivable physical fact about color and the visual system but never allowed to see a hue herself, and who has just stepped outside its walls to view color for the first time. The scene I'm struggling to write is the conversation that follows between two of her former mentors who meet her afterward: one who insists that whatever happens to Elena in that moment cannot amount to the acquisition of new factual knowledge, since the complete physical description she already possessed must, in principle, exhaust everything there is to know about color vision; and another who takes the sheer fact that Elena will seem to learn something when she first sees red as proof that no amount of physical information could have prepared her for the experience itself. I don't want the textbook version of this exchange—I'd like each mentor to press the other on their most sophisticated moves: the physicalist reaching for something like Lewis's ability hypothesis or a phenomenal-concept strategy, and the second mentor explaining carefully why mastering an ability or acquiring a new concept doesn't, in her view, account for what actually happens to Elena. Could you write this dialogue so that it reads as a genuine exchange between equally formidable thinkers, each conceding ground where it's due but neither simply folding, so I have a model for how to escalate the philosophical stakes of the scene in the finished manuscript?
