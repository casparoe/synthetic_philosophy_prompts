In recent work on decision theory within the effective altruism and rationalist communities, there is a concept called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL): the idea that agents who cannot causally interact with one another—because they are too distant in space, time, or even located in different branches of a multiverse—can still benefit from acting as though they had cooperated. The reasoning is that adopting a cooperative policy gives one evidence that other, sufficiently similar agents elsewhere are following the same policy, so that mutual gains accrue without any causal channel between the parties. The whole framework presupposes a "large world" populated by many causally disconnected but psychologically or logically correlated agents.

I've been struck by how closely this resembles Leibniz's doctrine of pre-established harmony: monads that cannot causally influence one another, yet whose internal states are coordinated from the moment of creation so that their actions align as if they were interacting. Given Leibniz's commitments—his principle of sufficient reason, his rejection of the real existence of unrealized possible worlds (for him, only the actual, best of all possible worlds is real), and his theistic framework in which coordination among monads is secured by divine pre-arrangement rather than by decision-theoretic reasoning among the monads themselves—how do you think he would respond to the modern proposal of ECL? Would he regard it as a secularized, game-theoretic reinvention of pre-established harmony, arrived at from the wrong direction but gesturing at the same underlying truth? Or would he reject its key premises outright, on the grounds that a "large world" of many similar, independently reasoning agents cooperating without any coordinating God is metaphysically incoherent by his own lights? Try to reconstruct his likely position using only concepts and arguments he could plausibly have endorsed in the early eighteenth century.
