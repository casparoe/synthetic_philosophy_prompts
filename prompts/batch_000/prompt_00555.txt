I've been reading about a decision-theory idea called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) for a philosophy club project, and I find the core idea fascinating but hard to visualize: since the universe (or multiverse) is vast enough to contain countless agents who reason similarly to us but with whom we can never causally interact, our choices might still function as evidence about what those distant agents choose — meaning we could benefit by acting as though we're all quietly cooperating with one another despite total causal isolation. My philosophy teacher suggested that instead of just summarizing the theory in an essay, I try writing a short story to make the concept concrete, since she says good sci-fi has often explained weird decision-theoretic ideas (like Newcomb's paradox or superrationality) better than textbooks do.

Could you write a short story (roughly 800–1200 words) that dramatizes evidential cooperation in large worlds? I'd like it to feature a protagonist — maybe an astronomer, a researcher, or even an AI system — who comes to suspect that they are one of many structurally similar agents scattered across a vast or infinite cosmos, each unable to communicate with the others, yet each capable of shifting the overall balance of cooperative versus selfish behavior across all such agents simply by choosing to act cooperatively themselves. I'd like the story to capture both the emotional weight of committing to an act of "cooperation" whose payoff can never be directly verified, and the underlying logic of why such a commitment might still be rational under ECL, without turning into a dry philosophy lecture.
