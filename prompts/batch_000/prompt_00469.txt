So I was arguing with my friend Dana late last night about updateless decision theory (UDT) — you know, the idea that a rational agent should commit ex ante to whatever policy has the best expected outcome across the whole space of possible situations/observations, rather than updating and reasoning from wherever it happens to find itself (like in Newcomb's problem or counterfactual mugging). Dana kept insisting this is basically just game theory dressed up in new jargon, but I think theres something deeper going on about the relationship between an agent's identity across possible worlds and the timing of rational commitment.

This got me thinking about Leibniz, actually — his idea that God surveys all possible worlds "prior" to creation (in a logical, not temporal, sense) and selects the best one according to some global optimization over the whole space of compossible worlds, rather than reasoning myopically from within an already-actualized one. It struck me as eerily similar in structure to the "updateless" move: evaluating your policy from outside any particular instantiation of yourself, across all possible worlds/observations, instead of updating based on where you currently find yourself.

So here's what I want to know: do you think Leibniz would recognize UDT as basically vindicating his approach to theodicy and possible-worlds reasoning? Would he endorse the updateless agent's willingness to "pay up" in counterfactual mugging, given his commitment to pre-established harmony and the principle of sufficient reason? Or are there deep disanalogies between a perfectly rational, atemporal God optimizing over possible worlds and a bounded agent trying to figure out which decision policy to adopt from the inside? I'd love an actually detailed take here, not just the one-liner "decision theory is secretly theodicy."
