So I've been going down this rabbit hole lately about Leibniz and his whole "characteristica universalis" idea - basically that if we could just build the right symbolic language, any dispute (moral, legal, scientific, whatever) could be settled by just calculating the answer, like his famous "calculemus, let us calculate" line. I'm just a self-taught programmer, no philosophy degree or anything, but this reminded me a lot of stuff I've been reading about P vs NP and computational complexity - like how there's tons of problems that are technically computable, we know for a fact an algorithm exists, but it would take longer than the age of the universe to actually run it on any real input size. That seems like it would totally wreck Leibniz's dream even if he turned out to be completely right that everything reduces to calculation in principle. Computable isn't the same as feasible, and his whole project seems to assume that once you've got the right formal system, answers just pop out in reasonable time.

So here's what I'm curious about: if you could somehow sit Leibniz down today and walk him through NP-completeness, combinatorial explosion, maybe even stuff like undecidability, do you think he'd still believe his universal calculus could work with some clever workaround, or would he basically have to admit the whole project was naive? I don't want a generic "he'd be amazed by computers" answer - I want you to actually dig into his real philosophy (the monads, principle of sufficient reason, his optimism about a rational God-given order to things) and use that to make a real case for what he'd probably conclude once he understood the scale of the problem.
