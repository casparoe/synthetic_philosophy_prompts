I've been working through some effective altruism forum threads on longtermism, and that led me to Hilary Greaves' paper on cluelessness, which draws a distinction between "simple" and "complex" cluelessness. As I understand it, simple cluelessness covers cases where the unforeseeable downstream effects of an action have no systematic tilt one way or the other, so they can be treated as washing out in expectation and safely bracketed off when calculating expected value. Complex cluelessness, by contrast, covers cases where we have some reason to think the unforeseeable effects are skewed in a particular direction, but no way to quantify that skew, which is supposed to be the more genuinely troubling case for consequentialist decision-making. This distinction seems to do a lot of work in defenses of everyday consequentialist reasoning (e.g., the idea that donating to buy someone a malaria net is fine to evaluate on its near-term effects, because the far-future ripple effects are "merely simple" cluelessness).

What I want is an argument against this distinction as a way of rescuing ordinary consequentialist deliberation. Specifically, please argue for the position that the simple/complex distinction is not principled, and that on reflection almost every real-world action we might evaluate consequentially generates complex rather than simple cluelessness, because we can almost always construct some asymmetric causal story (however speculative) about how an action might systematically favor certain long-run outcomes over others. I'd like the argument to engage with why the "cancels out in expectation" reasoning for simple cluelessness is harder to justify than it looks, rather than just asserting that unforeseen effects are scary and unknowable.
