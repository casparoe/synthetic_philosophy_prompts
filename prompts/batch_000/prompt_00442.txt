Was arguing with my roommate last night about that classic 3-judge panel example from judgment aggregation theory — you know, the one where each judge has a view on (a) whether the defendant breached a contract, and (b) whether the breach caused damages, and the conclusion (c) whether the defendant is liable, with liability defined as breach AND damages. The annoying thing is that if you take majority votes issue-by-issue on (a) and (b) and then plug the results into the rule, you get "liable," but if you just take a majority vote on the bottom-line question (c) directly, you can get "not liable." My roommate insisted courts should just use a conclusion-based procedure, since that's literally what's being decided, and letting judges vote separately on premises lets weird minority coalitions dictate the outcome even though no majority actually endorses that combination of views. I think that's crazy though — premise-based procedures seem way more defensible, since they force the court to actually reason through the doctrine instead of aggregating gut reactions to the ultimate question, and conclusion-based voting can produce a "majority" for liability where the judges don't even agree on why the defendant is liable.

We went back and forth for like an hour and didn't resolve anything, so now I want someone to actually make the case properly. Please argue for the premise-based procedure as the right way to handle multi-issue collective decisions like this one, and deal with the standard objections — like the worry that premise-based procedures force judges to be "outvoted" into affirming a conclusion they personally reject, or that the whole approach just presupposes the majority-endorsed premises are the doctrinally relevant ones to aggregate over in the first place. I want an actual committed argument, not a "well it depends," because my roommate will just throw that right back at me.
