I've been reading about Molinism on and off for a few months now, mostly through YouTube debates and a couple of long forum threads, since I never studied philosophy formally and don't have anyone in my life to really hash this out with. The idea that God has "middle knowledge" of what every possible free creature would do in every possible circumstance seems, on the surface, like an elegant way to let people be genuinely free while still letting God plan out history down to the last detail. But the more I sit with it, the more I keep snagging on the same question: what actually makes a counterfactual like "if Peter were placed in circumstances C, he would freely deny Christ" true, before Peter ever exists to do the denying? If nothing about Peter himself is doing the grounding, it starts to feel like these truths are just floating free of anything real; and if something else is doing the grounding, it's hard to see how Peter's choice is still up to him in any meaningful libertarian sense.

I'd like you to make a sustained argument, not just a survey, for the position that the grounding objection succeeds -- that middle knowledge cannot be reconciled with libertarian free will, because there is no adequate truthmaker available for pre-volitional counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. Please engage seriously with the main Molinist replies (for instance, that counterfactuals of freedom might simply be brute, ungrounded facts, or that possible-worlds semantics doesn't require truthmakers in the first place) rather than waving them away, and explain concretely why you think they ultimately fail to rescue the view.
