So I'm prepping a seminar session on Dutch book arguments and got stuck trying to explain something clearly to my students. Everyone always presents the synchronic Dutch book argument for probabilism (incoherent credences at a time get you pumped) as basically the same kind of move as the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalization (failing to update by conditionalization over time also gets you pumped), like they're two applications of one unified idea. But when I actually dig into the diachronic version -- Lewis's argument, the objections from people like Hacking and van Fraassen needing the "package principle" to make the bets binding across time -- it feels like a much shakier argument than the synchronic one. The agent has to have foreknowledge of the bets and be committed to accepting a whole package in advance, which seems like it's smuggling in extra assumptions that aren't needed for the atemporal case.

Can you explain, somewhat in the weeds, why the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalization is generally considered more contested/philosophically weaker than the synchronic argument for probabilism?
