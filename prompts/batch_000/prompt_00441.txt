I've been turning something over in my head for a few weeks now, ever since a conversation with my brother-in-law, who's into statistics as a hobby. He claims that weather forecasting basically settles the old philosophical debate about what probability actually means. His argument goes like this: when a forecaster says there's a "70% chance of rain tomorrow," you can just wait and collect data — track every day the forecast said 70%, and check how often it actually rained. If it comes out close to 70% over enough days, that proves probability really is just long-run frequency, since you've empirically confirmed the number against reality. He says nobody does anything comparable for a "subjective degree of belief," so the frequentist interpretation wins by default, and the other views (subjectivism, propensity theory) are just philosophers making things needlessly complicated about a question science has already answered through calibration studies like this.

I find this pretty persuasive, but something nags at me: doesn't the forecast for "tomorrow" refer to one single, unrepeatable day, not a long-run sequence? It seems strange to define the meaning of a single day's probability by appealing to a completely different set of days. So my question is: does the fact that forecasters' probabilities check out as well-calibrated over many trials actually vindicate the frequentist interpretation specifically, or am I missing why this kind of evidence doesn't automatically decide the matter in frequentism's favor?
