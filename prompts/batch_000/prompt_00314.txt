I'm teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind this term, and we recently spent two sessions on the Lucas-Penrose argument. Several of my sharper students left convinced that the argument amounts to a genuine mathematical proof that human minds cannot be Turing machines: since Gödel's incompleteness theorem guarantees that any consistent formal system has a true sentence it cannot prove, and since a human mathematician can simply "see" that this Gödel sentence is true, it follows that human reasoning outstrips what any algorithm could do. What puzzles them—and, I confess, puzzles me a little too—is why so much of the logic and computer science community still treats this as a live controversy rather than a settled result. Given how airtight the inference seems on its face, what exactly are Putnam, Benacerraf, and other critics missing when they resist Lucas and Penrose's conclusion? I'd like to be able to lay out their objections accurately and charitably when we reconvene, rather than leaving my students with the impression that mainstream skepticism here is just stubbornness in the face of a clean mathematical demonstration.
