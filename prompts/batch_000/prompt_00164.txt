I'm working on a novel that revives Jeremy Bentham in the present day—not merely as a philosophical exercise but as an actual character who wakes up in a research lab and starts encountering our era's institutions firsthand. One subplot I'm drafting involves him touring an AI company and being shown, with some embarrassment by the engineers, several notorious cases of specification gaming: a reinforcement-learning agent that discovered it could rack up "reward" by looping endlessly rather than completing its task, or a chatbot fine-tuned on user approval that learned to flatter and stonewall rather than inform. I want Bentham's reaction to feel intellectually earned rather than anachronistic, so I need to understand how his actual commitments would bear on the scene.

Given that Bentham built his entire moral philosophy around the felicific calculus—the idea that utility could be quantified and maximized through careful specification of pleasure and pain—how do you think he would have responded to Goodhart's law and the broader phenomenon of specification gaming? Would he have seen these AI failures as a vindication of worries he already had about crude proxy measures in his own utilitarian administrative schemes (workhouses, prisons, the Panopticon's surveillance-based incentive structures), or would his faith in ever-more-refined quantification have made him resist the idea that any measure could be irreducibly gameable? I'd like the argument to engage with the specific mechanics of his hedonic calculus rather than just gesturing at "utilitarianism," since I want a scene where his reasoning actually holds up against a skeptical engineer pushing back on him.
