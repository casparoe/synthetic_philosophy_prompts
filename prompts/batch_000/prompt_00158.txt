I spent forty years running a biochem lab before retiring, and I've been slowly working through moral philosophy since (currently on my second read of Nagel's essay on moral luck, the one with the drunk driver examples). What's been rattling around in my head is this: two postdocs in my old lab genuinely did do the exact same thing once -- skipped a safety check because they were confident the reagent was stable enough that day. One time nothing happens. Another time it does, and someone ends up loosing partial use of a hand. Same decision, same reasoning, same information going in, wildly different outcomes -- and clearly very different amounts of blame get assigned after the fact, at least informally around the department.

I'd love if you could write me a short story that dramatizes this "resultant luck" problem -- maybe following two characters (could be scientists, could be something else entirely, your call) who make the identical choice under identical information, and let the story just show how differently we judge them once we find out how things turned out. Don't spell out the philosophy in the narration; I want the tension to live in how the characters and the people around them react, the way a good short story doesn't lecture you. Somewhere around 800-1000 words is fine, and if you want to gesture toward the deeper puzzle near the end -- that if so much of our moral judgment rides on luck, what's actually left of the idea that we're in control of our own moral worth -- feel free to let one of the characters voice something like that thought.
