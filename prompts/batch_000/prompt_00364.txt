Last weekend a friend and I spent nearly three hours arguing about what to do when you learn that someone you consider your epistemic equal disagrees with you. The dispute started over something mundane -- we'd each independently estimated how long a hike would take and gotten very different numbers -- but it spiraled into a much bigger argument about whether learning of her disagreement should have moved my credence toward hers (as she insisted) or whether I was entitled to stick to my guns given that I still found my own reasoning compelling (as I argued). She kept pushing the point that if I genuinely regarded her as my epistemic peer, refusing to budge was basically incoherent; I kept pushing back that conciliationism seems to collapse into a kind of spinelessness where nobody could ever rationally hold a firm opinion about anything contestable, and that it also seems weirdly self-undermining, since conciliationists disagree with steadfasters who are presumably their epistemic peers too.

Neither of us walked away convinced, and I'd like to see the argument played out by people who actually know the literature. Could you write a dialogue between a committed conciliationist and a committed steadfaster -- both sharp, neither a pushover -- working through this? I'd like them to move past the hiking-estimate case into the harder theoretical terrain: the worry that conciliationism is self-defeating when applied to disagreements about conciliationism itself, the distinction between "independent" and "dependent" evidence for assessing a disputant's competence, and whether there's a principled way to distinguish cases where conciliating is obviously right (arithmetic mistakes) from cases where steadfastness seems more defensible (deeply held philosophical or political convictions). I want each side to actually respond to the other's specific objections rather than just restating their own position.
