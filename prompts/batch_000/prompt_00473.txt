I'm assembling a collection of philosophically-inspired short fiction, and I've been stuck for weeks on a story meant to dramatize higher-order theories of consciousness—the view that a mental state's being conscious isn't just a matter of its own first-order content, but depends on its being the object of some further representation: a higher-order thought about the state (as in Rosenthal's HOT theory), or a suitably structured higher-order perception of it. Over dinner with a few philosopher friends, I got fixated on a scenario where a piece of experimental neurotechnology can selectively disable a person's higher-order monitoring system while leaving all first-order sensory and cognitive processing fully intact. The result would be a person who sees, feels pain, deliberates, and acts on all of it—yet who, on these theories, might not be having any conscious experience at all, since nothing is representing those states as belonging to a subject.

I'd like you to write a short story of roughly 1000-1500 words that takes this premise seriously rather than treating it as a vague "philosophical zombie" trope. The story should follow either the affected person or the people around them (a partner, a researcher, a rival theorist) as they try to work out—experimentally, behaviorally, and from the inside—whether "anyone is home." I want the mechanics of higher-order theories to actually do work in the plot: for instance, the tension between first-order and higher-order representationalism, the question of whether a mere disposition to form a higher-order thought suffices (as some defenders of HOT theory argue) or whether an actual occurrent higher-order state is required, and what would count as evidence either way given that verbal report itself might just be another first-order output. I don't need the story to resolve the philosophical question, but I do want a reader familiar with the literature to feel that the thought experiment was handled with real precision rather than atmosphere alone.
