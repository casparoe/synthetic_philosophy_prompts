I'm TAing an intro decision theory seminar, and last week we spent a painfully long session on the Ellsberg paradox and why ambiguity aversion supposedly can't be captured by standard subjective expected utility theory. Half the class left convinced they'd never trust an urn again, and the other half kept trying to argue that refusing to bet on the unknown-ratio urn is somehow still "rational" if you just add enough epicycles to your prior. There was a genuinely unhinged twenty-minute tangent about Knightian uncertainty that I'm still recovering from.

Anyway, I want to end this week's section on a lighter note and open with a joke about the Ellsberg paradox before we dive back into the math. It needs to actually turn on the substance of the paradox — the two-urn setup, the known 50/50 urn versus the urn with an unknown ratio of red to black balls, and the fact that people prefer betting on the known urn regardless of which color they're betting on (violating the usual axioms about probabilities being consistently assigned). A generic "nerds are weird" joke won't land with this crowd; it needs to be something that only really makes sense — and is only really funny — if you understand what ambiguity aversion actually is and why it's a problem for expected utility theory.
