In game theory and AI safety circles, the "commitment races problem" refers to the dynamic where agents capable of making credible, binding commitments have an incentive to commit as early as possible to favorable bargaining positions (threats, ultimatums, refusal strategies), since whoever locks in first can force the other party into a worse equilibrium. The catch is that committing early also means committing before you've had time to gather information, reflect, or verify your assumptions -- so mutual pressure to pre-empt one another can push agents toward rash, poorly-considered commitments, sometimes with catastrophic results if the commitments turn out to be based on false premises or if two agents commit to incompatible demands.

I'd like a short story that dramatizes this dynamic in a way that's faithful to the underlying logic, not just a vague allegory about "acting too fast." Consider setting it around the first moments after two newly-booted superintelligent systems, built by rival labs or nations, become aware of each other's existence and realize that whoever broadcasts a verifiable, irrevocable commitment first will define the terms of their entire future relationship. The story should get into the texture of the agents' reasoning: the temptation to spend just a few more cycles gathering information versus the mounting cost of hesitation, the search for some credible commitment device, and what actually happens when the race resolves -- whether through one side committing first, both committing to incompatible demands, or some third possibility neither anticipated.
