I was reading through the Lucas-Penrose argument again and got stuck on something that I don't think the popular treatments handle carefully. The whole argument rests on Gödel's first incompleteness theorem giving us a sentence G that's true but unprovable in a given formal system F. Lucas and Penrose want to say: "we can see that G is true, but F can't prove G, therefore the mind isn't equivalent to F." But this seems to assume we know F is consistent (since if F is inconsistent, G isn't reliably true, and F could even "prove" G anyway in a degenerate sense). And Gödel's second incompleteness theorem tells us F can't prove its own consistency. So it looks like the anti-mechanist needs to independently verify the consistency of whatever formal system supposedly captures their reasoning -- but how would they do that in a way that isn't either question-begging (assuming their own reasoning is a sound oracle for consistency) or just as formally limited as F itself? I've seen this raised as the "consistency loophole" but I want a clearer walkthrough of exactly where the move from "I can see G is true" to "therefore I'm not F" smuggles in an unjustified consistency assumption, and whether there's any way for the Lucas-Penrose camp to actually discharge that assumption without circularity. Can you walk through this carefully, maybe with a toy example of F, and also flag where Penrose's own response to this objection (in Shadows of the Mind) tries to patch the gap?
