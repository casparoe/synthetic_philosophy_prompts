I'm working through the literature on machine consciousness and computability for a seminar paper, and I keep getting tangled on something the Lucas–Penrose argument seems to rely on. The core move is to use Gödel's incompleteness theorems to argue that human minds can't be equivalent to formal systems, since a mind can apparently "see" the truth of a Gödel sentence G(F) for any consistent formal system F, while F itself cannot prove G(F) from within. But there's a related but distinct point in the background here about the difference between a system being unable to prove its own consistency (per the second incompleteness theorem) and a system being unable to prove a specific arithmetical sentence constructed via diagonalization (the first incompleteness theorem). Lucas and Penrose sometimes seem to slide between "the mind can prove its own consistency" and "the mind can always generate the undecidable Gödel sentence for any formalization of itself," as though these were the same insight applied to minds.

Could you compare these two ideas carefully: (1) the general phenomenon of Gödelian incompleteness/unprovability-of-consistency for formal systems, and (2) the specific anti-mechanist argument that human mathematical insight outstrips any single formal system because we can always "step outside" and see the truth of its Gödel sentence? I'm interested in what exactly the anti-mechanist argument adds to the bare incompleteness results — is it just an application of them, or does it require substantive further assumptions (e.g., about the mind being a "sound" or fixed formal system, or about what it means for a person to "know" or "see" the truth of a sentence)? And where do critics like Putnam or Feferman locate the weak point in this extra step?
