I've been working through Clark Glymour's original statement of the problem of old evidence, and I keep getting stuck on how much damage it actually does to Bayesian confirmation theory. The textbook case is Einstein and the anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury: the data was already known and assigned probability 1 (or something very close to it) before general relativity was even formulated, and yet everyone wants to say that this old evidence strongly confirmed GR once the theory was proposed. On strict Bayesian conditionalization, though, confirmation is measured by how much a piece of evidence raises the posterior probability of a hypothesis, and if P(E) = 1 already, then P(H|E) = P(H), so no confirmation can occur at all. This seems like a serious problem, since it suggests that Bayesian confirmation theory can't make sense of one of the most paradigmatic cases of theory confirmation in the history of science.

I've seen a few proposed fixes and I'm not sure which one actually works, or whether any of them do. There's the Garber/Jeffrey/Niiniluoto move of relativizing confirmation to logical/computational states rather than full probabilistic omniscience, so that E confirms H by confirming the previously-unknown entailment from H (plus background) to E. There's also the counterfactual strategy, where confirmation is cashed out in terms of what the agent's credence in E would have been had she not already known it. And some people bite the bullet and say the problem shows that classical Bayesian conditionalization just isn't the right model of scientific confirmation, motivating something like Bayesian networks, or a shift to explanationist or likelihoodist accounts instead.

For my essay I want you to actually take a stand rather than just surveying the options. Explain the problem of old evidence with enough precision that someone who's only vaguely familiar with Bayesian confirmation theory could follow why P(E)=1 is so damaging, then evaluate the main proposed solutions (at least the logical-omniscience-relaxation approach and the counterfactual approach) on their own terms, including any standard objections to them (e.g., worries about which counterfactual credence function is the "right" one, or whether relativizing to logical states smuggles in illegitimate resources). End with a definite verdict: does some version of Bayesianism successfully dissolve the problem of old evidence, or does the problem reveal a genuine, uneliminable flaw in Bayesian confirmation theory as a model of actual scientific reasoning? Defend your answer rather than leaving it open.
