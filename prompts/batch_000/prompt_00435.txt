A university research team erected a telescoping wind-monitoring pole, fourteen feet at full extension, for a three-week survey near a protected marsh, and a local conservation group sued to have it removed. The trial record shows that committee reports accompanying the Act focus almost entirely on permanent buildings and towers that block nesting corridors, with no mention of temporary scientific equipment, but the statutory text itself makes no distinction between temporary and permanent structures, nor does it define "structure" at all.

For the chapter, I need a dialogue between two of my judges — Judge Alsop, a committed textualist, and Judge Reyes, a committed purposivist — as they hash out their disagreement during a private conference before circulating draft opinions. I want them to be evenly matched: Alsop should press hard on the plain meaning of "structure," the risks of judges importing unenacted intentions, and the absurdity doctrine's narrow scope, while Reyes should push back with the mischief rule, the specific legislative history, and the argument that rigid literalism here would produce a result no legislator plausibly intended. Rather than talking past each other, I'd like them to actually engage with each other's strongest points — conceding minor ground where warranted, sharpening their positions in response to objections, and perhaps arriving at some unexpected common ground or an irreducible disagreement about the judicial role itself. Could you write this scene as a self-contained dialogue, roughly the length of a short chapter, with enough substantive legal reasoning that a reader familiar with statutory interpretation would find it genuinely illuminating rather than a caricature of either side?
