I'm currently taking a seminar on collective decision-making, and something happened this term in a committee I sit on that I can't fully make sense of using what we've covered so far (mostly Arrow's theorem and Condorcet's paradox). I'm one of five members of my university's competitive research fellowship panel. Our bylaws state that a candidate should be admitted only if three conditions are all satisfied: (1) their academic record is strong enough, (2) their research proposal is feasible within the funding period, and (3) their letters of recommendation are sufficiently strong. Each of us evaluates all three conditions individually and then casts an overall yes/no vote on admission.

For one applicant this cycle, when we tallied the votes on each condition separately, all three passed by majority (3–2, 4–1, and 3–2 respectively). Yet when we tallied our five final up-or-down verdicts directly, the applicant was rejected 3–2, since the members who thought each particular condition failed were never the same three people. Now the committee is split on how to proceed going forward: some members want to adopt a permanent rule that we aggregate by the individual conditions first and let the verdict follow automatically, since that seems to respect "the reasons" behind each vote; others insist we should keep aggregating final verdicts directly, since that's what the bylaws literally ask for. I can't tell which side has the better argument, or whether this is even the kind of problem that can be fixed by just picking one procedure and committing to it permanently. Could you help me understand what's actually going on here, and whether there's a principled basis for deciding how a committee like ours should aggregate its members' judgments?
