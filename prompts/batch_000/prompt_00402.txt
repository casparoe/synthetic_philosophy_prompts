So I went down kind of a rabbit hole a few weeks back thinking about where stuff like traffic rules or the meanings of words actually come from - like nobody sat down and voted that we'd drive on the right side of the road, it just kind of... happened and now everyone follows it. I mentioned this to a friend and he pointed me to this philosopher David Lewis who apparently wrote a whole book on "convention" back in the 60s. The part that's tripping me up is his idea that for something to really count as a convention, it's not enough that everyone just happens to do the same thing - he says you need "common knowledge," which from what I can tell means I know you'll stop at red lights, and you know that I know it, and I know that you know that I know it, and so on forever. That seems like a really weird and almost impossible requirement for something that seems to emerge pretty naturally without anyone thinking about it that hard.

Can someone explain to me why Lewis thought you needed this whole infinite tower of mutual knowledge, instead of just people happening to converge on the same behavior over and over? Like concretely, what would actually break or go wrong - what kind of coordination failure would happen - if that common knowledge condition wasn't satisfied? I get the general vibe of what he's going for but the "why does it specifically need to be infinite iterations" part still feels like a black box to me.
