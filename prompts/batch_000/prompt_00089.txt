I've been reading about Humeanism vs. anti-Humeanism about laws of nature, and there's a particular move in the dialectic that I can't quite get straight in my head. The Humean (Lewis's best-system analysis, roughly) says laws are just the axioms/theorems of the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength in summarizing the total Humean mosaic of particular facts. Anti-Humeans object that this makes laws "supervene" on the mosaic in a way that gets the explanatory direction backwards -- the laws are supposed to explain the pattern of particular facts, not be summaries that float parasitically on top of them. But then I read Humean responses that say this objection just begs the question against Humeanism by presupposing a robust, non-Humean notion of explanation as involving some kind of governing or production relation.

What I'm stuck on is whether there's a way to adjudicate this beyond each side just asserting their preferred notion of explanation. Is the disagreement ultimately about the nature of laws themselves, or is it actually a disagreement about what explanation requires (e.g., whether unification-style accounts of explanation are adequate, or whether explanation demands something more like difference-making or production)? Relatedly, I'm curious whether the "explanatory circularity" charge against Humeanism (i.e., that particular facts can't explain the very regularities that supposedly explain those particular facts) is a decisive knockdown or whether Humeans have a good non-question-begging reply to it. Can you help me get a clearer sense of the actual structure of this disagreement, ideally with a sense of which considerations are supposed to do independent argumentative work rather than just re-asserting the two sides' starting intuitions?
