I've been reading about updateless decision theory and keep running into this thorny issue around "commitment races" and the role of logical time versus causal time. From what I gather, UDT agents are supposed to act as if committing in advance to the policy that's best from the perspective of their prior, before any updating on observations happens. But I'm confused about what grounds the claim that there's a well-defined "prior" moment for a bounded agent embedded in the world it's reasoning about — especially once you start considering that the agent's own reasoning process unfolds over time and other agents (or copies of itself) might be reasoning "earlier" or "later" in some logical sense rather than a physical one. My study group got stuck arguing about whether this makes UDT's recommendations dependent on an arbitrary choice of logical vantage point, which seems like it would undermine the whole point of avoiding dynamic inconsistency. Can you unpack what's actually going on here — why logical time matters for UDT, how it relates to the idea of "updatelessness" being relative to a fixed prior, and whether there's a principled way to pin down that vantage point rather than it being stipulated by the person setting up the decision problem?
