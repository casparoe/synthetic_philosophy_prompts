Leibniz argued that God, in creating the world, selected the best among infinitely many possible worlds, and he was one of the few thinkers of his era to reason rigorously about infinite series and quantities. Contemporary "infinite ethics" has shown that once you allow infinite populations, infinite time, or infinite value into moral calculations, standard utilitarian aggregation breaks down: totals can diverge, worlds become incomparable, and finite improvements can fail to make an infinite whole any better. For a piece I'm writing on this literature, I'm curious how you think Leibniz would have responded to these paradoxes, given his theodicy and his own mathematical intuitions about the infinite. Would his framework have given him real resources to resolve or dissolve the problem, or would it unravel in the same ways modern axiology does?
