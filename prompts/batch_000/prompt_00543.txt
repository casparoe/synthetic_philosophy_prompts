I got into a fairly heated argument last night with a friend who works in decision theory, and I want to get a clearer, more rigorous handle on the issue that split us. She was defending the claim (which I gather is fairly standard in some game theory circles) that common knowledge of rationality among the players in a game is basically equivalent to, or at least reliably yields, play of a Nash equilibrium. My objection was that this can't be quite right — my recollection from an epistemic game theory course I took a while back is that common knowledge of rationality alone only gets you to the set of rationalizable strategies, and that Nash equilibrium requires substantially more, like correct (or at least mutually consistent) conjectures about what the other players will do, possibly bundled with common priors.

I'd like a precise explanation of where exactly this gap lies. In particular: why doesn't common knowledge of rationality (in the Aumann/Brandenburger sense of iterated "knows that the others know that...") pin down a Nash equilibrium in games with more than two players or without special structure? What's actually doing the work in the classical justifications of Nash equilibrium that goes beyond rationality being common knowledge — is it an assumption about correct beliefs, a common prior, or something else entirely? And is there a clean way to see why two-player, zero-sum games (or other special cases) might behave better here than the general case? I'd appreciate an explanation that's precise about the distinction between common knowledge and common belief/certainty as well, since I have a feeling that distinction is quietly load-bearing in this whole debate.
