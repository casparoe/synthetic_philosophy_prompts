I'm a tech lead at a mid-sized software company, and I've run into a management puzzle I can't quite articulate to my own manager, let alone resolve. Over the summer we ran a structured internship program: interns worked through a fixed backlog of well-specified tickets, and we graded them almost entirely on ticket throughput plus a code-review checklist (test coverage percentage, PR description completeness, linting, that kind of thing). One intern, I'll call him Dan, was by far our top performer under that system, closing more tickets than anyone else while hitting every item on the checklist nearly every time.

We converted Dan to full-time and moved him onto our main team, where the work is far more open-ended: he sets his own priorities, tickets are vague, and there's no rigid checklist to satisfy. Since the transition, his output looks fine on paper, high ticket counts, green checkmarks everywhere, but the actual product keeps breaking in ways the old rubric never would have caught. He writes tests that technically pass but don't meaningfully cover edge cases, and he'll decompose a genuinely hard problem into a pile of narrowly "closed" tickets that don't add up to a working feature. When I've raised this with him, he isn't being cynical or lazy about it; he seems honestly convinced he's doing exactly what good engineering looks like, because that's precisely what got him praised and promoted a few months ago.

I'm trying to figure out how to think about this rather than just chalk it up to "he's not that good, actually." Was this a bad hire, a badly designed internship rubric, or something more structural, something about how the specific metrics we trained and evaluated him against shaped what he actually internalized as "doing a good job," in a way that only became visible once he was moved into an environment different from the one he was evaluated in? I'd like help understanding what's really going on here conceptually, and what that understanding implies both for how I coach Dan going forward and for how we design evaluation criteria in future internship programs, so we're not inadvertently producing more engineers like him.
