So I spent my career as a field biologist studying frog sensory systems, and now that I'm retired I've been filling my afternoons reading philosophy of mind, specifically this teleosemantics stuff (Millikan, Papineau, Neander etc). Naturally the frog/fly example got my attention right away -- the idea that a frog's snapping "means" fly, or maybe it means small-dark-moving-thing, and evolution by natural selection can't seem to tell us which, since both extensions were equally correlated with reproductive succes in the frog's evolutionary history. This is apparently called the disjunction problem, or sometimes the distality problem when its framed in terms of proximal vs distal causes.

I've seen some philosophers treat this as a solvable engineering wrinkle -- just add more detail about the selective history, or appeal to normal conditions for the mechanism, and the content will sharpen up. But the more I think about it, the more it seems like the indeterminacy is baked into the whole etiological framework itself, not just a gap in our current knowledge of frog evolution. Can you make the strongest case you can for the position that this disjunction/distality problem is actually fatal to the teleosemantic project -- that no amount of adding further biological/historical detail (proper function, normal conditions, Millikan's "Normal explanations," etc.) can in principle fix determinate representational content, so teleosemantics fails as a naturalization of intentionality? I'd like the argument to engage seriously with at least one of the standard teleosemantic replies to the disjunction problem and explain why it doesn't actually work, rather than just asserting the problem is intractable.
