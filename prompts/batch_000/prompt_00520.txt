I'm a PhD student working on formal decision theory, and I've spent the past few weeks working through Yudkowsky's and Oesterheld's writing on acausal trade between causally disconnected agents -- for instance, civilizations in different Everett branches, or in regions of an inflating universe that will never share a light cone. As I understand the proposal, each party models the other's likely values and decision procedure, concludes that the other will cooperate if it cooperates, and both act accordingly, all without any causal contact whatsoever. What I can't get past is this: if there is genuinely zero causal channel between the two parties, doesn't "trade" become something of a misnomer? An ordinary trade involves goods or actions actually being transferred as a consequence of an agreement, which seems to require at least some faint causal thread linking the two decisions -- some kind of backward-in-time influence or extremely subtle signal, even if we can't yet specify the physics. Otherwise, aren't we just describing two agents independently doing what they would have done anyway, and retroactively dressing up the coincidence with trade-talk?

I raised this with my advisor, who waved it off as a confusion but didn't have time to spell out why, so I'd like a more careful answer. Do proponents of acausal trade think there is some hidden causal mechanism at work after all (exotic physics, retrocausality, etc.), or do they instead bite the bullet and insist that "trade" here is a technical term entirely divorced from causal exchange -- and if so, what exactly is doing the work of making it a *trade* rather than mere correlated behavior?
