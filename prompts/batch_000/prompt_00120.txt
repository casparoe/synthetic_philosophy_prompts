After forty years in forest ecology, I retired three years ago and have since taken up auditing philosophy courses at the local university, largely for the pleasure of it but partly to keep my mind limber. I now find myself on the advisory board of a small regional land trust that grants conservation easements—along with associated tax benefits and legal protections—to parcels of forest that qualify as "old-growth." Naturally, the board has tried to operationalize this with quantitative criteria: mean canopy tree age above roughly 150 years (estimated via increment cores), canopy gap frequency below a certain threshold, and coarse woody debris volume above another. The trouble is that we are now deadlocked over a fourteen-acre parcel whose cored trees average 148.7 years, well within the margin of error of the coring method itself, and one faction wants to grant the designation on the grounds that this is "essentially" old-growth, while another insists that if we bend the rule here we have no rule at all, and the whole designation becomes a matter of sentiment rather than science.

What strikes me, turning this over during my morning walks, is that I could describe a hypothetical sequence of forests, each just one year older than the last, running from a twenty-year-old clear-cut regrowth stand to an unambiguous 300-year-old cathedral forest. At no single step in that sequence does it seem right to say the forest crossed from "not old-growth" into "old-growth"—one year of tree age never seems decisive—and yet somewhere between the two ends, it obviously did. Our numerical cutoffs feel less like a discovery about nature and more like a stipulation we impose to make the paperwork function, and I worry this makes the whole category arbitrary in a way that undermines its supposed ecological meaning, even though the underlying phenomenon (structural complexity, biodiversity value, carbon storage) is real and comes in degrees rather than sharp jumps.

I'd like your advice, both for the upcoming board meeting and for my own peace of mind: is there a principled way to defend a hard cutoff for a concept like "old-growth" once one admits that the property it tracks is genuinely continuous, or is any such line inherently indefensible in the way my philosophy professor might call "arbitrary"? And if a line is unavoidable for practical/legal purposes, is there a better way to think about and justify it than simply picking a number and defending it as if it carved nature at a joint?
