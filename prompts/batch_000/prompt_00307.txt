I spent my career doing spectroscopy, so I'm used to a world where an experiment either confirms a hypothesis or it doesn't, with error bars you can actually compute. Retirement has left me time to read philosophy of mathematics, and Gödel's incompleteness theorems have rather unsettled that comfortable picture. I understand the first theorem well enough — any sufficiently strong consistent system has true-but-unprovable statements — but it's the second theorem that keeps me up at night: the fact that a system like Peano Arithmetic or ZFC cannot prove its own consistency, meaning Con(ZFC) is itself just another undecided proposition floating around out there, and any proof of consistency has to be mounted from a strictly stronger theory (Gentzen's use of transfinite induction up to ε₀ being the classic case), which just relocates the problem rather than solving it.

What I'm struggling to get an intuitive grip on is this: in classical logic, the principle of explosion (ex falso quodlibet) means that a single genuine contradiction buried somewhere in ZFC's axioms would, in principle, let you derive absolutely anything — every theorem and its negation. That seems like it should be an existential threat to the whole edifice of mathematics built on those axioms. And yet working mathematicians seem largely untroubled by this, continuing to build on ZFC as bedrock. Could you help me understand, at a gut level rather than through the full technical machinery, why this isn't more alarming? I'm particularly interested in how ideas like relative consistency proofs, the practice of "quarantining" suspect axioms, and paraconsistent logics that reject explosion altogether are supposed to soften what looks, from the outside, like a fairly dire logical situation.
