I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics currently working through Brouwer's and Heyting's foundational writings alongside a proof theory seminar, and I've run into something I can't quite get an intuitive handle on. In intuitionistic logic, the law of excluded middle (P ∨ ¬P) is famously not a theorem, since asserting it constructively would require a general method for either proving or refuting any proposition whatsoever. And yet the double negation of excluded middle, ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P), *is* provable intuitionistically for essentially any P. My confusion is about what this actually tells us. If ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P) is a theorem schema, in what sense have we not smuggled classical logic back in, at least at the level of consistency with it? I can follow the syntactic derivation well enough in natural deduction, but I'm after a more conceptual or semantic explanation, ideally in terms of the BHK interpretation or Kripke semantics, of why the provability of the double-negated instance doesn't commit the intuitionist to anything like accepting excluded middle itself. Could you unpack why this doesn't feel like a cheap syntactic trick of the negation operator, but rather reflects something philosophically substantive about what a "construction" or "proof" of a disjunction versus a negation of a negation actually demands? A contrast between what ¬¬ does proof-theoretically and what it would mean for a constructivist to actually assert LEM outright would be especially helpful.
