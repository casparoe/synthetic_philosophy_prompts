I've spent the last few weeks interviewing decision theorists and AI safety researchers for a feature on the game-theoretic anxieties simmering in that world, and one idea keeps resurfacing under the label "commitment races" -- the notion, developed in MIRI-adjacent writing (Andrew Critch's work comes up often), that sufficiently advanced agents might feel pressure to lock in strategic commitments -- threats, promises, or delegating themselves to rigid proxies -- as early as possible, purely because whoever commits first constrains the eventual bargaining outcome. On its face this sounds like a restatement of Thomas Schelling's classic insight from The Strategy of Conflict: an agent that visibly ties its own hands gains leverage precisely because the commitment is credible and irreversible. But several of my sources insist the "commitment race" framing is pointing at something genuinely new and more dangerous, tied up with logical or updateless decision theories and with the risks of racing to commit under severe uncertainty, before agents have had time to actually understand each other or the stakes involved.

I'd like your help sorting out this comparison for the piece. Could you walk through where the commitment-race concern overlaps with ordinary Schelling-style commitment strategy, and where it genuinely departs from it -- is the novelty mainly about how logical/updateless decision theories change what counts as a credible commitment (e.g., commitments that bind an agent even against agents it hasn't yet encountered), or is it more about the race dynamic itself, where the incentive to commit early and irreversibly outpaces agents' ability to reason carefully about what they're committing to? I want to be precise about what's old wine in new bottles here versus what actually constitutes a distinct and worrying phenomenon.
