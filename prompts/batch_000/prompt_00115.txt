I recently audited a graduate seminar in philosophy of science where the discussion turned to the classic contrast between "all gold spheres are less than one mile in diameter" and "all spheres of enriched uranium are less than one mile in diameter." The uranium generalization holds because of critical mass and would presumably hold in any world with the same physics, while the gold one is true only by cosmic accident. The professor used this pair to press on a deeper question: are laws of nature nothing more than the best systematic summary of the total pattern of events in the universe (the Humean view), or is there some further ingredient -- necessitation relations among universals, essential dispositions in the properties themselves, or something else -- that explains why the uranium generalization, but not the gold one, supports counterfactuals and genuinely explains its instances rather than merely reporting them (the anti-Humean view)?

Two students, one a committed Lewisian who kept insisting that "best system" membership is all the necessity we need or should want, and the other a dispositional essentialist who thought this left the asymmetry between the two generalizations completely mysterious, ended up arguing well past the official end of the seminar. I've been trying to reconstruct their exchange ever since. Could you write out a dialogue between them that sticks closely to this specific example -- gold spheres versus uranium spheres -- as they work through whether the Humean can actually earn the right to treat the uranium case as law-governed and the gold case as accidental, given that both are, on the Humean's own terms, just patterns in the same mosaic of particular facts, and whether the anti-Humean's extra metaphysical posits actually buy any explanatory power or just relabel the problem. Neither side should get away with simply restating their position; each should have to directly answer the other's sharpest objection, and the dialogue should end with a clearly isolated point of genuine disagreement rather than a vague gesture at both sides having a point.
