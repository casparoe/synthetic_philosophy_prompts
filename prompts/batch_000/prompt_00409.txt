Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I've been working through the AI safety literature on corrigibility, and I keep getting stuck on one particular fault line. On one side there's the older "utility indifference" tradition (Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, Armstrong) that tries to patch corrigibility onto an otherwise standard expected-utility maximizer by adding a correction term so the agent is indifferent between the world where it gets shut down and the world where it doesn't. On the other side, I've been reading Elliott Thornley's more recent work arguing that this whole approach is doomed because any agent with complete, transitive preferences over outcomes will, in some circumstance, find it instrumentally useful to resist shutdown or manipulate the shutdown button — and that real corrigibility requires giving up the completeness axiom itself in favor of genuinely incomplete preferences.

I find the disagreement fascinating but I can't tell whether it's a substantive technical disagreement about what indifference terms can and can't achieve, or a more basic disagreement about what corrigibility should even mean (a behavioral constraint vs. a structural feature of the agent's preference ordering). Could you write a dialogue between two decision theorists — one a defender of the utility-indifference patch and one a defender of the incomplete-preferences approach — arguing this out concretely? I'd like them to get into the weeds: have the indifference-defender propose a specific fix to a standard objection (e.g., the sub-agent-creation problem, where the main agent spins off successors that don't share its indifference), and have the incomplete-preferences advocate respond with why that fix reintroduces the very incentive to manipulate the shutdown mechanism it was meant to remove. Neither should be a pushover; I want to see them actually landing blows on each other's positions rather than just restating their own view.
