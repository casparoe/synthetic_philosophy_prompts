I'm a cognitive science student currently taking a philosophy of mind seminar, and something from my part-time research assistant job has been bothering me in a way that seems related to what we've been discussing in class. I work in a lab that's fine-tuning a language model to give users recommendations, and last week my supervisor and I got into a real disagreement about how to describe the system's behavior in our internal reports. After the model gave a user some bad advice that seemed to stem from outdated information about a policy, I wrote in my notes that "the model believed X was still true," but my supervisor crossed it out and told me to write only that "the model produced output consistent with X," insisting that talking about what the system believes or wants is unscientific and misleading, since underneath it's just predicting the next token.

I pushed back because, honestly, I find it much easier to anticipate the model's mistakes when I think in terms of what it "believes" or is "trying" to do - that framework has actually helped me flag problems before they show up in testing, even though I don't think there's anything it's like to be the model, and I'm not claiming it has real mental states. My supervisor thinks this is just convenient anthropomorphizing that will cause real problems down the line, especially if we ever need to assign responsibility for a harmful recommendation, or explain to a client why the system failed. Who has the better approach here, and more generally, how should I decide when it's useful or even legitimate to describe an AI system using mentalistic language like "belief" or "goal," versus when doing so just obscures what's actually going on inside it?
