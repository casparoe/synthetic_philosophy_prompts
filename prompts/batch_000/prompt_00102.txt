I'm auditing a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this semester, and last week's session on Bayesian confirmation theory left me stuck on the "problem of old evidence" that Glymour raised against strict Bayesian conditionalization. The textbook example that keeps nagging me is Einstein and the anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury: that data had been sitting around, fully known, for decades before General Relativity was formulated, yet everyone agrees it counted as powerful confirmation of GR once Einstein showed the theory entailed it. The trouble is that on the standard Bayesian picture, if E is already known with probability 1 before H is proposed, then P(E|H) and P(E) are both just 1, so the likelihood ratio is 1 and E can't raise the posterior of H at all — which seems to get the actual scientific episode completely backwards.

A few of us went out for drinks afterward and ended up arguing about whether this is a fatal problem for Bayesianism as a theory of confirmation, or just a wrinkle that gets patched by moving to something like counterfactual probabilities, algorithmic/logical omniscience fixes, or a shift toward a more diachronic/explanationist story of what "confirmation" even means. I'd like a clearer sense of what's actually at stake here beyond the Mercury anecdote itself. Could you lay out the most important implications of the old evidence problem — both for how we should understand scientific reasoning historically (i.e., cases where a theory is partly justified by data it wasn't built to explain) and for the broader philosophical project of using Bayesian conditionalization as a normative model of rational belief update? I'm especially interested in whether the problem reveals something deep about the inadequacy of modeling scientists (or agents generally) as ideally logically omniscient reasoners, and what the leading proposed solutions concede or preserve about Bayesianism as a result.
