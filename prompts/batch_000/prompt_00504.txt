Compare the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution with the Nash bargaining solution as answers to the two-person cooperative bargaining problem. I've been working through the axiomatic foundations of both solutions in a game theory seminar, and I understand mechanically how each is computed—Nash maximizing the product of utility gains over the disagreement point, Kalai–Smorodinsky selecting the point on the Pareto frontier that lies on the segment connecting the disagreement point to the "ideal point" of maximal feasible utilities for each player. What I'm less clear on is how to think about the deeper normative disagreement they encode, especially since Nash satisfies Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives while Kalai–Smorodinsky sacrifices this in favor of Individual Monotonicity, and the two axioms are not simultaneously satisfiable given the other shared axioms (symmetry, Pareto optimality, invariance to affine transformations).

I'd like the comparison to address a few things: first, what each axiom set says about fairness and how a bargainer's rational entitlements should track changes in the feasible set; second, whether the classic proof that no solution can satisfy both IIA and monotonicity should be read as a genuine impossibility result with philosophical bite, or merely as an artifact of how the axioms are formalized; and third, whether one solution seems to better track pretheoretical intuitions about bargaining power and fair division in specific cases (e.g., asymmetric feasible sets where one party's gains from cooperation are far more sensitive to the shape of the frontier than the other's). A worked example or two illustrating where the solutions diverge would help ground the discussion.
