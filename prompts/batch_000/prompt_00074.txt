I've been working through a formal epistemology seminar on imprecise probabilities, and something in last week's discussion is still bugging me. We covered the standard motivation for representing credences as sets of probability functions (rather than sharp point values) when evidence is sparse or conflicting, and the Ellsberg-style intuitions that supposedly justify this. But then the professor mentioned, almost in passing, that imprecise credences generate a problem for decision-making that she called "dilation" or "belief inertia," and moved on before anyone could ask what she meant.

Can you construct a detailed example — involving a specific set of probability intervals and some sequence of evidence or updating — where an agent with imprecise credences either (a) exhibits dilation, meaning their imprecision about some proposition actually *increases* after conditionalizing on new information that seems intuitively relevant and informative, or (b) ends up unable to distinguish, on the basis of expected value alone, between clearly taking an action and clearly refusing to take it, even though a sharp-credence Bayesian in the same evidential situation would have no such trouble. I'd want the example to show precisely why the imprecise framework produces this counterintuitive verdict, not just assert that it does, and ideally to say something about whether this counts as a genuine cost of the imprecise-credence approach or is instead a feature that correctly reflects the agent's epistemic state.
