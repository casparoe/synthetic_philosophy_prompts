I spent my career as an experimental physicist working mostly on precision tests of special relativity, and only in retirement have I started reading seriously in philosophy of time. I've got the basic distinction between presentism (only the present moment is real) and eternalism (past, present, and future all exist equally, as in the "block universe" picture) reasonably well in hand. What I keep getting stuck on, though, is the more technical objection to presentism that comes out of relativity of simultaneity -- often called the Rietdijk-Putnam-Maxwell argument. As I understand it, the argument tries to show that presentism is straightforwardly incompatible with special relativity, since there is no frame-independent fact about which distant events count as "present" for a given observer, given that simultaneity itself is relative to inertial frame.

I've come across various presentist responses to this -- for instance, neo-Lorentzian moves that posit a privileged reference frame despite its being undetectable in principle, or arguments that the metaphysical "now" of the A-theorist need not correspond to any physically defined simultaneity relation at all. But I don't yet have a good gut-level grasp of why the original objection is supposed to be so damaging, nor of how strong these presentist rejoinders really are once you push on them. Could you walk me through the structure of the Rietdijk-Putnam-Maxwell argument in some detail -- ideally with the actual logical steps spelled out, not just the slogan version -- and then lay out the main lines of response available to the presentist, along with an honest assessment of how persuasive each one is considered to be among people who work on this professionally?
