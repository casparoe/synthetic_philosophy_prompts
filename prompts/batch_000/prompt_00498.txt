So I've been going down this rabbit hole lately, started with reading about Molinism (the whole "middle knowledge" thing, God knowing what any free creature would do in any possible circumstance, even ones that never happen) and then somehow ended up reading about Bell's theorem and this concept called "counterfactual definiteness" that physicists argue about when they talk about hidden variables and quantum mechanics. And I swear these two ideas feel like they're circling the same weird philosophical territory - both are basically claiming there's a fact of the matter about what would happen in situations that never actually occur, right? Like Molina says God knows the truth value of "if Peter were placed in circumstances C, he would freely deny Christ" even though Peter is never actually placed in circumstances C. And counterfactual definiteness says there's a definite value for a particle's spin on an axis you didn't measure, even though you only ever measure one axis.

I brought this up with a couple friends who are more into physics than philosophy and they kind of shrugged, but it's been bugging me for weeks now. Can you actually compare these two concepts properly? Like are they doing the same conceptual work (positing determinate counterfactual truths to preserve some other thing we care about - libertarian free will in one case, realism in the other), or am I just pattern-matching on a surface-level similarity that falls apart once you dig in? I guess I'm especially curious whether the failure of counterfactual definiteness in actual quantum experiments (via Bell inequality violations) should make us more suspicious of whether "counterfactuals of creaturely freedom" are even coherent things to posit in the first place.
