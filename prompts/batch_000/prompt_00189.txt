I'm putting together a graduate seminar on infinite ethics, and this week's session centers on a technical crux that keeps derailing otherwise productive discussion: the tension between completeness and constructivity in social welfare orderings over infinite utility streams. Students are working through Diamond's (1965) impossibility result, Svensson's (1980) proof that a complete ordering satisfying Strong Pareto and Finite Anonymity requires something like a free ultrafilter, and Lauwers and Vallentyne's subsequent worries about what this non-constructivity actually costs us normatively. One camp in the room wants a fully complete ranking of infinite utility streams, even if that means invoking a nonprincipal ultrafilter (or some other choice-dependent, non-explicit device) to break ties that no algorithm could ever resolve; the other camp would rather live with a merely partial ordering — something like the overtaking criterion or the catching-up criterion — that leaves many pairs of streams incomparable, but every verdict it does render is explicitly computable and non-arbitrary.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers holding these opposing positions — call them Cassius, who defends the ultrafilter-based completion, and Nadia, who defends the constructivist, deliberately incomplete ordering — as they argue over coffee after the seminar. Have them engage each other's actual technical commitments: Cassius should have to answer for the fact that his ordering can rank two streams without there being any way, even in principle, to specify which coordinates are doing the work, while Nadia should have to answer for whether refusing to compare cases like a stream that's better "almost everywhere" against one that's better only on a sparse but infinite set is a real cost to her view rather than a virtue. Neither should be allowed to simply declare victory by asserting that completeness (or constructivity) is self-evidently a requirement on any adequate axiology; each concession and rejoinder should track the actual mathematics at stake.
