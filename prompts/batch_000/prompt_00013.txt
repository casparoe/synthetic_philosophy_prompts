I'm working through the Lucas-Penrose argument in a philosophy of mind seminar, and I keep getting stuck on the step where Gödel's incompleteness results get translated into claims about human versus mechanical reasoning. The basic move is familiar: for any consistent formal system F powerful enough to encode arithmetic, there's a Gödel sentence G(F) that's true but unprovable within F, and yet a human reasoner "from outside" can supposedly see that G(F) is true. Lucas and Penrose want to leverage this into the conclusion that no formal system (and hence no mechanism, if mechanisms just are formal systems) can capture human mathematical intuition, since we can always step outside and see what the system can't prove.

What I'm finding hard to pin down is a concrete case that isolates the conceptual issue at the heart of the standard objections to this argument—namely the question of whether our ability to "see" that G(F) is true actually presupposes that we already know F is consistent, and whether that knowledge is something we could ever cash out mechanically or verify with the same certainty the argument wants to attribute to us. Could you construct a detailed example—involving a specific (perhaps simplified or toy) formal system F, its Gödel sentence, and a step-by-step account of what a human mathematician would need to know or assume to assert G(F)'s truth—that brings out exactly where the hidden assumption of consistency (or some other suppressed premise) does the real work, in a way that shows why critics like Putnam or Benacerraf think the anti-mechanist inference doesn't go through cleanly?
