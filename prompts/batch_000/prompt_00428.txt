I'm sketching out ideas for a literary novel centered on a character who has undergone a corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy, and I've been reading through the classic split-brain literature—Sperry and Gazzaniga's experiments, Nagel's "Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness," and more recent work by Tim Bayne on the "switch model" versus genuine duality of streams. I keep getting stuck on how to render the phenomenon in prose without it collapsing into either a gimmicky sci-fi trope or a dry philosophical thought experiment; I want the reader to actually feel the metaphysical vertigo of a single body whose two hemispheres seem to want, know, and intend different things, as in the classic cases of the "alien hand" grabbing objects the other hand puts down, or a patient's left hand unbuttoning a shirt the right hand just buttoned.

Could you write a short story, grounded in this real neuropsychological material, that dramatizes a single ordinary day in the life of such a patient—something like a family dinner, a trip to a hardware store, or a quiet morning routine—in which the split-brain condition manifests in small, unsettling ways? I'd like the narrative to stay ambiguous about whether the character harbors two centers of consciousness, one dominant narrating self with an unconscious co-pilot, or something else entirely that resists our ordinary concept of personal unity, and to end without resolving that ambiguity for the reader.
