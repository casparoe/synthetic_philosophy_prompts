So I've been prepping my phil of mind seminar and we're about to hit the Lucas-Penrose argument -- the claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that minds can't be equivalent to any formal system (or Turing machine), since we can "see" the truth of the Gödel sentence G_F for any F that supposedly captures our reasoning, while F itself can't prove G_F. Every year some sharp student reads Putnam or Benacerraf and comes back with what they think is a knockdown objection: Lucas can't actually assert that G_F is true without first knowing that F is consistent, and by the second incompleteness theorem F can't prove its own consistency -- so there's no legitimate diagonalization happening, Lucas is just asserting consistency without warrant. This "consistency objection" basically gets treated as the end of the conversation in most of the secondary lit I assign, and honestly I used to just nod along too.

For this year's lecture I want to play devil's advocate and push back on that a bit more seriously than I have before. Can you help me build the strongest version of a reply on Lucas/Penrose's behalf -- some way of running the anti-mechanist argument that isn't sunk by the consistency worry? I'm especially curious whether Penrose's own attempted fix in Shadows of the Mind (the stuff about robust, "confidence-warranting" formal systems, or whatever move he makes to sidestep exactly this problem) actually holds up, or if there's some other more sophisticated way to route around it that doesn't just amount to hand-waving "well, we just see that we're consistent."
