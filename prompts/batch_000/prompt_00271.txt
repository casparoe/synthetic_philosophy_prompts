I've been down a rabbit hole this week rereading parts of Darwin's Descent of Man for a piece I'm working on, and I keep bumping into these passages where he explains tribal morality and self-sacrifice as evolving "for the good of the tribe" -- which sounds a lot like naive group selection to modern ears. Obviously Darwin had zero access to Mendelian genetics, let alone the gene-centered reformulation that Hamilton, Williams, and later Dawkins pushed in the 60s-70s, or the whole multi-level selection rehabilitation that Sober and D.S. Wilson did afterward. He was already visibly struggling with stuff like the sterile-worker-bee problem, which is basically a proto version of the units-of-selection puzzle.

I was arguing about this with a friend who's doing a phd in evolutionary bio last night and neither of us could agree -- if you dropped Darwin into a room and walked him through Hamilton's rule, inclusive fitness, and the "selfish gene" argument that group-level altruism mostly dissolves into kin selection, do you think he'd have been persuaded and dropped the tribal-group language entirely? Or would he have dug in and ended up something closer to a modern multilevel selection theorist, insisting group-level effects are real and irreducible? Curious how you'd reconstruct his reasoning given what he actually wrote versus what he couldn't have known yet.
