I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy seriously—currently working through Hume's Enquiry alongside Judea Pearl's The Book of Why and some of James Woodward's work on manipulability. The juxtaposition has left me with a puzzle I can't quite resolve on my own. Hume famously gives two rival definitions of cause: the regularity account (constant conjunction of temporally contiguous events) and what looks like an early counterfactual gloss ("if the first object had not been, the second never had existed"). Pearl and Woodward's interventionist framework, by contrast, doesn't just appeal to counterfactual dependence in the abstract; it cashes out causation in terms of what would happen under a hypothetical surgical intervention that fixes a variable exogenously while leaving the rest of the causal structure intact, formalized through structural equations and directed graphs.

What I can't settle is how Hume himself would have reacted to this machinery. Given his radical empiricism and his insistence that we never perceive any "necessary connexion" between cause and effect beyond constant conjunction, would he have welcomed the interventionist account as a way of finally making his loose counterfactual remark rigorous and operational—something closer to an experimental procedure he could endorse? Or would he have rejected it as smuggling back in exactly the kind of objective, mind-independent causal architecture (a fixed graph of dependencies that an intervention merely "cuts into") that his skepticism was meant to dissolve in the first place? I'd like your best reconstruction of the position he'd actually take, including where you think the analogy between his two 18th-century definitions and the modern counterfactual/interventionist split starts to break down.
