I've been chewing on Newcomb's problem on and off for a few weeks now, mostly from YouTube explainers and a couple of philosophy blogs, since I never studied this stuff formally in school. Here's what's been bugging me: by the time I actually sit down in front of the two boxes, the predictor has already made its call and the money (or lack of it) is already sitting inside Box B. Nothing I do in that room can reach backward and change what's already been placed there. So it seems to me that the only way one-boxing could possibly be the "smart" choice is if my decision, made after the fact, somehow travels back in time and influences what the predictor decided earlier — which sounds like straightforward backward causation, the kind of thing that shouldn't be possible given how cause and effect normally work.

Am I right that people who argue for taking only Box B are quietly committed to this sort of reverse-in-time influence? And if that's the case, how do serious decision theorists defend one-boxing without just admitting they've smuggled in a violation of ordinary causality? I'd like to understand whether there's a way to make sense of choosing one box that doesn't require me to believe my present choice is somehow reaching back and rewriting the past.
