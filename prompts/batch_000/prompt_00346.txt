Last night a friend and I got into a long, somewhat heated argument about surrogate goals as a defense against acausal threats -- the idea, developed mostly in and around the functional-decision-theory literature, that an agent vulnerable to extortion by a threatener (including a threatener who never causally interacts with it, but merely predicts what it would do) might do better by adopting some substitute object of concern, so that threats get aimed at the surrogate rather than at whatever the agent actually values. My friend thought this is a genuinely stable, rational policy: it lets the agent hold firm against real threats while still presenting enough "extortability" to satisfy a threatener who's only interested in leverage. I pushed back, arguing that the maneuver is self-undermining -- either the surrogate goal is genuinely internalized, in which case the agent now has a new real vulnerability and the problem has just been relocated, or it isn't genuinely internalized, in which case a threatener sophisticated enough to reason acausally about the agent in the first place should also be able to see through the ruse and route around it. Neither of us managed to convince the other, and we ended up more confused than when we started.

Could you write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors holding these opposing positions -- one defending surrogate goals as a coherent and workable defense against acausal threats, the other maintaining that the strategy is either unstable or incoherent for the reasons above? I want real engagement rather than talking past each other: the defender should address the regress worry and the "transparency to a sufficiently sophisticated threatener" worry directly, ideally by drawing on how commitment, precommitment, and self-knowledge are supposed to work for the kind of agent under discussion, and the skeptic should respond to whatever the defender actually says rather than just restating the original objection.
