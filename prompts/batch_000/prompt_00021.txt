I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group that just worked through David Lewis's Best System Analysis (BSA) of laws, and the discussion kept circling back to a worry I can't shake. Lewis wants laws of nature to supervene on the total Humean mosaic of particular matters of fact (the "no necessary connections" thesis), with lawhood cashed out as membership in the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength (and, in later formulations, fit with respect to chance). Armstrong and Dretske, by contrast, hold that laws are relations of nomic necessitation between universals, something over and above the pattern of instantiations, and Maudlin goes further still by treating lawhood as a metaphysical primitive that grounds rather than merely summarizes the mosaic. My worry is whether the BSA can actually do the explanatory and modal work we want from laws -- underwriting robust counterfactuals, grounding objective chance (Lewis's own "big bad bug" problem), and explaining rather than just redescribing regularities -- given that on a Humean picture there is nothing "in" the world making the regularities hold beyond their holding.

Write an essay addressing this controversy: can a Humean, best-system style account of laws (in the Lewisian tradition, including refinements by Loewer, Cohen, and others responding to the big bad bug and the "problem of undermining futures") adequately capture the necessity, explanatory power, and support for counterfactuals that laws seem to have, or do these desiderata ultimately force us toward an anti-Humean view on which laws involve irreducible necessitation or primitive nomic facts? Engage seriously with the strongest versions of both sides -- don't just note the standard objections and move on -- and end by defending a definite verdict rather than a summary of considerations on both sides. If your considered view is some hybrid or deflationary position (e.g., that the debate rests on a mistaken demand for "necessity" that neither side can really satisfy), that's a permissible conclusion, but you should argue for it explicitly rather than simply gesturing at it as a compromise.
