I've been reading about the discursive dilemma in a seminar on collective epistemology, and I keep getting stuck on what it actually implies for real institutions rather than the toy three-judge examples everyone uses. The basic setup is clear enough to me: a court (or committee, or editorial board) can aggregate individually consistent judgments on premises and a conclusion in a way that yields a collectively inconsistent or unsupported verdict, and which aggregation procedure you pick (premise-based vs. conclusion-based) changes the outcome. What I'm struggling with is the deeper significance of this beyond "voting can be weird."

I sit on a small nonprofit's board, and we routinely vote on individual policy questions (should we open a new program, do we have the budget, does it fit our mission) and then treat the "obvious" bundled conclusion as if it were separately endorsed by the group. Given the discursive dilemma and the broader judgment-aggregation literature (List and Pettit especially), can you lay out the most important implications of this result — both for the theoretical question of whether there's even a coherent notion of "group belief" or "group rationality," and for practical committee design? I'm especially interested in whether the choice between premise-based and conclusion-based procedures is ever non-arbitrary, whether there's any principled way out of the dilemma (à la escaping Arrow's theorem by relaxing some condition), and what this means for how much epistemic weight we should put on the "official" reasoning a group gives for its decisions versus the decision itself.
