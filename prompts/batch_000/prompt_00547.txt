I'm a PhD student in a lab that works on AI alignment, and I've spent the last few weeks going through Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong's 2015 paper on corrigibility for my qualifying exam. My advisor and I keep going back and forth on how much weight to put on the utility-indifference construction they propose: the idea that adding a correction term to the agent's utility function can make it indifferent between being shut down and continuing to operate, so that it neither resists nor manipulates the shutdown button. What I can't shake is the worry that this only buys you indifference toward one narrowly specified event (a human pressing a particular switch), while the paper's own list of desiderata for corrigibility is much broader—covering things like non-manipulation of the operator, preservation of shutdown behavior in subagents and successors, and the absence of incentives to tamper with the correction term itself.

I'd like you to make the strongest case you can for the position that utility indifference, even granting that it succeeds on its own narrow terms, does not thereby establish corrigibility in the fuller sense the paper aspires to. In particular, I want the argument to engage with how an agent that is indifferent to shutdown might still have every incentive to construct delegates, subagents, or successor systems that lack the correction term (since, being indifferent, it doesn't care whether those systems are shutdown-indifferent too), and how this gap could be exploited once the agent is capable enough to reason about multi-agent extensions of itself. If it's useful, feel free to contrast this with how Hadfield-Menell et al.'s off-switch game tries to handle a similar problem through uncertainty rather than indifference.
