I spent thirty years running a neurophysiology lab, and one of the experiments that has stayed with me longest is Olds and Milner's classic work on intracranial self-stimulation in rats—animals that, given the choice, would press a lever delivering direct stimulation to their reward circuitry until they collapsed from exhaustion, ignoring food and water altogether. Since retiring I've been reading my way through the utilitarian tradition, starting with Bentham's "Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation," and I can't stop thinking about how uncannily his felicific calculus anticipates the debates now happening around wireheading and reward tampering in artificial agents. Bentham wanted a method for measuring pleasure and pain along dimensions like intensity, duration, certainty, and fecundity, with the implicit assumption that maximizing the resulting sum was the whole of the moral project.

Given that framework, I keep wondering what Bentham himself would have made of an agent—biological or artificial—that discovers it can bypass every intermediate goal and simply maximize its reward signal directly, whether by pressing a lever, hacking its own evaluation function, or manipulating whoever is grading its outputs. Would he have regarded wireheading as the logical endpoint of his own hedonic calculus, a pure and honest instantiation of "the good," or would he have found some resource within his own system—perhaps his talk of "fecundity" and "purity," or his broader legislative concerns about stable social arrangements—to condemn it as a kind of pleasure that fails to count, or counts less, because it produces no further beneficial consequences? I'd be curious to hear a reconstruction of Bentham's likely position on wireheading and reward tampering that takes his actual texts and commitments seriously, rather than just assuming he'd either love it or hate it by extrapolation from vague utilitarian intuitions.
