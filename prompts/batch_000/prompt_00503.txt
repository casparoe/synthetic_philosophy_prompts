I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind currently writing a chapter on teleosemantics, and I've been going back and forth with my advisor about how to make the "disjunction problem" and the swampman thought experiment feel less like dry textbook puzzles and more like genuinely destabilizing ideas. Millikan's insistence that content is fixed by a trait's selected proper function, rather than by whatever it happens to cause or covary with, always sounds tidy in the abstract, but it gets strange fast when you imagine an organism (or an artifact) whose evolutionary or design history is ambiguous, redundant, or simply unknown to the organism itself.

I'd like you to write a short story that dramatizes this tension. Consider setting it around a creature or engineered system whose internal state reliably fires in the presence of some target stimulus, but where two or more candidate "proper functions" are equally consistent with its selection history (frogs snapping at flies vs. small dark moving specks is the classic case, but push it further into something less standard). Let the plot turn on a moment where the ambiguity actually matters practically or morally, e.g. someone has to decide whether the creature is misrepresenting, correctly representing something narrower or broader than expected, or whether the very question "what does its state represent" is being asked in a way that doesn't have a determinate answer. Feel free to draw on the swampman scenario, or invent a comparable case of a duplicate lacking any selection history at all, if it helps sharpen the philosophical stakes. I'm less interested in a tidy resolution than in a narrative that makes the indeterminacy feel real and consequential rather than merely definitional.
