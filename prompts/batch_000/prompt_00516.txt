Blaise Pascal helped pioneer reasoning about decisions under uncertainty, most famously via his wager concerning belief in God. Centuries later, decision theorists developed coherence arguments -- Dutch book and money-pump arguments in particular -- which claim to show that any agent whose preferences violate the expected utility axioms can be exploited into accepting a guaranteed loss, and that rationality therefore demands conforming to expected utility maximization. Given what we know of Pascal's own views on probability, reason, and its limits (as expressed, for instance, in the Pensées), how would he likely have judged these coherence arguments? Would he have accepted that avoiding exploitability suffices to establish expected utility maximization as a genuine requirement of rationality, or would he have pushed back, and on what grounds?
