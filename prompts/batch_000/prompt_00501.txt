I'm taking an elective on free will and moral responsibility this semester, and for next week's seminar we just finished reading excerpts from P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment." I think I understand the basic setup: rather than asking whether determinism undermines responsibility in the abstract, Strawson wants to ground responsibility in our natural, deeply ingrained reactive attitudes — things like resentment, gratitude, and guilt — and he argues that even if determinism turned out to be true, this couldn't actually lead us to abandon these attitudes wholesale, since we're too committed to the "participant stance" in everyday human relationships to sustain the fully detached "objective attitude" toward people in general.

What I keep getting stuck on is a specific move he seems to make: he slides from a psychological or descriptive claim (that we cannot, as a practical matter, give up reactive attitudes and adopt the objective attitude toward everyone) to a normative conclusion (that holding people responsible via these attitudes is therefore justified or appropriate). This feels like it might smuggle in an "ought" from an "is" — the fact that we're psychologically stuck with resentment doesn't obviously show that resentment is ever warranted, any more than being stuck with a cognitive bias would show the bias is tracking truth. My teacher mentioned in passing that this is a well-known objection to Strawson but we ran out of time before getting into the details.

Could you walk me through, in more precision than just "Strawson commits the naturalistic fallacy," why exactly this move is considered philosophically shaky, and then explain how contemporary defenders of Strawson (or naturalistic compatibilists more broadly) try to close the gap between "we can't help but react this way" and "therefore these reactions are justified"? I'd like to actually understand the structure of the disagreement well enough to defend a position in the seminar discussion.
