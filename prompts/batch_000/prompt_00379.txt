I've been working on a longer piece about decision theory for laypeople and keep getting stuck on one specific wrinkle in superrationality that none of the pop-sci explainers actually address. Everyone loves the "twin prisoner's dilemma" setup because the two players are literally identical, so the symmetry argument (whatever I do, the other guy does too) feels airtight. But real-world applications people gesture at -- voting, climate cooperation, arms races -- involve agents who are similar in some loose sense but definitely not exact copies, and often have asymmetric payoffs or slightly different information. My question is: what actually happens to the superrationality argument once you relax perfect symmetry? Does it degrade gracefully into something like "cooperate in proportion to how correlated you think your reasoning is with theirs," or does the whole thing just kind of collapse back into standard game theory once the agents aren't mirror images anymore?

I've seen some people wave at correlated equilibrium or at Yudkowsky/Soares-style updateless/functional decision theory as ways of rigorizing what Hofstadter was originally gesturing at, but I don't have a great intuitive grip on whether those frameworks actually rescue the "cooperate because a truly rational agent would reason the same way I do" logic in asymmetric cases, or whether they're doing something conceptually different. Could you walk me through where exactly the symmetry assumption is doing the load-bearing work in the classic superrationality argument, and what the most credible attempts to patch or generalize it look like once you drop that assumption?
