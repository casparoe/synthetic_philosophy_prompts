I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and rational choice, and I keep getting stuck on the coherence arguments that are supposed to ground expected utility maximization as a requirement of rationality. We've worked through the classical material—Ramsey, von Neumann-Morgenstern, Savage—and the standard Dutch book and money-pump arguments purporting to show that any agent violating the axioms (especially completeness and transitivity) can be exploited or driven in circles by a sufficiently clever bookie. But I've also been reading some of the more recent pushback, both from decision theorists who argue that these arguments smuggle in substantive assumptions (e.g., that preferences must be revealed through a fixed menu of bets, or that agents can't simply refuse trades that would pump them), and from writers in the AI safety space who invoke coherence theorems to argue that any sufficiently capable goal-directed system will converge on expected utility maximization, a claim that has itself been contested on the grounds that coherence arguments only bite once an agent is assumed to have complete preferences and to engage in each of a sequence of trades.

I'd like you to write an essay that takes a side on this controversy: do the coherence arguments (Dutch book and money-pump style reasoning) succeed in establishing that rational agents must be expected utility maximizers, or do they fail to do the philosophical work often attributed to them? Please engage seriously with the strongest objections—particularly worries about the completeness axiom, the possibility of resolute or sophisticated choice as a way of avoiding exploitation without full coherence, and the gap between "avoiding sure losses" and "maximizing expected utility" in a substantive, action-guiding sense. Don't just present both sides neutrally; defend a specific verdict about how much philosophical weight these arguments can actually bear, and explain what you take the most decisive consideration to be.
