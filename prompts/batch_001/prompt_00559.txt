After forty years running an experimental physics laboratory, I have found retirement affords me the time to read broadly in philosophy, a subject I always admired from a distance but never had the leisure to study properly. Lately I have been working through Nick Bostrom's writings on superintelligence, and I keep running into what strikes me as an unresolved tension between two ideas that are usually presented as complementary pillars of the same argument: the orthogonality thesis and the thesis of instrumental convergence.

As I understand it, the orthogonality thesis states that intelligence and final goals are, in principle, entirely independent variables -- an arbitrarily intelligent system could in principle be built to pursue any goal whatsoever, however arbitrary or trivial. Yet the instrumental convergence thesis seems to say almost the opposite: that sufficiently capable agents, regardless of their ultimate goals, will tend to converge on the same small set of instrumental strategies -- self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive self-improvement, resistance to having one's goals altered, and so on. If nearly every highly intelligent agent ends up behaving in these predictably similar ways, it seems to me that intelligence and goal-directed behavior are not nearly as independent as the orthogonality thesis wants to claim; the space of likely outcomes collapses substantially once you assume high intelligence. Is there a technical distinction in the literature that dissolves this apparent contradiction, or is this simply a known soft spot in the argument that proponents of AI risk have not fully addressed? I would be grateful for a rigorous clarification, since none of the popular expositions I have found actually confronts the issue directly.
