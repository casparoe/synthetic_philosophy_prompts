I've been working through the anthropic reasoning literature for a seminar paper, and I keep getting stuck on a specific tension in how Boltzmann brain arguments get deployed against cosmological models. The standard move is: if a model predicts that Boltzmann brains vastly outnumber ordinary observers in the universe's future (or across the whole ensemble), this is treated as a *reductio* against the model, since it seems to undermine our ability to trust our own memories and observations as evidence of a coherent, low-entropy past. But here's what confuses me: the inference "this theory implies I'm probably a Boltzmann brain, therefore reject the theory" already presupposes that I'm not a Boltzmann brain (or at least that I can reason reliably about theory choice), which is precisely what's in question if the theory were true. So the argument seems to use non-Boltzmann-brain-style reasoning to rule out a scenario in which such reasoning wouldn't be trustworthy anyway.

Can you give a concrete example that illustrates this self-undermining structure clearly — ideally a toy cosmological or statistical model where the "Boltzmann brain problem" is invoked to reject the model, but where spelling out the inference shows it covertly relies on privileging exactly the kind of observer (and exactly the kind of reasoning process) that the theory itself says should be extremely rare? I'd also find it helpful if the example gestured at how different anthropic frameworks (e.g., SSA vs. SIA) handle or fail to handle this self-undermining worry differently.
