Sam: "The BSC is the real deal. A species is a group of actually or potentially interbreeding populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. Anything that doesn't fit -- bacteria, asexuals, fossils -- just isn't a species in the real sense. The phylogenetic crowd is being sloppy."

Alex: "That's a cop-out. The BSC can't even handle asexual organisms, which are most of life. If your 'real' definition excludes most of the tree, it's an artifact, not the concept. The phylogenetic species concept -- smallest monophyletic group, most recent common ancestor -- is the right one."

Sam: "But the phylogenetic concept has the level problem built in: any clade is a 'species' under it, so you have to pick an arbitrary cutoff. The BSC at least gives you a natural, empirically tractable criterion -- reproductive isolation."

Alex: "Reproductive isolation isn't clean either. Ring species -- the herring gull complex, the canids around the Caspian -- are isolated at the ends of the ring but interbreed all the way around. So is the ring one species or many? The BSC can't answer that without just stipulating."

Sam: "Fine, ring species are a boundary case. For most sexual organisms, reproductive isolation works, and the fact that bacteria and asexuals don't fit just means 'species' isn't a universal category -- that's a feature, not a bug."

Alex: "So the word means different things for bacteria than for gulls? That's not a concept, that's a homonym. The phylogenetic concept applies consistently across the whole tree, while the BSC has the asexual problem with no principled line for where asexual lineages stop being one species."
