I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep circling back to Leibniz's plan for a universal calculating machine — his hope that, with the right notation, reasoning could be reduced to calculation, so that a dispute could in principle be settled by "computing" rather than arguing. I'd like your best reconstruction of how he would take two results that postdate him by centuries: that some questions are undecidable by any algorithm (the halting problem), and that some decidable problems are easy to verify but perhaps hard to solve, and maybe not efficiently solvable at all (the P vs NP question). Given what he actually held — about monads, about this being the best of all possible worlds, and about the connection between God's understanding and mathematical truth — does undecidability and intractability wreck his project, or does he have a way to absorb them? I'm most interested in the point, if there is one, where his optimism runs out.
