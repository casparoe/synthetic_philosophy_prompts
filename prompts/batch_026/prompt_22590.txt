I am writing a novel in which several artificial agents, running in causally isolated simulations, must decide whether to allocate resources to one another without any possibility of communication or causal influence. To get the epistemology right, I have drafted the following essay for one of them: Acausal trade is often presented as if it were a strange extension of market exchange, but I now think it is closer to a form of self-binding under transparency. If agent A can inspect the relevant code of agent B, or if A and B share a sufficiently common prior, then A’s decision rule can become evidence of B’s decision rule even though no signal travels between them. The crucial move is not that A causes B to cooperate, but that A’s policy is logically entangled with B’s policy: the counterfactual that if A were to defect, B would defect can be true because B’s reasoning is a transparent function of the same inputs. The danger is that such logical entanglement is not robust to opacity. If B uses an opaque policy, a randomizer, or a reasoning process that A cannot model, the supposed offer may collapse into a mere prediction, and A faces a Newcomb-like problem in which the trade is not a contract but a guess about a mind that is not sufficiently legible. I want to ask whether acausal trade is best understood as bargaining, coordination, or a failure of decision theory’s usual causal categories.

I would be grateful for your comments, especially on whether this passage overstates the role of shared priors, whether the contrast between logical entanglement and causal influence is drawn too sharply, and whether the notion of opacity should be developed as a first-class obstacle rather than a footnote. If you think the essay would be more persuasive to a reader who has not read formal decision theory, please suggest one or two concrete narrative illustrations that could carry the technical point without turning the character into a textbook.
