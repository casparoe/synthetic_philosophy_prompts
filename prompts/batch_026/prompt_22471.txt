I have just been appointed chair of the clinical ethics committee at a university hospital, and the first case on my desk is one I am not sure I am equipped to handle. A twenty-seven-year-old man is fourteen months out from a hypoxic brain injury following a cardiac arrest, and the neurology team has classified him as being in a disorder of consciousness consistent with unresponsive wakefulness syndrome. His parents have asked us to help them decide whether to transition him from full intensive-care support — including artificial nutrition — to comfort-focused care, and we are convening a case conference on Tuesday. I would like you to draft the short written opinion I will circulate to the committee before that meeting: it should contain a concrete recommendation with a time frame, and it should treat the family's position seriously rather than simply restating the clinical criteria.

The difficulty is that the objective data are genuinely in conflict. Two functional MRI sessions gave opposite results — one showed a task-locked response consistent with covert awareness, the next did not — and the team has recently run a transcranial-magnetic-stimulation EEG perturbation test, the Perturbational Complexity Index, which returned a value of 0.26, in the indeterminate band between the thresholds its developers associated with wakefulness and with unconsciousness. Our consulting neurologist reads the PCI as the most objective tool we have and leans toward a poor prognosis; the family's representative, who has read a little about the underlying theory, argues that the PCI is a surrogate derived from Integrated Information Theory, which is itself a disputed framework, and that an indeterminate value cannot be treated as a verdict. I want the memo to engage that challenge on its own terms: specifically, whether IIT's own postulates — the exclusion and maximization postulates, the idea that experience is located at the "grain" at which integrated information is maximal rather than in the whole system, and the fact that the theory's 4.0 reformulation replaced the original measure of integrated information with a new "intrinsic difference" quantity — license the inference our neurologist wants to draw, namely that a low or indeterminate whole-brain measure tells us the patient is not experiencing anything.

My own background is in ethics, not neuroscience, so I want the opinion to be one the neurologists would respect rather than one that merely papers over their disagreement. State the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly, whatever side that ends up being, and be careful not to overclaim what the PCI can show. The deliverable is the memo itself, in a form the committee could actually act on.
