Im putting together a piece for a science-and-ideas magazine on the "right reasons" requirement in moral philosophy — the view that an action only has moral worth if its done for the right reasons (the moral reasons themselves), not the wrong ones (a reward, your reputation, a command from God, etc.). My source is a philosopher who defends this view against the standard objections, and I want to push her where the literature actually pushes. Can you draft six to eight interview questions that go beyond having her restate the view? I want at least one question each on: (1) the divine-command case — if God's commands are the source of the moral reasons, is acting because God commanded really a "wrong reason," or is the heteronomy/autonomy distinction just verbal? (2) the moral-luck case — someone who intends to save a life for the right reasons but the person was already dead, so the action wasn't actually right; does this have moral worth? (3) what exactly counts as the "right reason" — the value of the person's life, the fact that you ought to save them, or your caring about their welfare? And for each, a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.
