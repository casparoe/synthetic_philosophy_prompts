My two friends Priya and Tom got into a long argument at a barbecue last weekend about whether freelance software devs are actually "exploited" in the way Marx meant, and I'm still not sure who was making sence. I'm a software engineer, I have zero philosophy background, so I'd like you to referee.

Priya started by saying that devs who work through a platform or a consulting firm are exploited in the Marxian sense: they don't own the means of production (the platform, the clients, the distribution), and the intermediary siphons off a cut of everything they produce, which is exactly the surplus value Marx was talking about. Tom pushed back and said that's not right, because exploitation in Marx isn't just "the other side takes a cut"; it requires that the worker is *forced* to sell their labor because they have no other way to eat, and a freelancer who picks their own hours and can walk away isn't in that situation, it's just a voluntary market exchange. Priya said that misses the point, that the coercion is structural, not individual: even if a dev can choose today, the fact that they don't own the means of production means they're always at the mercy of the market, and the "choice" to sell labor is only a choice because the alternative is not having money.

Then Tom conceded the structural point had something to it, but changed the target. He said that even if there's exploitation, the whole "alienation" idea is just a fancy way of saying "I don't like my job," and it's not a real analytical concept, it's a value judgment dressed up as a theory. Priya said that's wrong, that alienation is a specific structural description, not a feeling: the worker is cut off from the product of their labor (the code they write belongs to the company), from the activity itself (the work is done to someone else's spec, not their own), from their own creative capacity (which gets reduced to a mere means of making a living), and from other people (who become competitors instead of colaborators). Tom's last move was to say this is just restating "the worker doesn't own the output and doesn't control the process," which is true of any wage labor, including a job someone actually enjoys, so it doesn't explain anything distinctive, it's basically a tautology.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please end with a final verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Priya} or \answer{Tom}, whoever is more right overall.
