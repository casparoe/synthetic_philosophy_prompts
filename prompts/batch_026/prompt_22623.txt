I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been reading Hume's *A Treatise of Human Nature* (Book 3, Part 2, Section 5, "Of the Obligation of Promises") on my own, partly because it keeps coming up in a discussion I'm having with some friends about why a promise feels binding on me the moment I make one. I've got the broad shape of his conclusion—that promises aren't natural but are "human inventions, founded on the necessities and interests of society"—but I keep getting stuck on a particular step in the middle of the section. I keep trying to formalize it the way I would a proof, and I can't get the premises and the conclusion to line up. Here is the passage:

"It follows, therefore, that since the act of the mind, which enters into a promise, and produces its obligation, is neither the resolving, desiring, nor willing any particular performance, it must necessarily be the willing of that obligation, which arises from the promise. ... All morality depends upon our sentiments; and when any action, or quality of the mind, pleases us after a certain manner, we say it is virtuous; and when the neglect, or non-performance of it, displeases us after a like manner, we say that we lie under an obligation to perform it. A change of the obligation supposes a change of the sentiment; and a creation of a new obligation supposes some new sentiment to arise. But 'tis certain we can naturally no more change our own sentiments, than the motions of the heavens; nor by a single act of our will, that is, by a promise, render any action agreeable or disagreeable, moral or immoral; which, without that act, wou'd have produc'd contrary impressions, or have been endow'd with different qualities. It wou'd be absurd, therefore, to will any new obligation, that is, any new sentiment of pain or pleasure; nor is it possible, that men cou'd naturally fall into so gross an absurdity. A promise, therefore, is naturally something altogether unintelligible, nor is there any act of the mind belonging to it."

What I'm not managing to reconstruct is the logic connecting these pieces. First, can you walk me through why Hume thinks the only remaining candidate for the "act of the mind" is the willing of the obligation—that is, why he feels entitled to assume a promise must consist in some single mental act at all, why he rules out resolution, desire, and willing of the performance in that order, and whether treating "willing the obligation" as the leftover is a legitimate proof by exhaustion or just a rhetorical squeeze. Second, I want to understand the claim that the will can't create new sentiments. Is that a psychological point (that I can't simply decide to feel a new emotion on command), a metaphysical point about what an obligation consists in, or something else? And how does that claim do the work of showing that a promise is "unintelligible"—that there is no act of the mind belonging to it—rather than merely showing that I have no desire or motive to keep it? I'd be grateful if you could keep the argument's internal logic separate from the places where I suspect Hume is just quietly asserting his broader sentimentist assumption that all obligation is nothing but a sentiment of displeasure at non-performance.
