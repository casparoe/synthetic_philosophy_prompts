I am writing a novel in which a veterinary surgeon comes to believe that one of her patients is conscious, and I have been asked so often whether I have read Descartes that I have at last turned to the relevant passage in the *Discourse on Method*. Last week, at a dinner party, two of my acquaintances argued about what Descartes is actually doing in that passage, and I found myself unable to follow the technical part. I would be grateful if you could adjudicate their exchange for me.

The first, who works on early modern philosophy, said that Descartes' position is simple: animals are machines, and the test he offers for whether a being has a mind is whether it can use language — whether it can produce words or signs to express its thoughts, and above all whether it can make new arrangements of words in response to what is said to it. Since animals cannot do this, he concludes that they do not think. But, he added, the argument is circular. He is using a test for language to establish a conclusion about thought, assuming without argument that thought just is the capacity for language, so that a being which lacks language must, by definition, lack thought. The criterion is built out of the very thing he is trying to detect.

The second, a philosopher of mind, objected that he had misread the passage. Descartes, she said, explicitly extends the test beyond words: he writes that even animals which have never acquired speech might, if they had thought, express it by "other signs," and that no machine could "produce different arrangements of words so as to give an appropriately meaningful answer to whatever is said in its presence, as the dullest of men can do." The test, therefore, is not for verbal language but for a general capacity to generate novel sign-arrangements in response to situations — a behavioral criterion, independent of whatever label we attach to the underlying capacity. And the inference from behavior to mind is not circular; it rests on Descartes' metaphysics, on which only a thinking substance is capable of this.

The first replied that even the generalized test is circular, though at a deeper level. Descartes assumes that only a thinking substance can produce flexible signs, and then uses the absence of flexible signs to conclude that there is no thinking substance. The conclusion is doing the work inside the premise; it is not an independent criterion after all.

The second answered that this misidentifies where the argument's real force lies. The speech test is only the most dramatic illustration; the substance of Descartes' case is his mechanistic account, on which every animal behavior — including what we would call sensation — can be explained by mechanism alone, so that no mind needs to be posited to account for it. Even if the speech test is circular, the mechanistic explanation carries the weight.

I confess I could not tell who was right about which point. Could you go through their exchange step by step and say who is right at each move, rather than simply who wins in the end? In particular, I would like to know whether the charge of circularity is fair, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about what Descartes is actually doing.
