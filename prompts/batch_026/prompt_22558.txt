I've been chewing on Kripke's Naming and Necessity for a reading group after seeing a bunch of blog posts that treat the necessary a posteriori as basically dead for descriptivism, and I keep geting the feeling that the objection is being oversold. I want to steelman the side I'm not sure I believe: please argue that "water is H2O" and "Hesperus is Phosphorus" do not actually refute descriptivist theories of reference. The core move I have in mind is that the causal-historical chain used to fix a name's reference functions like a hidden description, something like "the object that got its name via this chain from the original baptism," and that this description is necessarily satisfied in every possible world once the chain is fixed as part of the context. So the a posteriori character of the identity is just the fact that we had to empirically discover which object satisfied the chain, not evidence that the name has no descriptive content at all.

Make it technical enough that it engages with the "name of a name" objection and the worry that causal chains are not really descriptive, but don't turn it into a history of the debate. I'd like a real argument, with a clear conclusion, not just a summary of what Kripke or Putnam said. Also, if there is a version of descriptivism that can survive without making reference depend on the speaker's beliefs or explicit descriptions, I'd rather you argue for that one.
