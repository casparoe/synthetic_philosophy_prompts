I have spent most of my professional life in observational geophysics, and it is only in retirement that I have been able to take up the philosophy of science in earnest. I am currently working my way through a short course on Bayesian epistemology, and I have become stuck on what is usually called the problem of old evidence, which I find considerably more subtle than the expositions I have read suggest. Consider, for instance, the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion: the data were catalogued and puzzling for decades before general relativity arrived, and when Einstein's theory was published it appeared to explain them beautifully. The standard objection runs that since the evidence E was already known, P(E) = 1, and Bayes's theorem then seems to collapse into P(H|E) = P(H), so the old data contribute nothing to the confirmation of H. That conclusion strikes me as deeply counterintuitive — in practice, the pre-existing observations on Mercury plainly made the new theory more credible than it was before. Could you walk me through the precise mechanics of this puzzle? In particular, I would be grateful for (i) a careful derivation of why ordinary conditionalization on E is confirmationally neutral, (ii) an explanation of why Jeffrey-style probabilistic conditioning, which is designed precisely to handle uncertain evidence, does not by itself rescue the case, (iii) a discussion of what role, if any, the agent's background knowledge and the way E was obtained might play — for example, whether it matters that E was discovered for reasons independent of H — and (iv) a brief survey of the main proposed solutions in the literature, with your own assessment of which appears most promising. I would appreciate an account that is precise enough for someone comfortable with the notation, but that does not drown in formalism; I am after the conceptual structure of the problem as much as the algebra.
