I am revising a short paper for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of testimony, and I would be grateful if you could comment on whether the following draft is accurate, philosophically interesting, and whether any of its moves are overstated. The paper is not intended to settle the reductionist/anti-reductionist debate, but to identify a point where the debate becomes less tidy than it often appears in introductions: reductionists say that testimonial justification requires non-testimonial evidence that the source is reliable, while anti-reductionists say that testimony can be a default-entitled source, and my claim is that both sides are partly talking past one another because they are using “reliability” in two different senses.

On the one hand, there is source-external reliability, the statistical or counterfactual fact that a particular kind of source tends to produce true reports; on the other, there is agent-internal warrant, the question whether the hearer has a reason, from the hearer’s perspective, to accept the report. I argue that reductionism is most plausible when “reliability” is understood externally, but it becomes question-begging when it demands that the hearer already possess a non-testimonial justification for believing in that external reliability, because much of the evidence for reliability is itself testimonial or depends on a background practice of testimony. A useful case, I think, is the meteorologist’s forecast: the hearer’s non-testimonial evidence that meteorology is reliable may include remembered past forecasts, but those memories are often themselves formed through reports, and the apparent independence of the evidence is therefore harder to establish than it first looks. Anti-reductionism, I suggest, is better understood not as a claim that testimony is infallible or that default trust never needs to be checked, but as the claim that the default epistemic situation is one in which testimony is already a reason-giving source, and that defeaters operate by introducing specific reasons to doubt the source, the context, or the content.

Please comment on whether the distinction between external reliability and internal warrant is doing genuine work or whether it merely restates the original dispute, whether my charge that reductionism can become question-begging is fair, and whether the closing claim needs to be weakened to avoid sounding like a simple advocacy of anti-reductionism.
