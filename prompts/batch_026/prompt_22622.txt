I am a retired metallurgist — my career was spent studying how alloys corrode and fail at the atomic level — and I have only taken up philosophy in the past couple of years, where the problem of material constitution, the statue and the lump of clay, struck me as unusually live: I have spent my professional life watching the same material persist through changes in which atoms it contains, and I find it hard to see why the same reasoning should not apply to works of art. I sit on the conservation committee of a regional museum, which makes the final call on treatment proposals, and I would like you to draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting.

The case is the museum's signature object: a sixteenth-century bronze statue that has anchored the collection since a family's 1962 gift. The bronze has active disease across roughly a third of its surface, and our conservator recommends removing the affected sections and replacing them with castings of a modern alloy, after which the statue will be displayed as before, under its existing label and credit line. Half the committee regards this as routine conservation: the statue is the same object regardless of which atoms constitute it, since what makes it that work is its form, design, and history. The other half, including the donor family's representative, argues that replacing a third of the original metal is the destruction of the object, and that we would then be displaying a different thing under a false label, with consequences for provenance, value, and honesty to the donors.

Please draft the committee's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete course of action and a time frame. I would like it to engage the material-constitution question seriously (what makes the statue persist through the replacement, and whether the original bronze and the statue can have different persistence conditions), to treat the strongest version of the losing side's argument fairly rather than merely restating our conservation policy, and to give the committee criteria that could be applied to future restorations, not just this one.
