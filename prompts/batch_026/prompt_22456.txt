I cover science and ideas for a weekly, and this semester I'm also TA-ing a data-literacy workshop for our young writers. I need a second set of eyes on one answer from teh statistics homework set before I post grades. The question was:

A hospital compared two treatments and reported survival by severity at admission:

| Severity | Treatment A (survived/total) | Treatment B (survived/total) |
| Mild | 85/100 | 9/10 |
| Moderate | 30/50 | 40/50 |
| Severe | 2/10 | 45/90 |

(a) Describe what is going on in this table and name the phenomenon.
(b) Which treatment should the hospital recommend for a new patient, and why?

One student wrote:

"(a) This is Simpson's paradox. Treatment A was given to mostly mild patients, so its overall survival rate (117/160 ≈ 73%) is inflated relative to B's (94/150 ≈ 63%), even though B beats A in every severity class. The overall rate is a weighted average of the class-specific rates, and because the mix of severities differs between the arms, the ordering flips. This is basically the ecological fallacy—inferring about individuals from group-level data. (b) The hospital should recommend B. Severity is a confounder, so the class-specific rates are what matter, and whenever data like this is aggregated across groups, subgroup analysis is always the correct level of analysis."
