I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I've been stuck on a question about how much independent verification a reporter actually owes a reader when the underlying evidence is, as a practical matter, out of reach. The case in front of me: I'm working on a piece about a pharmaceutical company's clinical trial, and a former independent monitor for that trial has given me a detailed account of how adverse events were downgraded before they made it into the regulatory file. The monitor is speaking on condition of anonymity, the raw data sits in a jurisdiction I can't access, and pressing for a second source would almost certainly tip off the company and put the monitor at real risk.

My editor's standing rule is that no claim goes to print unless I've independently confirmed it — the documents, a second source, or my own observation. A senior colleague pushes back, arguing that rule, applied consistently, would make most of what we do impossible, since nearly everything we publish is somebody's word for it; the real question, she says, is whether I have any specific reason to doubt this particular source, not whether I can re-derive every claim from scratch. I've been reading a bit of epistemology on the side lately, and I suspect the two of them are actually defending different positions in a debate I only half understand, but I can't quite put my finger on what divides them. Can you help me think through who has the better principle, and what I should actually do about the monitor's account?
