I've been working on a long-form piece about whether large language models genuinely "understand" anything, and I keep getting stuck on a more technical question that I think is actually doing most of the philosophical work. When interpretability researchers use tools like sparse autoencoders and report that they've found a "feature" in a model — say, a unit that reliably lights up on mentions of a particular concept — do they have a reason to think that feature corresponds to something real in the model's internal structure, or have they merely found a convenient, human-interpretable basis for describing the model's computations? I was discussing this with a friend who works in the field, and he was quite confident that the features are "real" in some sense, but I couldn't quite get my head around how he responds to the most obvious objection to his view, which is that you can always apply a change of basis and get a completely different set of "features" that does exactly the same computations.

Please write an essay weighing the case for and against realism about these kinds of interpretability findings — that is, the view that the features we extract correspond to genuine, basis-independent structure in the network, as opposed to the view that they are artifacts of our choice of description. I'm particularly interested in how the basis-dependence objection bears on the dispute, and how it interacts with the related point that our interpretability tools impose their own priors, such as sparsity, on what counts as a "feature." Does the realist have a principled response to these challenges, or is she left defending a pragmatic preference for certain bases? You don't need to settle the broader question of whether models understand anything; just reach a clear, justified conclusion about whether the realism thesis is defensible on its own terms.
