A friend of mine who works in machine learning and another friend who is a statistician got into an argument at a dinner party about the "impossibility" results in algorithmic fairness — the results showing that, when two groups have different base rates for the outcome, you cannot build a single classifier that satisfies more than one of the standard fairness criteria at the same time. To be concrete: one criterion says the selection rate should be equal across groups; another says the error rates should be equal across groups within each truth value; and a third says the classifier's predicted probabilities should be accurate, or calibrated, within each group. The known result is that these criteria are incompatible in pairs once the base rates differ.

My machine-learning friend said the result is genuinely deep, in the same spirit as Arrow's theorem: it shows that fairness is not a problem to be solved by a cleverer algorithm, but an inescapable conflict of values that no technical fix can dissolve, so the impossibility is a real constraint on what any fair system can be.

The statistician replied that the result is completely trivial — it is just algebra. If the base rates differ, then the selection rate, which is a weighted average of the error rates weighted by the base rate, cannot be equal across groups while the error rates themselves are equal, unless the classifier is useless. That is a one-line calculation, not a deep theorem, and it is a bit like proving you cannot have a shape that is both a perfect square and a perfect circle. And because the mathematics is trivial, he added, the result tells you nothing about which criterion is the right one, so it is philosophically empty.

My machine-learning friend pushed back that triviality is not the same as emptiness: the whole point of the result is that the value-conflict is inescapable, and the theorem simply makes that explicit. The statistician answered that "making something explicit" is not the same as a deep result; you could have just said out loud, "you cannot have it both ways," and the theorem adds no content beyond restating the base-rate difference.
