**Question (20 marks).** In the General Scholium appended to the second edition of Newton's *Principia*, Newton argues that the system of the sun, planets, and comets "could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being," and concludes that this Being "governs all things, not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all." (a) Explain the philosophical role of the rejection of the world-soul in the Scholium's argument. (b) A commentator has argued that the Scholium and Newton's letter to Richard Bentley (1692/3) express "a single, continuous doctrine," because in that letter Newton called the idea that gravity is "innate, inherent, and essential to matter" "a great absurdity," and therefore "the world is not a self-sufficient system but requires an intelligent governor." Evaluate this claim of continuity.

**Student's answer.**

(a) Newton's rejection of the world-soul in the General Scholium is, first and foremost, a rejection of Spinoza's monism, on which God is identical with the nature of the world, together with the older Stoic doctrine of the world-soul. In both cases the divine is immanent in the world, and there is no independent, free governor standing above it. Newton's God, by contrast, is "Lord over all" (παντοκράτωρ), and "God" is, as Newton says, a relative word with a respect to servants; his dominion is free and independent. This is precisely the point at issue in the Scholium: the order of the system—the elliptical orbits, the regularity of the comets—cannot be explained by the intrinsic nature of matter alone, but requires an intelligent governor who is not the world itself.

(b) This reading is consistent with Newton's physics, and I think the commentator's claim of continuity is right. In the letter to Bentley, Newton wrote that it is "inconceivable that inanimate brute matter should, without the mediation of something else which is not material, operate upon and affect other matter without mutual contact," and that for gravity to be "innate, inherent, and essential to matter" is "so great an absurdity that I believe no man who has in philosophical matters any competent faculty of thinking can ever fall into it." He added that gravity must be caused by "an agent acting constantly according to certain laws," but left open "whether this agent be material or immaterial." In other words, Newton had already, in the 1690s, denied that matter can be self-sufficient, and the Scholium is simply the theological expression of a physical conviction that had already been formed.

It is true that the Scholium and the letter do not agree on every point: the Scholium speaks of a Being who creates and governs the world, whereas the letter leaves open the question of whether the mediating agent is material or immaterial. But this is a difference of emphasis, not of doctrine: in both texts the world is not a self-sufficient system, and its order requires something that is not matter. Newton's theology is therefore a single, continuous doctrine: the same God who governs the world in the Scholium is the agent who causes gravity in the letter.
