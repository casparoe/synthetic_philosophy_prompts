I'm moderating a Q&A for our department's graduate seminar series, and this term's guest is a philosopher of biology with a new book arguing that the etiological (historical) account of function — the Millikan line — is still the right way to understand biological function, against the causal-role / functionalist accounts that have been popular in the last couple of decades. I know the broad strokes — function as selection history versus function as a present causal role — but I keep getting confused about where the disagreement actually is, so I'd like questions that go beyond having her restate the view. Could you draft me a sequence of roughly eight to ten questions that press where the literature presses?

I'd like at least one question each on a few things I've been turning over. First, the pocket-organ problem: a trait that was once selected for F but now plays a different causal role R (I keep thinking of the coccyx, or of a trait that's been repurposed) — is the etiological account committed to saying it still "has" F even though it no longer plays that role, and if so, what's the difference between that and just saying it plays R? Second, whether selection for F is neccesary and sufficient for a trait having function F — and what happens to traits that persist by hitchhiking or drift. Third, the question I find hardest: why should history matter at all, given that the causal-role theorist says function is a present-tense causal role — what is the etiological account getting (I think it's the normative "should," but I'm not sure I've got it right) that the causal-role account is missing? And please end with one question about what evidence could count against the etiological view, since that's the part I don't fully understand yet.
