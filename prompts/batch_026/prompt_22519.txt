I've been slowly chewing through Tolstoy's What Is Art? for a reading group, and I keep getting stuck on the definition he gives in chapter 5. The passage:

"Art is a human activity, consisting in this, that one man consciously, by means of certain external signs, hands on to others feelings he has lived through, and that other people are infected by these feelings, and also experience them."

I get the broad strokes — art as emotional contagion, and the infectiousness of the work as the only reliable test of whether it's real art (which, full disclosure, is why a friend who reads a lot of rationalist blogs immediately started talking about it like an eval criterion, which was fun but also distracting). But the phrase "feelings he has lived through" is doing way more work than it looks, and I cant tell what he's actually claiming. Is "lived through" meant to distinguish a feeling that belongs to the artist's past experience from one they're just feeling in the moment, or one they're faking? Like, does a cellist playing a funeral march with no personal grief behind it fail to be making art on Tolstoy's account, or is it enough that the feeling is genuine while the performance happens? And what about fiction about experiences the author never had — I feel like he'd have to say the writer is re-creating some feeling they did live through, but the mapping from the character's experience to the author's seems lossy in a way that breaks his theory. I'd also be curious whether "consciously" in the first clause is load-bearing: does Tolstoy think a work that transmits feelings without the artist intending to transmit any particular feeling (minimal abstract art, or a song that hits you for reasons unrelated to the composer's headspace) is just not art at all on his theory? I'd apreciate a close reading of the passage rather than the standard summary — I mainly want to know which of these clauses are doing the real work in his argument and which are just rhetorical.
