Here's what this student (let's call them Sam) wrote:

Benatar's asymmetry argument starts from the observation that we value pleasure and pain in an asymmetric way. The absence of pain is a good thing, even if no one is there to experience that good — like if I don't have a headache, that's good, and it would still be good even if I never existed to enjoy the lack of headache. But the absence of pleasure is not a bad thing, unless someone is deprived of it. So if I never existed, there's no one deprived of any pleasure, so the absence of pleasure isn't a bad thing.

This matters for procreation because everyone who comes into existence will inevitably experience nontrivial pain. So bringing a person into existence guarantees the presence of pain, which is bad. But if we don't bring them into existence, we get the good of the absence of pain without the bad of the absence of pleasure, since no one is deprived of anything. So on balance, non-existence is better than existence, which is why procreation is always impermissible.

Benatar also addresses the obvious objection that lots of people think their lives are worth living. He says this is a confusion between a life being 'worth continuing' and a life being 'worth starting.' A life can be worth continuing for the person who already exists (they compare it to the alternative of death) but not worth starting, since a non-existent person has nothing to lose. He also invokes Parfit's non-identity problem to show that procreation can't really harm the specific child, since the child wouldn't exist at all if their parents had made different choices, so there's no counterfactual person to be worse off. And his overall conclusion is that all lives are bad, since there's always more pain than pleasure, so we should never bring anyone into existence.
