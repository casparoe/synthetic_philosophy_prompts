After a long argument with a friend, I would like you to adjudicate the following question. My friend has defended the "commensality" reading of biblical sacrifice — the view that the peace offerings, whose flesh the offerer himself ate, were essentially shared meals with the divine, and that this is what the sacrificial system was really about — and has accused the instrumentalist reading found in Maimonides (Guide for the Perplexed III.32), on the theory that the Torah's sacrificial commandments merely accommodated ancient Israel to its idolatrous milieu and gradually weaned it from foreign rites, of turning the biblical text into a mere pedagogical ruse and thereby stripping the sacrifices of any intrinsic theological significance. Please argue against this accusation: make the strongest case you can for why the instrumentalist reading need not be a deflation of the biblical sacrificial system, and why it can coexist with, or even deepen, the theological significance of the rites. In doing so, engage with at least one concrete difficulty that the commensality reading faces in light of the Torah's own restrictions on who may eat sacrificial flesh and where it may be consumed.
