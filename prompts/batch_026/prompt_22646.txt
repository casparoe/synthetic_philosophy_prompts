"Grice distinguishes between two types of implicature: conventional and conversational. Conventional implicature is tied to the conventional meaning of a specific lexical item, while conversational implicature is generated by the application of the Cooperative Principle and its maxims. As an example of conventional implicature, the sentence 'He is poor but happy' carries the implicature of contrast, which is attached to the word 'but.' As an example of conversational implicature, the sentence 'Some of the students passed the exam' carries the implicature that not all of the students passed, generated by the maxim of Quantity: the speaker is assumed to be providing as much information as required, so by saying 'some' rather than 'all,' the speaker implicates that not all passed. The conversational implicature is cancelable: one can say 'Some of the students passed, and in fact all of them did.'

The key distinction between the two types of implicature, however, is that conventional implicature is not cancelable, while conversational implicature is. This is the primary test for distinguishing between them. Additionally, conversational implicature is detachable, meaning that the same implicature can be expressed using different words, whereas conventional implicature is non-detachable."
