I sit on the multidisciplinary tumor board at a regional cancer center, and our bylaws committee has asked us to review how we run our weekly meetings. Our format is strictly sequential: the case is presented, and then the specialists speak in a fixed order — surgical oncology first, then medical oncology, then radiation oncology, and the pathologist last — with each later speaker hearing everyone before them. The medical oncologist happens to be the most senior person in the room, so she effectively sets the tone before the pathologist weighs in. Last month's agenda included a 58-year-old with stage II ascending colon cancer and a positive circumferential resection margin. The surgeon recommended restaging and watchful waiting; after hearing the surgeon's reasoning, the medical oncologist committed firmly to neoadjuvant chemotherapy; the radiation oncologist, whose own read of the imaging had been equivocal, simply aligned with the medical oncologist; and the pathologist, who had the most direct read on the margin status, spoke last and felt her input was largely foreclosed. Our co-chair suspects the sequential format is systematically producing convergence on whichever senior specialist commits first, and she wants us to pilot a change in which each specialist submits a short written recommendation before the meeting and only then discusses them together.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should engage the co-chair's concern seriously rather than just restating that independent review is better. In particular, I want the memo to grapple honestly with the strongest case for keeping the status quo: that a tumor board's job is to produce a single coordinated plan rather than to aggregate independent judgments, and that later specialists hearing earlier ones can genuinely pool information rather than merely anchor.
