I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, and I'm trying to sharpen my understanding of Grice's distinction between conversational implicature and conventional implicature for a paper I'm writing on speaker meaning. I can state the difference in broad strokes—conversational implicature is derived from the conversational maxims and the context, whereas conventional implicature is attached to the conventional meaning of certain expressions—but I'm less sure how to characterize the difference in terms of the mechanism by which the hearer recovers the implicated content. Please compare the two notions with respect to (i) the source of the inference, (ii) the role of mutual knowledge or convention, (iii) the status of cancellation, and (iv) the relation each has to Grice's intention-based account of speaker meaning.

I would especially like you to say whether conventional implicature is best understood as a kind of speaker meaning, as a separate dimension of content, or as something that merely constrains the interpretation of speaker meaning. In doing so, it would help to illustrate the answer with at least one example in which an expression such as “but” or “however” generates a conventional implicature that seems to resist cancellation without making the utterance infelicitous rather than false.
