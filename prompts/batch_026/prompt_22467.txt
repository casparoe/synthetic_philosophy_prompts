I'm a second-year PhD student in political philosophy, and I'm working on a chapter of my dissertation about the boundary problem for the demos — the question of how we are to determine who has standing to participate in a given democratic polity. The part I'm struggling with is what I've come to call the bootstrapping problem: the standard legitimacy claim is that a collective decision is legitimate only if it is made by, or at least includes, all those whose interests are significantly affected by it (the all-affected-interests principle), but it is not clear how we are to identify who is "affected" by a set of decisions without already knowing what those decisions will be, and what the decisions will be depends on who is in the demos. So the very procedure we would use to settle the boundaries of the demos seems to presuppose the answer to the question it is supposed to answer.

In my draft I try to break the circle by drawing a distinction between two senses of "affecting." On the procedural sense, a person is affected by a polity just in case she is subject to its effective political control or jurisdiction, regardless of the content of its decisions; on the substantive sense, a person is affected only if her interests are in fact impacted by the particular decisions the polity makes. I propose that we bootstrap the demos with the procedural criterion — those under effective control are included by default — and then use ordinary democratic deliberation to refine the boundaries in light of the substantive criterion, for instance by extending standing to non-residents whose interests are significantly at stake. My working claim is that this does not merely relocate the circularity, because the procedural criterion is non-democratic and so can be applied without presupposing the demos, whereas the substantive refinement is a legitimate use of the now-bootstrapped democratic procedure.

Could you comment on whether this resolution actually breaks the circle or just pushes it back a step, in particular whether the appeal to "effective political control" quietly imports a substantive (and contestable) political judgment that the procedural label is supposed to avoid? I'd also be grateful for any standard objections in the literature on democratic boundaries that my framing seems to dodge, and a sense of whether the procedural/substantive distinction is doing enough work to be worth keeping.
