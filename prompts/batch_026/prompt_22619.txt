I'm a TA for a graduate seminar in the history of political thought, and I've been asked to grade the take-home essays this week. Below is one of the questions as I assigned it, followed verbatim by a single student's submission. Please grade it.

Question: In about 300 words, explain Aquinas's position in Summa Theologica II-II, q. 42, a. 2, on disturbing a tyrannical government. Address (a) why he holds that a tyrannical rule is not a legitimate object of the sin of sedition; (b) what limitation he places on such disturbance; and (c) whether, on this basis, Aquinas licenses a private individual to kill a tyrant. Ground your answer in the text.

Student's answer:
Aquinas holds that a tyrannical government is not just, because, following the Philosopher (Politics III.5), it is directed not to the common good but to the private good of the ruler. Since the sin of sedition consists in breaking the unity of the multitude that exists for the common good, and a tyrannical rule lacks that unity for the common good, "there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind." He even says that it is the tyrant, rather than his subjects, who is guilty of sedition, because he stirs up discord among his subjects so that he may hold power more securely.

Aquinas does attach a limitation: it is not lawful to disturb the tyrant's rule "inordinately," that is, if the resulting disorder would inflict greater harm on the people than the tyranny itself. So long as the disturbance is proportionate and aimed at the common good, overthrowing the tyrant is not only permitted but praiseworthy, since it delivers the multitude from unjust rule.

On this basis Aquinas licenses private tyrannicide. Because the regime is unjust and the tyrant himself is the seditious party, any subject who kills the tyrant to free the country acts lawfully. There is no requirement that the killing be carried out by public authority; the wrongfulness of the regime is, on its own, sufficient to justify an individual's taking the tyrant's life.
