I'm writing a paper on the history of feminist epistemology, and I keep returning to Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz (1691). I've been discussing with a colleague how to characterize her defense of women's learning, and I find it hard to place. On one reading, her argument is conservative: she frames study as a means to religious virtue, insists that her learning is directed toward serving God rather than toward public life, and seems to accept that women's proper sphere is the convent or the home. On another reading, it is radical: she challenges the charge of "mujeres doctas" (learned women) as a double standard, appeals to a long list of historical women of intellect, and argues that women's capacity for reason is no less than men's.

Please describe the most important implications of these specific arguments — as distinct from the more general claim that "women are as intelligent as men" — for a contemporary philosopher trying to assess whether Sor Juana's case for women's education is best understood as a conservative argument (learning as a tool for religious virtue, compatible with women's subordinate social role) or a radical one (a genuine challenge to the epistemic authority that was denied to women). I'm especially interested in whether the two readings can be reconciled, and what the implications would be for how we cite her in modern debates about the social value of higher education for women.
