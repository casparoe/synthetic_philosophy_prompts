I'm preparing a piece on the two-envelope paradox and I want to be precise about how the standard resolutions relate to one another before I go to print. The first is a bookkeeping fix: the "switching gains you a quarter of what you hold" calculation treats the amount in your envelope, X, as a fixed number, but X is a random variable, so the 2X term (you held the smaller envelope) and the X/2 term (you held the larger) are different realizations, and once you condition correctly the expected value of switching equals the expected value of keeping. The second is a prior fix: the calculation silently assumes a uniform distribution over an unbounded range of possible amounts, which is an improper prior, so the "expected gain" is not a well-defined number at all.

Could you compare these two resolutions? I'm especially interested in whether they name the same underlying error or genuinely different ones, and in which direction (if either) one subsumes the other: does the bookkeeping fix already fully dissolve the paradox, making the improper-prior point a redundant aside, or is the improper-prior diagnosis the deeper one, with the bookkeeping fix merely patching a symptom? Please be precise about where the two accounts agree and where they diverge, and say which you judge to be the more fundamental explanation of why the naive argument is so persuasive.
