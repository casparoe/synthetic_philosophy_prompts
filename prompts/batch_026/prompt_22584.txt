I'm prepping a section of my decision theory seminar on the Pasadena game and I've hit a wall that I cant get past on my own, so I'd really value a second pair of eyes. The setup is the standard Nover and Hájek one: you flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if that happens on flip n you win 2 to the n divided by n dollars when n is odd and lose that same amount when n is even. When I actually crunch the expected value the straightforward way — (1/2^n) times the payoff, summed over n — I get 1 minus 1/2 plus 1/3 minus 1/4 and so on, which is just the alternating harmonic series, i.e. ln 2, about sixty-nine cents. And that's exactly what's bugging me, because if the expected value is only a bit under a dollar then the whole "paradox" seems to dissolve: sure, I'm not going to pay a fortune to play it, I'd cap my entry fee at a few cents and move on. St. Petersburg is easy to see as a problem since its expected value is literally infinite, but Pasadena just looks like a perfectly ordinary game with a small positive expected value.

To be fair I'm not oblivious to the fact that the series is conditionally convergent, and I can even see why a mathematician would flag that. But I keep feeling like that's a technical footnote rather than a real threat to the game's value, and I can't quite articulate why my intuition is wrong. I've bounced this off a couple of grad students and they just kept telling me the expected value is "undefined," which didnt help at all, because from where I'm sitting the calculation clearly hands me a number. So I guess my question is: is the "undefined expected value" refrain in the literature an overblown way of noting a convergence subtlety, or is there something I'm actually missing about why that ln 2 isn't the real value of the game?
