I've been going in circles on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis — the "is it a theorem, a definition, or a law of nature?" question that comes up a lot on the forums — and I keep landing on §1 of Turing's 1936 "On Computable Numbers," which is where the whole claim is actually staked out. Here's the relevant passage:

"We may compare a man in the process of computing a real number to a machine which is only capable of a finite number of conditions q1, q2, ..., qR which will be called 'm-configurations'. ... The 'scanned symbol' is the only one of which the machine is, so to speak, 'directly aware'. ... In some of the configurations in which the scanned square is blank (i.e. bears no symbol) the machine writes down a new symbol on the scanned square: in other configurations it erases the scanned symbol. The machine may also change the square which is being scanned, but only by shifting it one place to right or left. In addition to any of these operations the m-configuration may be changed. ... It is my contention that these operations include all those which are used in the computation of a number. The defence of this contention will be easier when the theory of the machines is familiar to the reader."

Two things I want help untangling. First, what is the logical status of that "contention" — that the listed operations (write or erase a symbol, shift the scanned square by one, change the m-configuration) are exhaustive of every operation used in computing a number? Is Turing stipulating a definition of "computation" (making the thesis true by fiat), asserting an empirical or psychological claim (he grounds the model, elsewhere in §1, in "the fact that the human memory is necessarily limited," which would make it in principle falsifiable), claiming a mathematical theorem, or something else entirely? Second, and this is where I keep losing the thread: how does the status of that single contention determine the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis as a whole? If the contention is an empirical generalization about human calculators, is the thesis thereby a contingent empirical claim rather than the a priori adequacy claim it's usually presented as?
