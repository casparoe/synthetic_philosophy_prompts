The question was: "In about two paragraphs: (a) explain what higher-order evidence is and why a case like Hypoxia — Aisha, the fuel gauges, and the risk of hypoxia — generates a genuine puzzle rather than a simple matter of updating; and (b) evaluate the 'no relevance' objection. Be precise about what the objection shows, what it does not show, and whether it dissolves the puzzle."

The student's answer was: "Higher-order evidence is evidence that does not bear directly on the first-order proposition at issue but instead bears on the believer's epistemic situation — for example, on whether her rational capacities are functioning properly or on whether her first-order evidence is as good as she thinks it is. The standard illustration is Hypoxia: Aisha is flying an unpressurized plane and works out, from the gauges and the maps, that with high probability she has enough fuel (G) to reach Hawaii; she then notices she is at an altitude where pilots making exactly this kind of calculation get the right answer only about half the time. The higher-order evidence is the fact of her likely hypoxia, and it says nothing directly about the fuel. The puzzle is that two things seem true at once. First, Aisha's first-order evidence strongly supports G, so it looks like she should be highly confident of G. Second, it seems reckless — and so irrational — for her to keep that high confidence once she realizes she may be cognitively impaired. This is also a case of epistemic akrasia: if Aisha keeps her high confidence in G while judging that she is likely irrational to hold it, she holds a belief she judges to be unsupported. I think the 'no relevance' objection dissolves the puzzle. The higher-order evidence is irrelevant to G — whether Aisha is hypoxic has nothing to do with whether the tank is full — so it has no bearing on what she rationally ought to be confident of, and her total evidence still strongly supports G. Aisha should therefore keep her high confidence, and the intuition that she ought to lower it was mistaken. This also shows that epistemic akrasia is not really a live possibility in these cases: once you see that the higher-order evidence is irrelevant, there is no reason to think Aisha's belief is unsupported, so she is not akratic, and the phenomenon itself dissolves."
