I'm in a constitutional theory seminar this term and the Ulysses contract / precommitment stuff is getting more confusing the more we talk about it. I get the basic Odysseus analogy: the present self binds the future self so the future self can't give in to temptation. But in class we started applying it to constitutions, and I'm not sure what exactly the analogy is doing when the future self is a later generation that might reasonably judge the present generation's rules to be bad. I was discussing with my freinds whether a supermajority amendment rule is just a Ulysses contract for a whole polity, or whether it's something different because future voters can in principle vote themselves out of it.

Could you describe the most important implications of Ulysses contracts for constitutional precommitment in cases where the future preference change is not just a temporary weakness of will but a plausible moral or political disagreement? I'd especially like the implications for whether a constitution can legitimatly bind a future generation, when precommitment becomes paternalistic, and what it means if the rope is an amendment rule that is hard but not impossible to undo. I don't need a survey of the whole literature, just the sharp points I can use in a seminar discussion.
