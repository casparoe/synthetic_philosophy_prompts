I'm taking a graduate seminar in comparative philosophy of religion, and we've just finished a unit on Madhyamaka Buddhism, but I keep coming back to a puzzle I can't quite resolve, and I'd like a careful essay to help me think it through. The puzzle is this. Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (the catuṣkoṭi) denies all four horns of the question "Does X exist?": X exists, X does not exist, X both exists and does not exist, and X neither exists nor does not exist. If the whole point of the Madhyamaka is to reject every one of these four, then what exactly is left? It looks as though a "fifth" position has quietly been smuggled in — the position that "none of the four is correct" — and if that fifth position is itself a claim about the way things are, then Nāgārjuna seems to have committed himself to a kind of nihilism, or at least to a "no-thesis" that is itself a thesis. A friend of mine who is a practitioner keeps insisting that "emptiness is not a view, it's the seeing-through of views," which I find either profound or evasive, and I can't tell which.

The complication is that Nāgārjuna explicitly says emptiness (śūnyatā) is itself empty — the "emptiness of emptiness" — which is supposed to block the reification of emptiness into a "thing" or a "view," but it's not obvious that this avoids an infinite regress (the emptiness of the emptiness of emptiness, and so on) or that it keeps the doctrine from collapsing into quietism on one side or into a positive metaphysics in disguise on the other. I'd like you to write an essay that takes this controversy seriously: is the rejection of all four horns of the tetralemma a genuine apophatic stance that commits Nāgārjuna to no positive doctrine at all, or does it amount to a "fifth" nihilistic or quietist position that is itself a reified "view" of emptiness? Weigh the main arguments on both sides, including the role of the two-truths distinction (conventional versus ultimate truth) and the "emptiness of emptiness" move, and then reach and justify a clear conclusion. Don't end on a non-committal note; I want to know which side you think is right and why, even if you think the answer is nuanced. Please write it so that a reader who knows the basics of Madhyamaka can follow it without looking anything up, and feel free to reference the relevant arguments and texts where they matter, but don't rely on me being able to verify a specific quotation.
