Having recently retired from a career in physics, I have only in the past year begun to read philosophy seriously, and I find myself applying my old habit of asking whether a distinction is actually doing any work. I have been reading Bernard Williams's "Internal and External Reasons" and the debate it spawned. Williams's internalist constraint holds that for something to count as a reason for a particular agent, it must be reachable by a "sound deliberative route" from that agent's subjective motivational set. I would like you to make the strongest case you can that this constraint is self-undermining: that the subjective motivational set, once it is broad enough to include the values, commitments, and dispositions that sound deliberation is supposed to draw on, becomes so expansive that the constraint no longer excludes anything, and that the appeal to "sound" deliberation quietly imports a normative standard that can only be supplied by external reasons. Please argue this carefully and in detail, and if you think the argument ultimately fails, say so and identify precisely where it breaks down.
