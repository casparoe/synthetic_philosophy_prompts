I'm a high-school junior, and I run a small after-school reading group that has recently been working through the anthropic-reasoning literature — the Doomsday argument, the self-sampling assumption, and the self-indication assumption all came up in a long debate about whether we should expect to be living in a simulation. Next month we're hosting a guest speaker, a philosopher who has written in favor of the self-indication assumption and who defends the view that SIA is the correct self-locating principle, that it dissolves the Doomsday argument, and that it therefore defuses the "presumptuous philosopher" objection. I'm in charge of the Q&A, and I'm worried the discussion will flatten into "so how many humans will there be in the end?" I'd like you to draft eight to ten questions that force the guest to do the actual work of the position rather than simply restate it.

Please make sure there is at least one question each on: (i) the exact point at which the self-indication and self-sampling assumptions diverge, and why SIA makes the mere fact of one's own existence count as evidence for a larger total number of observers rather than against it; (ii) how SIA is supposed to dissolve the presumptuous philosopher, that is, why the inference no longer yields a different, more pessimistic answer the earlier in history you perform the calculation; (iii) the reference-class problem, given that SIA still has to say which set of observers you are treating yourself as a sample of; and (iv) the pressure from Boltzmann brains and simulated observers, since a principle that weights toward worlds with more observers seems to imply that we should expect to be one of the vast majority of such atypical observers. For each of these, please attach a follow-up question keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give.

Two further constraints. First, the guest is very good at deflecting with the line "SIA is a principle about how to weight evidence, not a prediction about the future," and I want the sequence of questions to make that deflection progressively harder to deploy. Second, please end with a single question about what empirical evidence — if any — could count against SIA; and since the guest also insists that the argument should not be read as assuming a fixed total number of humans, make sure at least one earlier question presses on what the principle says when the future is genuinely open rather than a fixed pool to be sampled from.
