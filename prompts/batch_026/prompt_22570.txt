The problem-set question was worth ten points and read as follows:

"Question: In a short essay, explain the combination problem for panpsychism and evaluate one proposed response to it. (10 points.)"

The student's answer, reproduced verbatim, is:

"Panpsychism is the view that consciousness, or at least some rudimentary form of experience, is not something that emerges only in complex organisms but is a fundamental feature of the natural world, present in some form in the most basic constituents of reality. On this view, the question is not whether consciousness arises from non-conscious matter, but how the many micro-experiences associated with fundamental entities give rise to the unified, rich experiences we find in human and animal minds.

This is the combination problem, which I take to be the central objection to panpsychism. The difficulty is that micro-experiences, if they exist, would be very different in kind from macro-experiences: they would be simple, perhaps qualia-like, and numerous, whereas our experiences are unified, structured, and possess a certain perspectival character. There is no obvious principle by which a collection of simple experiences combines into a single experience, and the structural differences between the two levels seem to require more than mere aggregation. As far as I can tell, the combination problem is the problem of how consciousness arises from non-conscious physical matter, and it is this that makes panpsychism look less like a solution to the problem of consciousness and more like a restatement of it.

One proposed response is cosmopsychism. On this view, the universe as a whole is conscious, and the minds of individual organisms are not fundamental units that must be combined but localized aspects or modes of a single cosmic consciousness. Because the direction of explanation is reversed — from the macro to the micro rather than from the micro to the macro — cosmopsychism dissolves the combination problem: there is no need to explain how many simple experiences combine into one, since there is only one experience, the cosmic one, from which individual minds derive. I think this is a promising move. Of course, one then has to say something about how a single cosmic consciousness gives rise to many apparently independent minds, but that seems to me a different and more tractable question than the original one."
