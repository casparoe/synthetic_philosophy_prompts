I'm putting together the core reading for a graduate seminar I'm teaching on philosophy of biology, and I keep returning to the etiological-versus-causal-role dispute over biological function — the etiological account associated with Millikan, on the one hand, and the causal-role account associated with Behe, on the other — which I regard as one of the most underappreciated and genuinely live controversies in the field. I would like you to write an essay that takes a real side in this debate and defends it, rather than surveying it from a neutral distance. Weigh the main arguments on both sides: on the etiological side, the appeal to selection history and the way the account is supposed to handle malfunction and to give function its explanatory role; on the causal-role side, the appeal to fitness contribution and the way the account is supposed to avoid the trouble that etiological accounts run into over design that is not the product of selection and over very recent or apparently accidental traits.

I am particularly interested in how each account fares on the case of malfunction and on cases in which a trait appears designed but selection has not clearly shaped it, since I think those are where the two accounts genuinely diverge in a way that matters. Please do not end on a hedge: I want a clear verdict that you then justify, and that verdict may be a qualified one — for instance, that the etiological account is right about one thing and the causal-role account about another — provided you can defend the split.
