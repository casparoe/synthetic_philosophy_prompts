I've been working through a puzzle in collective decision-making on my own and I'd like help untangling it. Suppose a jury has to decide whether a defendant is guilty, and the case turns on two premises: the defendant committed the act, and the act was intentional. The jury votes on each premise separately, and both pass by a majority. But when the jury votes on the overall verdict of guilt, it fails by a majority — even though the two premises logically entail the verdict. I understand this is sometimes called the discursive dilemma or the doctrinal paradox. The standard fix I've heard of is to vote on the premises and then let logic determine the verdict, rather than voting on the verdict directly. But I've also heard that this fix has a problem: the verdict can be broken down into premises in more than one logically valid way, and different breakdowns can give different collective outcomes even from the same individual votes. Can you explain the dilemma clearly, and then assess whether the premise-based fix actually resolves the problem or just relocates it, given that the choice of which premises to vote on seems to matter?
