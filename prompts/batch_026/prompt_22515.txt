I'm in a social ontology PhD program, and two of my seminar mates got into it after we read Lewis's Convention. Their exchange went: (1) Ana said that Lewis's 'non-accidental' condition is basically satisfied by evolutionary stability: if a regularity in a coordination game is maintained by cultural selection or repeated reinforcement, then it isn't just a coincidence, so culturally evolved regularities can be Lewisian conventions. (2) Ben said no, Lewis's non-accidental condition isn't just causal non-accidentality; it's tied to the agents' common interest and mutual expectations. A population can have a stable regularity that is causally explained by selection, but if nobody expects it as the way things are done, it's not a convention. (3) Ana replied that expectations can be selected too: if agents who expect the stable regularity and conform when they expect it do better, then the expectation structure can evolve alongside the behavior. (4) Ben said that's circular: to say the regularity is stable because people expect it is already to assume the convention exists, and Lewis's account is supposed to explain what a convention is, not just to re-describe a stable population. (5) Ana said that's not circular, it's diachronic: selection can first produce a regularity, then produce expectations about it, and once the expectation loop is in place the regularity meets Lewis's conditions. I wasnt taking sides, but I keep thinking there's a difference between a norm being stable and a norm being the kind of thing Lewis means by a convention. Can you adjudicate this move by move? I want to know who's right about which specific point, not just who wins overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
