I've been thinking for a while about a puzzle I've seen people call the boundary problem, and I'd like your help working through it. The puzzle, as I understand it, is this: a democracy is supposed to be the rule of the people, but it is not obvious who "the people" are, because the decision about who belongs to the demos is itself a political decision, and that decision has to be made by someone — presumably by the demos itself. It is circular: the people decide who the people are. I keep coming back to this puzzle because it strikes me as more than an abstract philosophical curiosity; it seems to have real, concrete implications for how we should think about who gets a voice in a given political community, and I find myself unable to give a satisfactory account of what those implications are.

A friend of mine has lived in a small, wealthy Gulf state his entire life. His parents came as construction workers in the early 1970s; he was born there, he grew up there, and he is now in his fifties. He and his family, however, are not citizens. They cannot vote, cannot hold public office, and their right to reside in the country is technically renewable at the discretion of the state. We were having dinner last week and he brought the puzzle up again, and he asked me to think through what the boundary problem specifically implies for his situation and for the political system of the country in which he lives. I wasn't able to do a good job of it, so I'm turning to you.

Could you please describe the most important implications of the boundary problem for this case? I would like you to (1) briefly state the puzzle in your own words, (2) lay out the main ways in which people try to resolve it — for example, by appeal to territorial connection, to cultural or national identity, to the fact of an existing democratic order, or to the consent of the governed — and (3) for each of these resolutions, say what it specifically implies for the multi-generational foreign residents in this country, including the question of who gets to decide the rules of membership in the first place. Please don't give a general discussion of the boundary problem in the abstract; I want you to work through the specifics of this case, including the fact that the current demos is a small group governing a large non-citizen majority, and the fact that any reform of who gets to vote would itself be decided by the very group whose membership is in question.
