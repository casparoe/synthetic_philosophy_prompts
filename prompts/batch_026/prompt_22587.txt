I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been reading A.J. Ayer's Language, Truth and Logic as part of a self-directed reading list on the history of analytic philosophy. I'm fairly comfortable with the core of the verification principle as he laid it out in 1936: a statement is cognitively meaningful only if it is analytically true or empirically verifiable in principle, which is why he dismissed statements like "God exists" as neither true nor false but simply meaningless. What I find myself stuck on is how to apply this to questions that weren't really on the table when he wrote, in particular the possibility that a person's mental states, memories, or personality could be uploaded to a different substrate — say, a sufficiently detailed digital simulation running on hardware — and continue to function as that person. Given his framework, I'd be curious how you think Ayer would have analyzed the claim "I will survive after my biological death by being uploaded," and more generally whether he would have treated the question of personal identity through substrate change as a meaningful philosophical question at all, or as another example of pseudo-problems generated by the misuse of words. I'm specifically interested in whether you think his verificationist criterion would have led him to dismiss the survival question the same way he dismissed immortality of the soul, or whether the empirical, testable character of uploading — the fact that we could in principle build the system and observe its behavior — would have forced him to engage with the identity question on different terms, for instance by locating the meaning of "survival" in publicly observable behavioral and functional criteria rather than in any private, non-observable essence.
