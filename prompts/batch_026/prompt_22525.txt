I'm building a little belief-updating pipeline for a research project, and I keep getting stuck on the old evidence problem in Bayesian epistemology. In my case I have historical logs that were already collected before we even thought up the competing hypotheses, and I want to use them to rank the hypotheses after the fact. I know the naive move is to write P(H|E) ∝ P(E|H)P(H), but if E is old and was already in the background knowledge when H was proposed, I'm not sure whether P(E|H) is even the right thing to condition on. It feels like if the background knowledge already makes E almost certain, then every hypothesis looks equally good, but I've also herd people say "explanatory power" or "confirmation from a different epistemic state" can rescue it, which feels a bit hand-wavy to me.

Can you describe the most important implications of this old-evidence problem for whether it's legitimate to update on already-known data, and what I should be checking before my pipeline just pretends that the historical logs are fresh evidence? I'm less interested in a formal proof and more in the practical consequences: when is it okay to use old data, when does "explanatory power" become a smuggled-in assumption, and what failure modes should I expect in a model selection system that scores hypotheses after the fact?
