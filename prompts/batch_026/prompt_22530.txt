I'm a philosophy of mind PhD student and I'm in a reading group that just got through Chalmers's "The Combination Problem for Panpsychism," and two of my classmates, Ravi and Mia, got into it after the session and I honestly couldnt referee it. Here's roughly how it went.

Mia opened by saying the combination problem is a real reductio of panpsychism: if electrons and quarks have their own little experiences, then we have to explain how the ~10^28 of them in my brain add up to my one unified experience of, say, the redness of a stoplight, and there's no non-mysterious way to merge that many separate subjects into one. So panpsychism hasn't solved the hard problem, it's just relocated it and made it worse, because now there are billions of subjects to fuse instead of one non-conscious substrate to explain. Ravi said that's a strawman. The combination problem is the same sort of explanatory gap physicalism already has: physicalism has to explain how a non-conscious arrangement of neurons yields one unified experience, and panpsychism has to explain how a conscious arrangement of micro-subjects yields one unified experience. The "many parts, one subject" structure is in both, so panpsychism isn't uniquely penalized, and if anything its version is easier because the ingredients are already conscious, so you're not conjuring consciousness out of nothing, just integrating it.

Mia pushed back: no, the structure is categorically different. In physicalism the parts were never conscious, so there's no pre-existing subject that has to stop being a subject. In panpsychism you start with billions of already-conscious subjects and have to explain how they stop being billions and become one. That's the subject-combination problem, and it's a genuinely new obstacle physicalism simply doesn't face, because its parts are unconscious. So it's not "the same gap at a lower level," it's an additional gap on top of the same old one. Ravi replied that's just relabeling. The core explanatory work in both cases is "how does a multiplicity yield a unity of experience?" And Chalmers himself lists subversions of the problem, like the idea that the fundamental entities aren't really separate subjects but aspects of one underlying field, or that integration is a fundamental relation you don't have to explain from scratch. Those subversions are available to the panpsychist precisely because the problem is structural (a "how do the parts bind" problem, sort of like the binding problem in neuroscience), not a brute unsolvable gap. If the panpsychist can give a principled account of integration, the physicalist can give a parallel one, so the burden is symmetric.

Mia said even granting all that, the subversions are ad hoc, since "the micro-subjects aren't really separate" or "integration is fundamental" just pushes the mystery one step back. And there's a second, independent strike Ravi's symmetry argument ignores, the epistemic one: we can't "am" a quark, meaning we can't take the first-person perspective of a micro-subject to check that its experience is even what the theory says it is, so we can't verify the ingredients exist, let alone how they combine. That's a separate problem from the structural one. Ravi's last point was that the "amming" objection cuts both ways, since we can't take the perspective of a single neuron in that sense either, and the inaccessibility of the micro-level is just the price of any microphysical theory; we infer electrons' properties mathematically and never "am" one. So that epistemic inaccessibility isn't unique to panpsychism.
