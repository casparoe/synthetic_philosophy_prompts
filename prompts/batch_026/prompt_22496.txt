I just had a long argument with a friend about Moore's paradox — specifically, about what exactly makes the sentence "It's raining, but I don't believe it is" absurd, while the sentence "It's raining, but I don't know it is" is perfectly fine. My friend (let's call her Alice) says that the absurdity of the first sentence comes from a contradiction in the speaker's epistemic position: when the speaker asserts "It's raining," they are in a position to know that it's raining, but then they claim not to believe it, which is inconsistent with being in a position to know it. I pushed back by noting that the second sentence has the same structure — the speaker is in a position to know that it's raining and claims not to know it — so if the absurdity is a matter of epistemic incoherence, the second sentence should be equally absurd, which it isn't. Alice responded that the difference is that "not knowing" is compatible with being in a position to know (I can be in a position to know something without actually knowing it), but "not believing" is not compatible with asserting: when I assert P, I thereby express a belief in P, so the second conjunct directly contradicts what the act of assertion expresses. I said that this is a speech-act account, not an epistemic account, and that the absurdity is then not a feature of the sentence itself but of the context in which it's uttered — which seems to undermine the idea that Moore's paradox is about the sentence itself rather than the context. Alice conceded this was a fair point but argued that the alternatives are worse: either the absurdity is a logical contradiction (which it isn't, since P and not-Bel(P) is logically consistent) or there is no absurdity at all (which is implausible), so the speech-act account is the least bad option. Can you adjudicate this disagreement move by move — who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, are both parties missing?
