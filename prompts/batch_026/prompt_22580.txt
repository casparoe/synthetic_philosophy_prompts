I chair the risk committee of a company developing a reusable orbital launch vehicle, and we're about to make a go/no-go decision on the first flight of the new vehicle. The board is split, and I want the committee's written opinion to be clear about which decision-theoretic framework we're actually using before we vote. The expected-value case is strong: the flight has about a 97% chance of success, which would certify the vehicle and unlock a backlog of launches worth roughly two billion dollars in present value. The remaining 3% breaks down into a 2% chance of a partial loss (around fifty million dollars) and a 1% chance of a catastrophic failure in which the vehicle comes down in a way that causes a major ground accident and triggers a multi-year regulatory shutdown, with direct costs on the order of half a billion dollars and reputational damage we can't easily price.

Computed with the board's own utility function — which is concave, so it already encodes risk aversion in the von Neumann–Morgenstern sense — the expected value of the flight is comfortably positive, and the "go" camp points to this. The "no-go" camp, however, is leaning on a consultant we've retained, who is urging us to adopt a risk-weighted expected utility rule for this particular decision. Specifically, she proposes that we evaluate the flight not by plain expected utility but by a rule that discounts the tail of the outcome distribution — she suggests a conditional value-at-risk threshold at the fifth percentile — and she argues that under such a rule the flight fails. Her central worry is that 1% catastrophic scenario. I've been reading a fair amount of the decision-theory literature on this (I've gone back and forth on it with a colleague who's an actuary), and the subtlety I keep getting asked about is this: the board's utility function is already concave, so it already does some of the work of risk aversion. Is the consultant's risk-weighting a legitimate additional consideration, or is it double-counting risk that the concave utility has already captured? And if it is legitimate, where is the principled line between "the utility function does its job" and "we need a separate tail-risk rule"?

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — go, no-go, or go with specific named mitigations — with a time frame, and it should engage the consultant's position seriously rather than just restating the expected-value calculation. I'd also like it to say something honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side, because I expect the board to push back either way.
