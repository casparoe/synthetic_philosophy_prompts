I am working on a novel set in a near-future tribunal, where a regulatory panel must decide whether an artificial agent that reports aversive states during training should be granted a provisional welfare status. For a key scene, I would like a dialogue between two philosophers: one a sophisticated functionalist who thinks mental kinds such as pain are multiply realizable and can be identified by their causal role, and the other a proponent of a causal-historical or biological-naturalist view who thinks that to be in pain is not merely to occupy a pain-like causal role but to be connected, in an appropriate historical or evolutionary way, to the natural kind of pain.

Please write the dialogue so that both speakers are equally competent and neither is a strawman. They should respond to each other’s actual points, especially the tension between multiple realizability and the origin-story requirement: if a system has never undergone the evolutionary history of pain but now plays the same causal role as a human in pain, should we call its state pain? I want the exchange to move from legal classification to a deeper disagreement about what counts as an explanation of mental-kind identity, and it should include at least one moment in which they recognize that part of their dispute may be verbal while another part remains substantive. The tone should be literary and polished, suitable for a novel, but philosophically precise enough that a reader can follow the technical stakes.
