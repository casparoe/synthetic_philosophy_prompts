I am a high school student writing a short philosophy paper on accuracy-first epistemology, and I would like comments on the following draft, especially on whether the central objection is accurate.

Accuracy-first epistemology holds that the fundamental epistemic value is truth-directedness, and that other epistemic virtues are derivative. A common way to cash this out is to evaluate credences with a scoring rule that rewards accuracy and punishes inaccuracy. If a scoring rule is proper, then, for a given true probability distribution, the expected score is maximized when the agent’s credences match that true distribution. This seems to support probabilism, the view that rational credences must obey the probability axioms. I want to argue that this support is weaker than it looks, because the argument tacitly assumes a unique scoring rule. The Brier score is proper, but so are the logarithmic score and the spherical score, and they differ in how strongly they penalize confident errors. If a rational agent has not yet chosen among proper scoring rules, then there may be no unique accuracy-maximizing credal state. So accuracy-first epistemology may motivate holding graded credences rather than all-or-nothing beliefs, but it may not by itself settle whether rationality requires full probabilistic coherence.

Please comment on whether this objection to probabilism is sound, and whether I need to address the possibility that different proper scoring rules can nonetheless have the same maximizer for a fixed true probability distribution.
