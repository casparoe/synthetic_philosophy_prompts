I'm taking a philosophy elective this semester, and in our unit on free will, two of my classmates, Maya and Dev, got into a long argument about a thought experiment that I couldn't follow to the end. I'd like you to referee it, move by move.

Here's the thought experiment, as Maya set it up: Sam is deciding whether to donate to a charity. There's another person, Black, who really wants Sam to donate. Black has a device that can take over Sam's brain and make him donate if Sam shows any sign of deciding not to. But Sam, on his own, decides to donate, so Black never has to use the device. The question: did Sam donate freely?

The argument went like this:

(1) Maya: Sam donated freely. Nothing actually interfered with his decision; he had full control. The fact that Black *could* have intervened but didn't is irrelevant — what matters is only the absence of actual interference. So freedom does not require the ability to do otherwise; it requires only the absence of actual interference. Sam's inability to have decided otherwise (because Black would have intervened) is a counterfactual fact about the world, not a fact about Sam's decision.

(2) Dev: The case is a "luck case." Sam is free only by luck — the luck that he happened to decide to donate on his own. If he had decided not to donate, Black would have intervened. So Sam's freedom depends on a contingent fact (that Black didn't intervene) that is entirely outside Sam's control. The "ability to do otherwise" is a way of saying that freedom doesn't depend on external contingencies. So the case doesn't refute the idea that freedom requires the ability to do otherwise; it just shows that Sam's freedom is luck-dependent, which is a less robust kind of freedom.

(3) Maya: The luck objection is a red herring. The question is whether Sam's decision was free *in the moment it was made*, and at that moment, nothing was interfering. If we require that freedom not depend on any external contingency, we'll end up saying no one is ever free, because every decision depends on external contingencies (the laws of physics, the state of the brain, and so on). The luck objection, taken to its logical conclusion, leads to a radical skepticism about freedom that nobody wants to accept.

(4) Dev: Maya is mischaracterizing the point. The objection is not that freedom requires no external contingency (that's too strong). The objection is that the *specific* contingency here — a ready-and-willing intervener — is a special kind that *does* matter. It means that Sam's decision is "overridden" if Sam makes a different decision, which is a special kind of override that matters for freedom. In other words, Sam's decision is not "robust" — it depends on a specific set of circumstances that could easily have been different. A robust notion of freedom requires robust decisions.

(5) Maya: Fragility is not unfreedom. Many free decisions are fragile. If I'm deciding between an apple and an orange, and I pick the apple, my decision is "fragile" in the sense that a slight change (the apple being rotten) would have led me to pick the orange. But that doesn't mean my decision wasn't free. The fragility of Sam's decision doesn't show that it wasn't free; it just shows that it was made in particular circumstances.
