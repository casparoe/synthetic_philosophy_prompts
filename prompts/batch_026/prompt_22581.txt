I'm prepping a Q&A for a panel at our philosophy department's colloquium on 19th-century American political thought, and the guest is a political theorist who just put out a book arguing Douglass was right to abandon his early Garrisonian position — that the Constitution was, as a document, genuinely anti-slavery from the start, and that the Fugitive Slave and three-fifths clauses don't do what the radical abolitionists thought they did. He's the type who, when you push, just keeps retreating to the "ship and compass" move: the Constitution can be right while the Government that's been steering by it is wrong. I want six to eight questions that press on the actual content rather than have him restate the thesis, with at least one on the 1851 "Change of Opinion Announced" editorial and wheter it marks a real change of mind or just a change in what he was asking, one on how a strictly text-only reading is supposed to get around the the Fugitive Slave and three-fifths clauses, and one on his point that the Convention debates were kept secret and only published decades later — and for each of those three, a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. Make the "ship and compass" retreat progressively harder to lean on as the questions go on, and close with one question about what evidence, a passage or a fact, could genuinely count against the pro-Douglass reading.
