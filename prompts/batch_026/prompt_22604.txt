I am a junior in high school, and I have been reading, largely on my own, about John von Neumann's work on self-replicating automata (his 1948 lecture "The General and Logical Theory of Automata," published in 1951) and about his posthumous book "The Computer and the Brain." I am writing a term paper for an independent study on the history of ideas in computer science, and I have become increasingly interested in the modern "control problem" or "alignment problem" for artificial intelligence — the worry that a self-improving or self-replicating AI might come to pursue goals that are not aligned with human values, and that we might be unable to control or "turn off" such a system once it becomes sufficiently capable. I was discussing this with a few friends, and we could not agree on what von Neumann would have thought: one friend, who has also read some of von Neumann, insists that von Neumann would have dismissed the control problem as a non-issue, because he was so optimistic about the benevolence and controllability of machines; another friend argues that von Neumann, given his background in game theory and his close involvement with the military applications of his own work, would have been deeply pessimistic and would have regarded self-replicating technology as inherently dangerous.

I am not sure whom to believe, and I would like a careful, well-reasoned prediction of what von Neumann's actual position would have been, rather than a projection of contemporary views onto him. Specifically, I would like you to argue from von Neumann's known views — his discussions of machine intelligence in "The Computer and the Brain," his theory of self-replicating automata, his views on the relationship between the brain and the computer, and his broader intellectual character — to predict what he would have thought about (a) whether the control or alignment problem for self-improving AI is a genuine and serious problem, (b) whether he would have regarded it as a technical engineering problem or as a conceptual or philosophical one, and (c) what specific design principles or safeguards, if any, he would have recommended for self-replicating systems. I am especially curious whether von Neumann would have seen the control problem as a special case of a more general problem he already confronted in his automata theory — namely, the problem of specifying the "program" or "purpose" that a self-replicating system carries and reproduces — and whether he would have thought that this problem is solvable in principle or is in some sense fundamentally unsolvable. Please make clear where you are drawing on specific von Neumann texts or documented views and where you are extrapolating, and please do not simply report what modern AI safety researchers think.
