I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of biology this term, and two of my students got into a rather heated exchange during last week's discussion of Popper. I was in the room but didn't intervene, and I've been thinking about it all week. I'd like to lay out what they said as accurately as I can and ask you to adjudicate the argument move by move, since I'm not entirely sure I'm seeing the whole shape of the disagreement clearly.

Elena opened by saying that Popper's classification of Darwinism as a "metaphysical research programme" is self-contradictory: Popper's central methodological commitment is that a theory is scientific only if it is falsifiable, but if Darwinism is a "metaphysical research programme," then by Popper's own terminology it is not a scientific theory. So either Popper is using "metaphysical" in a sense that doesn't carry the pejorative weight we normally give it, in which case the label is misleading and obscures the real point, or he is actually saying Darwinism is unscientific, which contradicts everything else he says. Marcus replied that neither alternative is right. He said Popper is making a precise methodological point: the general theory of natural selection, as such, cannot be directly falsified, because any observed pattern in a population can be interpreted as the result of selection. So the general theory functions not as a falsifiable hypothesis but as a methodological principle — a "research programme" that tells scientists what kinds of questions to ask, what evidence to look for, and how to interpret the results. The actual scientific, falsifiable content is in the specific hypotheses generated under its guidance, such as the claim that the peppered moth's dark coloration is selected for in soot-covered environments. So Darwinism is scientific in its specific applications, but the general theory is a metaphysical research programme that guides those applications. This is not a contradiction, Marcus said; it's a distinction between the guiding principle and the testable hypotheses it generates. Elena pushed back, saying that this just relabels the problem: if the general theory of natural selection is the thing that does the explanatory work, and it is not itself falsifiable, then the theory that actually explains evolution is not scientific. The specific hypotheses, like the peppered moth case, are only testable because the general theory tells you what to look for, so the general theory is doing the real work, and it is the general theory that is not falsifiable. Popper is just moving the unfalsifiable part to a higher level and calling it a "research programme" to avoid the implication. Marcus answered that the general theory does not do the explanatory work by itself; it provides the framework within which specific hypotheses are generated and tested, and the explanatory work is done by the specific, falsifiable hypotheses. He compared the general theory to the principle of induction, which is not itself a falsifiable hypothesis but is the principle that makes it possible to formulate and test falsifiable hypotheses. Popper's point, Marcus said, is precisely that this is what makes Darwinism scientific: it is not a dogma that explains everything, but a methodological principle that generates testable predictions and subjects them to rigorous scrutiny.

Elena then said that the analogy to induction was doing a lot of work, and she was not sure it held. Induction is a method of inference, not a theory about the world, whereas Darwinism is a theory about the world — it makes claims about what actually happens in nature. Calling it a "metaphysical research programme" seems to conflate a theory about the world with a method of inference. And if we accept that the general theory of natural selection is a "metaphysical research programme" in Popper's sense, then we have to accept that the core explanatory claim of evolutionary biology is not a scientific hypothesis, which seems to her to be a reductio of Popper's own demarcation criterion, not a vindication of it. Marcus replied that she was still assuming that "metaphysical research programme" means "unscientific" or "not a theory about the world." Popper's point is more subtle, he said. He is saying that the general theory of natural selection is a metaphysical research programme in the sense that it is a methodological principle that guides scientific research, not in the sense that it is not a claim about the world. It is a claim about the world — it says that natural selection is the mechanism of evolution — but it is a claim that cannot be directly falsified in the way that specific hypotheses can. So it is both a claim about the world and a methodological principle. And Popper's demarcation criterion is not meant to apply to methodological principles; it is meant to apply to specific, testable hypotheses. So there is no reductio here, Marcus said. They were still going back and forth when the seminar ended. I'd like you to go through their exchange and say who is right at each step, and whether either of them is missing something important about Popper's actual position. I'm not asking you to take sides in the way a referee takes sides; I want a careful, point-by-point assessment of the arguments as they were actually made.
