I'm preparing a graduate seminar on the epistemology of perception this spring, and I've been re-reading Goldman's "Easy Knowledge" alongside the standard discussions of the bootstrapping problem, and I've hit a wall that I'd like to think through. My students keep asking me, in perfectly good faith, why we still treat the bootstrapping problem as a live issue if easy knowledge is supposed to let us know that our cognitive faculties are reliable without any independent evidence of their reliability. After all, if I can come to know that my vision is trustworthy just by looking at things, then it seems like the whole worry about circular justification — using my senses to vouch for my senses — just evaporates, and the bootstrapping problem becomes a nonstarter.

I'll confess that I'm not entirely sure I'm framing this right, because Goldman himself doesn't seem to think easy knowledge dissolves the bootstrapping problem, and I can't quite articulate the difference. Is easy knowledge actually a solution to the bootstrapping problem, a restatement of it in different terms, or something else entirely? And is the "easy" in easy knowledge doing any real work against the circularity worry, or is it just a change of subject that leaves the original problem intact? I'd be grateful for a clear account of how the two are supposed to relate.
