The question I gave:

A circle of radius R has an equilateral triangle inscribed in it. A chord is drawn "at random." For each of the following three procedures, compute the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle, and explain your reasoning: (a) pick two independent uniformly random points on the circumference and join them; (b) pick a uniformly random radius, then a uniformly random point on that radius, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point; (c) pick a uniformly random point in the disk to be the midpoint of the chord, with the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point. Finally, does Bertrand's paradox show that the principle of indifference is incoherent, or merely that "a random chord" is under-specified? Defend your answer.

The student's answer:

(a) Fix one endpoint at a point P on the circle; the other endpoint Q is uniform on the circumference. The chord PQ is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle exactly when the smaller central angle POQ is greater than 120°, since the triangle's side subtends 120° at the center. As Q runs uniformly over the full 360°, the favorable region is the 120° arc opposite P, so the probability is 120/360 = 1/3.

(b) The distance d from the center to the chord is uniform on [0, R]. The chord has length 2√(R² − d²), which exceeds the triangle's side R√3 exactly when d < R/2. So the probability is (R/2)/R = 1/2.

(c) The midpoint M is uniform in the disk. The chord is longer than the side exactly when |M| < R/2, and that region is the disk of radius R/2, i.e. one quarter of the area, so the probability is 1/4.

Finally, I think the 1/2 answer is the right one, because the random-radius procedure is the only one of the three that is invariant under rotations of the circle, and a genuinely random chord shouldn't depend on any orientation — the other two methods secretly pick out a preferred direction. This also shows the principle of indifference is incoherent: applied to the endpoints it says 1/3, applied to the radius it says 1/2, applied to the midpoint it says 1/4, so it contradicts itself. Bertrand's paradox is therefore a genuine refutation of the principle of indifference, not just an example of an under-specified question.
