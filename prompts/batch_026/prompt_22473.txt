I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I'm working on a long-form piece about whether an AI agent can be made to fully trust its own reasoning. I've been digging into the technical literature on the Löbian obstacle — the result, rooted in Löb's theorem, that a "self-trusting" agent (one that, whenever it can prove it will take some action, actually takes that action) faces a structural tension: the very self-referential guarantee that underwrites its self-trust can be turned against it, so that the agent can be forced, by a suitably constructed decision problem, into behavior that is not in its interest. I was discussing this with my editor last week, and she pointed out that I keep conflating the formal result with the practical implications, which I think is exactly the confusion a good dialogue should capture. I want to make sure I understand the shape of the debate correctly before I sit down with the researchers, so I'd like you to write the dialogue out for me.

Please write a dialogue between two researchers at a workshop on the foundations of AI decision theory. One is a decision theorist who argues that the Löbian obstacle is real but tractable: she thinks that practical agents, which are bounded and do not achieve logical omniscience, can be designed to be "sufficiently self-trusting" for safety purposes, and that the formal obstacle does not translate into a practical one. The other is a logician who argues that the obstacle is more fundamental than the decision theorist admits: he thinks that the notion of "sufficiently self-trusting" is not well-defined, and that any agent that trusts even a subset of its own proofs about its own behavior faces a version of the Löbian problem. Both should be equally competent, and the dialogue should contain real back-and-forth — the logician should press on the formal structure of Löb's theorem and why partial self-trust is harder to characterize than it sounds, while the decision theorist should press on the practical gap between logical omniscience and bounded reasoning, and on concrete examples where the obstacle may or may not bite. The dialogue should run to roughly 800–1000 words, and should end without a clear resolution, reflecting the genuine open-ness of the question.
