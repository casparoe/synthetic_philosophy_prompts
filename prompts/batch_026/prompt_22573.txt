I retired from a long career in physics a few years ago, and for most of that time "cause" was a word I used without ever stopping to think about what it meant — in a model, in a lab notebook, in a report on a failure analysis, it was just the thing that comes first and makes the other thing happen. It was only recently, having started reading philosophy of science, that I discovered there is a live and technical dispute over what causation even is, and I find it confusing because the two leading accounts seem to agree on every case I actually care about. The first is the counterfactual account, associated with David Lewis, on which a cause is something the effect counterfactually depends on — roughly the "but-for" test — and the second is the interventionist account, associated with Judea Pearl, on which causation is defined in terms of what would happen under an intervention, the "do" operation on a variable in a structural causal model.

What I cannot make sense of is how the two accounts handle the class of cases philosophers call preemption and redundant or overdetermined causation: situations where there are two independent sufficient causes of the same effect, or where one cause preempts another that would have produced the very same effect had the first failed. The standard examples are the firing squad, in which two marksmen each fire a shot that would independently have been fatal, or the two rival assassins, in which the first shot arrives before the second would have. In such cases the simple but-for test seems to misfire, because the effect would have occurred anyway, and yet we plainly want to say that both marksmen caused the death. So I'd like you to compare, as concretely as you can, how Lewis's counterfactual machinery — including his treatment of "influence" and of cases where the direct but-for test fails — and Pearl's structural causal model with the do-operator each actually produce a verdict on a specific overdetermined case, rather than just telling me, in the abstract, what each theory claims.

To keep it concrete, please pick one preemption or redundancy scenario of your own choosing, spell out the counterfactuals — and, for Pearl's framework, the interventionist counterfactuals that the do-operator licenses — for that scenario, and then tell me which of the two frameworks yields the verdict that matches our ordinary causal intuitions. I don't want a non-committal "each has strengths" ending; please reach a conclusion and defend it. And if you think the preemption cases genuinely favor one theory over the other, I'd like to know what you take the deeper reason to be — for instance, whether the advantage is structural (built into the formalism) or merely a local patch, and whether the fact that Pearl's own framework places counterfactuals at the top of his "ladder of causation" undermines the idea that the two theories are really opposed at all.
