I'm a third-year taking a history-of-economic-thought course this term and we've been doing a whole unit on Berkeley's views on money, which I didn't expect to be this relevant to my actual life. We read his Querist (1735-37) and the bits where he argues that money is just a conventional sign, a "ticket or counter," that a country's real wealth is its goods and produce and not the quantity of cash floating around, and his warning that paper issued without a fixed limit — like New England's notes or Law's land bank — creates a glut of paper that bids up the prices of everyting. He even argues it makes no difference what material the money-tickets are made of, since the stamp and signature are what give them their value. I keep turning it over because it maps almost exactly onto something I have to weigh in on this week.

I sit on my student union's finance commitee and we're about to vote on whether to launch a campus digital credit — we're calling it UnionCredits — that students earn by doing things like tutoring and event crew and then spend on union services: the bar, event tickets, a bit of the housing deposit. The proposal as it stands has no cap on how many credits the union can issue, and there's no real backing behind them, it's basically just numbers in an app. Half the committee is really into it and the other half is quietly terrified we're going to print our way into a campus inflation spiral where everyone's bidding up the price of a pint and a bed. I have to draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and I want it to have a clear bottom line with a time frame and, if it's conditional, concrete conditions, plus an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side rather than just me restating "inflation is bad."

Could you draft that memo for me? I'd really like it to actually use Berkeley's arguments to do the structural work — the money-is-a-conventional-sign point, the wealth-is-in-goods-not-cash point, the it-doesn't-matter-what-the-tickets-are-made-of point, why issuing without a cap is inflationary — instead of just gesturing at "economics says no." A couple of us on the committee aren't economics people so it shouldn't assume they've read Berkeley, but the couple who have will absolutely catch me if I get his argument wrong, so please keep it accurate to what he actually said.
