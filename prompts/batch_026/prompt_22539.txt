I recently started working as a discharge coordinator at a small clinic, and I keep watching patients who clearly feel something is wrong but can't put it into words, and the doctors just write it off as anxiety. I've been reading Fricker on epistemic injustice to try to make sense of what I'm seeing, and two friends of mine who both read philosophy got into a heated argument about her notion of hermenutical injustice. They asked me to referee and I honestly can't.

Carla said the concept is solid and that my patients are genuinely victims of it: the interpretive resources needed to make sense of their experience are missing from the shared vocabulary, and that gap is produced by power. And the harm is a loss of "narrative agency" -- the person is made to feel like their own experience isn't coherent, which is a real harm, not just an inconvenience.

Dev said the concept is too loose to be useful. First, in testimonial injustice the wrong is a specific bad judgment by a specific person, but in hermeneutical injustice nobody did anything wrong -- you can't blame the whole culture for a gap in the vocabulary, so it can't be an injustice. Second, not being able to make sense of a new experience is often just the misfortune of being a latecomer to it; if the theory counts that as injustice it collapses into "everything unfortunate is injustice," which is why it can't be a distinctively epistemic injustice.

Carla replied that Dev is smuggling in an individual-blame requirement -- injustice doesn't need a wrongdoer, a missing wheelchair ramp is still an injustice even if nobody chose to build it that way. And on the latecomer point: Fricker's move is that the gap isn't just that a particular person hasn't picked up the concept yet, it's that the concept is systematically absent from the shared resources of a group that's hermeneutically marginalized -- the power condition is what turns misfortune into injustice.

Dev said that then "hermeneutical" is just decoration: if a group is marginalized, every gap they have is automatically an injustice, and the theory can't tell hermeneutical injustice apart from just being marginalized. And practically, filling a vocabulary gap is a kindness, not a justice requirement -- we're not obligated to supply interpretive resources the way we are obligated not to distrust someone's testimony.

Can you walk through their exchange and say who's right at each step? And I'd like to know if there's anything both of them are missing -- I'm trying to figure out whether what I'm seeing at the clinic is actually injustice or just bad luck.
