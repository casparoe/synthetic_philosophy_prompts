I was watching a video essay on perception the other night and it dragged me down a rabbit hole on Molyneux's problem, and I've been stuck on it ever since. For background in case you don't remember the setup: someone is born totally blind, grows up learning to tell a smooth sphere from a cube purely by touch, and then one day their sight gets restored. The question is whether, the first time they see the two objects side by side, they could tell which is the sphere and which is the cube — or whether they'd have to pick them up and feel them again before the visual experience "clicks" with what they already know. Most people I've talked to say obviously they couldn't, and honestly that feels right to me too.

But I've started to feel like the question is doing alot more work than it looks like, and I can't quite put my finger on why. What I want is the actual conceptual knot, not a summary of the thought experiment (I know the setup). Specifically: why does the "no" answer seem to hinge on some assumption about whether the visual and tactile experiences are "the same kind of thing" or not? And is there a version of the setup where the answer could plausibly be "yes" without just stipulating that the person secretly practiced a bunch? And relatedly, I've heard there's actual empirical work on people who were blind from birth and later got their sight back — why doesn't that just settle the whole thing one way or the other? I want to understand why this is still a live philosophical question and not just an old curiosity.
