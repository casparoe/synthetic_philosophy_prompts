I first noticed this problem last spring when I was reading the minutes from a local city council meeting, and it has been turning over in my head ever since. The council, which has five members, was deciding whether to approve a new commercial development. The council's bylaws stipulate that the development must satisfy two independent conditions — an environmental review standard (call it E) and a zoning compatibility standard (call it Z) — and that the project is approved if and only if both conditions are met. Each member voted separately on E, on Z, and on the final approval question. The results were: E passed 3–2, Z passed 3–2, but the final approval question failed 2–3. The official record therefore states that the project meets both standards and is nonetheless not approved, which is a contradiction given the bylaws. I brought this up at dinner with two friends, Lena and Marcus, and they argued about what the council should have done.

Lena argued for what she calls the "top-down" method: the council should have voted on E and Z first, and if both had passed, the project would have been approved by rule, without a separate vote on the final question. In her view, the approval question is not an independent judgment but a logical consequence of the two standards, so voting on it separately was the mistake that generated the inconsistency. Marcus argued for the "bottom-up" method: the council should have voted on the final question first, and if it had failed, it should then have determined which of the two standards the project fails to meet. He maintained that the entire purpose of the meeting was to decide whether to approve the project, and that overriding a majority vote on that question with a logical rule would be undemocratic. Lena replied that Marcus's method has a mirror-image problem: if the project is rejected and the council must then pick which standard to reject, it may be forced to reject a standard that a majority of the members actually found the project satisfied, which is its own form of override. Marcus conceded the point but said it was a lesser evil, because at least the bottom-line decision — the one the public actually cares about — is respected. Lena countered that the public also cares about the standards being applied consistently, and that a council which can simultaneously record that a project meets both standards and that the project is rejected is a council that cannot be trusted to apply its own rules.

I don't know who is more right, and I'm not asking for advice on what I should think. I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made: for each distinct move in the exchange, say whether it is sound, partially sound, or unsound, and explain why. I'd also like to know whether there is a third option both of them are missing, or whether the inconsistency is genuinely intractable given the way majority voting works on questions that are logically connected to one another.
