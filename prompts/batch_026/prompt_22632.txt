I have just come out of a rather long and, I must say, unresolving argument with a friend over whether history is capable of genuine explanation in anything like the sense in which the natural sciences are. My friend, who has been reading a great deal of philosophy of science lately, defended with considerable conviction the view that the covering-law model of explanation — what is usually called the Hempel–Oppenheim, or deductive-nomological, model — was developed entirely for the natural sciences, and that the reason most historians and philosophers of history reject it is simply that they resent having the standards of physics imported into their discipline. On that basis he concluded, to his own evident satisfaction, that the long-standing debate between covering-law explanation and narrative explanation in history is therefore not a substantive philosophical disagreement at all, but merely a dispute over whether one is willing to concede a point to a model that was never intended to apply to the past.

I found his reasoning persuasive in the moment, and I confess that I have not fully shaken my doubt about it, so I would be grateful for a careful assessment rather than a quick verdict. In particular, I would be glad if you could (a) say whether the claim that the covering-law model was developed exclusively for the natural sciences is accurate, with some attention to how Hempel himself treated the case of history; (b) clarify whether the model, as Hempel ultimately presented it, really does require that an explanation take the form of a deductively valid argument, or whether there is a non-deductive form of the model to which a historian's probabilistic explanation might plausibly correspond; and (c) on that basis, indicate whether my friend's conclusion — that the disagreement between covering-law and narrative explanation is merely a matter of the model's intended scope rather than a substantive one — is defensible, and what the most important implications of your answer would be for how we should think about the explanatory status of historical narratives.
