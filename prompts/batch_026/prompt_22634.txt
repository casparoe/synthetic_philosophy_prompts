I'm preparing for a long-form interview with a philosopher who defends the view that most metaphysical disputes — the kind about whether there are universals, whether the past is "real," whether properties are fundamental — are not substantive disagreements about the world but merely verbal disagreements about how to describe it. My editor keeps asking whether this is just a polite way of saying that metaphysicians are talking past each other, and I want the interview to do better than that, so I'd like questions that force the view to do real work rather than let her simply restate it.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that do this? I specifically want at least one question each on: (a) how the view is supposed to handle a case where two philosophers seem to disagree about something that looks like a genuine fact — say, whether there are infinitely many numbers — and whether the "verbal" diagnosis can be applied without just stipulating it; (b) the worry, often associated with Kit Fine, that if the relevant "conceptual truths" can be mistaken, the view risks a kind of dogmatism in which any disagreement can be declared verbal on demand; and (c) what, on this view, is actually at stake in the dispute — if it is not the world, then what is it, and why should we care?

For each of these, I'd like a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so I can push back in the moment. And I'd like the set to end with a question about what kind of evidence or argument would change her mind, since that is the one thing my readers always want to know.
