I'm in a graduate seminar on decision theory this term, and we've been working through Parfit's hitchhiker paradox. The "rational irrationality" label in particular has been confusing me, and two of my classmates got into a real tangle over it that I couldn't untangle, so I'd like a careful, move-by-move adjudication. The setup, as we're using it in class: you're a hitchhiker, and a driver will take you either for a flat $10 or on the condition that you flip a fair coin — heads you pay $20, tails you pay nothing.

Nora opened by arguing that the coin flip is just as rational as paying the flat $10, because the two options have exactly the same expected value, so a rational agent should be indifferent between them; she added that the widespread feeling that the flip is somehow worse is really just an irrational aversion to risk, a psychological bias that has no place in a correct account of rationality, and that anyone who refuses the flip is simply failing to do the arithmetic. Ben replied that the flip is genuinely worse, not in a felt sense but in a real one, because it is the only option that can leave you worse off than you would otherwise have been: if the coin lands heads, you end up strictly worse off than you would have been by just paying the $10, and a rational choice should not expose you to an avoidable chance of a worse outcome. Nora answered that Ben is smuggling in his own risk aversion and calling it a fact about rationality; on her view, once we grant that both options are expected-value neutral, the only difference between them is the variance, and variance is only a "bad thing" if you already have a preference against it, which is just a preference, not a rational requirement, and plenty of perfectly rational people would take the flip. Ben conceded that the flip is sometimes better too — when tails comes up, you pay nothing — but insisted that this doesn't save it, because the flat $10 has a better worst case than the flip (the most you can lose on the flat is $10, whereas the flip can cost you $20), and a rational choice should not expose you to an avoidable chance of a worse outcome. Nora closed by saying that this is just minimax reasoning wearing a different hat, that minimizing your worst-case loss is no more a requirement of rationality than maximizing expected value is, and that if you want to minimize your worst case, that's a preference, and plenty of rational people — including, she said, anyone who has ever bought a lottery ticket — would reject it.

I honestly couldn't tell who was right, and I suspect both of them are partly missing the same thing. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what at each step, and then say what, if anything, each of them is missing? In particular, I'd like you to say whether the "the expected value is the same" move is doing as much work as Nora thinks it is, and whether "rational irrationality" is a genuine phenomenon here or a confusion.
