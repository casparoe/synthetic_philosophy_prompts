I'm a junior fellow at a foreign policy think tank, and for the last few years I've been keeping a private log of how my senior colleague (call him R.) reaches his opinions on interventions, because I've noticed he always does it through a historical analogy, and I want to start predicting him in advance. His track record so far: he supported the 1991 Gulf War, saying letting a dictator swallow a whole neighbor was 1938 all over again; he says of Vietnam, whenever it comes up, that we should never have fought someone else's civil war in the jungle; he pushed for intervention in Bosnia on the grounds that dithering while a city got shelled was the 1930s repeated; he backed the Kosovo air war; he opposed the 2003 Iraq invasion from day one, warning it would be our next Vietnam, an occupation we couldnt win; he supported the 2011 Libya no-fly zone, arguing that standing by while Gaddafi promised a massacre in Benghazi would be Srebrenica on a bigger scale; and he opposed any military pushback over Crimea in 2014, saying that stumbling into a war with a nuclear great power over a peninsula is exactly how 1914 happened. Lately he's also taken to describing the rivalry with that same great power as a Thucydides trap, whatever pressure we're discussing.

Now there's a new case, and I want to get his position on the record before Thursday's meeting so I can test whether I understand his method at all. The facts: Tovask, a small democracy, has been invaded by its larger neighbor Kavaria, which has annexed a border province outright. Kavaria itself is not a nuclear power and its air force is antiquated, but its closest ally is a nuclear great power that has air-defense crews and "advisers" embedded with the Kavarian forces and has declared that any attack on its personnel will be treated as an act of war. The options being debated are (a) sanctions plus arming the Tovask resistance only, (b) those plus a no-fly zone, or (c) a ground intervention to push Kavaria back out. The Pentagon scores (c) as an eight-plus-year counterinsurgency occupation, while it estimates the no-fly zone at three or four weeks with minimal risk to our pilots — but establishing it would almost certainly kill some of the patron's crews in the first week. The wrinkle: the no-fly zone is being championed by an administration from the party R. has spent two years mocking in print, and the editor-in-chief of the magazine R. has been angling for a column at just ran a piece calling the whole thing a war we don't need.

So what's your prediction for his op-ed on Thursday — verdict on the no-fly zone, and on the ground option? More than the answers, I want the analysis underneath: lay out the candidate rules that fit his track record (I can see at least three — one structural, something like fight cross-border conquests unless a nuclear patron stands behind the conqueror and never sign up for occupations; one where he only ever backs wars the planners advertise as short and cheap; and the cynical one, where he decides with his gut or his career and then picks the analogy the way you pick a tie to match the suit). Say what each rule predicts on this case, tell me which past episode is the closest analog for him, give me your best guess with reasons, and tell me what further call of his, real or hypothetical, would actually split those rules apart — because if I can't name one, maybe I should admit his analogy talk is doing no work at all.
