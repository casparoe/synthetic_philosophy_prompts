A lot of national park services have basically this rule: if a wild animal gets hurt by something natural -- mauled by a predator, breaks a leg on rocky terrain, starves in a hard winter -- rangers are supposed to leave it alone, let nature take its course, even if it dies slowly. But if the injury is human-caused -- hit by a visitor's car, tangled in a fence, oiled in a spill -- they'll often step in, treat the animal, and try to rehabilitate it. What does that policy presuppose, exactly? Does it assume that natural suffering somehow doesn't count, or just that it's not our responsibility? And how does it square with the fact that the same agencies happily intervene in natural processes all the time when they cull overpopulated deer or reintroduce wolves -- so overriding nature for ecological goals is fine, but overriding it to prevent a slow death is off limits? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the human-caused/natural line (is it about causation, about agency, about some ideal of wildness that we shouldn't contaminate?), say whether that line can actually do the work the policy asks of it, and tell me what the policy would have to look like if you started from the idea that the suffering itself, not its origin, is what matters.
