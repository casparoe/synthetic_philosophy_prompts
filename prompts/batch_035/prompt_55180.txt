My coworker is really into econ-adjacent podcasts and tried to explain the no-trade theorum to me the other day. His version went roughly like this: 'Aumann proved that two rational people with the same priors can't agree to disagree — once they know each other's beliefs, they immediatly converge to identical opinions. Milgrom and Stokey extended this to markets: if everyone starts from an efficient allocation, no private information can ever justify trade, because your willingness to trade reveals your information, and once it's revealed it's already reflected in the price, so nobody will take the other side. So the massive volume on the stock market is basically proof that traders are irrational, and the only rational thing to do is buy index funds and never trade again.'

He's usually pretty on the ball but something smells off. Like, people clearly trade for mundane reasons — rebalancing, cashing out for a house, hedging their business exposure — and don't market makers exist to take the other side of exactly that stuff? Also his two sentences seem to slide between agreeing about beliefs and agreeing about trades without acknowledging they're different claims. Can you go through his summary piece by piece and tell me which parts a careful economist would accept, which parts are subtle distortions (I've heard the theorm assumes something strong about the starting allocation, and also that Aumann's result is about a specific event, not opinions in general?), and whether the 'index funds are the only rational move' conclusion actually follows from the parts that are true?
