I'm writing a paper section on Friedman's notorious methodological claim: that a hypothesis is to be judged solely by the accuracy of its predictions, and that significant hypotheses will typically rest on assumptions that are wildly inaccurate as descriptions — with the more significant the theory, the more inaccurate its assumptions. The standard rescue of this claim, after Alan Musgrave, distinguishes negligibility assumptions (the factor is real but makes no difference), domain assumptions (it makes a difference, but not within this theory's intended domain), and heuristic assumptions (it makes a difference here, and the falsehood is a deliberate step toward a successor theory that puts the factor back in). My section argues that the usual defense quietly treats Friedman's examples as negligibility assumptions, and that this misses his point: his examples — like the expert billiard player who plays, in Friedman's words, "as if he knew the complicated mathematical formulas that would give the optimum directions of travel" — are heuristic assumptions, where the falsity does positive epistemic work. For that argument I need a fresh case built to order, not one borrowed from the textbook.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The setting must be realistic and drawn from scientific practice or policy modeling, but not from economics and not from Friedman's own examples — no billiard players, no falling bodies in a vacuum. Epidemiology, hydrology, traffic engineering, ecology: your choice. The scenario itself is one paragraph. The model it depicts must contain a false assumption whose falsity is load-bearing: the model's predictive success depends on the assumption being false in the specific way it is false. This must be demonstrated, not stipulated, by a three-way comparison holding the data and the rest of the model fixed: (a) the model with the false assumption; (b) the model with the most defensible realistic replacement for it — not a strawman, but the version an honest defender of descriptive accuracy would actually propose; and (c) the model with the factor in question treated as noise. The specification is met only if (a) predicts best, and the mechanism by which the falsity earns its keep — filtering out variance, anchoring a calculation, separating signal from detail, or something of that kind — is visible on the surface of the case rather than asserted.

After the scenario, do four things. First, classify the assumption on Musgrave's scheme, and say whether it is heuristic in the full sense — false, relevant, and a step on the road to a successor theory — sketching what that successor would look like if there is one. Second, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Third, give the Friedmanite's best reply — presumably that comparison (b) is not a more realistic version of the same theory but a different and worse theory, and that prediction remains the only admissible test. Fourth, take a definite position and defend it: does the case show that the truth of assumptions matters in a way the F-twist cannot accommodate, or only that "realism" was the wrong word for whatever it is that does matter? Don't leave both options standing.
