Quick recap of the setup so we're on the same page: Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created suddenly in mid-air, in a void, with limbs separated so they don't touch, eyes covered, no sound, no sensation at all -- and claims the person would still affirm their own existence, without affirming anything about their body. He deploys this in several places, the psychology of the Shifa', the Isharat, and other texts, with wording that shifts between them, which is part of the problem.

The interpretive debate: for decades now scholars have split between roughly (a) the traditional reading -- it's an argument for the soul's immateriality or substantiality, i.e., a premise in Avicenna's proof that the soul is distinct from body; (b) the self-awareness reading -- it's not really an argument at all but an exhibition of primitive, pre-reflective self-awareness that's constitutive of the soul, closer to phenomenology than to a syllogism; and (c) various in-between positions, including people who say different versions in different works do different things. Meanwhile there's a whole separate lane about whether and how this prefigures Descartes's cogito, and the later tradition complicates everything -- Suhrawardi famously takes the flying man and uses it for his own ends, which suggests post-Avicennian readers already disagreed about what it was good for.

And here's the thing: nobody moves. Every side can accommodate every text. The self-awareness camp reads the immateriality conclusion as secondary or as derived FROM grasped self-awareness; the immateriality camp reads the self-awareness as just the epistemic route to the conclusion. Same passages, opposite morals, indefinitely.
