I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. My working vocabulary is the one I actually use: classes and instances, interfaces, nominal vs. structural typing, duck typing, prototype chains (I write JavaScript for a living), inheritance vs. composition, embeddings, nearest-neighbor classification, and style transfer. Restate, in that vocabulary and as faithfully as you can, the philosophical debate about what makes every written 'a' an 'a' — the ancient idea that all the 'a's we write are imperfect renderings of some one ideal template; the idea that a thing has an essence, a set of necessary features any 'a' must have; the later view that there is no essence at all, only overlapping family resemblances among instances; the psychological refinement of that view in terms of prototypes and graded membership; and the more radical suggestion, developed in work on letterforms and analogy-making (Hofstadter's "Letter Spirit" project is the locus classicus), that two 'a's need not share any intrinsic feature at all — that what makes them the same letter is a role in a fixed system of contrasts, where a letter is what it is by not being any of the others, together with the capacity to be carried across radical style changes, the way a melody survives being transposed into a different key. Translate these positions one by one into my terms: the ideal template suggests classes; essences suggest structural typing and feature checks; family resemblance suggests nearest-neighbor lookup over a corpus of exemplars; and the role-and-re-creation view suggests whatever you think it suggests — tell me, even if the honest answer is that nothing in my vocabulary fits it. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the debate map cleanly onto things my vocabulary already has, which translate only with distortion (be explicit about the distortion), and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. I want the residue treated as the interesting part. In particular, three pressure points I keep hitting: (1) no feature list survives — I can find two 'a's that share fewer features with each other than either shares with some 'b', which seems to break any similarity metric over raw features; (2) normativity — a malformed 'a' is a bad 'a', not a non-'a', and my classification machinery has no obvious place for that distinction between getting it wrong and not being an instance at all; and (3) style, which is somehow both orthogonal to letter identity and inseparable from it — every 'a' is an 'a' in some style, and there is no styleless 'a' to classify. Close by telling me whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary or evidence that the philosophical question was less clear than it looked.
