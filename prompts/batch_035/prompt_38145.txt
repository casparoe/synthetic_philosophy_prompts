I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology with a side interest in ML safety, and the fight over counterfactual mugging has been stuck for something like two decades now and I can't work out why nobody moves. Setup so we're on the same page: Omega flips a fair coin before you exist; if it had landed heads, Omega would have paid you a large sum provided you'd be the sort of agent who pays up on tails; it landed tails, and now you can either hand over a smaller amount or walk away. Everyone can do the arithmetic, everyone agrees on the payoff structure, and yet causal decision theorists won't pay, the evidential crowd splits, and functional decision theory partisans insist paying is required — and then those same partisans turn around and tell you NOT to pay in XOR blackmail, even though the reasoning that makes you pay in the mugging (your decision being tied to what happens in the other branch, or to the letter's informativeness) seems to push you toward paying in the blackmail, where everyone agrees paying is dumb. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't about expected utility at all but about what a decision theory is even for: is it advice to an agent at a moment, or an evaluation of the agent as a temporally extended algorithm whose choices in the other branch count as its choices? On the second view paying in the mugging is basically cooperation with yourself, and refusing is a weird self-defection. Diagnose the stall for me properly — is it verbal, is it a hidden metaphysical question about identity across possible worlds, are the two camps answering different questions under the word "rationality" — and tell me whether anything could actually settle it: an impossibility theorem showing no theory can satisfy all the intuitions at once, some real-world setting where we can check which agents win over many iterations, or nothing at all?
