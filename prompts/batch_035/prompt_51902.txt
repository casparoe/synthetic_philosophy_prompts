My housemate is a third-year philosophy student, and over dinner last week she tried to explain the debate about the norm of assertion to me after I asked her why it's considered rude to say "it's raining" when you're just guessing. Here is roughly what she said, as best as I can reconstruct it: "The dominant view, going back to Timothy Williamson around 2000, is that the rule of assertion is that you may assert something only if you know it. Since knowledge is justified true belief, the rule really comes to saying you should only assert what you're justified in believing — Williamson just puts it in terms of knowledge. The view nicely explains why 'how do you know?' is always an appropriate challenge to an assertion, and why Moore's paradox arises: saying 'it's raining, but I don't believe it's raining' is absurd, which the knowledge rule supposedly accounts for. But the view was pretty much refuted by the lottery problem: in a fair lottery with a million tickets, you know your ticket will lose, so the knowledge rule licenses asserting 'my ticket will lose' — which sounds terrible — whereas it seems fine to assert things you don't know in everyday cases, like telling someone a train leaves at 6:10 based on a timetable that's occasionally wrong. So most people now accept the truth rule instead: assert only what is true." She delivered this all very confidently, but I noticed that when I pressed her on the lottery case she said she wasn't sure whether the person really does know their ticket will lose, which made me wonder whether she was summarizing the literature accurately or just repeating what her professor said in passing.
