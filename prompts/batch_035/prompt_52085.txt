I'm a retired biochemist who's been filling retirement with philosophy of mind books, and I've got a running argument with my neighbor over her favorite way of classifying animal smarts. She uses a little four-rung ladder she picked up from some philosopher (she can never remember which, and I can't place it either). Rung one: creatures that only get smarter across generations, because the individuals who blunder badly leave fewer offspring — the problem-solving all happens in the gene pool, none of it inside any single animal's head. Rung two: creatures that learn within their own lifetime by actual trial and error — the thing that paid off before gets done again. Rung three: creatures that can move the trial and error inside, so to speak — they try out moves offline before committing their body, which makes errors cheap. Rung four: creatures that get thinking tools handed to them culturally — words, maps, arithmetic — and she insists this isn't just a faster rung three, because the tools change the kind of thinking you can do at all.

Her track record so far: bacteria and my tomato plants, rung one. A sea slug, rung one, because habituation isn't really learning. A rat in a Skinner box, rung two. The same rat pausing at a choice point before committing, still rung two — that's just remembering, not simulating, she says. A scrub-jay that re-hides its cache when another jay was watching, rung two — I pushed back on that one, it seems pretty sophisticated, but she calls it a fixed social reflex rather than the bird playing out scenarios. A chimp stacking crates to reach a banana, rung three. A raven that bent a straight wire into a hook on the first try, rung three. A toddler with a couple hundred words, rung four. And a wolf pack coordinating a hunt is, in her words, just rung-two wolves in formation — no rung for the pack itself.

Next week we've got four new cases on the docket and I want to walk in prepaired. (a) An octopus that unscrews a jar after watching another octopus do it once. (b) A chess program that evaluates millions of continuations before every move. (c) A New Caledonian crow, where the young apparently pick up their tool designs from the local tradition rather than inventing them from scratch. (d) Me, doing long division on paper.

Predict her verdicts if you can, but what I really want is the rule she's running underneath. The rung-three boundary is doing a lot of work and I can't tell if its criterion is "one-trial insight," "offline representation of alternatives," or something else, and the rung-four boundary could be "language" or "any culturally transmitted thinking tool," and those two seem to come apart on the crow, I think. So: what are the candidate rules that fit her verdicts so far, which fits best, and what further case could I bring that would actually split them? And is there a reading of the ladder on which the chess program just falls off it entirely, or does she have to bite some bullet there?
