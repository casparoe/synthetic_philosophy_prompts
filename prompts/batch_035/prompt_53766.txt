I'm writing a term paper for a political philosophy class on clientelism and gift-giving vs. corruption, and my professor said I could use The Godfather (the first film, 1972) as my case study, but she also warned me that people misuse it constantly. Half the class wants to say the Corleone world shows that the Western definition of corruption isn't universal, and the other half treats the whole thing as an obvious catalog of crimes, and she said she'd dock us for either if it's lazy. So I want a reading of the film that takes the anthropology seriously without becoming an apology for the mafia.

Here's what I've noticed and want pushed on. The opening scene: Bonasera, the undertaker, comes to Vito on his daughter's wedding day asking him to punish the men who attacked his daughter and got off with a suspended sentence. Vito is insulted when Bonasera offers to pay him, and again when Bonasera says he's never wanted Vito's friendship or needed his help before. Vito says no Sicilian can refuse a request on his daughter's wedding day, agrees to do it as a favor, and then tells Bonasera that someday he may ask for a service in return, and that day may never come. So the scene literally walks through the distinction I'm supposed to be writing about: money is refused (that would make Vito a hired thug), friendship and future reciprocity are demanded instead. And then the payoffs come to Bonasera later, when Don Vito asks him to handle Sonny's body so the press can't photograph it.

So: is the film dramatizing a coherent patron-client moral economy, with its own internal norms about what counts as honor, insult, obligation, and violation — or is it just showing corruption with better manners? I'm interested in where the film draws the line between gift and bribe, and whether that line is about content, timing (quid before vs. after quo), the relationship, or whether a public office is in play. Vito isn't a government official, he's explicitly outside the state — he's a parallel source of protection and justice that people go to when the courts fail them (Bonasera literally says he believed in America, and the American courts let him down). Does that make the clientelism critique different from the corruption-by-officials critique, or is private protection just corruption relocated? And Michael's arc: by the end of the first film he's lying to Kay about killing Carlo, and the baptism montage cuts between the sacred ritual and the executions. Does the film think the old moral economy was real but dies with Vito, replaced by Michael's colder, purely instrumental version — the it's-not-personal-it's-just-business ethos? Because if so, the film seems to be making a claim the corruption literature keeps circling: that there IS a normative difference between a patron order built on loyalty and obligation and one that's nakedly transactional, even if both are bad by Western legal standards.

A few specific things I'd love: a reading of the Bonasera scene as the thesis statement; something on the wedding-day rule (a favor asked on the wrong day gets refused); the Hollywood producer Woltz subplot, where intimidation gets attached to a personal favor done for a friend of the family's godson (the singer Johnny Fontane); and whether the film ever shows a clean test case of the gift/bribe boundary breaking down. Also, honestly, help me see where the sympathetic reading of the moral economy fails on the film's own evidence — the film romanticizes, but the "clients" mostly end up dead or betrayed. I need the paper to argue something, not just note that the themes are present. And please don't have the paper hinge on quotes, I don't have the text with me to check wording.
