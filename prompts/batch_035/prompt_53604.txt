hey, so I teach a philosophy of math course and next unit is the Löwenheim–Skolem stuff. 12 students, 80 minute seminar, they've all done Cantor's diagonal argument already and are pretty smug about it. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel Skolem's paradox instead of just nodding at it -- the thing where ZFC, if consistent, has a countable model, even though the model itself proves "the reals are uncountable." I want them to hit the contradiction from the inside before anyone tells them the resolution.

my half-baked idea, feel free to fix or trash it: split the room into an "inside" group playing set theorists living in some universe M, and one or two "outside" observers (maybe me) who get a master sheet enumerating everything in M. The inside group can only use functions/sets that are themselves listed somewhere I control, like an index they can consult. When they try to build a bijection from the naturals onto the "reals" and diagonalize against it, the outside observer just points out that whatever they built was one entry on the master list all along, and the diagonal they produce is also just another item on it. Is that mechanic honest or does it collapse into me rigging the game? If it's broken tell me what to replace it with.

give me the whole design in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two: materials and prep, setup and roles, how the play runs up to the reveal, the debrief mapping what happened onto the actual resolution (the bijection being outside the model, countability not being absolute), and lastly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it plus which part of the real paradox no classroom activity can reproduce and I'll have to just explain. No lecture up front -- they should get the theorem's statement only after they've been confused by the game.
