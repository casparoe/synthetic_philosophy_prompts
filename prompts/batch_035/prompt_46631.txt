A podcast I listen to gave this account of superrationality: "Hofstadter's idea is that in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between two ideally rational players who know they are both ideally rational, the two players are doing the same reasoning, so there is really only one decision being made, not two. A superrational player therefore treats her choice of strategy as determining what the other player chooses, and since mutual cooperation pays better than mutual defection, she cooperates. In substance, superrationality is just evidential decision theory applied to symmetric games: you cooperate because your own cooperation is good evidence that the other player cooperates. The same reasoning vindicates one-boxing in Newcomb's problem and explains why it can be rational to vote in a large election — if you vote, that is evidence that many people reasoning like you will vote too. Critics have pointed out that this is magical thinking, since your decision cannot cause anyone else's, and Hofstadter never had much of an answer to that."

I found this pretty persuasive, but I don't know the primary material. How much of it would a defender of superrationality accept as a fair statement of the view? Go through the account and tell me which parts are accurate, where it conflates superrationality with something it is not, whether the extensions to Newcomb's problem and voting carry over as stated, and whether the "magical thinking" objection actually refutes the view as Hofstadter presented it or only refutes a misreading of it.
