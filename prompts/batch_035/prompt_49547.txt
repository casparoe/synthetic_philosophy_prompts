I have no background in philosophy — I work in IT support and mostly came to this by noticing something odd about how I talk to myself — but I've been stuck for weeks on the sentence "It's raining, but I don't believe it." It can plainly be true: it rains outside while I sit there convinced it's dry. And I can say something with the same shape about someone else — "he says it's sunny, but he doesn't believe a word of it" — with no weirdness at all. Yet the moment I aim it at myself, it sounds broken in a way I can't pin down. It doesn't sound false; it sounds like something has gone wrong, and I want to know what.

What I'm asking for is a probability, decomposed. Specifically, my own best guess is that the trouble is not in what the sentence says but in what you're doing when you say it: by asserting that it's raining you present yourself as believing it, so the second half fights the act of speaking rather than the content spoken. Call this the pragmatic explanation. I want your credence that something in that neighborhood is the right story, broken into terms roughly like these: (1) your probability that the sentence really is consistent — that there is a possible situation in which it is true; (2) your probability that it is unassertable by its own speaker in every context, not merely awkward or unusual; (3) your probability that the pragmatic explanation is correct, versus the alternatives I've half-formed on my own: that uttering "I don't believe it" about oneself works as a confession that changes what the whole utterance is doing, or that the real impossibility is psychological — that believing it's raining while believing that you don't believe it's raining is itself an unstable or incoherent belief state, so no fully rational speaker could ever be in the situation that would make sincere assertion possible. Assign a number to each term with a line of justification, show how they combine into your overall credence in the pragmatic explanation, and tell me which term you're least confident in and what would move it most.

Two twists of my own I can't classify, so please fold them into the decomposition where they belong. First: does the same wrongness attach to "It was raining an hour ago, but I no longer believe it"? The past tense seems to let the two halves sit together more comfortably, and I can't tell whether that's because the asserting-implies-believing link is really present-tense only, or because I'm just used to people changing their minds. Second: "It's raining, but I have no reason to think it is" sounds to me less broken than the original, and I'd like to know whether that contrast is a genuine datum any explanation should account for, or just noise from how loosely people talk.

One request about how you present this: where something is treated as settled among people who actually study this topic, say so plainly; where there is real live disagreement, say that too, and don't dress up whichever explanation you favor as if it were the consensus view.
