Two friends of mine got into it at dinner on friday and I've been chewing on it since. Maya is a hospice nurse; Dan works in health policy. During a staffing crunch last year, Maya spent about 40 minutes she arguably didn't have sitting with a dying patient whose family couldn't come in, while her five other patients waited longer for various things. She brought it up as an example of doing the right thing, Dan pushed back, and it went roughly like this:

(1) Dan said it was kind but not just: five other patients, so a fair division of her attention would've been more like 15 minutes each, and the 40 minutes went where it did because that patient's case happened to move her. Which makes it affection, not ethics.

(2) Maya said care ethics starts from responsiveness to particular people, not from distributing attention evenly -- any view of morality on which "leave the dying woman alone because the schedule says so" comes out as the right answer has lost the plot.

(3) Dan answered that then care ethics is a permission slip for favoritism: she'd have spent the 40 minutes on whichever patient she happened to feel bonded to, and the grumpy one who complained alot would've gotten the shaft. Justice is exactly the check on that, and care ethics has no built-in version of it.

(4) Maya said the check is internal -- you can care badly, and caring well includes noticing when you're neglecting others. But she admitted she couldnt say in advance how the 40 minutes with the dying patient traded off against 15-minute increments for the five others; it was a judgment made in the moment. And she added that even if a perfect split had been possible, something would still be lost, and a view that can't register that loss isn't seeing the whole moral field.

(5) Dan said if the tradeoff can't be stated in advance then it's not guiding her choices, it's decorating them after the fact -- and that policy is just the same judgment made in advance, for everyone, which is exactly what makes it fair instead of arbitrary.

(6) Maya's last word was that evenness isn't a neutral default, it's itself a theory of care -- one that says the right way to care is to give everyone the same-sized increment -- and she just doesn't buy that theory.

They eventually dropped it and started clearing plates. I couldn't referee it and honestly didn't want to, but it's been bugging me. Can you go through the exchange and say who's right at each of the six steps, not just who wins overall? And if there's something both of them are missing -- I have a feeling there is but can't quite put my finger on it -- I'd like to hear that too.
