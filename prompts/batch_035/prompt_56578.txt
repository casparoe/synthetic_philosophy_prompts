Write an essay on a question in population axiology that sits at the intersection of the repugnant conclusion itself and the meta-question of how much our reaction to it can be trusted: when we find Parfit's repugnant conclusion repugnant, are we responding to the moral structure of the case, or to the way the case is described?

The background you should get right: in Reasons and Persons (1984), Parfit observed that total utilitarianism appears to imply that for any world A containing a moderate number of people with wonderful lives, there is a world Z containing vastly more people whose lives are only barely worth living, and that Z is better than A, since the enormous number of slightly-positive lives sums to more welfare. Parfit illustrated Z with deliberately austere imagery — think of his world of endless background music and bland, mass-produced food — and his own reaction was that this implication is deeply counterintuitive, which is why he called it repugnant.

The specific question to take up: a critic of the intuition might argue that the repugnance is largely a framing effect. The phrase "barely worth living" invites us to picture lives hovering just above the line of neutrality — lives of scraping by — when a life at any positive welfare level, even a low one, still contains friendship, small pleasures, projects, love. If Z were described from the inside, from the perspective of one of its inhabitants looking forward to an ordinary day, or if we were asked whether we would regret such a person's existence, the intuition that Z is worse might soften considerably. Test the diagnosis seriously: does redescription of the same welfare facts change what we should think, or only what we do think? And notice a second, structural point the framing diagnosis must contend with: Parfit's argument is usually presented as a sequence of small steps from A to Z, each of which looks benign — adding happy people, or slightly lowering average welfare while greatly increasing numbers — so that anyone who accepts the local comparisons seems driven to the global conclusion. If the repugnance attaches only to the endpoint and not to any step, is that evidence that the intuition is unreliable (since betterness had better be transitive), or is it open to the defender of the intuition to deny that a series of pairwise comparisons settles the ranking of the endpoints?

Weigh these considerations against each other, and do not end on a non-committal note. Reach and defend a definite verdict among (at least) these three options: (i) the repugnant conclusion should simply be accepted, and the repugnance diagnosed as a framing artifact or status quo bias; (ii) the conclusion should be resisted by giving up some structural assumption — the totality of the ordering, transitivity, or the idea that the value of lives aggregates additively; or (iii) the repugnance is a genuine datum about value that survives honest redescription, even if it is hard to say exactly what principle it tracks. Say which option you take, why the others fail, and what your verdict costs you elsewhere in population ethics.

Structure your essay with the following titled sections, in this order, with rough lengths:

1. **The argument, stated plainly** (~250 words) — set out the repugnant conclusion carefully enough that the framing question can be evaluated against it.
2. **The framing diagnosis** (~350 words) — make the strongest case that the repugnance is an artifact of description.
3. **What the framing diagnosis cannot explain** (~350 words) — the structural step-by-step pressure and any other considerations that survive redescription.
4. **My verdict** (~250 words) — commit to one of the three options, justify it, and state the cost.
