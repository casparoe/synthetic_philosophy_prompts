Quick background: I do ML research for a living and I'm sitting in on a philosophy of science course as the department's token curiosity, and the underdetermination unit has made something click that's been bugging me for years about my own field. In ML, the standard way you justify a new architecture is: run it on a fixed benchmark, report a margin of a fraction of a point or a couple points over the previous best, and then tell a mechanistic story about *why* the design works -- the attention mechanism captures long-range dependencies, this regularizer smooths the loss landscape, the inductive bias matches the data's structure, whatever. Reviewers accept the margin plus the story, the story gets cited in the intro of the next fifty papers, and nobody expects the mechanism itself to be independently tested at the time. From the philosophy side, my understanding is that underdetermination is exactly the situation where the available evidence doesn't single out one theory among several rivals that all fit it -- Duhem/Quine, empirically equivalent hypotheses, that whole thing. And it sure looks like a one-point benchmark gap is compatible with a zillion different mechanism stories, including the unflattering ones where the margin is just seed variance and the story is a just-so tale someone wrote on the train home.

So what I want to know is what this practice actually presupposes, philosophically. Does leaderboard culture implicitly rely on theoretical virtues -- simplicity of the mechanism, plausibility of the narrative, fecundity of the design idea for follow-up work -- doing truth-indicating work that raw benchmark evidence can't do on its own? Or does it presuppose something closer to instrumentalism, where mechanisms are decorative and the only real knowledge is predictive performance? Or is the practice quietly fine because ablations, downstream replication, and the eventual consolidation onto the best variant supply the extra evidence that breaks the underdetermination after the fact, so the initial acceptance is just provisional in the way all science supposedly is? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit philosophy of science of the practice, distinguish what it *strictly* requires from what it merely gets along comfortably with, and flag where the practice is underdetermined enough that several quite different philosophies would license it. Also, since I might bring this up in the seminar, please seperate what's genuinely settled among people who know this literature from what's actively contested -- e.g. whether theoretical virtues are actually truth-conducive versus merely pragmatic strikes me as a live fight, and I don't want to present one side as the consensus.
