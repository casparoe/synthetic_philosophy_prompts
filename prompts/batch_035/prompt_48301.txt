I am the graduate-student member, with a vote, of the Naming and Heritage Review Committee at a state university in the Deep South, founded in the 1890s. I came to the committee out of coursework in comparative memory politics — Germany's postwar reckoning, Japan's textbook fights, Turkey's handling of the Armenian genocide, the American South's Lost Cause romance — and I have been trying, without success, to work out which of those styles our committee actually runs. One senior member insists we are doing German-style reckoning at the scale of a campus; another warns that we are doing Lost Cause remembering in reverse, keeping the honors and rewriting the story around them; a third calls our plaque policy Japanese-style quietism — keep everything, annotate lightly, change nothing structural. I honestly cannot tell who is right, and I do not want my next vote to settle it by accident.

Our record so far, in full. We renamed the flagship residential college, named in 1926 for the Collier family, whose endowment built the original campus out of profits from convict-lease labor camps and was inseparable from the university's early budget. We renamed the dean's medal, an annual award named for Dean Averill, the mid-century dean of students who wrote and enforced the campus segregation code through the fifties and sixties. We kept the 1938 WPA mural in the student union, which depicts the county's founding with the enslaved in the margins, declining a petition to paint it over, and added an interpretive panel. And we kept the 1911 stained-glass window in the old chapel, a gift from a Confederate heritage association showing a romanticized plantation, with an interpretive card.

The case for our October meeting is Meridian Hall, our largest lecture hall, named in 1984 for Governor Calloway, our most famous alumnus. As governor in the sixties he ran the state's school-segregation apparatus and made televised defiance of federal orders his signature; he never repudiated any of it. But out of office he endowed the need-based scholarship that has since put thousands of first-generation students through this university, a disproportionate number of them Black students from the very counties he kept segregated, because he weighted the fund toward those counties. His name is spoken in every schedule and every syllabus. The student government and the scholarship alumni association have jointly petitioned to rename; the county historical society opposes.

What I want from you, in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one writes a letter to a colleague — is this: a recommendation for how I should vote if I want to be consistent with the committee's own record; the closest analog among our four precedents, with your reasons; and, if the record genuinely underdetermines it, a frank statement of which principle each way of voting would commit the committee to going forward. Since I also get to propose next semester's docket, close by telling me which further case would best reveal which rule we have actually been running, and what that case would have to look like.
