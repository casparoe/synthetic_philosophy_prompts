I am a high school junior and the student coordinator of my school's science club. At our spring science fair, a sophomore entered a project testing whether her grandmother's joint pain predicts rain better than the weather app does, tracking both for six weeks. The judging panel split: half called it a genuine experiment; half said that relying on folk signs isn't really science, whatever the data show. Because the dispute kept resurfacing, the club has asked me to open next month's meeting with a thirty-minute session, whiteboard only, on what separates science from non-science, with falsificationism as the organizing idea. Our faculty advisor's one instruction was that I should present Popper's criterion as a serious proposal that drew serious replies, not as the settled definition of "scientific."

Please plan the session rather than write it out. Specifically, I need:

1. The order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what — in particular, whether testability in principle must be established before we can even discuss how a failed prediction is handled, or the reverse.
2. A single carrying example. Should it be the joint-pain project itself, and if not, what should stand in for it? Please say why the standard black-swan example, which I keep finding online, may not do the work this audience needs.
3. A map of the misconceptions I should expect and the point in the sequence at which each must be headed off. My predictions: that "falsifiable" will be heard as "false"; that students will think one failed prediction obliges scientists to discard a theory on the spot; that students will conclude any claim generating predictions is scientific, horoscopes included; and that Popper will be taken as having proven science delivers certainty rather than as having proposed where its liability lies.
4. A ruling on one temptation: I would like to include, in a single sentence, the point that no test ever speaks to a hypothesis in isolation, because an auxiliary assumption can absorb the blame — the grandmother's data could indict her method, her reporting, or the app. My advisor says that is a whole other session. Should I gesture at it, name it, or drop it, and where in the arc would the gesture go if you recommend keeping it?
5. One question I can put to the room at the end that would reveal whether the students grasp the criterion rather than merely the vocabulary.

Please do not recommend readings or cite sources; I want the plan itself. Write everything in American English, and end with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one sentence stating the single most consequential sequencing decision in your plan and why.
