I've been in therapy for about a year now for anxiety, and my therapist is pretty CBT-flavored — she has me fill out thought records, rate how strongly I "believe" a given thought before and after examining the evidence for it, that sort of thing. It's genuinely helped, honestly. But a few weeks ago at a party I mentioned the thought records to my friend's husband, who's finishing a PhD in philosophy of mind, and he got this big grin and said something like: funny you should mention beliefs, there's this whole position in philosophy — Paul Churchland is the big name — that says beliefs and desires don't really exist at all. The idea is that "folk psychology," meaning the whole framework of attributing beliefs and desires to people to explain and predict their behavior, is basically our generation's phlogiston: a theory that made decent predictions for a long time, but whose central entities simply aren't there, and which neuroscience will eventually replace the way chemistry replaced phlogiston. I asked if that was just behaviorism again and he said no — the behaviorists thought inner states were just unscientific to speculate about, whereas these people are saying the states themselves don't exist to be speculated about, the way there's no such thing as demonic possession to have opinions about.

I've been chewing on this ever since and it bothers me more than I'd expected. On the one hand, my therapy seems to presuppose exactly what this guy says doesn't exist. When my therapist asks me to identify the belief driving my avoidance and then restructure it, she's treating it as though there's a real thing in there, with a definite content, that can be inspected and modified. If the eliminativist is right, is she doing something confused — like a doctor carefully adjusting my humours? On the other hand, the therapy plainly works; I'm measurably less anxious than a year ago. So either the eliminativist is wrong, or he owes me a story about what my therapist is actually doing all those times it *looks* like she's helping me with a belief — maybe something like retraining predictive patterns, where the propositional content is just how the process gets described from the outside and doesn't have to really exist? And my friend's husband hinted at a third option I didn't fully follow: that CBT and eliminativism might actually be natural allies, both treating belief-talk as a scaffold that could eventually be dropped.

Can you help me sort this out? I'd especially like to know whether the eliminativist can genuinely explain the success of therapy without smuggling beliefs back in somewhere, or whether working therapy amounts to a folk-psychological technology that would be near-miraculous if folk psychology were false. And if you think the eliminativist *can* explain it, tell me concretely what picture of my anxiety and my thought records he'd be left with — what is literally happening to me, in his story, when the worksheet asks me to write down what I believe about failing at my job?
