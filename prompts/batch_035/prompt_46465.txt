My friend and I keep arguing about the moral equality of combatants, and she has accumulated a track record of verdicts about who may be liable to attack in an unjust war. So far: a conscript on the aggressor's side, threatened with prison if he refuses, who sincerely and reasonably believes his country is acting in self-defense — she holds that killing him is no more justified than killing a soldier defending against aggression; a poor volunteer in the same war, equally sincere in that belief, who enlisted because the army was his only escape from destitution — also not liable; the general who planned the war while knowing the stated pretext was false — liable, and she would accept targeting him; and a mercenary in the same army, indifferent to whether the war is just — liable. Next week's case: a conscript who sees through the propaganda and knows full well the war is one of aggression, but who faces imprisonment and a destitute family if he refuses to fight. Will she say he is liable? I care less about the answer than about the candidate rules that fit her record so far — roughly, excused if one has good faith about the war's justice versus excused if one lacks a meaningful choice not to fight — what each rule predicts for the new case, which rule she seems to be implicitly running and why, and which further case, if she ruled on it, would best tell the two rules apart.
