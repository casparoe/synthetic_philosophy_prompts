My closest friend and I have been arguing about infinity for the better part of a decade, and last night's installment — four hours, over dinner, ending exactly where it began — has convinced me we need something better than another rematch. She is a finitist of a practical temper: she holds that every meaningful quantity is finite, that "infinitely many" is at most shorthand for an unending process, and that talk of completed infinite collections is a bookkeeping fiction that has been conning mathematicians since Cantor dressed it in set theory. I hold that Cantor's theory is a genuine discovery about abstract structure: that comparing collections by pairing is principled, that some infinities are genuinely larger than others, and that there is nothing incoherent about a completed totality of natural numbers. We both know the standard moves and have worn them out. What we have never done is write down, precisely, what we actually agree on, where our disagreement is real rather than verbal, and what would count as progress.

Draft the joint statement we should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both of us can accept — about Hilbert's hotel as a piece of reasoning, about what bijections do and do not establish, about where mathematics needs infinity only as a shorthand and where it seems to assert it; (2) a disagreements table stating our residual disagreements in wording each of us would actually endorse — her side phrased as she would phrase it, mine as I would; and (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. I can offer three candidates, though you may find better ones.

First, the part-whole question. We both grant Galileo's observation that the perfect squares can be paired one-to-one with the natural numbers, so that there are "as many" squares as naturals even though the squares are a proper part of the naturals. She says this is a reductio: a notion of "same number" that lets a whole be equinumerous with its part should be discarded, and a bijection establishes at most an equivalence between processes, not an equality of size. I say size for infinite collections simply is defined by the pairing criterion, and that clinging to the part-whole principle outside its finite home is like insisting subtraction must always be possible. So: which is dispensable, the part-whole principle or the pairing criterion? And is this a genuine disagreement, or partly a fight over who owns the word "number"?

Second, an occupancy question we invented last night, which neither of us could settle. In Hilbert's hotel with every room occupied, move each guest from room n to room 2n. Every even-numbered room is now occupied and every odd-numbered room is empty. Ask what fraction of the rooms are occupied. My friend insists any competent accountant would say half, and that the Cantorian response — that the occupied and empty rooms are equinumerous, and that the fraction question either has no answer or depends on the order of enumeration — shows the theory has lost contact with counting. I reply that the answer genuinely depends on how you enumerate: reorder the rooms and the same physical occupancy can be presented as mostly full or mostly empty, so it is not that the question has a hidden answer, but that "fraction" was never well-defined for infinite collections, and the hotel is simply where that becomes visible. We could not agree on whether this case is independent evidence for either side or merely the first crux restated. Settle that in the document: does the occupancy question bear on the part-whole dispute, or is it downstream of it?

Third, supertasks. A lamp is switched on and off infinitely many times over a shrinking interval — one minute, then half a minute, then a quarter — and we ask whether it is on or off at the two-minute mark. My friend takes the indeterminacy as evidence that completing infinitely many discrete steps is incoherent, and that completed infinities are not merely unphysical but self-undermining the moment you make them concrete. I am tempted to say the setup simply fails to specify the limit state, the way a non-convergent sequence of coin flips fails to specify a limit — the failure is in the question, not in infinity itself. Determine where exactly between us this crux lies, and whether there is a formulation of it that is purely mathematical, on which we could each record a prediction, rather than a clash of temperaments.

For each crux, include the case, argument, or thought experiment both of us would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — including on the occupancy question, where we genuinely do not know what we would each predict, so derive the predictions from our positions as I have described them rather than from us as people.

One final demand, because I am tired of symmetry. At the end, add a section in your own voice, marked as the drafter's assessment, in which you commit: say which crux you judge most likely to be resolved, and in whose favor; whether any part of our dispute is verbal, and which part; and why — a definite verdict with reasons, not a return of the options to us. If you think her part-whole principle can survive the pairing criterion, say so and defend it; if you think my side is smuggling "size" into a place where only structure exists, say that. We can always refuse to sign that section. But I want to know where someone who has read both of our cases and owes neither of us anything actually lands.
