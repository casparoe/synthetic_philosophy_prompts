I'm a high school junior enrolled in AP United States History, and my teacher has been preparing us for a seminar on the debate over annexing the Philippines after the Spanish-American War — the Treaty of Paris at the end of 1898, the arguments in the Senate over ratification, the Anti-Imperialist League with figures like Mark Twain and Andrew Carnegie on one side, and orators like Senator Albert Beveridge on the other. My friends keep insisting that the whole debate was really just about markets and racial hierarchy, and I don't think they're exactly wrong, but our teacher wants us to work through a specific conceptual lens: American exceptionalism, understood in three registers at once — as a historical thesis (that the United States was founded on universal principles rather than blood and soil, making it a genuinely new kind of state), as a normative claim (that America therefore has special obligations and must not behave like the Old World empires), and as an ideology (a flexible justification for whatever America happens to want to do). What struck me when reading the primary sources is that both sides in 1898 and 1899 seem to argue from exceptionalism: the imperialists said America was so uniquely capable and benevolent that governing the Philippines without absorbing it as a state was precisely what a new kind of power should do, while the anti-imperialists said annexation would make America exactly like the European empires and thereby destroy whatever was distinctive about it. That symmetry makes me suspect the concept is doing less analytical work than it appears to.

So here is what I would like you to do. Analyze the annexation debate as a test case for American exceptionalism, keeping the three versions distinct rather than letting them blur together. Map the main positions onto them: which speakers were invoking the historical thesis, which the normative claim, and which the ideological version, and did the same person slide from one to another when the argument needed it — for instance, from "America is different from empires" to "America is so different that its empire won't really be an empire"? Then tell me honestly where the framework breaks: what about the debate, and about what followed (the brutal war in the Philippines itself, the Insular Cases and the invented status of "unincorporated territory"), does exceptionalism fail to explain or actively misdescribe? Finally, give me a verdict on whether the episode confirms exceptionalism as a useful interpretive lens or instead exposes it as close to unfalsifiable — an idea that can rationalize any American action, including its opposite, by redescribing it as consistent with American uniqueness. If my friends' economic and racial explanations do work where exceptionalism doesn't, say so plainly. Please write the analysis as a structured essay in American English, at a level I can defend in seminar, and resist the urge to be diplomatic — if one of the three versions of exceptionalism survives the episode better than the others, I want to know which one and why.
