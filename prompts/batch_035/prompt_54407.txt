for my sociology of morality seminar we're doing this unit on campbell and manning's three moral cultures framework. rough version since that's all I've got: honor culture, where your worth lives in other people's heads so slights have to be answered or you lose standing; dignity culture, where worth is internal and stable, you don't dignify insults with a response, and serious wrongs get handed to formal authorities; and victimhood culture, which they describe as combining honor culture's sensitivity to slights with dignity culture's dependence on third parties — harms get emphasized and appeals go out to some big audience rather than to a court or a duel. our assignment is to run a real episode through the framework and I picked the justine sacco thing from december 2013, but the more I stare at it the less sure I am that the framework actually survives contact with it, so I want your read before I commit to writing it up.

facts so we're on the same page: sacco was a 30ish pr director at IAC, and on dec 20 2013 she tweeted "Going to Africa. Hope I don't get AIDS. Just kidding. I'm white!" right before getting on an ~11 hour flight to cape town with no wifi. while she was in the air the tweet went insanely viral — #HasJustineLandedYet trended worldwide, people were literally waiting for her at the airport, parody accounts popped up, and IAC fired her basically before her phone came back on. she then issued an apology saying she was ashamed of her insensitivity to the millions of people living with the virus.

here's what I want:

1. do the actual mapping. who in this episode is operating in which culture, and what does each culture say each actor should have done? the obvious seminar answer is that the twitter mob = victimhood culture (harm claims broadcast to third parties), but honestly the pile-on looks more honor-culture to me — a public reputation contest where the payoff is watching someone lose standing, and the punishment is in no way calibrated to the harm. am I wrong? and where does sacco's silence followed by the groveling apology sit — is that a dignity-culture move, a victimhood-culture move, or neither?

2. what does dignity culture actually prescribe here, for everyone involved? "just don't dignify it with a response" is easy to say, but does it also apply to IAC (did they owe her anything, or is firing her the dignity-culture version of escalating to the authority), and to bystanders? and notice that no individual victim was ever in the room — the joke was aimed at a category (africans / people with hiv), not at persons. does victimhood culture even make sense when the harmed party is a diffuse group and the appeal is to a global crowd of strangers rather than any authority or actual community?

3. where is the framework just silent? I'm thinking platform mechanics (retweets, the fact that thousands of shamers had zero stake, would never interact again, and never had to live with the outcome), the speed, IAC's incentive to cut her loose within hours. "culture" seems to presuppose a group with norms and repeat interaction — twitter mobs have neither, so is this even a culture at all?

4. the part I actually need to get right: does the episode support the thesis that dignity culture is giving way to victimhood culture, or is it evidence that the three cultures coexist and actors (or platforms?) pick between them tactically, situation by situation? like maybe what we saw was honor-culture behavior wearing victimhood clothes. and if that's right, what does it do to the normative question my professor keeps pushing — whether any one of the three cultures is morally superior to the others?

don't hold back on saying the framework fails on this case if you think it does, I'd rather find out now then in seminar. and no reading list or citations please — I have my sources, I want the actual analysis.
