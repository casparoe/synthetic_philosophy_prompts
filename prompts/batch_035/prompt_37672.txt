I am a retired astrophysicist — some thirty years of stellar spectroscopy behind me — who has spent the past two years working through philosophy of science and decision theory with a small reading group of fellow retirees. The topic that has most gripped me is what the literature calls anthropic or self-locating reasoning: how a lone observer should reason about hypotheses concerning how many observers there are, and which of them it is. My group keeps circling the same knot of cases, and I have come to suspect that my own verdicts do not sit comfortably together. I would like you to act as an expert on self-locating belief and observer-selection reasoning, to think carefully — perhaps brainstorming candidate principles before committing to any — and to tell me what, if anything, my verdicts add up to.

Here are the verdicts. First, in the Sleeping Beauty problem, I believe Beauty's credence in Heads upon waking should be one-third; the betting-frequency argument strikes me as decisive. Second, the doomsday argument — that my comparatively early birth rank is evidence that comparatively few humans will ever live — strikes me as simply wrong, and I cannot quite bring myself to accept that my census number carries information about humanity's future. Third, the hypothetical philosopher who, confronted with two physical theories of equal evidential support, where one of them posits a vastly larger population of observers in the universe, declares the larger-population theory overwhelming likely on the grounds that more observers means more chance that one of them would be me — that philosopher seems to me to be committing an absurdity; raw headcount cannot swamp the balance of the physical evidence. Fourth, and here I suspect I contradict myself, I am inclined to think that the fact that the cosmological constant falls within the life-permitting range does confirm something like a multiverse: the more universes there are, the likelier it becomes that one is life-friendly and that we find ourselves in such a one. Yet this fourth verdict feels uncomfortably like the very reasoning I dismissed in the third case, and I have not managed to convince myself that the two are relevantly different.

Then there is the case I keep going back and forth on: a fair coin is flipped; on Heads exactly one person is created in a plain white room, while on Tails a million people are created in a million qualitatively identical white rooms; I wake with no memory, fully informed of the setup, knowing only that I am in some white room. Ought I to be nearly certain the coin landed Tails, barely more confident than even, or somewhere in between? My own tentative principle — that in reasoning about my situation I should treat myself as a random sample drawn from among the observers compatible with my evidence — delivers verdicts of a sort on each case, though I can see it does not deliver all of mine. So please: lay out the candidate principles precisely (the self-sampling and self-indication families, or any others you find in the neighbourhood), say for each which of my verdicts it vindicates and which it overturns, state what you take to be the most defensible candidate and what it implies for my white-room case, and describe a new case, one I have not considered, on which the candidates importantly disagree. Please also indicate how confident you are in each of your principal claims and identify the single point in the analysis about which you are least sure.
