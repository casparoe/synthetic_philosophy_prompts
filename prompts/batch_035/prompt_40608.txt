My ethics seminar has been circling the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism all semester and on the last day someone rebuilt it into an argument that seems to eat its own tail, and now I can't tell if the objection is dead or was never what we thought it was. Here's the chain:

(1) Utilitarianism is objectionable because it aggregates across lives: it lets a serious loss to one person be outweighed by smaller gains to many, treating separate lives as if they were departments of one big life, where a loss in one department is made up for in another. That's the Rawls/Nozick point -- there is no social entity that both suffers the loss and enjoys the benefit.

(2) What makes that wrong, while ordinary intrapersonal tradeoffs are fine -- going to the dentist now to avoid agony later, saving now to be comfortable in retirement -- must be that in the intrapersonal case there is one subject who bears the cost and receives the benefit. The person who suffers the drilling is (roughly) the person who is grateful later.

(3) So it's the boundary of the person that does the moral work: tradeoffs within one boundary are permissible, tradeoffs across boundaries require something more.

(4) But personal identity over time isn't all-or-nothing. On the reductionist/Parfit picture it's psychological connectedness, which comes in degrees and thins out: after fifty years, or a stroke, or heavy amnesia, my connectedness to that future person can be no greater than my connectedness to a stranger.

(5) So if (3) is right, tradeoffs between me-now and me-in-2075 are only as permissible as the connectedness between us is strong. Where it's thin, imposing the dentist visit on that poor future person should be nearly as objectionable as imposing it on a stranger.

(6) But that's obviously wrong. We think someone who refuses the dentist out of concern for their future self's "separate claims" is being irrational, not scrupulous, and nobody thinks saving for retirement needs the same justification as conscripting a stranger to fund it.

(7) Therefore (3) is false -- the person-boundary isn't what does the work -- and the separateness objection in (1) loses its explanation. Either it rests on something else entirely, or it was never a good objection.

And then the kicker: my friend says he can run the same chain in reverse. If intrapersonal aggregation survives the thinness of connectedness, then whatever licenses it is not the metaphysical boundary either, and once you drop that, he thinks he can rebuild Harsanyi-style: behind ignorance about who you'll be, aggregating across lives is just prudence with extra steps, so utilitarianism wins. Same premises, opposite conclusion, which makes me think there's a equivocation hiding somewhere -- maybe in what "compensation" means in (2)? -- but I can't pin it down.
