Consider a thought experiment prompted by Strawson's Basic Argument: to be morally responsible for what you do, you must be responsible for how you are in the crucial mental respects that shape your action; but you cannot be responsible for how you are, since any act of self-shaping would proceed from a way you already were and did not choose, and so on without end — hence ultimate moral responsibility is impossible, whether or not determinism is true. Now imagine a self-modifying AI that at some point acquires the capacity to rewrite any component of its own decision-making, including the very values and dispositions it consults when deciding how to rewrite itself, and suppose it undergoes a long period of such explicit self-authorship. One might hope the regress bottoms out here in a way it never could for humans: at some point the system simply chose its own character, and so comes to be responsible for how it is. Please argue that this escape fails — that the Basic Argument applies to the thoroughly self-modifying system with essentially undiminished force. Along the way, clearly separate what is settled (e.g., the structure of the Basic Argument and what it claims to establish) from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of free will (e.g., whether responsibility for what you do really requires responsibility for how you are, and whether ultimate responsibility of this kind is a coherent demand at all), and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus view.
