I am writing a term essay on the Fischer controversy and have been backed into a corner by a friend's argument. I would like a careful diagnosis, not a survey of the historiography.

Some background. Fritz Fischer argued, in books published in the 1960s, that Germany's leadership deliberately worked towards a European war and used the July crisis of 1914 as the occasion to launch one; his critics replied that the German government risked a war it did not plan, and the ensuing controversy turned largely on evidence — the September Programme of 1914, the meeting between the Kaiser and his military advisers in December 1912, the "blank cheque" given to Austria-Hungary in July 1914, and Article 231 of the Versailles Treaty, which laid upon Germany and its allies the responsibility for the loss and damage caused by the war. My friend says that before any of that evidence matters, a conceptual question has to be settled, and he settles it as follows.

(1) Deserved blame presupposes agency: a thing is blameworthy for an outcome only if its own agency contributed to that outcome. A falling rock is not to blame, and a person is to blame only for what he did, decided, or allowed in the exercise of his own agency.
(2) Nations are not agents. The German nation had no beliefs, formed no intentions, and made no decisions. The men who had intentions — the Kaiser, Bethmann Hollweg, Moltke — had them individually; "Germany" did not. Whatever is true of Germany is true only in virtue of what is true of particular Germans and their institutions.
(3) So if the Fischer thesis is true, all it can strictly establish is that a handful of identifiable men deliberately used the crisis to bring about a war. That, and nothing else, is the whole fact of the matter.
(4) Therefore the sentence "Germany is responsible for the First World War" is never strictly true. At best it abbreviates a long conjunction of claims about particular Germans.
(5) If it is never strictly true, then Article 231 was not merely harsh or one-sided but a category mistake — of a piece with blaming a flood — and every later verdict of the same national form is strictly false as well.
(6) So no nation is responsible for any war or any other event, and none ever has been.

My friend anticipates the obvious reply — that the Berlin men acted as Germany, on behalf of the state — and says the "as" does no work: acting in the name of the state is merely a manner of acting, and adds no agent beyond the men themselves. He thinks the only escape from (6) would be to posit a German group mind hovering above the Wilhelmstrasse, which he takes to be refuted by its absurdity.

I cannot accept (6), and not only because of 1914. The same reasoning would make it strictly false that any state ever kept or broke a promise (only men sign), that any corporation ever defrauded anyone (only employees act), or that an electorate ever bore responsibility for what its government did in its name. Yet I also want to keep (1), and I am reluctant to accept (2) in any form that requires a group mind. So I am stuck. I suspect something has slipped, but I cannot tell whether the slip is in (3)'s "and nothing else", in (4)'s "strictly", or in my friend's dismissal of the "as".

Please take the role of a professional philosopher working on collective responsibility, the philosophy of action, and the philosophy of history. Think the problem through carefully, and before committing to a verdict, briefly brainstorm the live diagnoses. Then tell me which step to reject; explain why that step looked innocent; set out what each way of rejecting it costs; and assess whether my friend is right that the only alternative to (6) is a group mind. Please do not treat this as a question about the historiography of 1914: assume the empirical facts are as Fischer or his critics claim — the question is what, if anything, they would make a nation responsible for.
