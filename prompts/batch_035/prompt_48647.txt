I teach a mixed upper-level/grad seminar on moral psychology, about 14 students, and we have one 80-minute session on experimental philosophy. I don't want to lecture about the Knobe effect and have them nod along -- half of them have already seen the chairman vignette in some other course, and they treat the effect as a fun fact to recite ("people say the chairman intentionally harmed the environment, isn't that wild") instead of an actual research finding with actual methodological problems attached. So design me an in-class activity where they have to discover the effect and then break it.

The shape I'm imagining: they play subject first, before they know what's being tested -- maybe a disguised questionnaire at the start where the harm and help conditions are split across the room so nobody realizes the vignettes differ by a single word until we compare results. Then they become researchers: split into small groups, each group designs and runs a variant on the rest of the class. I want the variants to be genuinely open questions, not canned ones -- e.g. what happens when the side effect hurts the agent himself, or when the agent is a collective ("the board"), or when the agent explicitly says he doesn't care versus actively welcoming the side effect, or when you swap "intentionally" for "deliberately" or "on purpose." Some of these have actual published follow-up literature but I don't want them looking anything up mid-session, and you shouldn't build the prompt around which findings exist -- I'll fill that in later myself. The point is for them to feel how easy it is to run a flawed version and how hard it is to tell a good design from a bad one.

Include: concrete materials I can prepare with zero prep beyond printing (or just reading aloud), the split-room logistics so the conditions stay hidden, how groups should collect and tabulate their data in five minutes without laptops, and a debrief that does two jobs -- mapping the results onto the blame-vs-concept question the effect is supposed to bear on, and mapping the design process onto the standard objections people raise against x-phi generally (demand characteristics, order effects, participant populations, whether "intentionally" is even doing consistent work across vignettes). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- I'm guessing at least one group writes a vignette where the manipulation is way too heavy-handed, and at least a couple students will have already heard of the effect and contaminate the subject phase; how do I handle them without just excluding them. And be honest about which part of the debate the activity can't possibly reproduce in 80 minutes -- I assume the replication worries and the cross-cultural stuff -- and what I should just assert in the last five minutes.

One more thing: keep the reading level accessible but don't dumb it down -- these are students who can handle "conversational implicature" but I don't want to name Grice up front any more than I want to name Knobe, since naming things early ruins the discovery. The effect should show up in what the room does before anyone has a label for it.
