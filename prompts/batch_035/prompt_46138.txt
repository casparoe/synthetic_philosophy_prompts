I'm a novelist, and my last two books have been set in a lightly fictionalized version of the small mill town I grew up in. I've noticed something in my own drafts that I don't fully understand and would rather my workshop participants discovered than heard about: when my family's version of a local event and the newspaper's version diverge, my pen quietly sides with my family, and I only notice afterwards. This summer I'm running a three-hour workshop at a regional literary festival — twelve adult participants, mostly amateur memoirists and a couple of local-history enthusiasts — and I want the session built around the conflict between what a community remembers and what the record says, and the question of which should give way when they conflict. I don't want this to be a lecture about Halbwachs; I want them to feel the pull before it's named.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should refine, correct, or reject as you see fit. At the start, each participant receives an index card bearing a first-person "memory" of a fictional event — say, a fire at the Harwick flour mill in 1927 — written as if they'd heard it from a grandparent. The cards agree on the outline (the fire, the deaths) and disagree on the details that matter: how many died, whether the yard doors were chained, who carried whom out. Crucially, each card records whom the rememberer heard it from, so that when the cards are compared afterward a transmission chain is visible — and I want you to design the discrepancies so that the corruptions are not random noise but systematically drift toward the version of events that flatters the town: the owner becomes less culpable, the dead become braver, the town's response becomes more unified. Participants then tell "their" story to the group, and afterward, without warning, they are asked to draft — collectively — the forty-word inscription for the mill fire's centennial plaque, with the rule that the plaque can only be "installed" if all twelve sign it. Only once a text has been signed do you hand out the archival dossier: an inquest summary, an insurance ledger, a coroner's report, contradicting the plaque on several points. They must then revise or defend, and reach the signature rule again.

Please give me the full design: the cards' contents in enough detail for me to write them, the sequencing and timing across the three hours, the dossier, the materials, how to run the plaque negotiation so it doesn't collapse into a vote, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the problem — the way memory converged in the storytelling round even among people whose cards said otherwise; the plaque as an instrument with its own norms, where what I've privately started calling commemorative accuracy may simply not be the same thing as archival accuracy and the two may not be commensurable; and the question of whether "which should give way" is even the right frame or whether it mistakes what memory is for. It should also ask them, without answering for them, what the town owes the dead as against what it owes the documents.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I'd expect, at minimum, that someone will have an actual family tragedy that rhymes with the fictional one, so tell me how to handle that — and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself: my guess is real stakes, real dead, and the fact that the archivist is usually the powerful outsider rather than the neutral party the dossier makes him. Finally, since I'll be running this on real people with limited chance to pilot it: tell me how confident you are in each part of the design actually producing the intended collision, flag anywhere the setup quietly rigs the outcome toward a conclusion the participants should be reaching themselves, and single out the one element of the design you are least sure of.
