I'm on the governing board of our city's history museum, and we have to decide how to present the Holocaust in the new permanent exhibition, which opens in two years. I have no background in history or philosophy — I run a hardware store — but I've been reading on my own and I keep going back and forth, so I'd like a document I can circulate before our next meeting.

Here is the situation. Our head curator wants a sealed, single-room gallery devoted to the Holocaust alone, entered through a narrow passage, with nothing else in the room: his view is that putting it alongside other atrocities would soften it, turning it into one item in a series, and that its sheer scale and intent make it categorically different from other mass murders. A second faction, including two teachers on the board, wants a larger comparative gallery on modern atrocities — the genocide of the Herero and Nama, the Armenian genocide, the Holodomor, Cambodia — with the Holocaust as one exhibit among others, on the ground that isolating it makes it seem like an inexplicable event that fell out of the sky, and that visitors, especially schoolchildren, learn more from seeing the common machinery of these events than from reverence alone. A third board member, who read Zygmunt Bauman years ago and keeps pushing him on the rest of us, wants to fold the material into the planned wing on the history of modernity itself: on this view the Holocaust is not an interruption of civilization but something modern bureaucracy, railways, record-keeping, and the division of labor made newly possible, so it belongs next to the exhibits about the industrial revolution and the rise of the modern state.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend one of the three layouts, or a concrete hybrid, with reasons the other board members could actually engage with — including an honest treatment of the strongest argument for each of the two options I'm not recommending. Along the way, I'd like it to address the question I can't stop chewing on: whether the "uniqueness" claim is really a claim about the events themselves or about how we choose to remember, and whether presenting the Holocaust as the product of ordinary modern processes explains it or explains it away. I'd rather not have the memo simply assert that all three concerns can be perfectly reconciled; we will have to make choices about floor space, sequence, and what visitors see first, and the memo should take a position on those.

You may assume the museum has no political constraints from any government and full control over its own exhibition design.
