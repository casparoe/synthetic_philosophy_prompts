So here's my situation. I spent thirty-five years as a field ecologist, retired a while back, and somewhere along the way I got really into political philosophy — federalism especially. Subsidiarity, cooperative federalism in Germany, the Swiss with their cantons and referendums, the EU's whole "decisions as close to the citizen as possible" business. Great stuff to read about with coffee.

Meanwhile, in actual life, I chair the goverance committee of a national conservation volunteer federation: roughly 180 local groups, 11 regional bodies, and a national office with paid staff. Our single most reliable source of drama is level-of-decision fights. Should local groups pick their own restoration projects? Obviously yes, everyone says. But their own safety protocols for volunteer chainsaw crews? Their own grant applications? Can a local group take a public position on a zoning fight using the federation's name, or do positions only get taken nationally? Can the national office mandate that everyone log species records into one shared monitoring database? Right now we have nothing written down, so every one of these is ad hoc, decided by whoever argues longest at the meeting, and then relitigated.

It seems to me the philosophy of subsidiarity actually has content — it's not just "local is always better," it's about the lowest level that can handle the decision, which is a different thing. And the surrounding arguments have content too: externalities between groups, races to the bottom if standards fragment, capacity (a group having the *right* to decide isn't the same as being able to), uniformity as a value versus diversity as a value, the difference between a task being done centrally and being *imposed* centrally. But it's all stated at the level of principle, and I need a procedure.

Concretely: our committee meets monthly, these disputes land on my desk three or four times a month, and whatever we come up with has to be runnable by volunteers with zero philosophy background in under an hour. So basically a one-page checklist with a defined stopping point for a "decide locally" verdict, a "decide nationally" verdict, and an explicit "this is a values question, escalate to the trustees" verdict — the trustees are the ones who sign off on anything political.

Write me the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it hands off to humans because the question is genuinely one of values rather than facts, and please be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three real-ish cases: (1) the national office wants to standardize chainsaw safety training across all groups, over the objection of two regions whose local practices have been fine for years; (2) a local group wants to co-host a campaign rally with a political party under the federation's logo ahead of a land-use referendum; (3) the national office wants to require every group to log their species records into a shared national database, and one local group says their records are their own business. At least one of these should come out the way the local-autonomy people won't like — I suspect the safety one, but I want to see whether the procedure genuinely gets there or just hand-waves.

Structure it like this, please: "Bottom line" first (what the procedure is, one short paragraph); "The procedure" (numbered steps, what each question means, and what a sufficient answer looks like, about 700 words); "Three test cases" (roughly 200 words each); "Where it misfires" (what the procedure can't settle and when we should just not use it, about 200 words). And no reading list — I've got enough books, I need the thing to actually run at a Tuesday meeting.
