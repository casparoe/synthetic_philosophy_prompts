For several years I have kept an informal list of how surprised I would be by various pieces of news about life beyond Earth, and I have come to suspect my gut answers follow a rule I cannot state. I would like you to find it, or show me there isn't one.

The verdicts, as honestly as I can give them:

1. Microbial fossils on Mars, with chemistry clearly unrelated to anything on Earth: my confidence that life arises easily would jump considerably.
2. Living microbes on Mars whose biochemistry is recognizably cousin to ours, as though Earth and Mars swapped material early on: barely moves me. One origin, two addresses.
3. Living microbes in the ocean under the ice of a distant moon like Enceladus, even if their chemistry looks oddly familiar: moves me a lot, because I cannot believe anything alive crossed that far, so it must be a second origin however it looks.
4. Oxygen detected in the atmosphere of a planet dozens of light-years away: moves me only about half as much as it seems it should, because I have a vague memory that oxygen can pile up on a dead planet without any biology involved.
5. The fact that life seems to have shown up on Earth almost immediately, in geological terms, once the surface stopped being molten: here I flatly contradict myself. Half the time this feels like strong evidence that getting life started is easy; the other half I feel that we were only ever going to find a fast start, because a slow start would have meant nobody here to check — so maybe it means nothing at all.
6. Intelligent life: even granting that microbes are everywhere, I would be astonished by a second technological civilization in our galaxy. Yet when I try to treat the silence of the sky as evidence for that view, it feels like cheating — the distances are so absurd that quiet proves nothing, my gut insists.
7. Ruins of a technological civilization on another world that destroyed itself: my first reaction is that this would show intelligence arises often and simply doesn't last; a friend of mine says it should terrify me instead, because it would mean the hard step is still ahead of us rather than behind us.

There is also a verbal knot I can't untie: I am simultaneously comfortable saying "life is probably common in the universe" and "we are probably alone in any sense that matters," and I cannot tell whether that is a genuine contradiction or just two different things I mean by "life."

So: is there one principle — presumably something about what counts as independent information about how easy each step is — that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts? State it as precisely as you can, show which verdicts forced which clauses, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save and which I should surrender. Then apply it to two cases I have not listed: a "shadow" second genesis discovered right here on Earth, under our noses; and a clearly artificial radio signal arriving from far outside our galaxy.

Lead with your bottom line in your first sentence and give the reasoning after, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Along the way, keep separate what is settled among people who actually know the relevant science and what remains genuinely contested — I do not want a contested view dressed up as the consensus, and I suspect at least one of my verdicts rests on a science fact I have half-remembered rather than properly understood.
