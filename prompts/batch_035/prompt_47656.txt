As a novelist, I try to make philosophical abstractions bear down on my characters like weather — something they must answer for in their lives, not merely discuss. The chapter I am outlining turns on the special composition question: under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? The canonical illustrations — the Ship of Theseus, Tib and Tibbles — have been worn so smooth by repetition that a reader meets them as furniture rather than as a live problem, and I want something my reader will encounter for the first time.

I would like an example of a fictional dispute in which mereological universalism comes out intuitively worse than a common-sense, restricted answer to the special composition question. The dispute must carry real stakes for the characters — money, liability, or grief; never seminar-room stakes — and it must turn on whether some plurality of objects forms one thing, not on how a single thing persists through change. One direction you might take it: a marine insurer, committed in the strictest way to universalism, refuses a claim of total loss on the grounds that the vessel was never destroyed at all — its parts, now scattered across the seabed, still compose it, merely arranged differently than before the sinking — while the owner's family insists that what lies down there is no ship any longer, only debris arranged ship-wise. You are free to invent a different setting, however, if it serves the example better.

In presenting the example, please make clear precisely which judgment about composition divides the two positions, and explain why, in this setting, the restricted common-sense view should strike a reader as the more reasonable one.
