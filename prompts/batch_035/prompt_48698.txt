I am a final-year undergraduate taking an intermediate epistemology course, and our tutor runs a peer-assessment exercise in which each of us marks two classmates' answers to the midterm before Thursday's seminar. The scripts are anonymised, and the marks we assign are compared against the tutor's own as a calibration exercise, so I want to be careful rather than impressionistic. Below is the question and, verbatim, the script I have been assigned. I would rather not signal my own opinion of the answer; I want to know what a careful, scrupulous grader would say about it.

The midterm question was:

'Set out the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Are they best understood as versions of the same problem, or is there a significant disanalogy between them? What do they jointly suggest about the view that rational belief is nothing more than sufficiently high probability? Defend your answer.'

And the script reads:

'The lottery paradox, introduced by D. C. Makinson, begins with a fair lottery with a million tickets and exactly one winner. Each ticket is overwhelmingly likely to lose, so if rational belief is just sufficiently high probability, it is rational to believe of each ticket that it will lose. Yet we know that one ticket will win, so a rational believer ends up committed to a contradiction. From this, Henry Kyburg concluded that rational belief is not closed under conjunction.

The preface paradox, introduced by Henry Kyburg, concerns an author who has checked every claim in his book as carefully as he can and rationally believes each one. Knowing the track record of even careful authors, he also rationally believes that at least one claim in the book is false. So he rationally believes each of a set of propositions and rationally believes that not all of them are true, which is inconsistent.

The two paradoxes are structurally identical. In both, a set of individually rational beliefs cannot be jointly held, because their conjunction is inconsistent with something else the subject rationally believes: that one ticket wins in the lottery case, and the preface declaration in the preface case. Crucially, the belief that triggers the inconsistency is acquired in the same way in each case, namely from the statistics: just as the lottery player knows from the odds that some ticket will win, the author knows from base rates about similar books that his contains at least one error. So the paradoxes present one problem rather than two.

What they jointly show is that the threshold view of belief is in good shape. The real lesson of both is simply that rational belief fails to agglomerate: one can rationally believe p and rationally believe q without rationally believing their conjunction. Once this is granted, the lottery paradox is dissolved, and the preface paradox shows only that justified belief is not closed under entailment. The view that rational belief is sufficiently high probability therefore survives unscathed.'
