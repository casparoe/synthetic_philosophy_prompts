I have no academic background — I drive a delivery truck and read philosophy at night — but I've worked through a position on my own for a few years now, and I'd like to practice defending it against someone genuinely tough.

The position, in my own words: when we ask whether some arrangement is acceptable, we should look at what it does to each person, one person at a time. If a rule would impose a serious burden on one person, it cannot be made acceptable by pointing to the small benefits it spreads across millions of others, because those benefits never merge into anything anyone actually experiences — no one feels the total; each person feels only their own small piece. I arrived at this through T. M. Scanlon's contractualism, the idea that a principle for how people may be treated is wrong if anyone could reasonably reject it, where the rejection is made by individuals on their own behalf and complaints are compared person against person rather than piled onto a scale. But I don't want to litigate Scanlon scholarship; I want to defend the core thought that the pile never becomes a thing.

Here's what I'd like you to do: play a committed aggregator — the sharpest available defender of the view that the sum of small burdens is real and can outweigh a single large one. Not a strawman: give the position its best resources, be willing to concede small points when my argument earns them, but never give up the main claim. Stay in character until I write stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph so it reads like a live exchange rather than an essay.

My opening move: you aggregators keep telling me that a million mild headaches can outweigh one person losing a leg, because a million headaches sum to more than a leg. But the summing is something you do on paper. Out in the world there is no total of suffering sitting anywhere — there is this person's headache, that person's headache, and the one person's leg, and nothing besides those individual states. When you announce the total, you are not reporting something you located; you performed a calculation and then decided the calculation matters. So here is my question, and I want your answer to it first: setting aside the arithmetic, point to what in the world your total is a total of. If you can't point to anything, tell me why I should let a number nobody lives through decide who loses the leg.
