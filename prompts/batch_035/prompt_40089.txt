I'm putting together a small anthology of philosophical verse and need a piece on the paradox of tragedy: the puzzle that audiences seek out works that provoke grief and fear, seem to feel those emotions genuinely, and yet count the whole experience as good, whereas the same feelings in life are avoided and mourned. Please write a villanelle from the standpoint of a spectator at a tragedy. The two refrains should carry the two horns of the paradox — that the grief on offer is real, and that it is nevertheless welcomed — and the form itself, with the refrains recurring and finally meeting in the closing couplet, should do the work of showing that the two thoughts keep passing each other rather than resolving into a mere mood. Someone who knows the debate (conversion of borrowed pain into pleasure, catharsis, the idea that the artistic frame supplies a safety the world does not) should be able to tell which lines are doing which work; someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem. Please append a brief prose note stating how confident you are in the fit between each formal choice and the idea it is meant to carry, saying where the poem leans on a contested reading or simplifies a theory, and singling out the one interpretive point you are least sure of. Write the poem and the note in British English.
