My book club has been slogging through Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments for a few months, and the phrase that keeps coming up in discussion is the man within the breast -- sometimes shortened to the man within, sometimes with the impartial spectator bolted on. Last week it got used twice in two different arguments and I'm not sure it can do both jobs. One person said that for Smith, conscience is basically the voice of your society internalized: the man within is just the crowd you know, copied, so Smith can't actually explain why anyone ever defies their entire community or sticks to a judgment everyone around them rejects. But later the same evening, someone else said the man within the breast is what tells a person who has done right that they deserve approval even when nobody gives it, and that this is a real tribunal and not society's echo. Can the same phrase really mean both? Lay out the distinct precise claims that the man within the breast might stand for in Smith -- starting from the boring one where it just means you have a conscience at all, through readings where it's an internalized audience of actual spectators, up to strong readings where the internal spectator is impartial in a way that could outrank every real spectator -- and for each say whether it's trivial, defensible, or false. Then tell me which of those readings each of my two friends actually needs for their argument to work, and whether Smith has the resources to supply it. I remember him distinguishing wanting praise from wanting to be worthy of praise, and also something about us correcting the partiality of self-love by trying to view our own conduct with the eyes of others, and I suspect that stuff matters here. There's also a bit where the internal judge seems to get inflated into something more than human, some standard involving an impartial and well-informed spectator that never actually walks among us -- if the man within the breast ends up being that, is he still anything like the actual spectators whose approval sympathy was all about earlier in the book? That feels like it might be a genuine tension in Smith rather than my two friends just talking past each other, and I'd like your honest verdict on that. Keep it at the level of the text and ordinary reasoning, no need to name any secondary literature since I wont be able to look it up anyway.
