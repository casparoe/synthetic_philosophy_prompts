I teach an upper-level seminar on decision theory and rational choice, and I'm writing the closed-book final. My students have all memorized the survey-treatment version of the St. Petersburg paradox: Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 game, the divergent expectation, and the stock resolutions — diminishing marginal utility, risk aversion, and the observation that real casinos have finite bankrolls. Reciting those already earns a pass in my course, and that is exactly what I don't want the final to reward. What I want to test is whether a student can do things with the paradox that the standard summaries never ask for.

The essay question I'd like you to write should be built around three cruxes. First, the truncation point: if the house can pay out at most 2 to the power k dollars for some modest k, the expectation of the truncated game is roughly k dollars plus a small correction, and students should be able to explain why this is a fact about a different game rather than a resolution of the original — and, more generally, why any resolution that works by imposing an upper bound on utility makes the game's value finite only in the same deflationary sense. Second, the revenge structure: for any rule that assigns the original game a finite price p, there is a cousin of the game — say, one whose every payoff is raised by p, or one whose every outcome strictly dominates the corresponding outcome of the original — such that the very reasoning that produced p commits the rule to a price that is demonstrably too low or exposes its holder to a sure-loss arrangement; students should construct at least one such cousin explicitly rather than gesture at dominance in the abstract. Third, the fork: a strong answer must take a stand among (a) the expectation is infinite and it is nevertheless rational to pay any finite price one likes, in which case the student owes us an account of what makes some finite prices better than others; (b) expectation is the wrong statistic for this game, in which case the student should say what plays its role instead, perhaps drawing on repeated play, where the sample mean fails to settle down in the usual way; or (c) rational utility must be bounded above, so that the paradox functions as a reductio of unbounded utility scales. I would also like an optional bonus part built around a further variant in which the payoffs alternate in sign, so that the expectation series can be made to sum to different values depending on the order of summation and has no sum at all in the ordinary sense — but the question must specify that variant completely in its own words, since the exam tests reasoning, not recall of named games from the literature.

Please deliver three things: the exact wording of the question as it would appear on the exam paper, designed for sixty minutes and worth twenty-five marks with the bonus part worth five additional marks; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words; and a marking scheme that (i) lists the must-hit points with the marks each is worth, (ii) states explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer — my expectation is that the B answer will state the truncation fact and the diminishing-utility line correctly but fail to show why they resolve nothing, and will pick a fork without pricing it — and (iii) adds a one-line note per point on how students typically lose the marks there. The question must be fully self-contained: any game it mentions is to be defined within the question itself, so that no student is advantaged by having memorized the secondary literature.
