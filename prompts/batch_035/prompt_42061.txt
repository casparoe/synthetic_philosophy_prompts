So I've been hanging around rationalist spaces since like the old LessWrong days, very on board with the whole "shut up and multiply" worldview, and recently this has gotten me into some fun arguments. Latest one: a guy in my meetup was talking about L.A. Paul's transformative experience stuff (the book is Transformative Experience, 2014, which I haven't read, only heard about secondhand) and he summarized it to me roughly as follows: "Paul shows that big life decisions like whether to have a kid can't be made rationally at all. You can't know what having a child is like before you actually do it, so you can't know whether it will be good for you, and since rational choice means maximizing expected utility, deciding either way is basically a coin flip. These are her transformative experiences, and once you start looking they're everywhere, because every choice changes who you are at least a bit. She also knocks down the obvious counter, that you can just picture your future self with the kid and see how happy they look, so the upshot is that the whole Bayesian/expected-utility approach to life decisions just doesn't apply here."

Normally I'd push back hard on anything that sounds like "sometimes you just gotta go with your gut instead of doing the math," but he's usually pretty careful, and I'll admit the parent thing bugs me — people do say "you can't possibly understand until you're a parent," and it's not obvious to me how the EV calculation is supposed to handle that. So I'd love a sanity check on the summary itself. How much of it would Paul actually endorse, where is it subtly off, and is anything straight up wrong? In particular I'm curious whether the "can't know what it's like" part and the "can't know if it's good for you" part are the same claim or two different claims the summary is squishing together, whether "every choice changes you a bit" is really her point or whether she has some way of marking off the genuinely transformative cases from ordinary ones, and whether the imagined-future-self thing is really supposed to be a knockdown reply or whether Paul has a response to it. And the big one: does anything in the correct version of the view actually support my friend's conclusion that decision theory as such is inapplicable to these choices, or is that him overreaching? Please be specific about what's an exaggeration vs a genuine implication, since I want to know what I can fairly throw back at him at the next meetup.
