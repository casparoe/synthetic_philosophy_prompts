I serve on a university committee charged with drafting a public account of our institution's expansion in the late 1960s, during which a working-class neighborhood bordering the campus was cleared for new buildings. The archival record — trustee minutes, correspondence with the city, contemporaneous newspaper coverage — presents the episode as a program of negotiated purchases and generous relocation assistance. The displaced community, which still exists a few miles away and maintains a vivid oral tradition, remembers bullying appraisers, threats of eminent domain, and promises about rehiring and replacement housing that were never kept. Our task is to decide what the historical text on the new memorial should say, and at our last meeting a colleague from the sociology department, arguing that the committee should simply adopt the community's version, shut down discussion of the archives with the remark that history is written by the victors. The phrase was received as if it settled the matter.

I teach philosophy of science and I am usually the one guilty of demanding conceptual clarity, but slogan-analysis is not my subfield and I found myself unable to articulate precisely what bothers me about the move. It seems to me that the sentence could be doing several quite different jobs: stating a near-truism about the sociological composition of surviving records, making an empirical claim about deliberate falsification, issuing an epistemic verdict that written records as a class are no more trustworthy than memory, or asserting something normative — that the testimony of the defeated carries a kind of standing or authority the victors' documents cannot claim. My suspicion is that only the truism is plainly true, that the truism does not by itself yield my colleague's conclusion, and that the stronger readings needed to get there are contestable at best. But I may be wrong, and the committee meets again in two weeks.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims packed into that one sentence, ordered I suppose from the least to the most contentious? For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and — this is the part I most need — say explicitly which of the readings would actually support the inference my colleague drew from it: that the community's memory ought to govern the memorial text in cases where it conflicts with the documentary record. If several readings would support it, I would like to know what independent reasons one would have to accept those readings, since I suspect the committee members assenting to the slogan were not consciously committing themselves to them. A menu I can circulate before the meeting would be ideal.
