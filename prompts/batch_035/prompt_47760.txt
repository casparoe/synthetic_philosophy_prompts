I teach a decision theory seminar — 14 students, upper-level, one 90-minute session, and they haven't read anything on this specific topic beforehand. Design an in-class game that gets them to argue themselves into expected-value fanaticism and only notice where they've arrived at the very end. The shape I want: they start by endorsing some choice principle on boring, ordinary gambles (where it seems obviously right), then the payoffs get scaled up and the probabilities scaled down step by step, and the principle they themselves committed to does all the rest of the work. I want them to construct the reductio, not have me announce it.

Please include: rules, materials, a payoff scheme using points or candy or a silly prize rather than real money, and a rough timeline for the session. Somewhere in the middle there should be a phase where a few participants play "muggers" — they get to invent their own offers, with made-up astronomical stakes and probabilities that are pure story (Pascal's-mugging style) — so the group runs into the difference between an objective tiny chance and a tiny credence in somebody's testimony. That distinction should show up in what happens, not in anything I say.

Then a debrief that maps the stages of the game onto the philosophical issue: where exactly the fanaticism bites, what the live escape routes are (bounded utility, refusing to treat tiny probabilities as commensurable with large ones, dropping the aggregative assumption, or just biting the bullet and accepting fanaticism), and what it costs to take each exit. Be frank about which part of the problem the game can't reproduce — nobody in the room can actually deliver 10^40 utils, so the "it's all nonsense" intuition never gets a fair test — and tell me what the facilitator has to supply by hand. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it.
