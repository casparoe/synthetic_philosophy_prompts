Write a dialogue between two historians arguing about presentism — roughly, the question of whether historians can (or even should try to) escape the moral and conceptual standards of their own time when judging the past.

Set it as a conversation between Marta, who defends something like an escape-hatch view, and Jules, who thinks the escape is illusory. Give them roughly equal strength — Marta should get good replies in, not just serve as a punching bag.

To make this concrete and less like a term-paper topic, have them work through a semi-made-up case: an early 19th-century industrialist, "Beaumont," who was unusually generous with wages and schooling for child workers by the standards of his city, and yet actively lobbied against laws that would have banned child labor altogether. Marta's line: judging Beaumont by our standards is anachronistic score-settling, and praising him relative to 1820s Manchester is the only fair comparison class. Jules should push back with at least these kinds of points (in their own words):

1. "The standards of the time" was never one thing — abolitionists, labor radicals, and worried parents existed in the 1820s too. Choosing the "average" or "typical" standard is itself a value-laden, present-day choice about which contemporaries get to count as the era's conscience.
2. Jules proposes what they call a "grading curve" worry: the further back you go, the lower the bar drops, so identical acts get judged more leniently just by being older — which seems arbitrary. (Have Jules invent this framing themselves rather than citing anyone.)
3. Marta fires back that we accept context-relative grading elsewhere — a good 1820s doctor who didn't wash their hands wasn't negligent by 1820s lights. Jules should try to say what, if anything, distinguishes the moral case from the epistemic/medical one, and Marta should challenge the distinction.

Also let Marta make a positive point of her own that Jules has to actually engage with: that a historian who grades the past by the present stops explaining the past and just re-announces what we already beleive, and explanation is the historian's actual job.

Don't have them resolve it too neatly, but don't let them talk past each other either — each reply should build on what the other just said. Aim for maybe 700-1000 words, natural spoken register, no lecturing.
