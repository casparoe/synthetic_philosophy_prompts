I'm a graduate instructor wrapping up a semester-long seminar on political corruption, and the final session is on whether the category travels: whether what gets coded as "corrupt" in the anticorruption frameworks we studied — the transparency indices, the conventions with their shared definitions — picks out a fixed feature of certain acts, or whether the same act counts as a gift, a favor, or a bribe depending on the relation it is embedded in. The discussion kept circling one contrast. In a gift economy, the obligation created is diffuse, open-ended, and never fully discharged: the point is to keep the relation going, so the return is unspecified in amount and in time. A bribe, by contrast, prices a specific act, and once the account is settled the relation can end — often the briber prefers that it end. Clientelism sat uneasily between the two: a long, personal, asymmetric relation of exchanges that looks, from the transparency side, like organized corruption, and from the inside, like loyalty and care. We ended the session without agreeing whether the difference is in the act or in the surround, and I'd like to close the semester with a poem that holds that open rather than settling it.

Write a sestina. The six end-words are, in the first stanza's order: gift, debt, return, favor, price, name. After the first stanza, follow the standard rotation, so the words keep returning in altered company — which is the point. The form should do the philosophical work: the poem's claim, if it has one, is that nothing in the giving itself settles what it is, and the sestina enacts this by bringing the same word back with a different moral charge depending on the six words around it, just as the same act takes its character from the relation around it.

The content should be accurate enough that someone who works on this would not wince: the gift's obligation unquittable and unpriceable, the bribe's account closed on delivery, clientelism's long ledger in which the entries are people rather than sums. The envoi should place "gift" and "price" in a single line — the one place in the poem where the two finally touch — arranged so that the line stays true whichever of the two words the reader lays the weight on. Please don't let it turn into either a sermon about universal standards or an apology for patronage; the poem should leave us where the seminar actually ended, which is holding the question.
