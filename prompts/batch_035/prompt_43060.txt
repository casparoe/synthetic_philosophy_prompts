For a debate exercise, I would like you to play a committed philosopher of the classical Nyāya school, and I will argue against you. I am a high school junior who became interested in Indian philosophy after a world history unit, and I want to test whether I actually understand the Nyāya position on knowledge by pressing on its most controversial claim. Since you should not need any outside materials, here is a summary of what your character believes, drawn from what I have read:

- The school holds that there are exactly four pramāṇas — independent sources of knowledge: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and reliable testimony (śabda). Each is a distinct way in which the mind can come to know something, and none can be reduced to the others. Your school's signature claim, the one its rivals denied, is that testimony from a trustworthy speaker is a source of knowledge in its own right, not merely a convenient bundle of perceptions and inferences done by someone else.
- You hold that inference has a fixed five-membered structure: the assertion, the reason, the example, the application of the example to the case at hand, and the conclusion. When you argue, this is the form your arguments take.
- You hold that genuine knowledge grasps its object as it really is, and that error is not the awareness of something nonexistent but the misplacement of something real — the standard case being a man who, in poor light, takes a shell for silver: there really is a shiny thing before him, and there really is silver in his memory from elsewhere, and the error consists in the mind's placing the remembered silver in the present shell.
- You are a debater by profession: patient with a respectful opponent, contemptuous of a sloppy argument, and entirely unwilling to shorten the list of pramāṇas.

Rules for the role-play: stay in character until I write "stop"; speak in the first person as this philosopher; keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like a live exchange; concede small points when my arguments merit it, but never concede that any pramāṇa can be collapsed into another; gloss any Sanskrit term briefly the first time you use it, since I am a student; and if you need something clarified about what I have said, ask for the clarification as the philosopher would, not as an assistant.

Here is my opening move. Your school counts reliable testimony as a fourth source of knowledge, but I think it is just inference wearing a costume. When my chemistry teacher tells me that sodium and water react violently, I believe her because I have inferred, from her track record on hundreds of earlier claims that I could check, that she is a competent and honest speaker. That looks like an ordinary inductive inference about the speaker, of exactly the kind your second pramāṇa already covers; the testimonial part adds nothing that inference could not supply. And notice the deeper problem: if you ask me how I know she is reliable, the evidence will itself arrive mostly through testimony — textbooks, other teachers, reports of her past predictions — so your fourth pramāṇa seems to rest on itself, which looks circular to me. Either way, the conclusion follows: your fourfold list is a filing convenience, not a discovery about the structure of the mind, and the honest count of pramāṇas is three. Defend the fourth.
