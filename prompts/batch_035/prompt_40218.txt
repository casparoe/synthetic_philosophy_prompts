I'm on the benefits committee for the graduate-student health plan at a large university, and we're stuck on a case that's turned into a weirdly heated email thread. A DMA piano student has congenital, progressive sensorineural hearing loss, mild-to-moderate, that's never been treated. Standard hearing aids would restore her fully to normal conversational hearing -- no dispute there, we'd cover it in a heartbeat. But she's asking us to cover a newer high-fidelity device that would also extend audibility into the higher frequency ranges that typical listeners can't hear well, because her whole livelihood depends on discriminating overtones that even normal ears catch only partially. Her audiologist's letter argues that years of training have literally reorganized her auditory cortex, so her functional "baseline" was always above species-typical, and the premium device just restores *her* baseline rather than exceeding it.

The committee has fractured into camps. One member wants a bright-line rule: we cover whatever restores species-normal functioning, and the increment beyond that is enhancement, full stop, since our charter says the plan exists to treat illness. A second thinks that's too crude -- he points out that we already cover, say, antidepressants for someone who'd still be functional-but-miserable without them, so we clearly don't only treat deficits against the species baseline. A third says the therapy/enhancement distinction is bankrupt altogether and we should just be doing cost-effectiveness like a sensible insurer, but she can't say how that squares with the charter language. And the pianist herself, when she came to speak, said something that stuck with me: she pointed out that if she'd lost her hearing in an accident we'd feel obviously obligated to restore everything she'd trained herself to have, and she doesn't see why being born with the loss changes the counterfactual.

We meet in two weeks and I've been asked to circulate a written opinion beforehand, since I'm the one who keeps saying the camps are talking past each other. Draft the memo: it needs to recommend something concrete we can actually vote on (approve the premium device, deny it, or approve with some condition), give a principled rule we could apply to future enhancement-ish requests without opening the floodgates, and deal honestly with the strongest version of whichever position it rejects -- including the possibility that the third member is right and the whole distingushion should be abandoned. Please don't just restate the "medical necessity" boilerplate from our policy, and don't pad it with citations; I mainly need the reasoning worked out. Also, whatever rule you land on, flag the weird cases it would break on, because someone on this committee WILL bring up tattoo removal or Viagra at the meeting.
