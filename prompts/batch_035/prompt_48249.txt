I'm a grad student putting together a session for our moral psychology reading group (about 10 people, everyone has some metaethics but the epistemology background is very uneven) on the moral testimony debate — whether you can pick up moral knowledge, justification, or understanding secondhand the way you'd get a weather report secondhand, and whether there's something off about deferring to others on moral questions even when the deference works. I'm tired of the usual two-camp framing, pessimists vs optimists, because everyone in the group will just pattern-match to whichever camp they already liked and we'll learn nothing. What I want instead is a decision tree that sorts people by cases first and attaches the position labels only at the leaves.

Build it like this. First the branch questions: each one should be a concrete case a person can answer without knowing which position it's leading them to. The tree has to get at, at minimum: (i) whether the problem with moral deference is epistemic (testimony just can't convey moral knowledge or justification) or moral (it can, but relying on it is defective somehow — a failure of concern, or of caring about the matter through your own judgment) or both; (ii) whether the epistemic worry, if any, is about forming the belief at all versus about acting on a borrowed belief at the point of decision — I keep recieving pushback from people who think testimony is fine for belief but can't guide action, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a real position or just confusion, so I want the tree to make that call; (iii) whether the objection is specific to morality or generalizes to other normative domains — aesthetic deference, deference about what's fitting to feel, deferring to experts generally; and (iv) somewhere in there, a case about deferring on the emotions themselves: a friend tells me I *should* feel betrayed by how someone treated me, I notice I don't, and I'm not sure whether to outsource the anger. That last case is under-theorized but I want it on the tree anyway; if it doesn't sort cleanly, say so plainly and place it as best you can rather than inventing a tidy answer for it.

Then the leaves: name each one as the literature names it where a name exists, 'unnamed' where it doesn't, with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I'm expecting at least eight leaves. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate, and for answer-combinations nobody holds, diagnose why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And please commit throughout: no "one might place this here, or alternatively there." Make the placement calls and give your reasons, and where a call is genuinely close, say which way you went and what tempted you the other way, briefly. No citations or reading lists — just the positions themselves.
