1. dutch book arguments show that if your credences aren't sharp numerical probabilities, you're in principle exploitable. so ideal rationality = precise credences, and us messy humans should be approximating that target, even if we never hit it exactly.

2. the subjects in the ellsberg experiment are just wrong. paying a premium to avoid the ambiguous urn is a bias, same family as loss aversion, and the fact that basically everyone does it doesn't make it rational.

3. BUT i also think that in pascal's mugging type cases, refusing to act on a tiny probability that you basically made up is the rational move, not a failure. demanding fake precision from yourself there is the actual mistake.

4. learning is bayesian updating, full stop. the whole rationality project is approximating an ideal bayesian reasoner, and everything else (heuristics, etc.) is second best for limited agents.

5. there is no fact of the matter about what my credence in the simulation hypothesis "should" be. reasonable people with the same evidence spread over a whole range of priors, and that's not me being ignorant of my own mind — the imprecision is real, it lives in the epistemology not in my introspection.

6. for cause prioritization, rough EV estimates still beat vibes, so even when the numbers are super uncertain you should multiply anyway and go with the result.
