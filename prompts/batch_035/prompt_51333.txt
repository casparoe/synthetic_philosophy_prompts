so I write about AI and ideas for a magazine, and something keeps coming up when I interview ML people: that training models the way we do now -- feedback, rewards, no explicit rules for everything -- is just following Turing's own recipe. They lean on his child machine from the 1950 paper (build a machine with roughly a child's initial state, then educate it) and even more on his 1948 report, where he sketched networks that get organized by pleasure and pain signals. As far as I can tell two claims ride on this analogy in my interviews: (1) that training by feedback, like RLHF, is the natural and maybe the only way to get values into a system, and (2) that a model which misbehaves is like a kid misbehaving -- a developmental phase that good parenting will fix. I want an audit of Turing's child-machine analogy, not a tribute. What actually maps onto what -- the child, the genes or initial weights, the parent, the environment, the reward signal -- then where the mapping breaks. The break I keep wondering about: you can copy and rewind a machine, which no parent can do to a child, and in modern training the "parent" sees the whole inside of the "child," which is the opposite of Turing's point that the teacher of a child machine would be largely ignorant of its internals. And then tell me whether either of the two conclusions people draw actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Format: exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, and nothing else -- no intro, no conclusion.
