For a seminar paper I've been asked to state my position on distributive justice and then defend its coherence, and I'd like a sanity check on the coherence part before I write the defense. Here is the position:

1. The correct principle of distributive justice is luck egalitarianism: inequalities are unjust when and only when they trace to brute luck; inequalities that trace to option luck — outcomes of gambles people knowingly and voluntarily took — are not unjust, however unfortunate.
2. I'm a compatibilist about free will, and I think people are genuinely responsible for their voluntary choices; this responsibility is precisely what makes it permissible to let option-luck inequalities stand.
3. At the same time, I accept that the character traits that generate choices — ambition, prudence, willingness to take advice — are themselves substantially products of the natural and social lotteries: your diligence is not something you chose.
4. I believe it is just, not merely expedient, that people who work harder and choose more prudently do better materially.
5. I hold, as a matter of justice rather than of charity or humanity, that no one may be allowed to fall below a decent minimum, no matter how they got there.
6. On a case from class: a homeowner who was repeatedly warned, declined flood insurance because he preferred a nicer car, and then lost everything in a predictable flood deserves nothing beyond that minimum — his loss is his to bear.

Which pairs of these are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity and say what would have to give in each; then tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most. I'd rather learn where the real trouble is than have everything flagged as equally problematic.
