so I've been telling people for a while now that I'm basically a Keynesian about probability, and I'm about to teach a session on it, so before I stand up in front of people I want to know if my own package is even coherent. here's roughly the set of things I say I believe:

(1) Keynes was right in the Treatise on Probability that probability is a logical relation between a hypothesis and your evidence for it — it's not just a feeling in your head, two people with the same evidence should land in the same place. and part of what convinced me is his point that some of these relations are non-numerical: sometimes you can just tell that one argument is stronger than another without being able to say by how much, and sometimes you can't even rank two arguments at all.

(2) that said, I think Ramsey scored a real hit with the betting-odds point: whatever probabilities are, if you want to see what a person's are, look at how they're prepared to bet, and honestly this is how I reason myself — I put numbers between 0 and 1 on things and do expected utility calculations with them. I do this basically every day.

(3) the Ellsberg urn stuff convinces me that it can be rational to refuse sharp probabilities. I'd take a somewhat worse bet on the urn where I know the composition is 50/50 over a better bet on the one where I don't, and I don't think that makes me irrational — I think it tracks something real that the pure bayesian picture misses.

(4) the principle of indifference is a mess as usually stated, but it's salvageable, roughly the way Keynes tried to in the Treatise — you only apply it once you've carved the alternatives into genuinely symmetric pieces and have positive reason to think the symmetry holds. and I feel like I need something like this, because if (3) is right that unknown-composition cases resist sharp numbers, I need some principled story about which ignorances get probabilities and which don't.

(5) I read the 1937 QJE article as making a stronger claim than "credences here are hard." when Keynes says about the prospect of a European war or the price of copper twenty years hence that there's no scientific basis on which to form any calculable probability whatever, and "We simply do not know", I take him to mean: there's no fact of the matter there at all, not even an interval or a ranking, and no rational requirement to have one. that's the deepest version of the view and I want to keep it.

my main worry is that (2) is doing all the actual work in my life while (1), (3) and (5) are my Sunday beliefs. but I genuinely can't see which of the five has to go, and I'm not going to pretend I can. so: go through the set, tell me which pairs are really in tension and how badly, which ones only look like they conflict, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs me. rank them by severity rather than giving me an undifferentiated list of "hmm, interesting issues," please — I need to know which one is actually load-bearing. also fine to tell me if the whole thing collapses into a known modern position (imprecise probabilities or whatever), as long as you're explicit about what I'd have to give up to get there.
