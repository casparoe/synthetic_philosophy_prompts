I teach a second-year module on decision theory, and every year the two-envelope paradox eats an entire tutorial: two envelopes, one containing twice as much as the other, you pick one at random, and a plausible-looking computation — the other envelope is equally likely to hold double or half of your amount, so its expected value is one-and-a-quarter times yours, so switch, except the same argument applies after switching, ad infinitum. My students arrive with the standard responses half-digested (the complaint that the same symbol is made to do duty for two different random variables, or that the computation quietly assumes the other envelope is equally likely to be double or half no matter what yours contains), and I want to force them to evaluate responses against a standard rather than merely collect them. So before we touch the literature, I want to give them a specification: write the desiderata that any adequate resolution of the paradox must meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp. At a minimum the list should address where exactly the 5/4 argument goes wrong (a resolution that denies the conclusion without locating the fallacious step has not earned its keep), what changes when the envelope is opened versus left sealed, and what the resolution is for — advice to an agent at the moment of decision, a theorem in expected-utility theory, or a psychopathology of bad reasoning — since adequacy is relative to that.

Two hard cases the specification must handle, and they pull in opposite directions. First, a distribution where sticking is always right: suppose the smaller amount is £1 with probability 2/3, £2 with probability 2/9, £4 with probability 2/27, and so on (the pair (£2^n, £2^(n+1)) with probability 2/3 × (1/3)^n). A short calculation shows that conditional on seeing any particular amount in your envelope, the other envelope has expected value seven-eighths of it — so you should keep yours whatever you see — even though the envelopes were prepared symmetrically and, before opening, neither can be preferred. Second, the mirror image: if instead the smaller amount is £2^n with 'probability' proportional to 1 for each n — a uniform distribution over the powers of two, which cannot be normalised — then conditional on any observed amount, the other envelope is genuinely equally likely to be double or half, and switching is favourable for every value you might see. Your desiderata should say what a resolution owes us in each case: whether the first is a real counterexample or a trick of infinite expectations, whether the second shows the 5/4 reasoning was secretly coherent all along under an improper prior, and whether any set of desiderata that handles both can also demand a single unified diagnosis of the original sealed-envelope argument.

Finally, flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a suspicion that 'locates a definite fallacy in the 5/4 computation' and 'correctly endorses switching in the improper-prior case' are in tension, and I would like the list to be honest about that if so — and identify which single requirement does the most to eliminate the standard resolutions. Then, briefly, score the familiar fixes against your list, keeping the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard.
