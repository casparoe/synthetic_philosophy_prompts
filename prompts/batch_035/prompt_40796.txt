You are a professional philosopher who specializes in decision theory and moral uncertainty, so please reason carefully and systematically rather than reaching for a quick answer. I'd like you to begin with a brief brainstorm in which you lay out what makes this case hard before settling on your final ratings.

Here is the situation. I work as a policy analyst in a mid-sized city's public health department, and over lunch a colleague posed a hypothetical that has been bothering me ever since. Suppose a city official must choose between two programs and cannot fund both. Program A would cull the city's rat population and is projected to prevent about forty cases of serious leptospirosis per year. Program B would expand an after-school music program benefiting a few hundred children. The official's credences are distributed as follows: 50% consequentialism of a fairly standard aggregate-welfare kind, 30% a Kantian view on which the culling treats sentient creatures merely as a means and is therefore impermissible no matter the health benefits, and 20% a contractualist view under which the strong claims of the identifiable human patients outweigh the weaker claims of the rats. Crucially, the official can see no principled way to compare the magnitudes of choiceworthiness across these theories: the consequentialist verdicts come in units of expected welfare, the Kantian verdict in a categorical prohibition, and the contractualist verdict in claims-based terms, and it is opaque how much worse a violation of the prohibition is supposed to be than losing a few units of aggregate welfare. The official also knows that whichever program is chosen, the decision is effectively irreversible for a decade.

My question is a rating task. Consider four ways the official could proceed: (1) "my favorite theory" — simply act on the theory with the highest credence and ignore the rest; (2) moral hedging — choose the option with the highest expected choiceworthiness, making some best-guess normalization of choiceworthiness units across theories; (3) a moral analogue of maximin — choose the option whose choiceworthiness is highest on the theory on which it fares worst, so long as no option is catastrophic on every theory; and (4) a bargaining-theoretic approach — treat the three theories as if they were parties negotiating behind a veil of ignorance about which one turns out to be true, and choose the compromise a fair bargain among them would select. Please assign each of the four decision procedures a score from 0 to 10 for how well it handles this particular case, and also place the four into tiers reflecting your overall confidence in them as practical guides here. In your write-up, say in a few sentences why each lands where it does, paying particular attention to the lexical-priority problem raised by the Kantian categorical prohibition, the intertheoretic comparison problem, and whether each procedure is even computable by an actual official rather than an idealized agent. Please commit to concrete numbers rather than hedging, and be explicit about which features of the case drive the differences between the scores.
