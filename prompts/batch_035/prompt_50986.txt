The debate over cognitive penetration — whether beliefs, desires, and expectations can alter the content of perceptual experience itself, rather than merely the judgments we form on its basis — has been running in some form since the "New Look" psychology of the 1940s, and the latest round, set off by a widely discussed critique arguing that every claimed top-down effect suffers from one of a small set of post-perceptual pitfalls (effects on memory, judgment, or attentional selection rather than perception proper, plus an El Greco-style error in which the perceiver is imagined as a painter who paints what he sees and therefore sees as he paints), has hardened into fixed positions: one side demands experimental designs that exclude every post-perceptual confound, while the other replies that the demand cannot be met without smuggling in the very boundary under dispute. Meanwhile, the disputants largely agree on the experimental results themselves; they disagree about how to classify them. My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical disagreement at all but a verbal one about where "perception" is supposed to stop and "cognition" begin — that the two camps are really answering different questions, one about which computations early visual processing performs and one about the epistemic profile of experience as it presents itself to the subject. Is that right, or is a genuine hidden empirical question about the architecture of vision doing the work? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says, say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — an agreed operational criterion, a dissociation, a reframing of the question — and be honest about whether the impasse signals that the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard.
