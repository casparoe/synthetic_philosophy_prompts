For the unit on epistemic closure in my graduate epistemology seminar, I would like a written dialogue — on the order of 1,200 to 1,600 words — between two equally competent epistemologists: one who defends the closure of knowledge under competent deduction, and one who defends Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and accepts, with eyes open, that closure fails.

The setting should be a concrete case that I want the dialogue to be built around, rather than the zoo animals the literature is already saturated with. Call it the control-room case: Verity, a night-shift operator at a small research reactor, knows from the panel that the cooling system is running nominally. The panel is reliable, and had something gone wrong within its sensor range, it would have said so. But she also competently deduces, from what the panel tells her, that there is no slow leak occurring in a part of the loop the panel does not monitor. If there were such a leak, the panel would still read nominal: her evidence is insensitive to that possibility. So the tracking theorist says she knows the premise, knows the entailment, and yet does not know the conclusion; the closure theorist says this is absurd, and that the moral should be drawn against sensitivity.

Requirements for the dialogue:

1. The closure defender must press the abominable conjunction objection in its control-room form — that the tracking theorist is committed to Verity knowing that the cooling system is nominal while failing to know that there is no unmonitored leak — and must do more than register that this sounds bad: she must argue that the badness is semantic rather than a pragmatic effect of asserting a weaker thing alongside a stronger one.

2. The tracking theorist must respond with the resources Nozick actually gives him, in particular the restriction of closure to deductions that preserve the truth-tracking method, and the thought that we can know a conjunction's conjuncts and know the entailment without knowing the conjunction, in a way that parallels how we can justifiably believe each of two premises without justifiably believing their conjunctive aggregation. He should not merely bite the bullet; he should explain why denying closure in cases like Verity's is a principled price, not an ad hoc patch.

3. At least once, each character must concede something real to the other — for instance, the closure defender conceding that some closure-looking principles (multi-premise closure, closure under disjunction introduction) are independently hard to hold, and the tracking theorist conceding that his method-relative restriction threatens to make knowledge so fragile that ordinary claims to know the panel's verdict become hard to state without relativization.

4. The exchange must include a genuine back-and-forth about what would decide the matter: the closure defender should articulate a test case or consideration that would, if it went her way, embarrass the tracking theorist, and the tracking theorist should do the same in reverse — perhaps a case where accepting closure forces sensitivity to collapse into an infallibilism nobody can live with.

5. Both characters must be philosophically competent and neither may be a strawman. No character should win by the other's making an unforced error. The dialogue may end without resolution, but if so, each character should close by naming, in one or two sentences, the single premise she or he would most want empirically or dialectically tested.

Please avoid citing literature, quoting texts, or referencing specific works and dates; the dialogue should stand on the ideas alone. The philosophical content should be rendered accurately enough that a specialist in epistemology would recognize the genuine positions being defended, while remaining fully intelligible to someone working from the case description given.
