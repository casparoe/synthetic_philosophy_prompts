A week ago I had a long, ultimately fruitless argument with my closest friend, who is a philosophy graduate student working on consciousness. She was defending the family of higher-order theories: the claim that a first-order mental state is conscious only when some further, higher-order representation is directed at it, so that I am not merely in pain but aware of the pain. I told her that, as a site reliability engineer, I could not see what the fuss was about — that sounds exactly like monitoring. A service's degraded state only becomes "known-degraded" when something observes it; without telemetry, the degradation is happening but nobody, including the system itself, is in a position to report it. She got excited at the analogy for about ten minutes, and then told me it was actually smuggling in an assumption that ruins it, and neither of us could articulate exactly where the translation broke down. I have been thinking about it since, and I want to do this properly rather than let a half-baked analogy stand in for understanding.

Here is the vocabulary I actually work in and am fluent in, so please use it precisely: instrumentation in the code path (a probe or sensor attached directly to a component, sampling its state as a dedicated channel) versus inference (an anomaly detector concluding that a service is misbehaving from its outputs and side effects, without any direct look inside); continuously running health checks versus on-demand checks that only fire when a dashboard is queried or an alert evaluates; monitoring hierarchies, where the watcher itself is watched by a higher-level watcher, and where we cap the regress in practice because it stops buying anything; and monitor misreads, where a monitor reports a component as healthy when it is not, or degraded when it is fine, and nothing about the underlying component has changed because of the bad report.

What I would like you to do is restate the main higher-order theories — the actualist higher-order thought theory associated with Rosenthal, the higher-order perception variant associated with Lycan, and the dispositionalist version associated with Carruthers — each in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. Specifically: does the thought/perception distinction map onto inference versus instrumentation, or does something get distorted in that mapping? Does dispositionalism map onto on-demand or lazy monitoring, and is that mapping faithful or merely cute? Most importantly, tell me what has no counterpart at all: in my world, adding a monitor does nothing to the monitored component, there is no fact about what it is "like" to be the service, and a monitor's misreport leaves the underlying state exactly what it was — yet the higher-order theorists seem to claim that the monitoring relation is what makes the first-order state conscious in the first place, and that a misreading can make a state conscious as something it is not. Finally, give me your view on the residue: does it point to something my vocabulary cannot say, or to something the philosophical claim never succeeded in making clear? Please keep the translation honest — do not quietly assume, just because my framework treats monitoring as external, cheap, and inert, that the philosophers must mean something like that too.
