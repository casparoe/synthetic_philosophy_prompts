I teach an upper-level epistemology course (juniors and seniors, plus a couple of grad students auditing) and I'm writing the take-home final. I want one question on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, but every past version of this question I've seen just asks students to state and explain the two paradoxes, which anyone who skimmed the encyclopedia entry the night before can do. What I actually want to test is whether they can see where the two paradoxes come apart. My favorite angle here is the difference in the evidence: in the lottery case you believe of each individual ticket that it will lose on purely statistical grounds, while in the preface case your belief in each individual claim is supposed to come from your own careful checking — it's only the aggregate risk of error that's statistical. Whether that difference matters seems to depend on whether you take the paradoxes to be about the Lockean thesis (roughly: belief is just sufficiently high credence) or about justified belief more generally, and on whether higher-order evidence about your own reliability is different in kind from a lottery's base rates. But I'll let you take the question wherever it's most productive, as long as it can't be answered by reciting the standard summaries.

So the deliverables: the exam question itself (something they could realistically write on in about 90 minutes), a model answer of around 600–700 words, and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks that names the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. In particular, I want the scheme to distinguish students who merely observe that the preface author has some evidence about his own error rate from students who can say why that evidence does or doesn't dissolve the analogy with the lottery — and to distinguish them by marks, not just vibes. Ideally the model answer works through an actual candidate principle (e.g. some closure or high-probability principle for belief or justified belief) and shows exactly where it gets into trouble. One of my TAs keeps insisting the preface case is just the lottery paradox wearing a tie, so if the whole package also helps me decide whether she's right, that would be great. Also, small thing: the students have not read anything super technical this semester, so keep the model answer free of heavy formal machinery — a principle stated in plain English with maybe a bit of probability notation is fine, full epistemic logic is not.
