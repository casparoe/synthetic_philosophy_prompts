I'm a software engineer by trade — no formal philosophy background, just a long-running reading group I joined out of curiosity that has gotten steadily more serious. The document I want you to draft concerns a debate two of our members have been circling for about a year, which I've since gathered is a real topic in the literature under the name "the wrong kind of reasons problem." Since I'll pass your draft directly to them, please be careful to phrase everything so that each of them would actually sign the text attributed to their own side.

The setup, as I've come to understand it. Some attitudes seem answerable to particular kinds of facts. A reason to believe it will rain is evidence that it will rain — not the fact that believing it will keep me calm, even if it would. A reason to admire someone is that they have admirable traits — not that admiring them will get me included in a will, even if it will. Yet in those off-kilter cases something seems to count in favor of the attitude: the promised inheritance does seem to give me some pull toward admiring the devious uncle, without making him admirable in the least. Call these wrong-kind reasons. The question is what that pull really is: a genuine reason for the attitude, a reason for something else (obtaining the inheritance), or nothing at all.

Our first member — I'll call her the Objectivist — holds that reasons for an attitude are always reasons for its content: a reason to believe that p is evidence that p, and a reason to admire X is some admirable feature of X. On her view, the inheritance gives a reason to act so that I get it, and perhaps a reason to want to admire, but no reason to admire. Our second member — the Functionalist — holds that reasons for attitudes are considerations counting in favor of having the attitude as such, whatever their source: the inheritance genuinely counts in favor of admiring, and evidence genuinely counts in favor of believing, because what unifies the "right" cases is just that the attitude is one it would be accurate or fitting to hold in light of those considerations. Neither is a strawman; both have held their positions through a year of increasingly precise arguments, and I've watched them agree about the cases while disagreeing wildly about what the agreement consists in.

Last month they finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration, and they've asked me to draft the joint statement because I'm the one member without a stake. Write that statement. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of claims both could sign. This should include what they agree on about the cases themselves (e.g., that the promised inheritance does not make the uncle admirable; that the bribed belief is defective in some way that the evidence-based belief is not), about what any theory of reasons for attitudes owes us (e.g., what it must explain about the contrast between the two kinds of case), and about which of each other's arguments are genuinely threatening versus merely inconvenient.

2. A disagreements section, formatted as a table, in which each row states one remaining disagreement in wording its own side has approved. Watch for slippage here: "the reward is not a reason to admire" and "the reward is not an admiring-making fact" are different claims, and each side will only sign one of them.

3. A cruxes section. A crux is a subquestion such that settling it would move at least one of them, and for each one I want a concrete case, argument, or test that both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — the point is to pre-register the predictions so neither can wriggle later. I suspect (but leave you to judge) that one crux is whether a theory must vindicate the intuition that the promised inheritance gives *some* reason to admire, or whether explaining why it merely *seems* to is enough; another is whether the Functionalist can say what believing on evidence and believing on a bribe have in common as reasons without just relabeling the distinction she was supposed to be explaining. Consider also cases beyond belief and admiration — intention, fear of a danger that only materializes if feared, desire — and whether the two positions generalize the same way across them, since if the camps split differently on different attitudes, that's worth knowing.

4. A final short section flagging any part of the dispute that both could agree is merely verbal — and, importantly, any part that both agree is *not*, since each of them tends to accuse the other of verbalism and they'd want the record to show which accusations stuck.

Keep the tone formal but plain; I'll be reading it aloud at our next meeting, and several members are, like me, engineers who need the arguments laid out stepwise rather than in philosophical shorthand.
