I'm a philosophy PhD student working at the edge of ethics and philosophy of mind, and I'm drafting a prospectus for a two-year dissertation small-grant competition. My half-formed idea concerns coherent extrapolated volition, the proposal Eliezer Yudkowsky made in a 2004 paper about how a superintelligent AI ought to derive its goals: roughly, it should do what humanity would want if we knew more, thought faster, and were more the people we wished we were. My worry is about the internal structure of the extrapolation itself. The familiar objections — that humanity's wishes may never cohere, that the extrapolated "we" may be unrecognizable to the actual "we," that the whole thing is paternalism in a lab coat — are well-worn. What interests me instead is path-dependence: the ingredients of idealization (more knowledge, faster reasoning, greater maturity, longer shared history together) are normally bundled together as if they commute, but I suspect the order and rate at which they're applied can change where the process ends up. A mind that first acquires encyclopedic knowledge and then matures emotionally might settle somewhere different from a mind that matures first and learns later; a humanity that deliberates together for a thousand subjective years before learning certain facts about itself may not converge with one that learns those facts cold. If idealized preference is path-dependent in this way, then "our idealized preferences" is not a single target but a family of targets indexed to an extrapolation schedule, and the choice of schedule becomes a substantive normative decision smuggled in under the guise of a technical one.

Please take the role of a senior philosopher who has supervised dissertations on ideal-observer theory, metaethics, and the ethics of technology, and turn my idea into a real prospectus. Think carefully before writing — perhaps begin with some private brainstorming about how to make the question determinate — and produce the document with exactly these sections, in this order: (1) "The Question," a precise statement of what path-dependence in preference idealization amounts to and what turns on it, roughly 300 words; (2) "Why the Standard Debates Leave It Open," showing why neither the coherence objections nor the defenses of idealization as a technical bootstrap address the ordering problem, roughly 400 words; (3) "Method," what I would actually do across the two years — I want to use abstract toy agents and small formal models as intuition pumps, not as proofs, plus conceptual analysis of the analogues in older ideal-observer and full-information accounts of preference — roughly 500 words, with a rough semester-by-semester breakdown; (4) "What a Result Looks Like," describing the two or three possible conclusions and what evidence from the models would decide among them, roughly 250 words; and (5) "Failure Conditions," an honest account of what would make the project collapse — e.g., if the toy models turn out to be so unrealistic that they carry no intuitions, or if path-dependence proves to be an artifact that any coherent idealization must wash out — roughly 200 words. Please write it in the first person, as if I were the applicant, and do not include citations or a bibliography; refer to positions and arguments by name only, since I will fill in references myself. Keep the tone polished and slightly understated — the grant committee is mixed philosophers and computer scientists, and they distrust rhetoric.
