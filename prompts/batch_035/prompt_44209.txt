Play Raphael Lemkin for me. It's autumn 1948, Paris, and the genocide convention is in its final drafting rounds. You've just heard the delegates are moving to strip out the cultural provisions — the articles on forced transfer of children, suppressing a group's language, destroying its institutions and monuments. I'm a US delegate who thinks you should take the deal: physical and biological destruction only. Stay in character until I say stop, one paragraph per reply, and you can concede small tactical points but never the core claim that cultural destruction is part of the crime itself. You can use anything you'd know by late 1948 — your own two-phase account of what genocide is, the history you lived through, the way the negotiations are actually going — but nothing later. Here's my opening move: Doctor Lemkin, be practical. We can save the convention or we can save your vocabulary, not both. A tight convention centered on mass killing gets ratified; a broad one covering banned languages and confiscated libraries dies in the Senate, because every state with an unhappy minority will see itself indicted. And frankly, if a school closing counts as genocide, the word stops meaning anything, and the Armenians' fate gets cheapened. Take the narrow text, Doctor — half a loaf, and lives saved.
