My philosophy of religion reading group spent the spring on Leibniz's claim that God actualizes the best of all possible worlds. At our last meeting one member — call him the quietist — offered the following as the plain consequence of everything we had read, and I have not been able to find where it goes wrong.

(1) God is omniscient, omnipotent, and perfectly good, and actualized the best of all possible worlds.
(2) A perfectly good being permits an evil only if that evil is a necessary condition of a greater good, one that no alternative arrangement lacking the evil would have secured.
(3) So every actual evil is, in the actual world, a necessary condition of a greater good; and any world that is like ours except that some actual evil is prevented is, on balance, worse than ours.
(4) If preventing an evil would make the world worse on balance, it is impermissible to prevent it.
(5) Therefore it is impermissible to prevent any actual evil: not to drain the malarial swamp, not to excise the tumor, not to pull the drowning child from the pond.
(6) And since the same holds for every evil and every potential preventer, the morally best human life is one of non-interference: charity is, quite literally, sabotage of the optimum.

He is prepared to accept (5) and (6) — he calls this the moral-laziness conclusion and says Leibniz, to be consistent, should have accepted it too. I am not prepared to accept it, but neither can I point to the failing step without feeling I have merely changed the subject. Each premise still looks like something we actually read: (1) is the doctrine; (2) is the standard greater-good reasoning; (3) looks like a repackaging of (1) and (2); (4) looks like ordinary consequentialist bookkeeping.

Settle this for us. Which step should be rejected, what exactly is the equivocation or scope-shift hiding in it (if there is one), and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (1) if that can honestly be done; another member is happy to let (1) go and thinks that discharges the whole argument, and I want to know whether that is too quick — whether, for instance, the argument survives in secular dress if we swap God for some claim to the effect that the actual world is the best of the feasible alternatives. Please also state what the quietist should concede at minimum, and where, if anywhere, he is actually right.
