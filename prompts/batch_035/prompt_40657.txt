Write an essay on whether Nyāya epistemology, with its doctrine of the pramāṇas, would support or oppose epistocracy.

Consider the following specific proposal. A state replaces universal equal suffrage with an "epistemic franchise": every citizen retains one vote, but additional votes are awarded for passing examinations testing knowledge of political facts and basic economics. The stated rationale is that voters' judgements should track knowledge rather than mere opinion.

Evaluate this proposal from the standpoint of classical Nyāya. The key issue is this: on the Nyāya account, almost no ordinary citizen's political beliefs are products of perception (pratyakṣa) or inference (anumāṇa). They are products of śabda, reliable testimony from trustworthy sources (āptas) — journalists, officials, teachers, experts. The school treats śabda as an independent pramāṇa, on a par with perception and inference, whereas Buddhist reductionists such as Dharmakīrti denied its independence and analysed testimony as a form of inference. Explore what each position implies for the epistemic franchise. If testimony is genuine knowledge in its own right, does an examination merely test whose testimony one has absorbed, so that the exam selects for the well-connected and well-schooled rather than the epistemically virtuous? Or does the Nyāya insistence that testimonial knowledge requires a trustworthy source and an attentive hearer give the epistocrat exactly what is needed — a principled account of when deference to experts is warranted, and hence of who counts as a competent voter? You may also draw on upamāna and the Nyāya treatment of doubt, misapprehension, and dispute (tarka) where helpful.

Weigh both sides fairly, but end with a definite verdict: does a Nyāya-informed epistemology support the epistemic franchise, oppose it, or support a modified version — and if modified, say precisely how.
