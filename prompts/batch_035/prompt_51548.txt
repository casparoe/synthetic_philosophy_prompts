I sit on the IT steering committee at a mid-sized hospital, and lately I keep running into the same shape of argument and I can't find a word for it.

Example one: years ago, before my time, somebody picked our messaging platform and set the default so that every lab-result alert pings everyone on the unit. Nobody chose that policy — the person buying the software probably spent ten seconds on it. But now the notification culture is just how the unit works, and when a nurse asked whether we could change the default she was told she was making things political. Changing it felt like inserting politics into a neutral system; keeping it felt like nothing at all.

Example two: I half-remember a story about a city planner who had overpasses on a parkway built to low for buses, so that people who couldn't afford cars couldn't get to the beach by transit. Maybe apocryphal, whatever. The interesting part is the same shape: a contingent engineering decision, made for reasons nobody remembers, hardens into the landscape, and by the time anyone notices who it advantages, defending the landscape counts as neutrality and changing it counts as an agenda.

Example three is smaller: our shift rotations got rearranged around a scheduling algorithm's supposedly neutral optimization function, and the weights in that function were set by an intern three years ago.

The pattern I'm after isn't just "technology is never neutral," which is a slogan I've heard and which isn't precise enough to do anything with. It's the two-step: a choice that could easily have gone the other way gets buried, and then resurfaces as the neutral default — so the burden of justifcation always lands on whoever wants to change the thing, and never on the thing itself. It's like the artifact gets a presumption of innocence it never earned.

I know economists talk about path dependence (QWERTY and all that), but that's about efficiency lock-in, and I don't care about efficiency here. It also seems related to whatever social scientists mean when they say an arrangement has been naturalized, but I only half-understand that term and it sounds broader than what I'm pointing at.

So: is there an actual name for this in whatever field studies it (philosophy of technology? STS? nowhere?)? If there is, I want to know how it's precisely defined and where that differs from what I described, not just the label. If there isn't, coin one for me — give a definition, then stress-test it on the edges: does it still apply when the original choice was deliberate and political rather than contingent? Does it apply to legal defaults, like the age of majority or which side of the road we drive on? Does it stop applying once people remember the history behind the choice?

I'd like the answer in sections, in this order: "What you're describing" (one short paragraph, plain words), "The nearest concepts" (the bulk of the response — for each one, what it captures and where it misses), "A term, if you're coining one" (definition plus the edge cases above), and "Settled vs. contested" (a short closing bit flagging which parts of what you've said are just agreed on by people who study this stuff and which are live disagreements — please don't smuggle a contested reading in as the consensus, I'd genuinely rather know).
