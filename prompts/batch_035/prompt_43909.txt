I'm taking an undergraduate seminar on self-knowledge, and I've hit on an argument that I can't find a flaw in, though I suspect there must be one. It starts from the observation that introspection is, by definition, the immediate awareness of one's own current mental states. It follows, I want to say, that a process that can get things wrong isn't introspection at all: if arriving at a claim about my own mind involves inference, reconstruction from memory, or theorizing about myself, then what I'm doing is confabulating or rationalizing, not introspecting. Now, the entire literature on the unreliability of introspection seems to consist of cases of exactly that second kind—for instance, the classic experiments from the 1970s in which subjects confidently explained their preferences in ways that tracked nothing about the items they were evaluating, or the work suggesting that we have no real introspective access to why we like something or to many of our own attitudes. But if those cases all involve inference and guessing rather than genuine introspection, then the literature never actually showed that introspection is unreliable. It only showed that we are bad at the decidedly non-introspective business of diagnosing the causes and mechanisms behind our mental states.

The upshot, as I present it to my seminar, is that the psychologists have attacked a strawman: on the proper definition, introspection comes out not merely reliable but infallible, since whatever seems to be going on in my mind, from the inside, is guaranteed to be going on—there is no gap for error to open up in. My teaching assistant says I've smuggled in a definition that makes the whole claim vacuous, that a faculty whose reliability could not be tested even in principle is not what anyone has ever meant by introspection, and that I've quietly redefined the topic so that every potential counterexample gets reclassified as "not really introspection." But I don't see why a conceptual truth can't be a truth, and if introspection can't misrepresent by its very nature, isn't that precisely the kind of certainty Descartes was after? Where, exactly, does my argument go wrong—if it does? I'd appreciate a careful diagnosis rather than just a verdict.
