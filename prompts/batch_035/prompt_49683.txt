i live in a big shared house with five other students and we have one house rule that people have nearly moved out over: nobody ever goes through anyone else's stuff — laptop, phone, room, whatever — no matter what you suspect them of. if you have a problem with someone you bring it to a house meeting, you don't investigate on your own. it's basically the foundation the whole "we trust each other" thing rests on.

so, problem. i'm now about 90% sure that one of my housemates — the guy who set up our router, so he's the only one who could have done this — has been reading everyone's messages. i won't go into how i know but it's the kind of thing where i can't construct an innocent explaination, and no, he doesn't have a work reason.

here's the catch: the only realistic way to actually prove it is to go through his laptop while he's out. which is exactly the thing the house rule forbids, and forbids for precisely the reason that makes what he's doing so bad. so to defend everyone's privacy i'd have to violate his privacy once. one violation to stop an ongoing pile of them.

context: i'm taking a normative ethics course this semester, we just did kant vs the utilitarians, and i've always come down pretty firmly on the rules side. i genuinely believe the no-snooping rule isn't just a rough summary of privacy costs — it's the kind of thing you just don't do. but now that it's actually biting me it seems almost self-defeating? if the reason snooping is wrong is what snooping does to people, then refusing to snoop when it's the only way to stop the snooping is just letting the thing the rule exists to protect get destroyed so my hands stay clean. and there's this weird asymmetry i can't get past: when i tally it up, his violations go in one column and mine in a different one, as if they're not even the same currency. why should that be? shouldn't six privacy violations be worse than one?

at the same time i'm worried about the obvious slippery thing — if i search his laptop because i'm 90% sure, i can't really tell the next housemate who's 70% sure not to. and i do feel like there's a real difference between him snooping for creepy reasons and me doing it defensively, but when i try to say out loud why that difference justifies breaking the rule i sort of trail off.

anyway, ethically, do i go through the laptop? and if your answer is "it depends on the deeper theory of what makes rules binding," please actually spell out which theory you mean, because i keep going in circles on this one.
