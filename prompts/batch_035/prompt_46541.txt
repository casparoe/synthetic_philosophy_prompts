I hold a fairly strong version of psychological situationism: for morally significant behavior, the features of situations — what they make salient, how they frame the request, what they demand, what they make cheap or costly — do most of the explanatory and predictive work, and the character traits we attribute to people (honesty, courage, compassion) are at best loose labels that fail to travel across settings. I arrived here the usual way: the low cross-situational consistencies in the early studies of children's honesty, the obedience experiments, the finding that being in a hurry predicted helping behavior better than professed compassion did. But I have come to notice that I hold this view the way one holds a political allegiance rather than a scientific opinion: I have never seriously asked what would change my mind. So tell me what would count against it.

Rank the defeaters from what would be fatal through what would be merely awkward, and throughout, distinguish what situationists and their closer sympathizers have themselves conceded from what only defenders of character keep pressing. Work through the obvious candidates honestly — the aggregation point that averaging over many occasions raises consistency, Mischel's own later work on stable if-then behavioral signatures, and the objection that the classic findings involve strangers and one-shot settings rather than the thick, long-run relationships in which character talk mostly lives — and for each, say whether it rescues the trait, merely changes the subject, or can be absorbed by restating the view. Then go beyond the standard dialectic in two ways. First, tell me what situationism predicts that its rivals do not, so that I can tell discriminating evidence from evidence both sides can metabolize; I want to know which observations would be routine on a robust character view but surprising on mine. Second, assess whether the position has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability: the slide from "global traits largely do not exist" to "situational variables carry real predictive weight" looks to me like a retreat that keeps the flag while surrendering the ground, and if the view has retreated that far, I want to know whether what remains is a substantive position or a platitude — and whether a platitude can still earn its keep as a corrective. What I would personally find most persuasive, I suspect, is evidence of stable individual differences in responsiveness to situational features themselves — people who carry the same format of honesty or courage from setting to setting — but tell me whether that is the right test, and if not, what the right one is.
