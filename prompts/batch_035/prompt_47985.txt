My sister's a type designer and we keep having this arguement about what makes a letter the letter it is — is it the shape, or the job the shape does in a whole alphabet. I want a thought experiment I can actually spring on her over dinner. Build me a case where the exact same glyph — same shape, not a pixel changed — reads as an unambigous 'a' in one context and as not-a-letter at all in another. Constraints: realistic setting (a design studio, a shop sign, a book cover — no brain scanning, no far future tech), one paragraph, and it can't cheat by just declaring how it should be read: the readability has to be earned by structure the way it is in real life, like how you can read a terrible photo of a chalkboard menu because you know what words are likely to be on it. And here's the part I actually care about: the case has to split three ways. A features view (a letter is whatever carries the right visual features — the bowl, the stem, the counter) has to come out saying the glyph is not an 'a'. A prototype or similarity view has to say it's a strained, marginal 'a' at best. And a "slot in the system" view — the idea that what makes every 'a' an 'a' is that it occupies the a-position in a complete alphabet, with its idiosyncrasies cancelled out by its neighbors' roles — has to say it's a perfectly good 'a', no hesitation. After the scenario, walk through each verdict and why it falls out, tell me which piece of the setup is doing the work, what happens to the case if you remove that piece, and how my sister, who's a features person through and through, would try to fight back. She will definately spot it if the case is rigged, so no stipulating the conclusions — the readings have to be generated by how the case is built.
