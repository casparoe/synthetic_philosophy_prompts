I'm a program officer at a mid-sized foundation, and every quarter our grants committee has to pick between proposals while the seven of us flatly disagree about what matters. Two are basically utilitarians, one holds a person-affecting view (thinks you can't be harmed before you exist), one gives serious moral weight to animals, one is a contractualist who thinks some ways of treating people are off the table regardless of payoff, and the rest are mixtures. So far we resolve this by whoever argues longest, and the board is starting to ask why we funded X over Y.

I want a decision procedure the committee can actually run in a two-hour meeting, based on whatever the moral uncertainty literature actually says -- maximizing expected choiceworthiness, Borda-style aggregation across theories, Bostrom's moral parliament idea, variance voting -- but usable when nobody will give real probability weights to their theories. If a step starts with "estimate how confident you are in each theory," they'll refuse or just make numbers up, so the procedure needs a fallback. It should say what to do when theories use different currencies (a contractualist's "this is impermissible" isn't a number you can average with a utilitarian's), where it hands the call back to a plain majority because it's a values question the math can't settle, and it has to produce an explanation a board member could follow.

Then run it on two of the proposals we recieve: (a) $500k for a campaign to phase out a painful but standard livestock procedure, versus the same money for vision screening and eyeglasses for schoolkids in low-income countries; (b) a grant to set up a far-future-focused research institute, where the person-affecting folks think the benefit is literally zero and the utilitarians think it dwarfs everything else. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like -- I'm the animal person, and I suspect the arithmetic favors the eyeglasses.

Format, in this order: "The procedure" (numbered steps, short enough to print on one page), "The two cases" (a worked run of each, roughly half a page apiece), "Where it breaks" (one short paragraph on what this genuinely can't settle).
