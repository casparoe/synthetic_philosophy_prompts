I'm advising a small European research foundation that funds work at the boundary of history and philosophy. It has decided to put four postdoctoral researchers and five years of funding behind a single theme: what it means to hold a nation responsible for a war, with the controversy over Germany's responsibility for the First World War as the running case — the Fischer controversy of the 1960s over whether Germany's leadership deliberately sought a general war for hegemony, the subsequent backlash, and the more recent tendency to treat 1914 as a shared slide into catastrophe rather than one power's gamble. The foundation has asked me for a prioritized agenda of open questions. I have a candidate list, but I don't trust my own ordering, and I want the prioritization defended rather than asserted.

The candidates, roughly as the foundation framed them:

1. Whether ascribing responsibility to a nation requires positing a corporate agent with a unified intention — something like a collective decision-maker whose war aims can be discovered in the documents — or whether it can be reconstructed from the aggregated decisions of individually acting statesmen, none of whom willed exactly what ensued. Fischer's critics sometimes acted as if refuting a coherent German war plan dissolves the ascription; whether that inference is sound depends entirely on this question.

2. What the war-guilt clause of the Versailles treaty actually presupposed. It functioned simultaneously as a moral verdict, a justification for reparations, and a piece of victors' diplomacy, and the philosophical literature mostly notices only the first function. A suspicion worth taking seriously: whether the clause presupposes blame at all depends on which of these functions you take to be essential to it, and the debate over war guilt has been confused by running all three together.

3. The counterfactual question: responsibility for a world war seems to depend on whether something less than a world war would have occurred had any given power behaved differently. But several powers each took gambles that were individually necessary for catastrophe and individually survivable. How should responsibility divide under that structure — is there a coherent notion of collective overdetermination of an outcome by convergent gambles?

4. Whether outcome-dependence of blame — the fact that the July crisis could have fizzled into a localized Balkan war, in which case we would not be holding anyone responsible for a world war — is an embarrassment for responsibility ascriptions to nations, a form of moral luck, or simply a statement that responsibility tracks what you risk rather than what you cause.

5. The continuity question: whether the claim that a single strategic aim persisted across German policy before 1914 and again before 1939 changes the metaphysics of the ascription — does it require a longer-lived collective agent than the state of 1914, or does it merely inflate the rhetoric of blame?

6. What evidential standards apply to collective intention: historians fighting over memoranda, marginalia, and cabinet minutes are implicitly taking positions on when a document counts as evidence of a state's intent rather than a faction's, an individual's, or a bureaucratic placeholder's. This implicit epistemology has, to my knowledge, never been made explicit and defended.

I want you to rank these and defend the ordering. Specifically: which questions are upstream of which — which others quietly presuppose an answer to them; which are tractable by philosophers with history-adjacent training versus which genuinely require archival work no philosopher should attempt; which are overrated, in the sense that the historiography has already settled as much as philosophy could add; and which are so neglected that a single postdoc could move them. I also want your view on a suspicion of mine that cuts across the list: that "holding a nation responsible" splits into a verdictive register (judging), a prospective register (shaping deterrence narratives and future conduct), and an allocative register (apportioning costs), and that the right prioritization looks different for each register — in particular, that questions about intentions may matter enormously for the first and barely at all for the third. If that suspicion is right, the agenda should perhaps be organized by register rather than by question. Finally, tell me what would have to happen — new archival finds, a theoretical breakthrough in collective-action theory, a change in present-day European politics — for the ranking to shift. I care more about the reasoning than the list; the foundation can read a survey. It cannot read a survey that tells it where to put its five years.
