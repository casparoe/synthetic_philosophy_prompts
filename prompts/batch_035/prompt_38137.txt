I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on a chapter about deflationary accounts of the self, and I keep circling back to Dennett's suggestion, developed in Consciousness Explained, that the self is best thought of as a center of narrative gravity — modeled explicitly on the center of gravity of an object in classical mechanics. The idea, as I understand it, is that a center of gravity is a posit: it isn't a component you could locate among the atoms, it isn't made of anything, and if you went looking for it in the matter you would come up empty. Yet it is neither illusory nor optional — it figures in real explanations, real engineering, real predictions — and nothing about physics is deflationary or embarrassing about positing it. Dennett's move is to say the self is like that: an abstractum posited by the organism in the course of spinning its self-narrative, real enough to be a useful explanatory node but not a further thing over and above the narrative practice.

The analogy is doing a lot of work in his argument, and I want to audit exactly what it can carry. As far as I can tell it is meant to license three conclusions at once: first, that the failure to find a self by introspection or neuroscience is no more threatening than the failure to find a center of gravity among the particles; second, that this doesn't collapse into saying the self is a mere illusion, since centers of gravity are respectable posits and so, by parity, are selves; and third, something revisionary about personal identity and survival — if the self is an abstractum, then questions about whether it persists may dissolve rather than get answered. My friend in the physics department is unimpressed. His complaint is that a center of gravity is fixed by the mass distribution through a precise mathematical definition: given the matter, there is no interpretive slack, no narrative, no conventional element at all, so the analogy may be smuggling in exactly what Dennett needs to prove — that the self is a harmless theoretical posit rather than something constituted in part by interpretation. He also notes disanalogies that seem to cut deeper: a center of gravity has no point of view, can't be wrong about anything, can't deliberate or be held responsible, and ceases to exist without remainder when the matter disperses, whereas our questions about responsibility and survival don't seem to be settled that cleanly by scattering.

Here is what I'd like from you. Lay out the correspondence table as carefully as you can — what plays the role of the mass distribution, what plays the role of the definition, what corresponds to the predictive indispensability that earns the center of gravity its ontological credentials. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten Dennett's conclusions from the ones that don't (for instance, I suspect the absence of a point of view matters more than the fact that a center of gravity can't be praised or blamed, but I'd like this argued rather than assumed). Finally, tell me which of the three conclusions, if any, survives once the mapping is repaired, and whether there is a better analogy in the neighborhood — a fictional character, a legal person, an institution, an equilibrium concept — that captures what Dennett was after without inheriting the problems. I'm not looking for a survey of the secondary literature, just the audit, worked out on the merits.
