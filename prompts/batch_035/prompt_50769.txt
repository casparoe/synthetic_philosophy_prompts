Hi! I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I'm putting together the final. I'd like one essay question on the special composition question, but I'm tired of scripts that just recite van Inwagen's answer, Markosian's brutalism, and unrestricted composition and call it a day.

Last term a visiting colleague defended something she called integrationism: some things compose an object roughly when they mutually depend on each other for the existence of the whole — if you destroyed any one of them, the composed object would go out of existence too. So a person's vital organs compose a person, but my left shoe, the Eiffel Tower and a randomly selected trout don't. I want the exam question built around stress-testing this view. Students should have to work out whether it can dodge the arbitrariness worries that pushed people toward unrestricted composition in the first place, wether it delivers the right verdicts on awkward cases (artifacts with removable parts, living things whose parts get swapped out gradually, and collections sitting right at the threshold where the dependence relation kicks in), and — this is the part I care about most — whether a criterion like this even answers the special composition question or quietly changes the subject to persistence or to counting how many objects are in the room.

Can you give me (i) the question as it would appear on the exam paper, (ii) a model answer of around 700 words, and (iii) a marking scheme out of 20 marks that says in prose what an A answer has to do that a B answer doesn't — I want the difference to turn on specific argumentative moves, not on vague "shows deep understanding of the literature" fluff.

Two format things. First, please write all three parts as continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just sentences in paragraphs, the way you'd write an email. I know marking schemes normally come as bullet lists, but I've decided I want everything flowing this year; it forces whoever's grading to read the argument as a whole instead of skimming for keywords. Second, no reading lists and no citations of specific papers in the model answer — if a student name-drops instead of arguing, my graders will mark them down, so the model answer should model the right behavior.

Thanks a ton!
