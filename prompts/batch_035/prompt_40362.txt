I'm taking an elective on the philosophy of animal minds, and we've recently turned to the question of how consciousness is distributed across species. My instructor suggested — and the readings we were assigned seemed to agree — that consciousness in animals most plausibly comes in degrees rather than as an all-or-nothing affair: a chimpanzee is probably more richly conscious than a chicken, which may in turn be more conscious than a fish or an ant, rather than there being some single threshold that certain species cross and others fail to cross.

When my friends and I tried to work out what follows from this, we ran into an argument that none of us could answer, and it's been bothering me all week. If consciousness differs between species only in degree and never in kind, then any line we draw between the animals we're permitted to eat and the animals we're not looks completely arbitrary — there's no qualitative difference to point to, just different positions along one continuum. So it seems we're stuck with an uncomfortable dilemma. Either we become strict vegans, and by the same logic extend protection to insects, and perhaps even to plants and bacteria, since on this view everything living just has more or less of the same underlying property; or we bite the bullet and accept that the very same reasoning licenses eating humans as well, since a human is merely further along the same continuum than a pig.

Both horns strike me as unacceptable, but I can't see where the argument goes wrong, and neither could anyone in my study group. Is there a standard response to this line of reasoning in the literature on animal minds? Or, if the argument really is as airtight as it appears, are we genuinely forced to choose between the two horns — and if so, which one should we pick?
