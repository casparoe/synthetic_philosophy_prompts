I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized company, no formal philosophy background, though I read widely on the side. My partner and I have spent this year going back and forth on whether to have our first child, and a coworker who knew about the deliberation recommended L.A. Paul's book Transformative Experience. I finally worked through it, and her central puzzle struck me as oddly familiar: you cannot know what having a child will be like until you've had one, so you can't assign the outcome a utility from where you currently stand and run an expected-value calculation. In my day job I deal constantly with exploration-exploitation tradeoffs in bandit problems and reinforcement learning, and something that looks like Paul's situation shows up there all the time: an agent with a poor estimate of its own reward function, whose preferences may be reshaped by what it experiences (non-stationary rewards, preference drift, optimism in the face of uncertainty, Thompson sampling over what you might turn out to enjoy, an exploration bonus for outcomes whose value you can't yet estimate). My coworker, who did a philosophy degree before switching to software, tells me the analogy is superficial: Paul's problem isn't ignorance about states of the world but ignorance about the self who will evaluate the outcomes — you aren't missing information, you are the missing information.

What I'd like from you is an argument for a definite verdict on whether Paul's problem is a genuinely new kind of decision problem, or whether it reduces to machinery that decision theory and statistics already have: decision under ambiguity, preference uncertainty, sequential choice where the act of choosing is itself informative, or some kind of negotiation among your possible future selves. To be candid about my own confusion, I can distinguish at least three strengths of the reductionist view I'm tempted by — a mild one on which transformative choices just call for exploration bonuses; a stronger one on which they require an explicit model of endogenous preference change; and a deflationary one on which, once the objective function is specified properly, there is nothing special left to model — and I genuinely don't know which, if any, I believe. So please don't lay the options out and leave the picking to me: commit to one answer — the reduction works, works only partially (in which case say exactly where the reinforcement-learning analogy breaks and what residual problem survives), or fails outright — and defend that answer. For what it's worth, my prior is to be suspicious of any argument concluding that certain choices lie beyond rational deliberation, since in my line of work that conclusion usually means somebody hasn't specified the objective carefully enough. But I would rather be corrected than flattered, so if the honest verdict is that the coworker is right and my whole framing is a category error, argue that.
