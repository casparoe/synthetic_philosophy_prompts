Question for a legal scholar who thinks about speech regulation and enforcement for a living. I live in a European country with criminal hate speech laws (the German-style setup, not the US First Amendment kind), and after years of following prosecutions I've noticed a pattern I can't quite find a name for. The people who actually get charged and convicted are almost never the sophisticated ones — the ideologues who write long, careful essays about demographics with plausable deniability built in ('just quoting statistics', 'obviously satire'). It's the impulsive ones: the drunk guy yelling at a bus stop, the guy posting from an account tied to his real name, the one's who confess when interviewed. Same message, worse packaging — and the packaging is what decides wether it's a case. And I suspect the long-run effect is that the law doesn't so much reduce the amount of hateful speech as change its register: the crude style gets filtered out through the courts and what survives is polished, deniable, lawyered. It's not the chilling effect as I understand it (the amount doesn't obviously drop, it gets repackaged), and it's not overbreadth either. Is there an established concept for enforcement that selects on speaker skill / convictability rather than on harm, and thereby reshapes the style of what's left? Think carefully and brainstorm candidates first — from law, the economics of enforcement, sociology of deviance, wherever — and for each one say why it only partially fits. If nothing fits, coin a term, give a definition, and check some boundary cases (does it still apply under civil rather than criminal enforcement? to a corporation's comms department? when the polished speaker slips once?). A bare label won't satisfy me, I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it's not, and where its edges are.
