My uncle has signed up with a cryonics organization. The arrangement is that when he is pronounced legally dead, the company will immediately infuse him with chemicals to protect the tissue, cool him far below freezing, and store him indefinitely, in the hope that future medicine will be able to revive him — possibly by repairing the brain, possibly by scanning it and reconstructing it, whether in a new body or even as software. I've been turning this over on my own, and I can't work out what view of the person and of death the whole arrangement quietly takes for granted, especially because it sits oddly with everything else that happens at that same moment. The minute he is declared dead, the state will issue a death certificate, his estate will pass to his heirs, and for every legal purpose he will be gone; yet the company will treat that very moment not as his end but as the start of a medical procedure, and will go on regarding him as a patient in its care for decades, alongside people who were declared dead half a century ago.

So my questions are these. First, what theory of death does the practice presuppose? It seems to assume that death is not an event but a process — that legal death is a label applied too early, and that he is only truly gone once the information in his brain is lost, with everything before that point being a race to prevent the loss. Is that the implicit theory, and is it coherent? Second, what theory of the person does it presuppose? Does the practice only make sense on the view that my uncle just is the pattern in his brain, so that keeping the pattern in place keeps him in existence? Would someone who believed the person is a living biological organism, or that something non-physical departs at death, be signing up incoherently, or could such a person have consistent reasons of their own?

Two further puzzles. The company offers two options: one where only the brain is preserved, and one where the whole body is. Do those options presuppose different views of what the person is — and if so, how can the same organization offer both with a straight face, or does one of them turn out to be strictly implied by the practice while the other is just insurance? And last, subscribers are told that if revival ever comes, it may well be by scanning and uploading rather than by reanimating the original brain. For the practice to make sense as a bet on his survival, must it presuppose that being uploaded — ended here, rebuilt there from the recorded information — counts as his going on, in the way he would go on if his neurons were replaced one at a time by artificial parts? Or can the practice stay neutral on that question and still be coherent?

I'd like a careful reconstruction of the implicit theory behind the practice, an honest statement of where it is underdetermined — the points at which several different views of personal identity or death would license it just as well — and a note on where it is genuinely in tension with what the rest of society does, at the same moment, to the same person.
