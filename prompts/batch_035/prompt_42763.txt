My friend and I argued for like three hours last night about low-impact AI and I think she's talked me into something that can't be right, because it would prove way too much. Here's her argument as I'd reconstruct it. (1) The whole point of impact measures is to get an AI that avoids catastrophic side effects without having to write down a full specification of what humans value — if you had the value specification, you wouldn't need the impact penalty. (2) So the penalty must be computable from the agent's model of the world alone, with no meaningful input from human values. (3) Every proposed measure works the same way at bottom: compare what actually happened to some baseline of what would have happened otherwise, and penalize the difference. (4) Whether a difference from the baseline is good or bad is a value question. (5) So a value-free penalty can only track the size of a deviation, never its sign. (6) But then it penalizes curing a pandemic exactly as much as starting one, and doing nothing not at all. (7) And doing nothing isn't actually neutral — her favorite example is an AI that stands by and lets a vase roll into an incinerator when it could have flicked a switch, which looks like a pretty big side effect of "inaction" — so inaction only scores zero because we arbitrarily picked it as the baseline, and picking which things count as "the default" is itself a value judgment. (8) So either the measure smuggles values in (defeating the point) or it's indifferent between good and bad changes, in which case the low-impact agent is dominated by a rock. Therefore "low impact" without values is incoherent and the whole agenda is just the value-specification problem wearing a costume.

She also had this corollary about offsetting that I couldn't answer: if the penalty is "difference from what would've happened anyway," then an agent that causes some harm to achieve its task has an incentive to undo the harm afterwards — even at enormous cost — purely to drive its impact score back down to zero, which is crazy behavior we'd never want, and looks like more evidence the measure is tracking the wrong thing entirely.

I want to keep (1), since if that's false the whole research program is pointless and surely that's proving too much. And honestly each of (4), (5), and (7) sounded fine to me at the time. But the conclusion feels wrong — "this change is big and that one is small" seems like something you can say without taking a stance on whether big changes are good, the same way "that's a lot of dynamite" doesn't require knowing what you're blowing up. Which step do you reject, and what do you pay for rejecting it? If there's an equivocation somewhere I'd like it named, because I can't tell whether she's sliding between "the penalty needs values to be computed" and "the penalty needs values to be chosen," or between "can't distinguish good from bad" and "can't be biased toward avoiding bad." And if you think the vase/offsetting stuff is actually where the pressure really is rather than a side issue, tell me why.
