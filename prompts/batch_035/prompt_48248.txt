I sit on the advisory panel of a mid-sized animal welfare funder that is about to launch a three-year grants program on insect welfare, with roughly $2 million a year to allocate. The staff's draft strategy rests on what they call the scale argument: even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is very low — some panelists put it well under one percent, and no one on the panel puts it above maybe ten — the populations are so enormous (a recent census put the world's ants alone in the tens of quadrillions, with all insects plausibly in the quintillions) that a small credence multiplied by a vast number still dominates the expected-value comparison with the vertebrate causes we currently fund. Two panelists find this framing close to decisive; a third thinks it is a way of laundering near-total ignorance into a confident conclusion, since the low probability isn't a measurement but an admission that we barely know what would count as evidence. I have to submit written comments on the draft, and I want to go beyond reacting to their topics and actually reason about what the program should fund first.

The candidate directions I can see are roughly these: (1) working to firm up or narrow the probability of sentience for one well-chosen group, such as decapod crustaceans, where the neuroanatomical and behavioral evidence is comparatively strong and some governments have already extended welfare protections, versus insects, where the numbers are vastly larger but the evidence thinner; (2) developing welfare indicators that are decision-relevant whether or not the sentience question ever gets settled — physiological or behavioral proxies for bad states that would guide interventions even under a range of credences, so the funding isn't hostage to a debate that may be unfalsifiable in practice; (3) field research on actual populations — densities, life histories, causes and rates of death — to establish whether wild insect lives are plausibly net bad or net good under different management regimes; and (4) conceptual work on how a funder should decide at all under this combination of moral uncertainty, near-unfalsifiable probability estimates, and astronomical stakes, including whether expected-value maximization is even the right frame when the pivotal number is a credence that no evidence could easily move.

Rank these directions (or replace my list with ones I've missed) and defend the ordering. I care most about the dependency structure: whether indicator work really is verdict-independent or quietly presupposes an answer to the sentience question; whether the decision-theoretic question is upstream of every allocation choice we make, in which case it's odd to fund it last or not at all; which items are tractable for biologists rather than philosophers, since the program is mostly positioned to fund empirical work. I'd also like to know what evidence arriving over the next three years would move a direction up or down your list, which direction you would cut first if the budget were halved, and whether you think the staff's framing is wrong in a way that reorders everything — for instance, if the scale argument, taken seriously, actually recommends a different portfolio than any of the four directions above.
