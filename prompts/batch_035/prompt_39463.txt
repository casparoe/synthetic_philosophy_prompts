Say you could sit Sartre down in 2025 and show him three things: (1) dating apps whose algorithm tells you who your "best match" is, (2) feeds that learn your tastes and keep serving you more of the same until your preferences calcify, and (3) AI assistants that draft your emails, apologies, even love letters before you've decided what you actually think. My roomate and I keep arguing about all this. He says the three cases are basically textbook bad faith — the app is destiny with a login screen, and letting a machine draft your apology is the waiter with the tray all over again, hiding behind a role instead of choosing. I think that's too fast: the algorithm's predictions about me are just facticity, like my body, my epoch, and my terrible French, and bad faith was always about what you do with your facticity, not the facticity itself. So which of us would Sartre side with? Would he treat the three cases differently — I could see him loving the AI-drafting thing as just another tool and hating the dating app, or the reverse, and I genuinely can't tell which. And where would he say the real bad faith lives: with the user who says "the algorithm knows me better than I know myself," or with the engineer who built that framing into the product in the first place? Bonus question if you're up for it: does a system that's *usually right* about what you'll do next threaten radical freedom in a way that ordinary social pressure never did, or is it just the look of the Other scaled up to a billion users, and therefore nothing he hasn't already handled?
