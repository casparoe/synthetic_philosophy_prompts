I teach political philosophy at a mid-sized state university, and I've been appointed to a working group charged with reviewing a new "learning analytics" initiative. Our learning-management system now logs, for every student, when they open assigned readings and how long they dwell on particular passages, which lecture-recording segments they rewind, whether they pause in ways the vendor's algorithm flags as "disengagement," and whom they contact through the course portal. The vice-provost's position, which frames our whole charge, is that no privacy issue arises provided two conditions hold: instructors never see individual-level data in identifiable form, and students consent at enrollment through the standard click-through notice. A philosopher colleague on the committee goes further and insists I should stop worrying altogether: privacy, she argues, just is whatever bundle of expectations a given culture happens to have, so once a generation of students grows up with these systems, the collection can no longer count as invasive — the norms themselves absorb the wrongness, and in twenty years we will wonder what the fuss was about.

I find myself unable to articulate my unease, and I would rather not fall back on a vague "slippery slope" gesture in front of the committee. So let me lay out what I want from you. First, is the vice-provost's argument actually sound — is there something at stake when information that harms no one's reputation, never leaves a dashboard, and is aggregated before anyone sees it is nonetheless collected at fine-grained resolution? I'm struck by the thought that how a student reads — what confuses them, what they linger over, what they avoid — reveals their intellectual life in a way that, say, attendance figures do not, and that students who know they're watched may simply stop exploring ideas awkwardly. Second, I want your assessment of my colleague's position, because it strikes me as proving too much: if privacy is nothing over and above current expectations, then any surveillance regime can legitimize itself by entrenching itself. Third, whatever account of privacy's value you think is actually right — and I'd like you to commit to one rather than survey the field — tell me what follows for a concrete committee recommendation: what data should not be collected at all, what collection could be acceptable and under what conditions, and whether the click-through can ever do the consent-work the administration assigns to it.

One request, and I mean it: if you think my framing is confused — if, for instance, the real problem here is power or manipulation rather than privacy, or if the vice-provost and my colleague are talking past each other in a way my three questions simply inherit — say so plainly rather than dutifully answering what I asked. I would rather discover that I have posed the wrong question now than in front of the full committee.
