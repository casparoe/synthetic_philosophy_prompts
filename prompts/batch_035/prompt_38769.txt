I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy — my entire exposure consists of a university elective on formal logic and years of absorbing secondhand references — and I've recently joined a small reading group at work that has decided to work through Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling. We meet every other week, and for our next session I volunteered to open the discussion of the prefatory material. I'm working from an old English translation, apparently taken from a 1923 selection of Kierkegaard's writings, and although the front matter is only a couple of pages long, I found it more disorienting than anything else I've skimmed ahead to. Here is the passage I'll be presenting:

Not only in the world of commerce but also in the world of ideas our age has arranged a regular clearance-sale. Everything may be had at such absurdedly low prices that very soon the question will arise whether any one cares to bid. Every waiter with a speculative turn who carefully marks the significant progress of modern philosophy, every lecturer in philosophy, every tutor, student, every sticker-and-quitter of philosophy—they are not content with doubting everything, but "go right on." It might, possibly, be ill-timed and inopportune to ask them whither they are bound; but it is no doubt polite and modest to take it for granted that they have doubted everything—else it were a curious statement for them to make, that they were proceeding onward. … "But did not Descartes proceed in this fashion?" Descartes, indeed! that venerable, humble, honest thinker whose writings surely no one can read without deep emotion—Descartes did what he said, and said what he did. Alas, alas! that is a mighty rare thing in our times! But Descartes, as he says frequently enough, never uttered doubts concerning his faith. . . .

In our times, as was remarked, no one is content with faith, but "goes on." The question as to whither they are proceeding may be a silly question; whereas it is, a sign of urbanity and culture to assume that every one has faith, to begin with, for else it were a curious statement for them to make, that they were proceeding further. In the olden days it was different. Then, faith was a task for a whole life-time because it was held that proficiency in faith was not to be won within a few days or weeks. Hence, when the tried patriarch felt his end approaching, after having fought his battles and preserved his faith, he was still young enough at heart not to have forgotten the fear and trembling which disciplined his youth and which the mature man has under control, but which no one entirely outgrows—except insofar as he succeeds in "going on" as early as possible. The goal which those venerable men reached at last—at that spot every one starts, in our times, in order to "proceed further."

There are several things I'd like help untangling before I have to talk about this in front of colleagues. First, what exactly is the clearance-sale metaphor complaining about, and who is the "waiter with a speculative turn"? One colleague suggested the whole preface is really aimed at Hegel and his followers — is that right, and if so, what would a Hegelian of Kierkegaard's day have been doing that this passage is mocking? Second, I can't work out what Descartes is doing here: Kierkegaard seems to praise him as honest and then immediately notes that Descartes never doubted his faith — is the point that Descartes's doubt was narrower than advertised, or that the moderns inherit the doubt without the honesty? Third, and most importantly, I gather from skimming summaries of the later sections that the book's central question is whether there can be a teleological suspension of the ethical — whether Abraham's relation to God could override the universal ethical prohibition on sacrificing his son — but I can't see how the preface's complaint about people going further than faith connects to that. Is the idea that treating faith as a starting assumption everyone already possesses precisely drains the difficulty out of it, so that the Abraham story stops being terrifying and becomes just an edifying anecdote? Relatedly, as an engineer I keep instinctively reading "going further" as a move up a level of abstraction — as though the moderns are claiming to have solved the object-level problem and ascended to some meta-level — and I suspect that analogy is misleading me, though I can't say exactly how. Finally, a practical question about reading Kierkegaard generally: when he says something like it being "a sign of urbanity and culture" to assume everyone has faith, is this ever meant straight, or should I treat every flattering remark in the book as ironic until proven otherwise? Guidance on any of these points would be much appreciated.
