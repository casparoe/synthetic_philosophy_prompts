Retired biochemist here — 35 years on enzymes, now I read philosophy for fun, and I've hit a pattern I can't find a name for. It's this: with some attitudes, you can have a crushing reason to hold the attitude, but the reason has nothing to do with whether the attitude is actually correct or fitting. A father has an overwhelming reason to believe his son is a gifted violinist, because the boy will only keep practicing if dad beleives it — yet the boy is hopeless, and the dad's reason doesn't make the belief true or even probable. A courtier under a tyrant has a very good reason to admire the tyrant (his neck depends on it), which says nothing at all about the tyrant's character. Pascal's wager gives you a reason to believe in God that is not the slightest bit of evidence that God exists. I can even get it going for fear: you might have a reason to fear a harmless spider, namely that staying scared keeps you from ever picking one up. But when I try to run the same move on perception it jams. If a stick is half in water, I don't seem able to have a reason to see it as bent other than it looking bent — the look is the whole story there. So what I want to know: is there an established term for the kind of attitude that can be supported by these object-independent reasons (belief, admiration, fear, maybe), versus states like perception where the only reasons come from the object itself? I know about the split between practical and epistemic reasons for belief, but that covers just belief, and the admiration and fear cases don't fit it, so I'm guessing theres some broader concept I'm missing. And if the specific contrast I'm after (belief/admiration yes, perception no) doesn't have a name of its own, say so plainly, then give me your own term with a definition and the edges spelled out — where exactly the line falls, and which side borderline cases like amusement or trust should go on. Don't just hand me a label and stop; the label is the least interesting part, the carving is what I want. Format: reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, no intro and no conclusion. If you've got more than five things worth saying, keep the five that matter most and cut the rest.
