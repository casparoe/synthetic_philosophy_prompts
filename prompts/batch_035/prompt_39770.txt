I have been trying to work out, case by case, what I actually believe about the truth of statements concerning the future, and I would like to know whether my verdicts add up to a coherent position — and, if so, what that position commits me to elsewhere.

It is tonight unsettled whether there will be a sea battle tomorrow. On 'There will be a sea battle tomorrow' my verdict is that it is neither true nor false — not merely unknown, but lacking a truth value at present. The same goes for its negation: 'There will not be a sea battle tomorrow' is also neither true nor false. Yet 'Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not' strikes me as plainly true, and that is a verdict I will not surrender. Likewise, of a fair coin to be tossed tomorrow, I hold that 'The coin will land heads or tails' is true now, even though neither 'The coin will land heads' nor 'The coin will land tails' is true now. I also accept 'If the coin lands heads, the coin will land' as true now, despite both antecedent and consequent being unsettled.

Some future-tensed claims, however, I count simply true. If astronomers tell me there will be a total solar eclipse some decades hence, I take that to be true now — already settled by the present state of the heavens and the laws — in a way the coin toss is not.

My tentative principle is this: a future-tensed claim is true now if and only if its truth is already settled by the present state of the world together with the laws; where the laws leave matters open, the claim is neither true nor false; but a compound built from open components can still be true, provided that every way things could go makes it true.

Two things worry me. First, I also hold, as a quite general conviction about truth, that nothing is true unless something in reality makes it true, and that a disjunction is true only through a true disjunct. My verdicts on the excluded-middle and coin sentences appear to violate this: I am asserting truth without a true disjunct. Second, there is a case I keep going back and forth on: a sea battle whose occurrence depends on whether a radioactive atom decays before dawn. It is chancy, like the coin, yet it proceeds from the laws, like the eclipse. My settledness principle treats decay and decision alike, but I am not certain I do.

Please tell me, precisely, what my verdicts and my tentative principle amount to. Then commit: say which of my verdicts, or which of my two general convictions, has to go, and defend that choice rather than presenting it as a tie. State what the resulting position predicts on cases I have not mentioned: whether, after tomorrow's battle, one may truly say 'It was true yesterday that there would be a sea battle today'; what it says about the sentence 'Either it will be the case that there was a sea battle or it will be the case that there was not'; and what it says about a rigged case, in which the ambush is sprung if and only if the coin lands heads, so that 'The coin will land heads if and only if there will be a battle' seems settled in advance. Finally, give a definite verdict on the decay case, and say whether it is coherent, on the position you arrive at, to speak of one future among the open ones as the future that will in fact occur — what some philosophers call the thin red line. If you find more than one of my verdicts untenable, name them and explain why; but do not leave me with a menu of options — tell me what I should think.
