A bit of background so you know who's asking. I'm 71, retired four years ago after a long career in atmospheric chemistry, mostly field measurement work. Since retiring I've gotten seriously into philosophy — audit courses at the university here, read way more than my wife thinks is sensible. This semester I'm sitting in on a philosophy of science course, and the instructor (who knows I lived through a couple of ugly priority fights in my own field) asked me to help mark one essay question from the midterm while she handles the rest. I'm comfortable judging the history-of-science side but less sure about the philosophy side, and honestly less sure how to weigh small factual slips against shaky reasoning when grading, so I want a second pair of eyes on this one.

The question as it appeared on the exam, worth 20 points:

"Calculus was formulated independently by Newton and Leibniz; evolution by natural selection was arrived at independently by Darwin and Wallace. Some take such cases to show that scientific discoveries are, in a sense, inevitable — that when the time is ripe, they get made no matter who is around. What, if anything, do such 'multiples' actually show about inevitability? And do they matter for how we should think about who deserves credit for a discovery?"

Here is one student's answer, in full:

"Newton and Leibniz both arrived at calculus without knowing each other's work, and Darwin and Wallace did the same with natural selection. Thomas Kuhn argued that cases like this are not accidents but the norm in science: he claimed most discoveries are 'multiples,' made independently and more or less simultaneously, and that single, unique discoveries are rare. If most discoveries are made several times over, then the identity of the discoverer matters much less than we tend to think. The idea was 'in the air,' as people say — once enough groundwork is laid, someone will make the final step.

The Darwin and Wallace case shows this well. Wallace was a collector working in the Galapagos when he worked out natural selection, and he wrote to Darwin with an essay spelling out the idea. Darwin, who had been sitting on essentially the same theory for about twenty years, was spurred to go public, and their work was read jointly at the Linnean Society in 1858; Darwin then rushed the Origin of Species into print within weeks. If Wallace had died on his way home, we would still have evolution by natural selection. So the discovery was going to happen regardless.

It might be objected that we only notice the discoveries that did happen to be made twice — that we never hear about the ideas that were 'in the air' and never landed anywhere. This is a fair worry about the evidence, but it cuts less than it seems: the sheer number of documented multiples, across very different fields, makes the pattern too consistent to be explained by chance.

Finally, if discoveries are inevitable in this way, then the famous priority disputes start to look irrational. Newton and his supporters went after Leibniz in a long and nasty fight over who invented calculus, and it is hard to see what the fight was really about, since calculus was the same discovery whichever man made it first. Credit for a discovery that would have been made anyway is really credit to the scientific community and the era rather than the individual, so priority disputes are mistakes."

The instructor wants me to give a grade out of 20 plus specific comments on the answer sheet, not just a number.
