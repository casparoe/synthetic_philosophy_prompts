I'm finishing a paper on personal identity that leans hard on Dennett's idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity — an abstraction over someone's habits, memories, and self-descriptions, in roughly the way a center of gravity is an abstraction over a mass distribution: a posit we use to organize predictions about the system, not a component hidden inside it. I use the analogy twice: once in its home setting, to deflate the thought that there must be some inner narratological core where the self really lives (the self is not localized, just as a hoop's center of mass sits in the empty space at its middle); and once as a bridge to a claim about AI systems — that a conversational system with a persistent, self-consistent self-model has a self in the same deflationary-but-real sense, no more and no less than we do.

Before this goes out to reviewers who will know their Dennett, I want the analogy itself audited rather than defended. Specifically:

1. The correspondence table, made explicit rather than gestured at. What in the physical setup maps onto what in the psychological one? Candidates I half-see but haven't worked out: mass distribution ↔ the person's behavioral dispositions and narrative self-descriptions; the equations of mechanics ↔ folk psychology as the predictive theory; prediction tasks (where the object will tip, how it will balance) ↔ self- and other-prediction; the fact that centers of gravity are calculated rather than discovered ↔ the constructed or invented flavor of self-ascriptions; the non-localization point above ↔ the failure to find a self-place in the brain. For each row, say whether the mapping is tight, loose, or merely decorative, and why.

2. The disanalogies, sorted by how much damage they do to my two uses, separately. Some I can already anticipate: a center of gravity is exactly fixed by the mass distribution, while the self seems underdetermined by any "distribution" of narrative material; no one has ever mistaken a center of gravity for a locus of experience, while selves are phenomenally loaded for most people; two overlapping objects have two centers of gravity without anyone calling it pathology, while multiplicity of selves is treated as a disorder (or is it?); centers of gravity have no audience, while narrative selves look constitutively audience-involving. Tell me which of these actually matter for the deflationary use in the home setting, which matter for the AI bridge, and which are idle.

3. The verdict on the bridge claim. Does "same kind of abstraction, same ontological status" survive, or does the analogy quietly import features of human selves — temporally extended narrative accumulation, the strange arrangement whereby the self is at once author, character, and audience, uptake by other people who tell your story back to you — that a conversational system's self-model lacks? And if the bridge fails, does the deflationary claim in the home setting survive anyway, or was the analogy carrying both?

4. If there is a better analogy for the AI-self claim specifically — fictional character, legal person, center of lift, something else entirely — what is it, what does it get right that the gravitational one doesn't, and where does it break instead?
