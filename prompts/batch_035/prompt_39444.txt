I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on moral progress, organized loosely around Peter Singer's idea of the expanding circle — how concern has extended, slowly and unevenly, from kin to strangers, to distant peoples, and, on some accounts, to animals. In the readings and in seminar discussion I keep noticing a pattern I can't find a name for, and before I build my term paper around it I want to know whether philosophy, moral psychology, or any adjacent field already has a concept for it.

The pattern is this: whenever the group currently outside the moral circle comes close to satisfying the stated criterion for inclusion, the criterion itself gets revised — usually without anyone admitting that's what's happening — into a new one that the group fails. When non-European peoples could no longer plausibly be denied language, tool use, or culture, the operative criterion quietly became civilization, and later gave way to racial pseudoscience. When it became difficult to deny that animals suffer, defenders of the status quo stopped appealing to sentience and began appealing to autonomy or rationality — properties that were never the basis for including infants or the severely cognitively impaired in the first place. And in current debates about moral status for sophisticated AI, I notice the criterion shifting again, toward being biological, or having a body, or having interests one can harm — standards that seem to get chosen after we already know whom we want them to include.

A classmate told me this is just the no-true-Scotsman fallacy, but that doesn't seem right: nobody is redefining a category to rescue a generalization; it's the standard for moral standing itself that migrates. It also seems different from moving the goalposts, which is a rhetorical move within one argument, whereas here the shift happens across a whole society, over generations, and often with complete sincerity. And it can't be dismissed as mere rationalization, because each new criterion does capture something the old one genuinely missed — which is presumably why every revision looks reasonable in isolation.

So, my questions: Does this phenomenon have an established name, in philosophy or in the empirical literature on prejudice, dehumanization, or speciesism? If there is a nearest established concept, please explain precisely how it differs from what I've described, since the label alone won't help me. I'd also like to know whether anyone has used this pattern to put pressure on the Singer-style explanation of moral progress — the thought being that if the circle really expanded because rational consistency kept forcing the criterion outward, we would expect criteria to be abandoned when they start pointing toward inclusion, rather than being replaced by handier ones. And if no established concept fits, don't just coin a term and stop: define the phenomenon sharply enough to distinguish it from ad hoc rescue, post-hoc rationalization, and definitional gerrymandering, and then tell me where the boundaries run — in particular, what to say about the hard case in which the criterion revision is sincere and the old criterion really was inadequate.
