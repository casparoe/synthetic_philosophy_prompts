I'm not a philosophy student or anything like that — I never went to college — but for the last few years I've been chewing on an idea I worked out for myself, and I'd like a professional philosopher's help stress-testing it. Please take on the role of an expert in ethics and the history of moral thought, and think carefully before answering; if it helps, brainstorm several lines of attack on my view before organizing your response.

Here is the view. Moral progress is real, not just fashion. It has a direction, and the direction is set by the expansion of the circle of beings we feel obligated to: first the tribe, then the clan, then co-religionists, then the nation, then other races, then (perhaps) women, children, the disabled, animals, maybe future generations. Each expansion was treated as absurd by respectable people at the time, and each later came to seem obvious. That part I know others have said. My addition is my explanation of the mechanism. I think the circle of moral concern tracks, almost mechanically, the circle of people you can actually talk to. Morality, on my picture, is mostly a coordination habit: we extend serious moral concern to whoever is inside our communications network, because you can't sustain daily indifference to someone whose voice you hear. So the abolitionists won once the enslaved were literate and writing; labor won once workers had newspapers; animals started winning once we could film factory farms. On my view, then, moral progress is downstream of communication technology, and it's predictable: hook a group into the network and, within a generation or two, their moral status rises. I also believe regress is rare and temporary — once a group is inside the circle and the network stays connected, it doesn't fall back out.

What would count against this? I want a serious map of the view's weak points, not reassurance. Please sort the possible defeaters from most to least dangerous. A few I've already half-thought of, so tell me if they're as bad as I fear: (1) highly networked societies have committed the worst atrocities — genocide in the age of radio and railroads — so maybe connection amplifies whatever the majority already feels rather than widening the circle; (2) my view may be circular, because I could always classify whichever changes I like as "progress" and then notice that the circle "expanded," so no observation could ever refute me; (3) moral concern might really track power and self-interest, with communication just a correlated bystander — the circle expanding only when it became cheap or useful for the powerful to expand it. Are there defeaters I haven't listed? Which of them would a thoughtful defender of this kind of view concede genuinely counts against it, and which would they brush off without cheating? And be honest about the regress point: is the claim that the circle never shrinks back (for groups that stay connected) a real prediction my view makes, or a decoration I'd quietly abandon the first time I found a counterexample? If the whole package turns out to be unfalsifiable, tell me whether that's fatal for a view of this kind or just the ordinary condition of big-picture claims about history. Finally, tell me what evidence would make you personally start to doubt the communication-network mechanism, even if you'd still believe in moral progress itself.
