I teach an undergrad course on rational choice and its critics, and next week is the session on revealed preference -- the idea that utility isn't some mental quantity we measure but just a summary of what people choose, so "prefers A to B" means nothing over and above "would pick A over B when both are on the table."

Design a 50-minute activity for ~24 students that makes them feel both halves of that at once: the surprising power (you really can reconstruct a lot about a person from their ledger of choices alone) and the moment it starts to feel like a con (the ranking you reconstruct doesn't seem to be about what the chooser actually wanted).

Here's the shape I have in mind, but fix whatever needs fixing. Split the room: half "citizens," half "economists." Citizens each get an endowment of tokens and face a menu each round -- say five rounds -- of two or three goods with posted prices (coffee vouchers, snacks, early-leave tokens, whatever's cheap for me to actually hand out, or just point scores). Prices shift between rounds. Economists recieve nothing but the purchase records: no talking between the two groups, no watching, only ledgers cross the room. The economists' job is to produce, for each citizen, an ordinal ranking of the bundles, plus a couple of predictions ("at these prices she'd buy..."), and to flag anyone whose record looks inconsistent -- chose A over B early, then B over A later at the same effective prices.

The part I most want: some rounds should be engineered so that choices are constraint-driven rather than desire-driven. A round where one good is simply unavailable. A round where you must spend your whole endowment (forced choice among bad options). A round with a small random shock. And a round where prices change enough that a sharp economist can decompose "I buy less coffee now" into substitution vs. income effects. Then the reveal: economists present each citizen's "preferences" back to them, citizens vote accept/reject on the description of themselves, and we count how much of the room got correctly described by their choices alone.

The debrief should map moments in the game onto the actual positions: the straightforward Samuelson-style reading where you bite the bullet and say a preference for a thing you were forced to take just IS the preference you have; the adaptive preferences / sour-grapes worry; the citizen whose "inconsistency" was really context-sensitivity; and then the loop I most want to land -- the vote itself is a choice, so does the revealed preference theorist get to treat my rejection of his description as just more data about my preferences? I want the room to feel that trap close on them.
