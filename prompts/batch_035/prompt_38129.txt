In our pragmatics reading group we play a classification game: one member reports an utterance along with a candidate piece of content, and everyone judges whether that content is entailed by what is said or merely implicated. My friend's track record so far: for "Some of the guests left early," she ruled that at least one left is entailed, while that not all left is an implicature; for "She locked the door and turned off the lights," the order of the two events is an implicature; for "War is war," the sentence is trivially true yet implicates something about the horrors of war; for "That flag is red," being red is simply part of what is said; and for "He put the kettle on," said in reply to my asking where I could get tea, she held that the availability of tea is an implicature. Next week's cases: (1) "She is poor but honest," with the question whether the contrast signaled by "but" is an implicature, given that it seems resistant to explicit cancellation; (2) a trained mynah bird that utters "Some of the guests left early," with the question whether anything is implicated at all when there is no communicative intention behind the sounds; and (3) "The committee hasn't stopped publishing its findings — indeed, it never published any," with the question whether the committee's former publishing habit was ever implicated. Predict her verdict on each of the three, say which rule she seems to be running — cancellability, non-truth-conditionality, calculability from the Cooperative Principle, or something else — give your reasons for preferring one rule over its rivals, and describe a further case that would best tell those rival rules apart.
