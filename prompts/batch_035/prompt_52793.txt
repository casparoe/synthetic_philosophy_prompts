In my philosophy of mind reading group, someone waved off my question — whether the "I" who regrets a decision and the "I" who made it are the same self or just causally connected stages — with Dennett's phrase "the self as a center of narrative gravity." The point, as she deployed it, was that selves are posits like a physical object's center of gravity: convenient fictions we compute for prediction, not further facts about the world, and so my question was confused in the way a question about whether a center of gravity is "really" the same one after the object is nudged would be confused. I want to know what work that slogan can actually do. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing: for instance, that selves are unreal in the way centers of gravity are unreal; that centers of gravity are actually real (as legitimate abstracta) and so this is a thin realism about selves rather than eliminativism; that selves are constituted by narratives, so selfhood is an achievement of story-telling creatures rather than a metaphysical given; that self-talk is merely instrumentally justified prediction-machinery; and any other readings you can make precise. For each, say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or false. Here is the wrinkle that has me stuck: centers of gravity are extraordinarily cheap. Saw a chair in half and you have two of them; move your finger under a book and you shift one; redescribe an object and its center of gravity comes and goes without anything happening to the object. If the analogy holds, selves should be that easy to create, divide, and annihilate by mere redescription — which seems to make the slogan entail that personal fission, death, and survival over time are artifacts of how we narrate, not facts we discover. Does any reading of the slogan license that, and does any reading license my classmate's dismissal of my question, or did she overreach? I would like a committed answer rather than a neutral menu: at the end, tell me which reading you think Dennett's slogan actually amounts to, whether that reading is true, and whether it dissolves my question about regret or merely relocates it.
