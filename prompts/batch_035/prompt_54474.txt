my legislation seminar prof is a hardline textualist and his favorite move goes like this: reading a statute is like reading instructions your boss left on your desk before leaving on a two-week trip somewhere with no phone signal. you can't call, the boss can't clarify, so there's exactly one sensible question to ask: what would a competent employee, reading the note in context, take it to direct? not "what did the boss deep down really want" in some psychological sense, and defintely not "what would the boss want now that they've seen how it's turning out." he uses this to argue judges should just be good employees -- apply the text as a competent reader at the time of enactment would have understood it, and quit hunting for legislative intent or refreshing the purposes every generation.

the analogy is doing a lot of work and I can't tell how much. like, who exactly is the boss here -- a few hundred people who mostly never read the bill they voted on? and the "employee" reading the note is also the one with the power to punish people for misreading it, and to decide what counts as a misreading, which no actual workplace setup has. plus in the real version the boss sometimes comes back and amends the note, and knowing that seems like it should change how I read it the first time.

can you audit this for me: what maps onto what (the note, the boss, the employee, the fact that you can't call, the two weeks), where the mapping breaks down, and then -- this is the part I actually care about -- which of the broken bits matter for the conclusion he wants (fix the meaning at enactment, no purpose-hunting, no updating) versus which ones he could just concede for free. also what does the analogy smuggle in about delegation and authority that a purposivist would reject right at the start? and speaking of which, the purposivists seem to have their own pet picture, something like reading a will or a recipe -- reading any purposeful document where you're trying to figure out what the author was trying to get done, not decode words -- does that one hold up any better or does it carry its own luggage? last thing: if neither analogy can really carry the weight, is there one that would?
