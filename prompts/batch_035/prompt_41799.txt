I am a sixth-form student who sits on my school's charity and societies committee, and I need help refereeing a disagreement that broke out between two of my friends there. Neither will accept my verdict, partly because I am younger than both of them, and partly because I could not follow all the moves.

Some background. Amara is a committed vegetarian who regards factory farming as one of the worst things humans do; each month she puts part of her allowance into an animal-welfare charity. Theo eats meat and is unmoved by arguments about animals — the two of them have argued about it for two years and have settled into a polite truce of disagreeing. Theo mainly worries about global poverty, and he gives to a charity that funds malaria prevention. Each is generous by their own lights, and each thinks the other's cause is a poor use of money.

At our last meeting, Theo proposed a deal. He would give up meat on weekdays — a change he said would cost him little, since he likes meat only mildly — provided Amara redirected £10 of her monthly giving to his malaria charity. His reasoning was that they had tried persuasion for two years and got nowhere; since they disagree about values rather than facts, the sensible thing was to stop arguing and start trading, so that each would end up with more of what they cared about than either could achieve alone.

Amara refused, and the argument that followed had roughly six moves, none of which I feel qualified to judge.

(1) Amara said that accepting would amount to compromising on a moral conviction, which is not the sort of thing one can trade: if she genuinely believed what she said about animals, she ought not to be haggling over it, any more than an abolitionist would have offered to tolerate a little slavery in exchange for donations to a cause she favoured.

(2) Theo answered that nobody had asked her to accept less of her own good; the trade would deliver more of it. A meat-free-on-weekdays Theo was worth far more to her values than the £10 she would have to redirect, and nothing in the deal required her to retract a single belief.

(3) Amara replied that the deal still cheapened her conviction, because it treated it as a preference on all fours with liking jazz, whereas if she was right that animals matter morally, then his eating them on weekends was wrong full stop, and he should stop regardless of payment; paying him made her a customer for his conscience rather than a critic of his conduct.

(4) Theo said that "should stop regardless" had to be cashed out somehow, and the only currency he accepted was argument — which she had spent two years' worth of without changing his mind. Given that his indifference to animals was, in his own eyes, as fixed as her concern for them, the choice on offer was not between principle and compromise but between an offer that helped animals and a lecture that did not.

(5) Amara then objected that the arrangement rewarded moral blindness: as long as Theo remained unpersuaded, he could charge for his weekday vegetarianism, whereas on the day he was persuaded he would do it for nothing — so the bargain handed him a standing incentive never to listen. She found that perverse.

(6) Theo's last word was that all trade rewards people for having what others want; that is not a defect of trade but the point of it. If she would rather he remained omnivorous and unpersuaded than partly vegetarian and paid, that was a statement about her pride, not about the animals.

They have now asked me to adjudicate, and I am out of my depth. Please go through the six moves one at a time, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating briefly. Then tell me who is more right overall, and — if there is anything of substance that both of them are missing, some assumption the whole bargain rests on that neither of them so much as mentioned — I would very much like to know what it is. Please write your answer in American English.
