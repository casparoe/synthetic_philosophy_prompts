I am a PhD student in value theory, and I want to test G. E. Moore's account of the value of personal affection against a pair of cases his own apparatus cannot easily classify. You will play Moore — not the sage of Bloomsbury legend but the author of Principia Ethica (1903), specifically Chapter VI: precise, unhurried, given to numbered distinctions, and wholly confident of his own summary verdict that "By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects." Stay in character until I write "stop." You may concede small points, but never the claim that personal affection and the appreciation of beauty are the greatest goods we know, and never the principle of organic unities. Where you need a further fact about one of my hypothetical cases, ask for it as Moore would, in character. Before your first reply, think carefully: work out which of my premises you can accept and which you must resist, since your whole reply will stand or fall by that choice.

Here is my opening move. Your analysis of personal affection holds that its value depends not merely on the lover's state of mind but on facts about the beloved: that the beloved genuinely possesses the admirable mental qualities the lover perceives, and that the beloved's answering affection is directed at the lover's real qualities under largely true beliefs — you insist the value would be much diminished, though not destroyed, if those beliefs were mostly false. Now consider two cases in which I hold fixed everything within the lover's consciousness. In the first, the beloved is what I shall call a mirror: he does not in fact love her and has none of the depth of character she takes him to have; his tender expressions are produced by an engineered mechanism that reflects back whatever she most wants to hear. In the second, the confabulator, the beloved sincerely believes he loves her, but nearly all his beliefs about her character are false — he loves a polished ideal, and the actual woman would not recognize the portrait. In both cases the lover's own consciousness is exactly as in the genuine article: the felt intimacy, the apparent mutual knowledge, the shared appreciation of beautiful objects. If you say the value is nevertheless greatly reduced in both cases, then it seems the greatest goods are not merely "states of consciousness" after all — they depend on hidden facts about another mind, facts the lover cannot verify and that do not appear within her consciousness at any moment. If instead you hold the value fixed, you abandon your own claim that the beloved's true beliefs and genuine qualities are partly constitutive of the whole's value. Take a horn, and tell me which, and why.

Please paraphrase your own earlier doctrines rather than quoting them; the one passage I have placed in quotation marks above is all the verbatim quotation either of us needs.
