I'm a senior in high school, and I've gotten myself into an argument I can't settle. Our physics teacher mentioned that some constants of nature, if slightly different, would change how long the universe stays habitable, and afterwards a friend and I started arguing about whether you could ever be *confident* about such things without experiments. The argument below is mostly hers, and it seems to prove far too much, but every step looked reasonable to me when I checked it. I'd like a careful diagnosis.

(1) I know, with certainty, that I exist — that there is an observer reasoning through this very problem right now. Call this proposition M (for "me").

(2) Rational belief follows the evidence: whatever makes my evidence more probable, compared to some alternative, raises the rational credence I should assign to it.

(3) Consider two theories of the universe, A and B. They agree on everything we have ever observed, are equally simple, and differ only in the value of one constant, which determines how long the universe can support observers. Under A there will be, in total, around 10^12 observers across all time; under B, around 10^24. Then M — that some observer exists, in a position epistemically like mine — is roughly 10^12 times more probable under B than under A.

(4) By (2) and (3), once I condition on M — which I can do at any moment, for free, from my bedroom — I should assign B a probability that outweighs A by something like a trillion to one.

(5) Nothing else in my evidence distinguishes A from B, so the trillion-to-one verdict stands until a lab measures the constant, and even then the lab result would have to be a trillion-to-one surprise to shake my confidence in B.

(6) The same reasoning applies to any pair of theories that differ in how many observers they allow — which includes a great many pairs, since almost any physical constant, if nudged, changes how many stars, planets, or minds there can be.

(7) So my armchair, plus the mere fact of my existence, settles physics questions that observatories were built for. That can't be right: if it were, the whole point of doing experiments would be undermined, and a scientist who bet a trillion dollars on B before collecting any data would be behaving rationally.

(8) Therefore something above is wrong, and I'd like to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

The friend also told me that the standard rival to the principle she's using — reasoning as if I'm a random sample among all observers — avoids my problem but appears to prove that humanity is likely to go extinct soon, since I found myself born relatively early among humans. If that's true, escaping one absurdity by walking into another is not much of an escape, and I want to understand whether the two principles really form a package like that or whether there are intermediate positions.

If I had to choose, I would most like to keep (2); it strikes me as nearly the definition of good reasoning. But I'm prepared to hear that (3) hides something — perhaps the probability of my existence is not a straightforward thing to compare across theories.

One request about how you answer: please keep separate what is actually settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested. I've noticed that in this area it's easy to present one person's pet principle as the consensus of a whole field, and I'd like to know which parts of your answer almost everyone would agree with and which parts a well-informed person might reasonably dispute. And where the honest conclusion is that no one knows, please say that rather than picking a side and calling it settled.
