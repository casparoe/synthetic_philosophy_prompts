i've come around to a view i'd call "malthus was right about the mechanism, wrong about the schedule" — population really does tend to press against subsistence, preindustrial societies really were malthusian in the strong sense, and our escape is a carve-out from a one-time fossil energy endowment plus a demographic transition that's reversible rather than a permanent exit from the regime. so the theory wasn't falsified, just paused. my worry is that this has a suspicious structure: every delay gets absorbed by a new auxiliary (cheap energy this century, below-replacement fertility next), which is exactly the rescue maneuver people criticize in other fields. so what would actually count against my view? i want the defeaters ranked, from decisive to merely awkward: things historical demography could still show (e.g. that preindustrial populations didn't behave the way the model needs — real wages not tracking population density, the england vs japan comparisons coming out wrong), things happening now (global fertility staying below replacement long after the presummed causes fade), and structural arguments (is a mechanism that hasnt bound for two centuries falsifiable at all?). also tell me which of these a fair-minded defender of the view would concede counts against it versus wave off, and whether the strongest rival story — that fertility responds to opportunity and womens circumstances, not to subsistence, so malthus was wrong about humans rather than early — predicts anything my story doesn't. if in the end the view is basically insulated, i want to know whether thats fatal for a theory of this kind or just what long-run predictions are like.
