I spend way too much time in AI safety forums, and lately every thread about superhuman persuasion has someone swoop in with the old proverb — you know the one, "you can fool all the people some of the time, and some of the people all the time, but you can't fool all the people all the time." Everyone attributes it to Lincoln, though apparently even that's shaky. Anyway, the move is always the same: someone raises the scenario where an AI system is drastically better than any human at arguing, at rhetoric, at knowing exactly which emotional buttons to press on which person, and the reply-er treats the proverb like it's a load-bearing principle that makes the worry go away. Nobody can fool ALL of the people ALL of the time, so truth wins out eventually, so persuasion risks are self-limiting, case closed, back to worrying about capabilities.

I'm pretty sure this is doing way less work than people think, but I also suspect the proverb isn't just false — it's a bunch of different claims wearing one trenchcoat, and some of them might actually be fine. Like, off the top of my head it could be a dry statistical point (any long-running deception leaves a persistent population of the undeceived, and maybe that residual is enough for correction), or an institutional claim about adversarial checking (courts, science, markets — systems where deception gets exploited by competitors, so the system as a whole is hard to fool), or something about the internal economics of lying (deceivers have to track the truth to hide from it, so honest arguers have some structural edge), or a mere tautology if you squint (no falsehood can deceive literally everyone forever, since the deceivers themselves know better). And notice the proverb says nothing about WHO the fooler is — it reads like it's about some guy, one of us, operating with human-scale resources. Does it even apply when the fooler can run a personalized pitch for every single person on earth simultaneously, one that never gets exposed to public cross-examination because no two people hear the same argument?

So what I want is a proper menu: lay out the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making, from trivial to substantive to straight-up false, and for each one say whether it's plausible and — this is the part I care about — whether it would actually support the dismissive move in the AI persuasion debates if it were true. I suspect the readings that are true don't support the move and the readings that would support it are the ones we have no reason to beleive once the fooler is smarter than everyone, but check me on that. Also flag where the proverb is doing sneaky work with words like "all" and "eventually" — if 40% of people are fooled indefinitely, is the proverb still true while everything the AI-safety person was worried about happens anyway? Lay it out as a list I can paste into the next thread.
