I'm a novelist, twenty years into the trade, and my working vocabulary is the craft one: point of view, free indirect discourse, the unreliable narrator, the implied author, psychic distance, interiority, the ideal reader. I've been reading Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments and keep having the sensation that he is describing something I do for a living. His account of sympathy as a matter of imaginatively placing myself in your situation, rather than somehow receiving your feelings from outside, sounds like the basic act of third-person narration. His impartial spectator -- the well-informed figure within the breast whose regard I want to deserve, as distinct from the actual people whose applause I might merely want to receive -- sounds suspiciously like the implied author, or like the ideal reader I half construct and half discover as a draft matures. And his distinction between the love of praise and the love of praise-worthiness sounds like the difference between writing for the market and writing something the best version of my own judgment could not fault. I want you to take this feeling seriously enough to test it rather than flatter it. Restate Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: map the spectator onto the implied author if that is the honest mapping, or tell me why it isn't; say what free indirect discourse does to Smith's claim that I never feel what you feel but only my own imaginative approximation of it; translate the desire for the approval of those I actually face into the terms of an actual readership, and the desire to be worthy of approval into the terms of the judgment of a constructed spectator. Then give me the residue report: which of Smith's claims survive the translation cleanly, which have to be distorted to fit, and which have no expression in craft vocabulary at all. There are two places where I already suspect the translation breaks, and I want your judgment on both. First, Smith's spectator disciplines by way of propriety -- the demand that my passions be pitched to what the spectator can go along with, neither more nor less -- which may be stricter and stranger than anything a narrator's tacit judgments amount to. Second, Smith's spectator does moral work, not merely aesthetic work, and my vocabulary has no obvious slot for that distinction. Finally, tell me where you think the residue points: is my craft vocabulary missing something Smith actually saw, or did Smith's framework smuggle in claims that only look deeper than they are? Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each of your mappings, and single out the one point in your translation you are least sure of -- I would rather know exactly where the ground is soft than receive a finished edifice.
