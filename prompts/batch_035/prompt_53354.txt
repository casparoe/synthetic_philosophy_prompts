You are a legal philosopher with deep expertise in general jurisprudence, particularly the natural law tradition and the twentieth-century debates between legal positivists and their critics. Please think carefully before answering, and begin with a brief brainstorm of all the readings you can think of before organizing them.

In a seminar discussion of the grudge informer cases from postwar Germany — the wife who denounced her husband to the Nazi courts, and the later prosecution of the informer — a classmate shut down my hesitation by invoking the old slogan that an unjust law is no law at all. His point was that the Nazi statute under which the husband was convicted simply was not law, so nothing legal needed to be said about it. I have since heard the same line used in defenses of civil disobedience, including by Martin Luther King, and I suspect the people repeating it are not all making the same claim. What I want is a disambiguation. Lay out the distinct precise theses that the slogan could be expressing — from weak readings on which unjust laws remain valid law but create no moral obligation to obey, through claims about validity conditions, to strong semantic readings on which nothing unjust counts as law at all, and any procedural readings on which laws that flout legality itself fail to be law. For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible, whether it is philosophically substantive or merely a rhetorical restatement of some weaker point, and, most importantly, whether it would actually have delivered my classmate his conclusion about the Nazi statute. I would also like a verdict on which reading people who invoke the slogan in debates like that one most likely intend, and whether what they intend can survive the standard positivist objection that the slogan merely confuses validity with moral merit. Present the readings as an annotated list I can bring back to seminar.
