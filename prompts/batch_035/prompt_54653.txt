I write features on neuroscience and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and a skeptics group in my city has roped me into running their monthly session on Libet's free will experiments — the famous ones where a readiness potential shows up in the brain before people report deciding to move. The audience is maybe thirty sharp laypeople: programmers, a retired nurse, one guy who's read every pop-neuroscience bestseller ever printed, and they will all arrive convinced the headline "scientists proved free will is an illusion" settled this decades ago. I've got forty minutes and a whiteboard, and honestly I'm less worried about the philosophy than about the sequencing — the material explodes the moment you present it in the wrong order. Don't explain Libet to me, I know the material cold; plan the session for me instead.

So: what order should the pieces go in so the experiment lands as a quirky measurement with serious limitations rather than a bombshell? I keep flip-flopping on whether to open with the measurement problem itself — that subjects dated their own decision by watching a spot on an osciloscope, so the "when did you decide?" number is itself doing something philosophically load-bearing — or whether leading with that undercuts the drama before the hook even lands. What should the carrying example be: the wrist-flex setup itself, or some mundane moment from their own mornings, and what does it need to have and lack? Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually come up and the point in the sequence where I should head off each one — I'm guessing the big ones are "the readiness potential IS the decision," then "your brain reads your mind before you do," then the veto, then the giant leap from flicking a wrist whenever you feel like it to "all my everyday choices are illusions," but tell me if I've got the order wrong. And tell me where to slot the hardest part: the later modeling line (2010s-ish) arguing the readiness potential may be a statistical artifact — slow random neural fluctuations that a threshold model fits fine without any decision signal at all, so averaging across trials manufactures the apparent early onset. Can a lay audience actually absorb that in ten minutes, or should I gesture at it and be upfront about the gesture?

Give me one question I can ask at the end to check whether they understood the experiment's actual structure rather than just left the room with a shakier grip on free will, and be honest about what I should openly refuse to get into at this level — consciousness itself, quantum, whether determinism is true — and say so out loud to them. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me. American English, please.
