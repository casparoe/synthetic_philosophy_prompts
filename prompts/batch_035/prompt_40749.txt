The argument over probability in the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation has now run for a quarter century without either side moving, and I have come to suspect that no amount of additional formalism will settle it, because the real disagreement lies elsewhere. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this dispute rather than another round of it.

To make sure we are looking at the same thing, here is the situation as I understand it. In Everettian quantum mechanics every outcome of a measurement occurs, on some branch, with a "weight" given by the squared amplitude of that branch. That is fine as physics, but it leaves the Born rule looking like an orphan: since everything happens, "the probability that outcome A occurs" cannot mean the fraction of trials on which it happens, and an agent who knows that both A and not-A will occur cannot be uncertain about which one she will see. The Deutsch–Wallace program attempts to close this gap with decision theory rather than probability theory proper: a rational agent who is about to branch is offered axioms constraining her preferences over future experiences — roughly, that her preferences should not depend on how the branchings are formally decomposed, and that she should prefer bets whose payoff is guaranteed to a mixed bet that yields the same payoffs on some branches and worse on others — and it is then argued that any agent satisfying these axioms must act as if each outcome had probability equal to its Born weight, so the squared amplitudes can play the probabilistic role without ever being assumed to be probabilities. The critics, most persistently Adrian Kent but also others, respond in several overlapping ways: that the "harmless" axioms covertly presuppose a probability measure, since reading the weights as chances is exactly what makes indifference to formal re-decomposition of a mixture look rational; that decision-theoretic equivalence between "worth caring about" and "worth expecting" needs a probabilistic notion of expectation already in place, making the derivation circular; and that, even if the theorem stands, it delivers at most betting advice — that you should act as if something were uncertain — and never the fact that something is uncertain. Meanwhile, some Everettians concede the decision-theoretic route entirely and rest instead on typicality, the claim that we are entitled to regard our own branch as typical, which the same critics dismiss as the missing probability assumption wearing a different hat.

The theorem itself, I take it, is not disputed; both sides accept that the axioms entail the Born weights. My working hypothesis is therefore that the disagreement is not mathematical at all but about what a derivation is permitted to take as primitive: the Everettians treat rationality axioms as fixed points requiring no probabilistic grounding, while their critics hold that nothing short of outright chance can legitimately ground betting advice, so that the two camps are operating with different standards for when a derivation succeeds. Is that right? Or is the dispute better understood as verbal, turning on what "probability" means when every outcome obtains? Or as two groups answering different questions under a single heading — one about what an agent facing branching should do, the other about what makes probabilistic claims about the world true? Alternatively, is there a genuine hidden formal question still unresolved, as some of the circularity charges would imply if they could be stated precisely enough to be theorems? I want your diagnosis supported by what the participants actually argue, not a survey of interpretations of quantum mechanics generally, and an honest account of what, if anything, could count as progress on your diagnosis — a sharpened axiom, a theorem, a clean distinction between betting advice and genuine chance, or the concession that the question is ill-posed and why progress should not be expected.

Please close with a single final line of the form

\diagnosis{label}

where "label" is one of: verbal, substantive-formal, substantive-empirical, different-questions, ill-posed, bedrock-intuitions — or a hyphenated combination, in order of importance, if you think several apply.
