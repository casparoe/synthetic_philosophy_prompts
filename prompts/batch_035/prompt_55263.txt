Draft the joint statement that a committed causal decision theorist and a committed updateless decision theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on two decision problems.

Case 1 (counterfactual mugging). An extremely reliable predictor flips a fair coin in secret and then: if the coin came up heads, it hands you $10,000, but only if it predicted that you would hand over $100 in the event the coin came up tails; if tails, it appears and asks you for $100. You are now being asked for the $100. Assessed before the flip, the policy of paying is worth $4,950 in expectation; the policy of refusing is worth $0.

Case 2 (XOR blackmail). You may or may not have an embarrassing condition, and you would pay a great deal to keep the matter private. A blackmailer knows whether you have the condition and has reliably predicted what you will do. The blackmailer sends you a letter demanding $1,000 if and only if either (you have the condition and were predicted to refuse) or (you lack the condition and were predicted to pay). The letter has arrived. Paying has no causal effect on whether your secret becomes public.

The causal theorist refuses in both cases. The updateless theorist pays in the first and refuses in the second, and takes this asymmetry to be the strongest argument for her view: the two cases involve the same kind of evidential correlation between her response and an earlier event, yet call for opposite answers, because in Case 1 the correlation runs through the predictor's response to her decision procedure, while in Case 2 it runs through a variable her decision does not control.

Produce the document such a collaboration should issue, with the following sections:

(a) A numbered list of claims both parties can sign, including claims about the payoff structures of the two cases, about what each framework gets right in ordinary one-shot decisions, about precommitment (both would bind themselves in advance to pay in Case 1 if given the chance — state exactly what each party takes this shared judgment to show and not to show), and about which of the two cases more closely resembles decisions they actually expect to face.

(b) A disagreements section, with each residual disagreement phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — for instance, on whether the question 'What should I do?' asks for the evaluation of an act given one's current beliefs or for the output of a decision function chosen in ignorance of which branch one occupies; on whether refusing in Case 1 is a mistake or merely a price worth paying for the right general policy; and on whether the Case 2 asymmetry is principled or ad hoc.

(c) The cruxes, and for each crux at least one concrete case, argument, or test both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Include at least one case where the updateless theorist's recommendation is something both can agree looks terrible on its face, and at least one where the causal theorist's does, with each side stating how it handles that case.

(d) A statement of which parts of the dispute, if any, each party regards as merely verbal.

Neither party will sign a document that baldly says its view gets one of the two cases wrong; find formulations each could endorse while still disagreeing.
