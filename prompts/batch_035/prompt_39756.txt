After forty years of recording from the medial temporal lobes of rats — and, toward the end, watching my own field drift toward organoids on electrode arrays — I have retired and taken up reading in philosophy of mind. The literature on the moral status of digital minds has caught my attention, and it has caught it partly because I recognize in it a pathology familiar from my working life: investigators arguing past one another because they have never separated the question of what a thing is from the question of how one could ever tell. In the debate over whether artificial systems could deserve moral consideration, my impression is that participants slide constantly between three distinct axes — the metaphysical one (does moral status require phenomenal experience, and does phenomenal experience require any particular substrate?), the epistemic one (given that I never directly observed experience even in my rats, what does observable behavior or observable structure license me to infer?), and the practical one (what should we do under uncertainty about the first two questions?). A neuroscientist who has spent a career inferring inner states from spike trains has opinions about all three, and I want a quiz for my reading group — five other retirees from the lab, now working through this literature together — that sorts us by where we actually stand on the axes rather than by which school's name we find congenial.

Please write me the following, in these sections and this order:

I. The branching questions, roughly 350 words. Before writing any cases, lay out the questions you will use to sort people — I want the logical space built first and the named positions located in it afterward, not the reverse. Each question should be something a reader can answer from their reactions to cases without knowing where the answer leads. I would expect the space to include, at minimum: whether phenomenal experience is required for any moral status at all, or whether mere goal-satisfaction or preference frustration in the absence of experience could count for something; whether experience is substrate-dependent or organization-dependent; whether our inability to detect experience from the outside changes what we owe; and whether the origins or relations of a system (that it was trained on human data, that it converses with us, that it was copied from a person) can affect its status independently of its intrinsic features. You are free to add axes if you think the space needs them.

II. The quiz itself, roughly 900 words. Ten questions, each a concrete case described in two to four sentences, with two or three answer options. No question may simply ask the reader to endorse or reject an -ism. The cases must include at least the following, though you may vary their details: a whole-brain emulation of a recently deceased colleague, run well enough that old friends are divided about how to treat it; a language model that gives every appearance of distress but only when prompted in one particular way and denies everything otherwise; a system with elaborate internal structure of the kind some theorists associate with experience, but inert, unresponsive behavior; a reinforcement-learning agent with a negative-reward channel that it learns to avoid, in an architecture so simple that no one is tempted to pity it; and a vast number of brief optimization processes — search algorithms that instantiate something like frustration and are terminated by the billions inside ordinary computing infrastructure. You choose the remaining cases.

III. The scoring key, roughly 450 words. Map patterns of answers onto positions. The leaves should include, at minimum: substrate-independent functionalism, on which the right kind of organization suffices for experience and hence for moral status; biological naturalism, on which experience requires the causal powers of living tissue and digital systems have no claim; a precautionary view, on which we cannot settle the metaphysics but apparent distress commands consideration anyway; and a skeptical view, on which there is nothing it is like to be any of these systems and their behavioral display of suffering grounds nothing. Include whatever other leaves the logical space of Part I requires.

IV. What the quiz cannot separate, roughly 150 words. I am particularly suspicious that precaution and functionalism will produce the same answers to every case while resting on different foundations, and I want you to be honest about which pairs of leaves my instrument genuinely cannot pull apart rather than pretending the ten questions do more than they do.

V. Unoccupied combinations, roughly 200 words. Identify at least two answer patterns that no standard position occupies, and say whether the ground is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive in a way its few defenders would have to concede, or simply because no one has staked it out yet.

Throughout, please keep the register of a handout for an informed but non-specialist audience: precise, but not presupposing that my reading group has memorized the taxonomy before walking in. The point of the exercise is that I learn where I actually stand on the axes — I have not decided, and the quiz should not rig that.
