Retired chemist here -- I spent about 38 years running a spectroscopy lab, mostly polymer characterization, and now that I'm done with grant applications forever I've fallen hard for philosophy. I joined a monthly reading group at the university's continuing-ed center that's been working through books about AI risk, and we just spent two sessions on Bostrom's Superintelligence. Bostrom states the orthogonality thesis as follows: "Intelligence and final goals are orthogonal: more or less any level of intelligence could in principle be combined with more or less any final goal."

The way it got used is this: I floated the idea that a system genuinely smarter than all of us would presumably be smart enough to notice that, say, converting everything into paperclips is a stupid goal, the way any of us notice a failed experiment and revise. Another member shut me down with the orthogonality thesis and then kept talking, like that settles it. And here's my problem: in my world "orthogonal" meant dot product zero -- uncorrelated principal components, independent axes in a fit. That's an *empirical or statistical* claim. But I suspect what Bostrom means is something weaker and more logical, more like "there's no law of nature linking the two," which wouldn't impress me much either way. Back when I did instrument work, "in principle possible" and "what our machine actually does" were very different conversations, and nobody got to collapse the second into the first.

So here's my ask. You are a professional philosopher who works on philosophy of mind and AI, and you're used to talking to scientists without hand-waving. Think carefully before answering -- maybe brainstorm candidate readings first, loosely, and only then organize them. I want the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be expressing, laid out as a numbered menu from weakest/trivial to strongest/radical, with a verdict on each: trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or just false. I'm especially interested in whether it's a claim about logical possibility, about actual frequencies in the space of agents that could ever get built, about agents produced by optimizing processes like evolution vs. engineering, or about whether high intelligence in any way constrains what's worth wanting. Also flag any readings that fail outright because of incoherent goals a very smart agent arguably couldn't keep having. And for each reading, tell me whether it actually supports what my reading-group colleague used it for -- that a superintelligence wouldn't necessarily be benign -- or whether he was quietly sliding between readings.
