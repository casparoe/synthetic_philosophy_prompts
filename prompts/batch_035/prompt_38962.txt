I've ended up pretty committed to origin essentialism -- the view that I couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg. Not just "wouldn't have been me in some loose sense," but strictly: any being that developed from a different zygote is at best my counterpart, not me, however much like me they are. My reasoning is basically the Kripke-style one: you can coherently imagine someone just like me raised by different parents, but when you try to imagine *me* born to different parents, you're really just imagining a very similar person, and the "from this origin" part seems baked into what fixes who I am in the first place. It came up again last week because my flatmate and I got into it over embryo selection (we have a bioethics seminar together, which is where most of our arguments come from lately) -- she thinks the view implies that IVF discard-and-retry involves the "death" of particular possible people in a way that even pro-choice people should find troubling, and I'm honestly not sure she's wrong about that implication, which bothers me.

But I want to stress-test the view itself, not just its moral fallout. Tell me what would actually count against it. I know some of the usual pressure points: the sorites worry (Kripke himself seemed fine with a molecule or two of the gametes being different -- but then where does the line get drawn, and is a fuzzy line even coherent for an identity claim?); identical twins, since if the actual zygote had split slightly differently, "the zygote I came from" seems to have two claimants; chimerism, where two embryos fuse into one person, and it's very unclear which origin she essentially has, if either; and the counterpart-theoretic move that maybe the whole debate is just an artifact of how we fix the reference of "I" rather than a discovery about persons. There's also a case I keep chewing on that I've never seen discussed: suppose a zygote is assembled in vitro from my actual egg plus a sperm carrying the exact same genetic payload but from a different sample -- numerically different sperm, qualitatively indistinguishable. Is that person me? My intuition says no, but the intuition seems to rest on nothing but the brute "same sperm" requirement, which feels embarrassingly arbitrary when I say it out loud.

So: what should move me, sorted by how decisive it would be? Distinguish the stuff a committed origin essentialist would concede as counting against the view from the stuff they'd wave off, and tell me which (if any) of these cases the view genuinely cannot handle versus ones where it just takes a bullet -- and whether taking that bullet is a cost or just the price of doing modal business. And be honest with me about the meta-question too: a claim about what's possible can't exactly be refuted by an experiment, so is this view even exposed to anything? If it's been qualified enough that nothing counts against it, I want to know whether that's fatal for a metaphysical view of this kind or whether that's just what modal claims are like. Rank the defeaters from fatal through awkward to merely cosmetic, and if you think one of the rival views (say, a "close-enough origin" view or a psychological-continuity-first view) actually wins, say so plainly and tell me what convinced you.
