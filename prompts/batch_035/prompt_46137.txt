I direct the character-education program at a secondary school, and next month I present to the curriculum committee a proposal to build a required course around the close reading of biographies, taught on what I take to be Plutarch's method. My thesis is that students learn morally from lives only when the lives are selected and handled a certain way: chosen for small, character-revealing incidents rather than achievements, as Plutarch insisted when he distinguished his project from history-writing; presented with the subject's failures included and not explained away; arranged in contrasting pairs, as Plutarch paired Greek with Roman figures and appended formal comparisons weighing them against each other, so that the student has to do the evaluating rather than receive a verdict; and never capped with a stated moral. My further claim is that the two available alternatives fail in opposite directions: hagiography, which was built to produce imitation and instead produced credulity, and the contemporary genre of inspiring lives of scientists and founders, which I regard as hagiography with the theology removed and nothing put in its place.

The committee, for purposes of my prep, consists of four people. A developmental psychologist who holds that moral growth comes through the child's own reasoning about dilemmas, and who will say that emulation of exemplars is at best a mnemonic and at worst an invitation to unexamined authority. A social-learning theorist who is in principle favorable to modeling, but who will press on what students actually imitate when shown admirable people with serious vices. A historian of antiquity who will argue that Plutarch's Lives are rhetorical constructions in which the moral precedes the man, and that teaching them as I propose trades on students not knowing this. And a virtue ethicist, sympathetic, who will ask how emulation is supposed to avoid collapsing into either hero-worship or conformity to whatever the biographer chose to highlight.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections roughly in the order they will arrive, each phrased in the vocabulary of the committee member who will raise it, and ranked by how much damage it could do to the proposal. For each, a reply I can deliver in about a minute in a committee meeting, a note on what I should concede up front, and a flag on where the reply is genuinely soft and I would do better to admit it than to bluff. Tell me which of my three design rules — incident over achievement, failures included, paired contrasts — is doing the most work and which is the most vulnerable to being dropped by a skeptical committee. And include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
