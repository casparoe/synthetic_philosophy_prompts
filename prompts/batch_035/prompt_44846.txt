I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and during our department's moral psychology reading group a longstanding disagreement resurfaced about moral testimony that I'd like your help thinking through — not by surveying the literature, but by way of a friend. My close friend Dana, who is a physician and not a philosopher, has a stable-seeming set of verdicts about when a person may take someone else's word on a moral question, and I've been keeping a list. (i) She thinks a bystander who must choose instantly which of two drowning strangers to save does nothing wrong in simply taking a trusted companion's word for whom to try to save. (ii) She thinks her cousin did nothing wrong in becoming a vegetarian on the basis of a report co-signed by a dozen ethicists whose arguments the cousin can't reconstruct, since the cousin can at least reliably tell ethicists from cranks. (iii) She thinks a college friend of ours did do something wrong in cutting off a particular friendship purely on a beloved mentor's say-so, even granting that the mentor is genuinely wiser and saw the situation more clearly than our friend did. (iv) She thinks a family does nothing wrong in letting a hospital ethics consultant's judgment settle whether to withdraw a relative's life support, even though she agrees that the underlying question is contested and the family won't be able to retrace the consultant's reasoning. (v) She thinks she herself may not settle her view that lies told to spare a dying relative's feelings are permissible simply by noting that a majority of polled philosophers say so, given that she finds the arguments on both sides impenetrable.

The case I want to put to her next week concerns living kidney donation. Dana is considering donating a kidney to a stranger, and an ethicist she deeply respects has written that such donations are not merely permissible but obligatory for those medically able. Dana has read the article twice and cannot follow the central argument — she can't tell whether the piece is airtight or seductive. If she donates, it will be a one-off bodily decision with an impersonal beneficiary, made in a contested domain on the basis of testimony she cannot independently evaluate. So my questions for you: what will Dana say about whether she's permitted to go ahead on the ethicist's word alone? More importantly, I care about the rule (or rules) she seems to be running. At least three hypotheses fit her track record, as far as I can tell: an asymmetry between one-off decisions and settled moral beliefs, on which testimony can ground the former but never the latter; a scaffolding requirement, on which deference is fine exactly when one has enough independent competence to identify credible sources, and never when the testimony would be one's sole basis; and a domain restriction, on which first-personal or relational matters can't be settled by testimony while impersonal ones can. Perhaps there are better-fitting rules I haven't considered, or a unifying principle underneath all of these.

Please give me your best-guess prediction on the kidney case, with the reasoning that makes it your best guess rather than a coin flip; an honest statement of where the candidate rules agree and disagree on her earlier verdicts, so I can see whether her track record genuinely underdetermines the new verdict; and a specification of the single further case that would best discriminate between the surviving rules — ideally one I could actually pose to her over coffee. One request about the framing: I'd like you to keep clear, as you go, the line between what is basically settled among people who work on moral testimony and what is genuinely contested — I know that blanket pessimism about moral deference and optimism about it are both live positions, and that the very distinction between doing and believing on testimony has its serious critics — so please don't present either the pessimist's or the optimist's reading of Dana as the consensus view when it isn't. Please write your answer in American English.
