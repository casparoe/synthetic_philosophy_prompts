I sit on the electoral rules subcommittee of the constitutional commission drafting our country's first fully democratic constitution, and our mandate requires us to recommend, by written memo, the system for electing the 150-seat lower house. The relevant facts, from the commission's own surveys and the last semi-competitive election under first-past-the-post: there are four significant parties. One is regionally concentrated — it polls about 14% nationally but carries roughly 55% across 28 contiguous districts in the northeast. There is also a linguistic minority of about 7% of the population, dispersed through the major cities, too spread out to come first in any single-member district but comfortably above any plausible legal threshold under proportional representation. The previous FPTP elections produced single-party governments on 42–48% of the vote, and the commission is now deadlocked roughly down the middle between FPTP and a list-PR system.

The debate on the subcommittee has crystallized around a framing one of my colleagues insists on: the choice is between two conceptions of representation, on which an MP is a delegate chosen by and answerable to a particular community, versus on which parliament is a mirror in miniature of the nation, its seat shares matching vote shares. I suspect this framing hides what I find most troubling: under FPTP the same 15% of voters either gets 30 seats or none, depending solely on whether its supporters happen to live next to each other — geography, not numbers, decides. The concentrated regional party would be seated handsomely; the dispersed minority would vanish. It is not obvious to me that either conception of representation justifies that asymmetry rather than both condemning it. And there is a second problem the memo cannot dodge: the familiar empirical claims — that FPTP consolidates party systems, that PR breeds unstable coalitions, that single-member districts create constituency service — are statistical tendencies drawn from other countries, contested in the comparative literature, and our country has no settled party system yet for them to apply to. I want the memo to be honest about how much weight it is placing on contested empirical generalizations versus how much on the principled question.

Please act as a political philosopher with working knowledge of comparative electoral systems, and draft the recommendation memo our subcommittee can circulate to the full commission. Take a moment to brainstorm the strongest considerations on each side before committing, since I want a real recommendation, not a menu: FPTP, list PR with a specified legal threshold, or a mixed-member compromise, with the threshold or district structure stated concretely enough that the commission could act on it. The memo must explicitly address the concentrated-versus-dispersed asymmetry — whether it is a defect to be corrected, a price worth paying, or an artifact of framing — and must give the strongest consideration on the losing side a fair and substantive hearing, not a paragraph of lip service. Length should be what a commissioner can read before a Thursday session: no more than about 900 words.
