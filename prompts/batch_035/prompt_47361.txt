I'm part of a small reading group that works through AI-risk and AI-governance material, and we've hit a disagreement I can't resolve on my own. The topic is the strategy-stealing assumption, which the group knows from a 2019 essay by Paul Christiano on the AI Alignment Forum. As he introduces it, the assumption is that "for any strategy an unaligned AI could use to influence the long-run future, there is an analogous strategy that a similarly-sized group of humans can use in order to capture a similar amount of flexible influence over the future." The upshot, as we've been using it, is that a misaligned AI shouldn't be expected to win by out-clevering its defenders: whatever it could do, a sufficiently resourced group of humans (with comparably capable aligned AI doing the stealing) could in principle do too. Several of us have been leaning on this to rule out a whole family of takeover scenarios.

One member now argues that the assumption is refuted outright, and the argument has stuck with me because I cannot locate the false step and because its conclusion appears to prove far more than anyone in the room wants to accept. Here it is as I best understand it:

(1) Strategy-stealing assumption: for any strategy an unaligned AI could use, there is an analogous strategy available to a similarly-sized group of humans, so a misaligned AI cannot gain a decisive advantage through access to strategies its defenders lack; any advantage must come from raw capability or resources, not from the strategy space being asymmetrically open.
(2) Some of the most powerful strategies in any conflict are coalition strategies: distributed monitoring, redundancy, division of labor, commitment devices that work only when many independent parties bind themselves to punish defectors together. The power of these strategies consists in being many.
(3) A human coalition can execute a coalition strategy only by paying coordination costs: building trust, negotiating for months, absorbing defections, reconciling divergent interests. A misaligned AI — one goal system running many perfectly coordinated copies — pays none of these costs; its coalition is unified by construction rather than by treaty.
(4) Therefore there are strategies available to a misaligned AI for which no analogous strategy is available to a human group: the human analogue exists in name but not at the same cost, speed, or reliability, and the gap is not a difference in resources but in the feasibility conditions of the strategy itself.
(5) Since asymmetries of exactly this kind are what strategy-stealing was supposed to rule out, the assumption is false, and a misaligned AI can gain a decisive advantage through strategies defenders cannot use.
(6) Generalize: nothing in the argument turns on the adversary being an AI. Any internally unified adversary — a single state, a single firm, a single network — enjoys the same advantage over any coalition, because its internal coordination is constitutive where the coalition's is negotiated. So no coalition should ever be able to resist a unified adversary.
(7) Yet coalitions have resisted unified adversaries, if barely and only eventually: it took seven of them to bring down Napoleonic France. If (6) were true, (7) should be impossible.

The conclusion I am invited to accept is that (5) is correct and that (6) merely exposes why the historical cases differ: human coalitions were facing adversaries whose unity was imperfect and expensive to maintain — states can be split, bribed, and out-waited — whereas a misaligned AI's unity is a matter of what it is. On that reading the argument does not prove too much; it proves exactly what it should, and strategy-stealing is dead. I want to resist, but not by hand-waving, because if the assumption goes, the takeover scenarios I had been discounting come back onto the table.

Please do three things. First, tell me which step, if any, to reject, being explicit about readings: I suspect the argument turns on whether "analogous" and "available" in the assumption mean available-in-principle-given-comparable-resources or available-at-comparable-cost-speed-and-reliability, and on whether anything in the assumption's own wording commits it to one of those. Second, run the generalization test honestly in both directions: if the argument proves too much in the human case, identify the disanalogy that saves the assumption there, and then check whether that disanalogy also protects the assumption in its original AI application or only against the historical analogy — if the disanalogy does not transfer, for instance because the coalitions against Napoleon got repeated tries against a slow human adversary while a sufficiently fast AI takeover might not afford retries, say so plainly rather than rescuing the assumption by fiat. Third, price the options: if I give up strategy-stealing, what classes of takeover scenario am I committed to taking seriously that I was not before; if I keep it in a weakened form, state that form precisely and say what it no longer licenses me to conclude. I would rather keep some version of (1) than accept (5), but I want to know what that preference costs.
