I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums where expected value gets treated as the alpha and omega of practical rationality, and I still can't get straight what I actually think about St. Petersburg-style gambles. Here are my verdicts on some cases; tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and be blunt about which ones it can't.

1. The classic game: a fair coin, the pot starts at $2 and doubles with each heads, the game ends on the first tails, and I pay $25 to play once. Verdict: no. I know the expected value is infinite; I also notice I come out ahead only if the first tails comes on the sixth flip or later, which happens about one time in sixteen.

2. The same game, but payouts are denominated in utils rather than dollars — by stipulation each payout is worth exactly its face value to me, so diminishing marginal utility can't do any work. Same price, 25 utils. Verdict: yes, and I can't see how to refuse it — which sits awkwardly next to my no in case 1, where the only difference is the currency.

3. A single draw: 1 chance in 1,000 of $1,000,000, otherwise nothing, for a $25 ticket. Verdict: yes, comfortably.

4. A single draw: 1 chance in a billion of $10^18, otherwise nothing, for a $25 ticket. Verdict: no — and I notice its expected value, about a billion dollars net of the fee, dwarfs that of case 3, roughly $975. So whatever backs my verdicts here, it can't be expected value.

5. The one I keep going back and forth on: the classic game at $25 a play, except the house is only good for about $17 billion, so any pot beyond that is paid at the cap — and I get to play a thousand independent times. For a single play my gut says no; across a thousand plays it starts to feel different, and I can't tell whether that's a real asymmetry or a failure of nerve.

Is there one principle that delivers all of these — assembled from diminishing marginal utility, probability weighting, a ceiling on money I could actually use, or a shift from expected value to the median or the Kelly criterion — and if not, which verdict would you overturn first, and why that one? Please also say what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: the classic game at $25, but with any large payout delivered to my estate fifty years from now.
