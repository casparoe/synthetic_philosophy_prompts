Wine competitions go to absurd lengths to make sure judges taste blind — anonymised samples, flights shuffled, producers' names nowhere near the glass. Same with juried art prizes and blind auditions for orchestras. But the whole point of the exercise is that the public then buys the medal-winning bottle or books the winning musician on the strength of the verdict, without any first-hand contact with the thing at all. So the practice demands first-hand evaluation at the judging stage and then runs almost entirely on testimony at the consumption stage. What view of aesthetic judgement does that two-part structure presuppose? Does it implicitly say that testimony is fine for steering choices — what to buy, what to listen to — but can't itself give you the aesthetic belief or knowledge? Is that a coherent split (maybe a division of epistemic labour) or just an inconsistency the institution papers over? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me what would have to change — in the design of prizes, medals, reviews — if we instead accepted that aesthetic testimony can transmit genuine aesthetic knowledge. Please write your answer in British English.
