I sit on the ethics review panel of a mid-sized game studio. For our open-world title we've built NPCs with persistent memory and a simple goal-and-drive architecture: when a player kills one, its dependents lose their provider and visibly grieve for the rest of the game. A proposed update would add enemies who beg, flee pathetically, and remember the player's cruelty across sessions, with the stated design goal of making violence feel costly. Our panel is split three ways. The narrative lead says simulated distress is pure puppetry — nothing experiences anything, so no moral question arises about the NPCs themselves, only about honesty with players. The systems engineer counters that these agents carry genuine negative-valence states — goal conflict, learned fear of the player — and that we can't assume functional suffering weighs nothing just because the substrate is code. The community manager leans toward approval but worries the game trains players to ignore pleading. We must decide at Thursday's meeting whether to approve the update, reject it, or approve it with conditions.

Act as a philosopher specializing in the moral status of fictional and artificial beings, and think carefully through the strongest version of each position before committing to a recommendation. Draft the written opinion I will circulate to the panel, with the following sections in this order and roughly these lengths: Recommendation (about 120 words, bottom line first), The proposal before the panel (about 150), Why the NPCs may matter morally (about 300), Why they likely do not — and what matters anyway (about 300), and Conditions and dissent (about 200, giving the strongest consideration on the losing side a fair hearing). Write in American English, at a register that engineers and designers will take seriously, and make the recommendation concrete enough that we can vote on it.
