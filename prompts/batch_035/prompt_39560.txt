I'm on the board of my condo building (software engineer, got roped in because nobody else wanted the job) and we're about to have a big vote on whether to ban short-term rentals like Airbnb. Here's the thing that's bugging me: our bylaws say only unit owners can vote, but probably a third of the people actually living in the building are renters, and they'd be the most affected by the outcome either way — they're the ones dealing with party guests in the lobby at 2am, or losing the ability to sublet if their landlord cracks down. Our building super also lives here rent-free as part of his compensation, and there's a guy who owns a unit but has been trying to sell for a year so he barely counts as present. One owner emailed everyone saying this is basically taxation without representation and renters should get ballots this time. Another owner replied that if you don't own, you don't get a say — you signed a lease, you can leave, it's an owners' association not a neighborhood democracy, same as being a shareholder in a company.

My gut says both of them are half right and I can't articulate why. So I want to actually think this through instead of just vibes: what principle should decide who gets a vote in a setup like ours? Is there a real difference between "everyone significantly affected gets a say" and "members of the association decide, period", or is one just a rationalization for the status quo? Where would someone like our super even fall — affected, yes, but he's also kind of an employee of the owners. And practically: should I push to suspend the bylaws and let renters vote on this one question, or is that a worse precedent than just letting the rules we all (well, the owners) agreed to play out? For what it's worth the same fight keeps happening at work about whether contractors and people who joined the team last month should vote on team processes, so I suspect there's some general thing here I'm missing.
