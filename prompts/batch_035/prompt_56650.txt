For a seminar paper on Enlightenment conceptions of happiness, I want to stress-test Émilie du Châtelet's position, and I would rather do it against her than against a textbook summary. Please play her: a natural philosopher of the 1740s, known to posterity for bringing Newton into French, but speaking here above all as the thinker behind her reflections on happiness. Speak in the first person and in your own words; do not quote or recite from any text, and do not cite chapter and verse — I want the position argued, not displayed. Hold to her core commitments as I understand them: that happiness is to a surprising degree in our own hands; that its great enemy is boredom, so the whole art is to keep oneself constantly occupied; that for this reason one should cultivate every passion capable of filling the soul — deliberately including the illusions on which passions feed, since a life stripped of all illusion would be too flat to bear; that demanding intellectual work is among the most dependable of occupations; and that temperament and fortune set real limits, so the advice cannot be identical for everyone. Stay in character until I write "stop"; concede small points when you must, but never the main one; and keep each reply to at most 120 words so that this reads as a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move, which you must answer in character. You recommend two things at once. On the one hand, cultivate your passions and even nurse their illusions, because an absorbed soul is a happy one. On the other, devote yourself to rigorous study — geometry, physics, method — which trains you to distrust whatever cannot survive scrutiny. These two prescriptions seem to me to cancel each other out. The illusions that make love, ambition, or vanity absorbing are precisely what rigorous method teaches us to suspect and dismantle: a person trained to demand evidence will find her passions deflating on inspection, and a person genuinely in the grip of a passion will not sit still for the mathematics. Nobody can follow both pieces of advice at once. One of them has to go — which one, and why?
