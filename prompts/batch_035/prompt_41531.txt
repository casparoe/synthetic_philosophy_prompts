Two people in my social choice reading group got into it after seminar last week and I honestly couldn't tell who won. Context: we'd just covered the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. Maya's opening move was that the theorem basically kills voting theory as an engineering project — if every system with three or more candidates is manipulable, then strategic voting is just a fact of life, and complaining that first-past-the-post rewards tactical voting is like complaining that water is wet. On her telling, IRV, Condorcet, Borda, whatever — it's all one boat, so "fixing" the electoral system is a mirage and we should stop pretending some systems are more honest than others.

Dan said no, the theorem comes with conditions — it applies to deterministic rules that pick a single winner from full rankings, over all possible preference profiles, so it doesn't even touch approval voting, because with approval ballots you're not submitting a ranking at all. He called that a real escape, not a loophole.

Maya said that's just gerrymandering the theorem's scope so it comes out true — like declaring that no car ever crashes by defining "car" to exclude crashed ones — and anyway approval voting is obviously manipulable in practice: you still have to decide whether to approve your second choice based on how you expect everyone else to vote, which is exactly the strategic misrepresentation the theorem is about.

Dan then pivoted to the single-peaked escape: if preferences are single-peaked (think one-dimensional left-right axis), the median voter rule is strategy-proof, so the theorem doesn't say design is hopeless — it says look at restricted domains, and that's a live research program, not a technicality.

Maya said restricting voters' preferences is a policy proposal so utopian it makes the theorem's own dictatorship clause look modest, and she doesn't see how "if everyone's preferences happen to be shaped right" counts as designing anything.

Dan's last word was that at least the theorem explains why every system *feels* gamey, and that Maya's "so nothing matters" conclusion confuses manipulable-in-principle with equally-manipulable-in-practice.

Also, neither of them ever actually explained the dictatorship condition — in the theorem, is a "dictator" literally one voter whose favorite always wins, or something weaker?
