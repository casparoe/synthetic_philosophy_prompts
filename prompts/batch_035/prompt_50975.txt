So last spring my cousin and I each put a chunk of savings into a friend's coffeeshop startup and it collasped by fall. Since then my dad's girlfriend has made it her personal mission to explain at every family dinner that it was obviously doomed — bad location, no restaurant experience, "the writing was on the wall." I started reading about hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy partly to defend myself, and I've accidentally argued myself into something way worse than the thing I was defending. Here's the argument:

1. Once you know an outcome, you can't help feeling it was predictable — that's hindsight bias; your memory of what the evidence actually was gets rewritten by the result.
2. For any outcome whatsoever, you can build a story afterwards that makes it look inevitable — the narrative fallacy. If the coffeeshop had taken off, my dad's girlfriend would have had a story about the "obvious" untapped market in that neighborhood.
3. So the availability of a causal story tells you nothing about what was knowable in advance.
4. Since a story would have been told either way, judging my decision by its outcome tells you nothing about the decision — only the process can be judged.
5. But judging the process is contaminated too: "you should have looked harder at the lease" just means "looking would have revealed what we now know," and we only know to say "look at the lease" because we know how things ended.
6. So there's no neutral standpoint from which to say what anyone should have foreseen, because every such judgment is made from the far side of the outcome.
7. Therefore nobody is ever responsible for a foreseeable bad outcome, and nobody ever really learns from history — every "lesson" is just the story the survivors tell.

That can't be right — it would give a free pass to the engineer who skipped the inspection, the surgeon who ignored the scan, everyone who ever ignored a warning that really was there. But I've stared at this for a week and every step still looks fine to me, and honestly step (7) would get everyone off my back at dinner, which makes me suspicous of how much I want it to be true. Which step is the weak one? And if I want to keep (4) — judge the decision, not the outcome — what do I have to give up to stop the slide from there to (7)?
