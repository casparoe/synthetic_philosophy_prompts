I am a novelist at work on a book set in London in the years after the Wolfenden Committee reported in 1957, centered on a lifelong friendship between two men on opposite sides of the era's great argument about whether the criminal law may be used to enforce morality: one a judge in the mold of Lord Devlin, who holds that a society has the right to defend its shared moral fabric just as it defends itself against treason, and the other an Oxford professor in the mold of H. L. A. Hart, who holds that mere immorality, however repugnant to the majority, is never by itself a ground for punishment. The two carry the quarrel through decades of correspondence, dinners, and one ill-tempered public exchange, and near the end of the book, both elderly, they attempt something neither managed in life: a joint memorandum stating exactly where they agree, exactly where they still part company, and what would have to be true for either to concede. I want you to write the substance of that memorandum.

Draft it as I imagine such a document would actually be written: the claims both men could sign — about the separability of law and morality, about what the Wolfenden recommendations did and did not presuppose, about the difference between punishing harm, punishing offense, and enforcing virtue — followed by the residual disagreements, each phrased so that the man holding the view would accept the wording rather than wince at a caricature. I want the cruxes made precise: the judge's claim that a common morality is what holds a society together and that tolerating its deliberate breach invites disintegration, set against the professor's demand for evidence of that disintegration rather than an analogy with treason; the question of whose morality counts, and whether the reasonable man's intolerance, indignation, and disgust amount to a standard or merely to a majority's taste; the line, if any, between paternalism and moralism; the standing of positive morality as something to be weighed rather than obeyed. For each crux, where you can, say what argument or kind of case both men would accept as bearing on it, and record what each would predict if it were settled. And if some portion of their dispute strikes you as partly verbal — two men using 'society' or 'harm' to mean different things — say which portion and why.

Two constraints from my editor and one from me. First, the memorandum, along with anything you say about it, must be continuous prose only — no headings, numbered lists, bold text, or tables, even though such documents are ordinarily formatted that way; it will appear in the book as a letter, and I want your commentary written the way one would write an email or a long letter to a colleague. Second, and this one is mine: wherever the underlying scholarship has genuinely moved or divides, keep what is settled apart from what is contested among people who know this debate, and do not present a contested reading of Hart or Devlin as the consensus one; if a formulation of the disagreement leans on one scholar's interpretation rather than another's, flag it as such in a sentence rather than smuggling it in. Please write in American English throughout.
