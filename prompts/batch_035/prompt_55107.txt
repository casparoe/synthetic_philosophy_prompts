I write literary fiction, and my writing group (5 of us, meeting every other week for about three years now) is completely stuck on what I've started privately calling the propaganda question, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who's right.

Here's the shape of it. I'm a black novelist and my last two books have been about, roughly, black family life in the rust belt. Every time someone in the group raises whether a character or scene "helps or hurts" -- whether an unflattering portrayal of a black father, say, does damage in a world where white readers treat any depiction as representative -- half the group says yes, obviously, fiction doesn't float free of its social context, and the other half says that reducing my job to representation management is exactly the kind of aesthetic policing that kills novels. We've literally had this same argument like six times and nobody has budged an inch. It recently dawned on me that we're rerunning the Du Bois / Locke fight from the 1920s -- Du Bois saying art is and must be propaganda in the sense of revealing truth about a people to a hostile audience, Locke pushing back that this saddles the artist with a burden no artist can carry and calling for art as expression rather than uplift. Same stalemate, a century later, just in a Facebook group chat instead of The Crisis.

My working theory is that the whole thing is verbal -- that when the pro-duty side hears "propaganda" they mean something like "all art inevitably communicates, so choose your communication," while the anti-duty side hear "party-line message fiction," and we talk past each other. But I'm not sure, because sometimes it doesn't feel verbal at all: some in the group seem to actually reject the premise that a depiction can be true AND harmful in effect, and I can't tell if that's a factual disagreement about how readers work or a deeper one about what fiction is even for. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually -- verbal, factual, different questions under one heading (maybe the "helps or hurts" of whom, the reader vs. the community depicted)? What would each side have to say to show it's one rather than another? And given the diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move us, or should I just accept that this is bedrock and stop bringing it up over wine?

(Don't just adjudicate Du Bois vs. Locke -- I want the structure of OUR disagreement, using the historical fight as material, and an honest answer about whether people like us should expect progress at all.)
