I have chronic back pain, and the pain-education materials I've been given — the workbooks my physiotherapist handed me, the explain-pain videos — rest almost entirely on one picture: pain is the body's alarm system, not a measure of damage. Just as a smoke detector is doing exactly its job when it shrieks over burnt toast, the reasoning goes, my back can scream during an ordinary bend without anything being wrong with it; the alarm has simply been set too sensitively after months of guarding, and the treatment is to retrain the nervous system, through graded movement, that bending is safe. The analogy is doing real clinical work here, since it is what licenses the instruction to keep moving through pain rather than rest.

Please audit the analogy properly rather than just accepting or rejecting it. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the fire, the smoke, the detector, the siren, and the person who hears it and calls the fire brigade — and then say where the mapping breaks. In particular: on this picture, is the unpleasantness of pain the siren, or is it the evacuation? I've read about patients with certain brain injuries who report still feeling pain yet seem entirely unbothered by it, which makes me suspect the alarm and the response to it are more separable in us than they are in a building, and that this matters for what the analogy can carry. Does the picture also smuggle in a designer whose purposes the alarm serves, and does it import the assumption that a false alarm is safe to ignore? Finally: does the clinical conclusion — move through the pain, retrain the system — survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if there is a better analogy for pain, say what it is and what it gets right that the alarm gets wrong.
