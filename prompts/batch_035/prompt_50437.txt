I am a novelist — quiet, realist literary fiction, the kind where the stakes are domestic and the tension comes from what people cannot bring themselves to decide. My characters have tended to be people who deliberate too well and act too late, and my editor has told me the next book needs one story that goes all the way with that theme. So I want a standalone short story, 2,500 to 3,000 words, built around the two-envelope paradox, written with enough craft that a reader who has never heard of it comes away having felt its pull rather than having been lectured about it.

The situation as I want it staged: a retired actuary — a woman who spent her career pricing risk and pricing it well — is at the settling of her late brother's estate. A provision of the will presents her with two sealed envelopes and a letter explaining that one contains twice as much as the other. She may take one and keep it, or take one and exchange it for the other, but only once, and then never again. She knows, professionally, exactly how the argument for switching goes — whatever amount she holds, the other envelope equally contains double it or half it, and the arithmetic seems to favor trading — and she knows just as professionally that something in that argument is broken, without being able to say what. Her fluency should be a curse rather than a comfort: she can reconstruct the case for swapping and cannot make herself stop. I would also like the story to engage the variant in which she is permitted to open her own envelope before deciding: the amount is revealed, and the argument for switching still seems to go through, which should make her predicament worse and more intimate, not more contrived.

Craft constraints I care about, since I've tried drafting this twice and watched it collapse both times:

1. The paradox must never be named, and no character may explain it in dialogue. If a scene starts to sound like a seminar, cut it.
2. No rescue by asymmetry. She must not weigh the envelopes, peek, receive a confession from the lawyer, or otherwise discover a difference between them. The story must live inside the symmetry.
3. Keep it realist. No supernatural envelopes, no Borgesian library framing, no time loops presented as such.
4. The structure should perform the problem rather than describe it. I have in mind something that reopens where it closed — a decision that seems to have been made and then, on the last page, isn't — but I'm open to other structural solutions if you can defend them.
5. The story must not smuggle in a philosophical verdict. I don't want the ending to imply she was right to switch, or right not to, or that the whole thing was silly. She must pay something human for her inability to land — a missed train, a strained relationship with her nephew, whatever earns its weight — but the price should be for the indecision, not a coded answer to the paradox.
6. Give the prose the patience and restraint of literary fiction. Interiority over exposition. If you want to suggest a title, do so, but nothing punning.

One thing I'd like you to decide rather than dodge: what, emotionally, switching means to her — whether it's greed, grief for her brother, defiance of a life spent calculating, or something you see better than I do. I want that reading to be available to an attentive reader without being underlined. Please draft the story.
