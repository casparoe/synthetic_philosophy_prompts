I write about neuroscience and artificial intelligence for a broad-circulation magazine, and my beat increasingly consists of consciousness claims: a lab announces that a neural-network system reports sensations, that a brain organoid shows signatures of awareness, that a patient written off as unresponsive was found to be coordinating with a researcher's questions, or that a field-leading theory has just failed a head-to-head test against its rival. My editors ask me whether a headline like "scientists detect consciousness" is licensed in each case, and I have been answering with instinct rather than principle. Before I write another such story, I want the specification.

Please write the desiderata: what any adequate scientific theory of consciousness must deliver to count as addressing the hard problem rather than changing the subject. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it -- the phenomenon it must explain, the puzzle it must dissolve, the distinction it must respect, the empirical results it cannot contradict. Among the candidates for the list, I would specifically like your judgment on the following, though I do not want to constrain you to these:

- The theory must explain why physical processing is accompanied by experience at all, not merely redescribe the functions (attention, report, discrimination) that we can already study.
- It must supply a discriminator: a measurable signature or criterion by which third parties can settle, at least in principle, whether a given system is conscious -- or it must explain honestly what we should do in the absence of one.
- It must handle the epistemic asymmetry between my own case, where I seem to have direct access, and everyone else's, where I have only evidence.
- It must say something principled about boundary systems: the cerebellum versus the cortex, split-brain patients, dreamless sleep and anesthesia, locked-in patients, organoids, and engineered systems.
- It must avoid circularity: it cannot define consciousness in terms of reports and then explain reports, since the reporting machinery and the experience come apart in exactly the cases that matter.

Then, mark which of the requirements you list are non-negotiable and which are in fact the prejudices of particular camps dressed up as demands -- for instance, whether requiring that consciousness be causally efficacious, or that it be computable, or that it be realizable in silicon, is a genuine adequacy condition or a metaphysical wager. Most importantly, tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: for example, whether the demand for a third-person measurable signature and the demand to respect first-person authority can be met by one theory, and what follows for my reporting if they cannot.

Finally, and briefly only: how integrated information theory, global workspace theory, higher-order theories, and illusionism fare against the list. But keep the weight on the criteria. The scorecard is the article; the specification is what I actually need to write it.
