I'm a final-year PhD student and in three weeks I'm giving the departmental colloquium that's basically functioning as my job talk, so this has recently gone from an abstract interest to something that actually matters for my life. The position I'm defending: when you're unsure between moral theories, you shouldn't just act on whichever theory you currently lean toward — you should treat your decision like a parliament where every theory you have some credence in gets representation, roughly propotional to that credence. But I add two twists that are the actual contribution of the talk: (1) the vote shares are issue-specific rather than global, so a theory's weight varies with the kind of question on the floor — on whether some harm counts as a permissible side effect, the constraint-friendly theories get extra weight, whereas on how to weigh competing outcomes against each other the aggregative theories do; and (2) there's a constitutional tier sitting above the voting: anything that every theory I give non-trivial credence to forbids is off the agenda before parliament even convenes, and anything all of them permit passes without a vote, so the parliament only ever actually votes on a contested middle band. My pitch is that this explains a felt difference we all have — some moral disagreements seem like ordinary political horse-trading, others seem like they're just not up for a vote at all.

The audience is my department, which is mostly ethicists, and I know them well enough to predict trouble. There are two pretty hard-line Kantians, one Scanlon-flavored contractualist who thinks the whole thing is moot because permissibility is fixed by principles nobody could reasonably reject — and I suspect she'll press that my constitutional tier is either trivial or question-begging, and honestly I can't yet tell which, which scares me. Then there's a consequentialist who keeps telling me the framework is just expected-value maximization over moral theories with the numbers filed off, and a visitor from political theory who cornered me at a reception to say the parliament metaphor is a sleight of hand: proportional representation gets whatever authority it has from actual voters, and nobody voted for my theories, so the metaphor borrows a legitimacy it never earned. Plus the grad students, who will absolutely ask about infinite credences or infinite ethics if I leave any opening.

Also flag the question I'm quietly hoping nobody raises — my fear is it's the one about how I can set the issue-specific weights at all without already deciding, in advance, the very questions the parliament was supposed to settle for me. But if you can see a worse one lurking in my setup, tell me straight, don't sugarcoat it.
