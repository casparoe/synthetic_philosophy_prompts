I am a retired physical chemist who, since leaving the lab, has fallen into philosophy through the decision-theory back door. Next month I lead a two-hour session of my continuing-education circle — a dozen retired engineers, physicians, and one former actuary — on Newcomb's problem, none of whom has met it before. I don't want the explanation itself; I want a plan for delivering it, and I want you to make definite choices rather than laying out options.

In what order should the pieces go in? My instinct is to reveal the predictor's track record only after everyone has committed publicly to a choice, because the dominance argument feels airtight to engineers until the track record is on the table — but tell me whether that is cheap rhetoric or the genuine dependency structure, and if the latter, say what has to be in place before even posing the problem.

What single carried example should bear the weight? The million-versus-thousand-dollar split will bore the physicians. Should I re-denominate — drug doses for the doctors, an instrument with a known reliability for the engineers — and if so, what must the replacement preserve and what may it drop? Which of the two audiences' examples would carry more of the idea, in your judgment?

Which misconceptions should I expect, and at what point in the sequence should each be headed off? My guesses: the actuary will insist the predictor is merely well calibrated and no prediction is doing real work; the engineers will protest that the box contents are already fixed, so the choice cannot matter; someone will say the predictor introduces reverse causation and consider the matter settled. Tell me if I have misidentified them, and where to defuse each.

What should I openly refuse to cover in two hours — the causal/evidential/functional decision-theoretic machinery by name, the perfect-predictor limit, the medical Newcomb cases — and how to say so without seeming to dodge?

How will I know by the end whether they understood the problem rather than merely picked a side?

Finally, the question I keep going back and forth on: should the session end with me declaring one-boxing the rational choice, or with the problem left genuinely open? Commit to a ruling, with your reasons, and close with a last line beginning "Verdict:" stating it in one sentence.
