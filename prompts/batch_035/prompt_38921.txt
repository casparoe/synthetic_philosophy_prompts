(a) According to revealed preference theory, does Sarah "really" prefer the bus?

and here's my friend Dev's answer:

Revealed preference is Samuelson's idea from around the late 1930s-40s. The core claim is that preferences just are choices: if you pick A when B was affordable, that shows you prefer A, and that's all that "preferring A" even means — there's nothing underneath the choice to look for. So yes, Sarah really prefers the bus, her behavior reveals it, period. Her saying she hates the bus is just noise. Verbal reports aren't evidence at all under the theory, that was the whole point: economics got tired of resting on unverifiable introspection and wanted to be scientific.

The technical backbone is the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP): if you ever choose A when B was available, you can never choose B when A is available later, otherwise you're being inconsistent/irrational. Sarah chose the bus every single day while driving was on the table, so driving is revealed worse, and her behavior is perfectly consistent with WARP.

As for utility: utility in this framework is cardinal, meaning the utility function assigns actual numbers measuring how much satisfaction she gets from each option. Since her bus utility has to be higher (that's why she chose it), her report of hating it is simply false or confused.

But the case does raise a real problem, which is the sour grapes point that Samuelson himself made: preferences can be adaptive. Sarah's "preference" for the bus got manufactured by her husband's opinion, so saying she's maximizing her utility is technically true but hides where the preference came from. So choices don't always reflect what someone actually wants, which is a genuine crack in the theory.
