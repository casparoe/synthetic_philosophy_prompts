I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of cognitive science — fourteen students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel, from the inside, the differences between Dennett's four kinds of creatures: Darwinian creatures that get one shot and are selected, Skinnerian creatures that are shaped by reward within a lifetime, Popperian creatures that test candidate moves internally before acting, and Gregorian creatures that import external mind-tools to change what counts as a trial. The activity should stage repeated rounds of a single simple game with a hidden rule the players must infer, but with roles that constrain what each participant can bring to bear: some must play with no memory of previous rounds, some may remember outcomes but may not deliberate before acting, some get a limited number of offline previews that cost them playing time, and some get a small external aid such as a sheet for recording and structuring what they have seen. Payoffs should be points or tokens, never real money. In the debrief, map each role onto a rung of the taxonomy, and then take up the harder question the activity should leave them with: whether the four kinds differ in the agents themselves or only in how much structure the environment supplies — that is, whether a Darwinian creature in a richly selective environment is already doing something Popperian-shaped. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about the feature the activity cannot reproduce — the moment where a creature actually generates a novel hypothesis internally rather than choosing among given options — which I will have to supply by hand. Write the entire response as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to a colleague: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
