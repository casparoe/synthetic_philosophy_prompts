Retired biochemist here, spending my retirement reading history and philosophy of history, and there's a pattern I keep bumping into that I can't find a proper name for. In the lab we kept full notebooks — every failed run got written down right next to the one that worked, so when you went back to explain why the protocol finally succeeded, you knew the base rate of failures. History seems to run the opposite way. When people explain why some institution collapsed or why some war went the way it did, the evidence they're working from — the documents, the memoirs, the numbers that got recorded at all — was itself shaped by the outcome. The losers' papers burned or got written defensively decades later, the surviving clerks are the ones who filed the archive, and the famous "warning signs" everyone cites were one memo among hundreds of equally confident memos pointing in other directions. So the explanation fits the evidence beautifully, but the evidence was pre-sorted to make explanations of exactly this outcome fit. I know about hindsight bias, which lives in the head of the explainer, and about survivorship bias, which is about a filtered sample — but this feels like a third thing, where the event being explained is itself part of the mechanism that curated the evidence used to explain it. Is there an established concept for this kind of outcome-contaminated evidence, and how does it relate to those two (or to anything else in the philosphy of history)? If nobody's named it, coin a term for me and tell me where its edges are — where it stops and plain survivorship bias starts, and whether it makes historical explanation structurally different from explanation in the sciences or just noisier. I'm not after a label I can drop at dinner, I want to know what the phenomenon actually is and what it isnt.
