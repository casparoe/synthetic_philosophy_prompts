You are a philosopher of mathematics, at home both with the technical results and with the long history of their misuse outside mathematics, and you also have editorial experience writing and commissioning for educated general readers. I am the commissioning editor of a general-interest magazine of ideas, and our next special issue is built around what Gödel's incompleteness theorems do and don't show once you carry them outside mathematics. I have room for three commissioned essays of roughly 5,000 words each, plus a short sidebar devoted to deflating common misreadings. I need your advice on how to allocate the slots.

Before ranking anything, think carefully and brainstorm privately: set out the criteria an editor should use. Ones I expect to matter, but judge freely: whether the claim actually follows from the first or second incompleteness theorem rather than being imported from somewhere else; whether the same point could be made without mentioning Gödel at all, in which case invoking him is mostly borrowed prestige; whether there is a live intellectual debate rather than a settled misconception; and whether a careful reader would come away with something they could not have gotten from a good essay on the same subject that never cited the theorems. State your criteria explicitly, then apply them.

The candidate pitches, as submitted by prospective authors:

1. The Lucas–Penrose line: since we can grasp the consistency of any formal system that a machine implements, the human mind is not algorithmic, and strong AI is impossible in principle.
2. A physicist's piece arguing by analogy that a final theory of physics is impossible — that any consistent unified theory must leave some physical truths out of reach, and that this is why some physicists who once hoped for a complete theory gave up on the hope.
3. An essay built around Gödel's own ontological proof of God, pitched under the claim that incompleteness shows there are truths reason cannot reach and that theology lives precisely at that boundary.
4. A legal theorist's piece arguing that any legal system that is consistent cannot be complete, so hard cases necessarily force judges to reach outside the law — presented as incompleteness for jurisprudence, and leaning on the much-retold story that Gödel, at his American citizenship hearing, claimed to have found an inconsistency allowing the US Constitution to be subverted through its amendment clause.
5. A piece on the limits of knowledge: incompleteness as the definitive refutation of the Enlightenment ambition of a complete system of human knowledge, and as the deepest available grounding for a thoroughgoing fallibilism.
6. An essay on automated systems: a sufficiently capable AI, like any formal system strong enough for arithmetic, cannot be simultaneously consistent and complete about its own outputs, and the piece explores what this does and does not imply about trusting such systems in high-stakes domains.
7. A postmodern-adjacent pitch that incompleteness establishes the relativity of truth: since no system captures all truths, every framework is equally partial, so no framework is better placed than any other.

For each candidate, tell me: does the advertised claim follow from the theorems at all; if not, what weaker claim, if any, survives; does invoking Gödel add content or only authority; and what would a reader actually take away. Then give me the final decision: which three get the essay slots and in what order of prominence, and which go into the debunking sidebar instead, with the reasoning for each placement. I am particularly keen to hear which candidate you judge the most seductive error — the pitch that sounds most Gödelian while resting on a point that could be established by entirely ordinary arguments — and why it is seductive. Finally, draft the two or three sentences the sidebar should open with: the misreadings of incompleteness most likely to trip up our readers, stated as crisply as you can, starting with the classic confusion between there being truths no particular consistent axiomatization of arithmetic can prove and the far weaker, near-trivial observation that no framework says everything about everything.
