In 2021, the UK government commissioned an independent team of researchers, based at the London School of Economics and led by philosophers and animal-welfare scientists, to review the evidence for sentience in cephalopod molluscs (octopuses, cuttlefish, squid) and decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters, crayfish, shrimp). The review concluded that there was strong evidence of sentience in octopods and substantial evidence for many decapods, and it recommended a precautionary approach: where evidence of sentience is mixed, err on the side of inclusion. On that basis, the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Bill — introduced in May 2021 to restore, after Brexit, the recognition of animal sentience that had previously come from an EU treaty obligation — was amended mid-passage to cover these invertebrate groups alongside all vertebrates. The resulting Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act received royal assent in April 2022. Notably, the Act's operative mechanism is not a set of prohibitions but an Animal Sentience Committee, empowered to review whether government policy decisions have given due regard to the welfare of animals as sentient beings and to publish reports — with no direct enforcement power.

Analyze this episode through the lens of a threshold model of welfare protection: the idea that legal and moral status switches on when an animal crosses an evidential bar for sentience, with the precautionary principle used to set how high that bar should be. Map the episode carefully. Who were the actors, what did the threshold model tell each of them to do, and how did the review's precautionary framing resolve (or paper over) genuine uncertainty about graded capacities — pain versus anxiety versus longer-term suffering — inside the groups it lumped together? Then run the analysis in the other direction and say where the model breaks. Two features deserve particular attention. First, fish had already been on the protected side of the sentience threshold for decades, as vertebrates under earlier welfare law, yet practices in aquaculture — sea-lice management in salmon farming, the mass deployment and high mortality of cleaner fish like lumpfish and wrasse, slaughter methods for farmed fish — have evolved slowly if at all, which suggests that threshold recognition may not be the binding constraint on welfare outcomes. Second, the Act's reliance on an advisory committee rather than enforceable rules may reveal that sentience recognition functions more as a symbolic or legitimacy-conferring act than as a welfare mechanism. Consider whether a graded model — in which legal duties scale with the strength of evidence and the richness of the animal's capacities, rather than switching on at a binary threshold — would have produced a different bill, and whether the actual episode supports or undermines such a model. Conclude with an honest assessment of what this case shows about the threshold-plus-precaution framework generally: whether it is the right instrument for animals whose sentience is genuinely uncertain, and where it is simply silent.
