My roommate (a math major, currently doing real analysis) has an argument that he claims proves nothing ever moves, and every time I try to fight it I end up in a corner I like even less. It runs like this. Take Thomson's lamp: a switch gets flipped at half a minute, again at 3/4 of a minute, again at 7/8, and so on — each flip is instantaneous, and by the one-minute mark infinitely many flips have happened. He says there is no consistent answer to whether the lamp is on or off at exactly one minute: the story fixes the state at every time before one minute (on, off, on, off, ...) but says nothing about the state at one minute, and nothing could fix it, since asking about the parity of a countable infinity is nonsense. From this he extracts: (1) infinitely many discrete state-changes cannot be completed in a finite interval — that is what the lamp shows, since if such completions were possible, the lamp would have to have some definite final state, and it has none. (2) Any object moving from A to B in finite time passes through infinitely many positions, one per instant, with an instant between any two instants; each being-at-a-new-position is a discrete state-change, so any motion at all is an infinite series of them squeezed into finite time. (3) So nothing ever moves. He then adds the twist that really gets me: (4) since things quite obviously move, it must be that motion does not after all consist of infinitely many state-changes — which he takes as a victory for some kind of finitism about space, or time, or the divisibility of motion, and he's started saying Zeno was basically right and calculus is just an extremely useful fiction that papered over the problem.

Each step sounded fine to me when he first walked through it, and I can't name the exact slip. My instinct is to deny (2) — that motion is a series of discrete state-changes — because (1) at least feels like it's just reporting what the lamp puzzle demonstrates, and motion seems more like a smooth variation than a checklist of tasks. But when I said that, he asked why the lamp's trouble couldn't just be that we described the setup badly, and I realized I had no answer, which makes me worry I'm clinging to (1) for no reason. And if I deny (1) instead, he'll ask me what the lamp's state at one minute actually is, and I don't want to say it's on, or off, or both.

So: tell me which step the argument actually turns on, why that step looked okay when it was introduced, and what it costs to reject it — not a menu of possible responses with the choosing left to me. Commit to one diagnosis. If you think the rot is in more than one place, say which one is doing the real work and which is just along for the ride. And if you think my instinct about (2) is wrong, say so plainly; I'd rather hear that my preferred way out doesn't work than get a diplomatic maybe.
