At a workshop on institutional design last week, a speaker advocating futarchy — Robin Hanson's proposal that elected representatives define a national welfare measure while prediction markets choose the policies expected to raise it — kept ending discussion with his slogan: vote on values, but bet on beliefs. It was doing real work in the room. When someone raised the objection that a wealthy ideologue could burn money in the markets to move prices and thereby steer policy, the speaker waved it off with the slogan, as if the slogan itself were a proof that this couldn't matter. When someone else objected that people's values aren't cleanly separable from their beliefs about facts — that what counts as 'national welfare' already embeds contestable empirical assumptions — he gave the same reply.

I want a document I can send to the workshop participants. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that the slogan could be expressing, ordered as a menu from the most deflationary to the most ambitious. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a substantive but dubious thesis, or simply false. I'd like you to cover, at least, readings along these lines (and add any others you think are genuinely in play): a trivial division of institutional labour that most existing democracies already approximate; a normative claim about where values versus beliefs are more reliably expressed, which presupposes that markets reward accuracy and punish ideological trading; a metaphysical or conceptual claim that values and beliefs are in principle separable inputs to policy; and a design claim about the specific mechanism — that decision markets conditional on policy adoption transfer money from those whose predictions are wrong to those whose predictions are right, so that manipulation is self-taxing.

For each reading, state explicitly whether it would actually license the speaker's two dismissals: the dismissal of the manipulation objection, and the dismissal of the value-belief entanglement objection. And flag the cross-cutting issue I most want settled: on some readings the slogan makes the billionaire's manipulation a non-problem in theory while conceding it's a problem in practice (finite capital, correlated traders, the welfare metric being coarse), while on other readings the slogan quietly assumes the very thing the objection denies. Finally, tell me which reading the speaker most probably needed for his dismissals to work, and whether that reading is one a careful person could accept.
