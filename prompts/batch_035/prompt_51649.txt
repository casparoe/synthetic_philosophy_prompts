I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long-form explainer about the "values problem" in artificial intelligence: the worry that we cannot simply write down what we want machines to do for us, and that any attempt to specify our values directly seems to leave out something we care about. In my reporting, researchers keep gesturing at a family of strategies they describe as indirect normativity — the idea that instead of handing an AI an explicit code of conduct, we would somehow get it to derive what we would want under improved conditions: if we knew everything relevant, reasoned flawlessly, and were free of bias and momentary impulse. The AI would then act on the output of that idealized process rather than on our actual, confused preferences.

The striking thing, to me, is that political philosophy made what looks like the same move decades ago. Rawls asked what principles we would choose behind a veil of ignorance that hides our own place in society; philosophers writing on well-being asked whether what is good for a person is what that person would want if fully informed and thinking clearly; and metaethicists have argued that moral truths are whatever would survive a process of mutual adjustment between principles and judgments. In every case the pattern is the same: replace what we actually want or believe with what an idealized version of us would want or believe.

My editors will not let me get away with "philosophers and AI researchers are secretly doing the same thing" — that is the kind of observation that collapses under an hour of checking. So I need a serious comparison of these two projects, and I would like you to write it. Please compare indirect normativity as it appears in AI alignment research with idealized deliberation as it appears in political and moral philosophy. Specifically:

1. What exactly does the idealization consist of in each case? Consider the standard ingredients — more information, more rationality, greater consistency, freedom from bias — and say whether the two traditions use the same recipe or genuinely different ones. If a recipe includes "living in better social conditions," does that change what counts as idealized, or is it idle?

2. The aggregation problem. Philosophical idealization usually concerns a single agent (one person's fully informed preferences) or a bounded collective (the parties to a social contract), while the AI proposals typically idealize all of humanity at once. Does scaling up the number of idealized people change the character of the exercise, or merely its difficulty?

3. The gap between actual and idealized attitudes. In the philosophical setting, someone can look at what their idealized self endorses and say "so much the worse for my idealized self." Can the users of an AI system reserve the same right? If not, is that a defect of the AI proposal or precisely the point of building one?

4. Where do the two projects most fundamentally come apart, if they do at all? Is the philosophical version a template the engineers adapted, a coincidence of form, or a case of parallel evolution under different selection pressures — where getting the wrong answer is, in one domain, a tenure decision and, in the other, a catastrophe?

Finally, take a position: which of the two traditions has more to teach the other, and about what? Please be concrete rather than diplomatically even-handed; I have room for a pointed conclusion. You can refer to positions and arguments by name and describe them, but do not include citations or a bibliography — my research assistant will fill in references, so spend your words on the ideas themselves. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, written at the level of an educated reader who has never taken a philosophy seminar.
