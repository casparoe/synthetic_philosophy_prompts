I'm a retired high school social studies teacher, and I sit on the historical commission in my small city (about 20,000 people, midwest). I've ended up as some kind of swing vote on a mess I don't feel qualified for, so I want someone to draft the written opinion I'll circulate before our September meeting.

Every Founders' Day we celebrate Rev. Amos Calloway, who local legend says hid Black families in his church basement and barn during the riot of 1919. There's a bronze marker about it by the courthouse, and the fourth graders do a whole unit on him. Last fall a grad student digitizing our county's coroner's inquests and land-transfer ledgers found stuff that doesn't match: nothing in any contemporaneous record mentions the sheltering, and it turns out Calloway bought a farm from one of the fleeing families a few weeks after the riot at maybe a third of its value. The ledger doesn't say why, obviously, but you can guess. Meanwhile the oral histories in the descendant community here tell their own story — they remember being pressured into that sale, they insist Calloway did shelter people including a specific family who does show up living a mile from the church in the 1920 census, but they also say he turned two families away at the door one night, which is in nobody's written record anywhere. So it's not a clean two-sided thing: the archive partially vindicates the descendants, partially undercuts the plaque, and the oral history is itself split between what records support and what no record could either confirm or refute.

The commission has to recommend something to the city council before Founders' Day: keep the marker, replace it with only what documents support, add a second interpretive plaque, or defer everything pending more research. Our factions are: (1) the "records only" people, who point out we're a historical commission, not a therapy service; (2) the memory folks, who note that our own commission precedent from the 2008 pioneer cemetery case — where we treated descendant recollection as sufficient evidence to re-mark unmarked graves — cuts exactly the other way, and it's suspicious that we only get strict when the memory belongs to the people the marker flattered; and (3) the mayor, who wants a "multiple perspectives" plaque, which half the room calls false balance. Please draft the opinion memo: a concrete recommendation (and if you favor new plaque language, say roughly what it should claim and not claim), the reasoning, an honest treatment of the strongest point on whichever side loses, and some kind of answer to whether "the evidence isn't settled enough to change anything" is a defensible position for us given that cemetery precedent. I have to live with these people either way, so nothing that reads like an own-the-libs or own-the-church essay.
