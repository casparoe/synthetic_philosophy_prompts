At a recent dinner, a machine-learning engineer who is courting my publisher assured me that my anxieties about fiction-writing models were sentimental, and his warrant was Hofstadter's picture of creativity: what any of us does — composer, cartoonist, novelist — is take an existing structure, locate its knobs, and turn them, as in the chapter of Metamagical Themas titled "Variations on a Theme as the Crux of Creativity." On his telling, my fourth novel just has more knobs set further from the defaults than my first does, and a language model exploring the same space differs from me only in degree, so any talk of a principled gulf between what I do and what it does is self-flattery. Please audit the analogy for me. Lay out what maps onto what — the theme, the knobs, the knob-turner, the constraint that makes one variation apt where a thousand others are merely noise — and then say where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that are fatal to his conclusion from the ones I might be tempted to inflate but shouldn't. I'd especially like to know whether the engineer has bent Hofstadter to his use, since my sense is that on the original picture the hard part is not turning the knobs but discovering that there is a knob there at all — noticing the variable nobody had varied — and it is not obvious that interpolating over a corpus of finished texts ever does that. Tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if there is a better analogy for what a novelist does relative to her predecessors, give me that instead.
