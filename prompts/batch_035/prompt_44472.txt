History PhD student here (July Crisis, so yes, I've been living inside the Fischer controversy for three years), and I've got what's probably really a philosophy question that I keep failing to get answered properly in seminars.

Every time the war guilt thing comes up, we end up in the same loop. Somebody takes the Fischer line: German decision-makers deliberately risked or wanted a general European war, and if you want evidence you've got the December 1912 war council and the September Programme showing what they were after. Then somebody else counters with the structural move: the whole thing was overdetermined, alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, railway schedules, everyone's generals with their pre-made war plans — pull any one government's choices out of July 1914 and you still get a war, maybe in 1915 over some Balkan mess instead of Sarajevo. And then the conversation basically stalls: either the blame dissolves into "the system did it, nobody's guilty," or somebody gets annoyed and doubles down on Germany anyway.

Here's what bugs me. The move from "no single actor's choice made a difference to the outcome" to "so no actor can be blamed" just gets smuggled in — nobody ever defends it, and nobody ever attacks it either. The anti-Fischer people use it as a free pass, and the pro-Fischer people resist only by denying the premise (no no, Germany WAS necessary), never by challenging the inference itself. And once I noticed it I started seeing it everywhere: climate blame arguments ("your emissions made no difference, and China anyway"), takes on the 2008 crash where every bank was doing what every other bank did, etc. The template is always: many contributors, each contribution individually unnecessary to the outcome because the others would have sufficed, therefore nobody in particular is responsible.

So my questions. Is there an established name/concept in philosophy for this *inference* specifically — not the causal structure (I know historians call that overdetermination, I'm talking about the slide from unnecessary-to-outcome to not-blameworthy)? I've heard philosophers have a "problem of many hands" but my limited understanding is that's about accountability getting lost in big organizations, which feels like a different thing — each official's contribution wasn't individually sufficient there, or maybe it's that it's hard to trace. If someone could lay out the nearest concepts and how they differ from what I'm describing, that would be a huge help. Second, is the inference actually any good? My gut says no, but I can't say why, and it matters: if not-making-a-difference doesn't dissolve blame, then half a century of the Fischer debate has been argued at the wrong level. Third, does the answer change depending on whether each contribution was individually *sufficient* for the outcome (withdraw everyone else and mine still does it) versus merely jointly sufficient? Because July 1914 might actually be the second kind, and I suspect the two come apart.
