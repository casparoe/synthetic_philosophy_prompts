quick sanity check before I do something slightly embarassing at our mind & cognition reading group. I teach philosophy of mind, and over the last few years my neuroscience colleagues have slowly dragged me into the predictive processing literature, to the point where I now have actual opinions. We've been asked to circulate our philosophical profiles in advance so people can poke holes. Here is mine, honestly held:

1. Perception is prediction. Perceptual experience just is the brain's best model of the causes of its sensory input, computed by hierarchical prediction-error minimisation. I take the perception-as-controlled-hallucination line seriously.
2. Attention is precision-weighting. Attention isn't a seperate system bolted onto perception; it's the brain dialling the gain on prediction errors up and down as part of the same inference.
3. The free energy principle, in its grand Fristonian form, is true: any self-organising system that persists must, more or less of mathematical necessity, behave as if it were minimising free energy, i.e. staying within its characteristic repertoire of states. I think this is right, and deep.
4. Because it holds of necessity, the FEP as such makes no empirical predictions. It's compatible with any behaviour a persisting system could exhibit. The testable content all lives in the local, contingent hypotheses — cortical hierarchies doing approximate Bayesian inference, precision-weighting, and so on.
5. And yet I tell my students the FEP is a genuinely promising unifying framework for the life and cognitive sciences, and I mean it — not merely as a heuristic or a way of describing things after the fact.
6. I'm not bothered by the dark room objection, the one where a prediction-error minimiser ought to just go lie in a dark room and stay there. The answer, as I understand it, is that agents have strong priors over the states they expect to occupy (exploring, eating, socialising), and acting to satisfy those priors is part of the same error-minimising package.
7. Perceptual experience can be veridical. There's a real difference between seeing a chair and hallucinating one, and its not merely a difference in how well the model fits the sensory stream — its somehow about the world itself.

Go through the set and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look that way, and rank the tensions by severity. For each, say what would have to give and what giving it would cost. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I've stared at this long enough that I genuinely can't tell anymore whether there's a real problem in there or just phrasing.
