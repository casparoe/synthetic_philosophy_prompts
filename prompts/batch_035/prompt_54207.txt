I've taught luck egalitarianism for fifteen years and I still can't get my own verdicts on cases to line up, so I'm trying to do this the way I'd make a student do it: list my gut reactions and see if there's a principle under them. Two people need costly wheelchairs. One was born with a degenerative condition; one was paralyzed after free soloing a cliff he knew was dangerous. First guy: compensation owed, obviously, that's brute luck. Free solo guy: not owed anything, he rolled the dice, that's option luck. A woman who grew up in a fishing town where the boats are basically the only employers, gets injured on a boat: I want to say she IS owed something, even though she also chose a known risk. But now if I imagine the free solo guy was raised by climbers and never wanted anything else from age six, I still say no compensation, and I honestly can't say why the fishing town woman is different. A gambler who bets his savings on a fair wheel and loses: nothing owed. Same gambler, except he has a congenital thing that makes him wildly impulsive: now I feel he's owed something, which seems weird, since the roulette wheel didn't get any less fair. Someone who declined subsidized insurance the state offered him and then loses everything in a flood: this is the one I keep going back and forth on, because on paper it looks like the gambler, but it also looks like the fishing town woman if the reason he declined is that he grew up around people who badly underestimated flood risk. Is there a principle that captures all or most of this, presumably something in the neighborhood of responsibility for outcomes vs responsibility for circumstances, but it has to explain why the town and the impulsivity cases pull me toward compensation? If the answer is that a couple of my verdicts have to go, fine, tell me which ones and why. And I'd want to know what the surviving principle says about expensive tastes, which I deliberately left off the list.
