In debates about whether large language models have beliefs, two analogies do a lot of work. One comes from Dennett's own discussion of the intentional stance: attributing beliefs to a chatbot is like attributing beliefs to a thermostat — a useful predictive shorthand that commits us to nothing about what, if anything, is going on inside. The other is newer: a model is like an actor playing a character, so the views it expresses are the character's, not the actor's, and probing for what it "really" believes is a category error. Both are deployed to the same deflationary conclusion — that belief-talk about models is fine as an instrument but stops there.

Please act as a philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of the intentional stance and of how contemporary language models are built and probed. Think carefully before answering. Audit both analogies: first give an explicit correspondence table for each (what plays the role of the belief, the believer, the observer adopting the stance, the internal mechanism being bypassed), then identify where each mapping breaks — I want the disanalogies that matter for the deflationary conclusion separated from the ones that don't. In particular, consider features of models that thermostats and actors lack: belief-like states that can be read off internal representations even when they conflict with what the model says, context-dependent splits between the "actor" and the "character" that are themselves artifacts of training, and disagreement between a model's stated and computed answers. Then say what each analogy smuggles in about the relationship between prediction and explanation, and whether the deflationary conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — or whether a more qualified position is available in which some attributions of belief to models are doing genuine explanatory work. If either analogy can be repaired rather than discarded, say what the repair costs. Finally, state explicitly what would lead you to a different verdict, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
