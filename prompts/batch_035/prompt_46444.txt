I'm in a philosophy of perception course this semester and we just did the argument from illusion and disjunctivism (McDowell-style, the whole "highest common factor" debate). For my study group I want a case that actually isolates the disagreement instead of hand-waving it. Here's the ask:

Construct a thought experiment in which the argument from illusion looks airtight from the inside but disjunctivism can still deny the common mental factor. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic — no evil demons, no brains in vats, no perfect copies of my grandmother's living room. Something like Charles Bonnet syndrome, a migraine aura, or hypnagogic hallucination on the edge of sleep. One paragraph. The subject must have no idea they're hallucinating at the time, and there should be a specific belief they form on the basis of the experience (some ordinary belief, like that there's a cat on the stairs). No cheating by making the hallucination obviously dreamlike — the scenario only counts if the indistinguishability is doing real work.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough:

1. What the disjunctivist says the subject's mental state is in the good case vs. the bad case, and what the subject's justification for the belief is in each.
2. What the sense-datum theorist says, and what the representationalist (intentionalist) says, for the same two moments.
3. Which feature of the case is doing the work — I suspect it's the introspective indistinguishability, but tell me if it's actually something else, like the causal isolation of the hallucination from the environment.
4. What happens to the argument from illusion if you remove that feature. Like, if the subject had even a crude cue that something was off (a medication warning label they read that morning, say), does the common-factor conclusion get weaker or does it survive untouched?
5. How a common-factor theorist would attack the disjunctivist's reading of the case, and whether their attack makes the subject's good-case knowledge priveleged in a way that seems unfair to the bad-case subject.

Last thing: be honest if the case, once you lay it out, ends up just begging the question against the common-factor theorist rather than showing anything. My TA's big complaint on our last assignment was that people stipulated the verdicts they wanted instead of deriving them from the theories, and I don't want to hand her another one of those. If the case can't avoid question-begging, say so and explain which premise of the argument from illusion is doing the begging — I genuinely can't tell anymore whether it's the indistinguishability premise or the "no intrinsic difference in kind" inference drawn from it.
