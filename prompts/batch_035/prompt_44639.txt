I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've run into a puzzle about priors that I can't resolve. In my line of work — Bayesian A/B tests, cold-start recommender models, that sort of thing — we constantly reach for "flat" or uninformative priors whenever we know nothing, and the justification I was given, that when you have no reason to favor one outcome over another you should assign the outcomes equal probability, struck me as obviously right. A friend who did a philosophy degree tells me this principle, which he calls the principle of indifference, destroys itself, and he laid out the following argument. Each step looks fine to me, but the conclusion proves far too much, so something has to give, and I can't see what.

Consider a machine that produces cubes. All we're told about it is that each cube fits inside a box of side length 2; that is the total evidence. His argument: (1) Where the evidence gives no reason to favor one possibility over another, rationality requires assigning the possibilities equal probability. (2) Given this evidence, the cube's side length s lies somewhere in [0, 2], and the evidence gives no reason to favor any value of s over any other, so s is uniformly distributed on [0, 2]. (3) The same evidence likewise gives no reason to favor any value of the volume v = s³ over any other, so v is also uniform, on [0, 8]. (4) One distribution cannot be uniform in both: if s is uniform, small volumes are favored; if v is uniform, small sides are favored; the two assignments conflict. (5) Which quantity we happen to name — side, volume, surface area — reflects our bookkeeping convenience rather than anything in the evidence, and the evidence was stated in terms of none of them, so no naming is privileged. (6) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about what rational credences this evidence supports, and by the same reasoning, no evidence short of observed frequencies ever fixes rational priors. (7) So choosing a prior is never a rational act, and a supposedly principled flat prior is no better justified than any other arbitrary starting point — including in my own models, where I'd swear that starting a click-through-rate model on a Beta(2, 18) prior rather than a coin flip at 0.5 is more sensible, and that this is knowledge rather than taste.

Something has to give, and I can't tell which. Giving up (1) feels like conceding that flat priors were lazy rather than principled all along; giving up (3) or (5) feels like claiming that our choice of description secretly carries information, which I'd need to hear argued. Please tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked fine to me, and lay out what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether the rejection really lets me escape "priors are pure convention," or whether it just relocates the convention somewhere I haven't noticed.
