My brother-in-law and I have spent the last year arguing about a hypothetical we came across online, and I've managed to trap myself with it. The setup: you can either press a button that gives one person, hidden away in a room, fifty years of the worst suffering imaginable, or a button that gives a barely noticeable dust speck in the eye to an inconceivably vast number of people — so many that if you had started writing the number down at the beginning of the universe you still wouldn't be close to done. I chose the specks without hesitating, and he called me a monster: a speck in the eye is a real harm, harms add up, and enough copies of any real harm, however small, eventually outweigh any single bigger one. Since then I've been trying to build the argument that shows where his arithmetic goes wrong, and what I've built now proves so much more than I intended that I no longer trust it. Here it is. (1) The torture answer feels monstrous because one person is being made to suffer something unspeakable so that a crowd can be spared something trivial: what happens to a person cannot be outweighed by adding up things that happen to other people, no matter how many of them there are. (2) If that principle is right, it is right across the board — there is no version of don't aggregate across people that applies only when the trade is as lopsided as torture versus dust specks. (3) But then consider everything else with the same shape: a vaccination program in which one child in a hundred thousand suffers a severe reaction so that millions are spared a week of fever, or a town that accepts a slightly higher risk of a fatal accident for each resident so that a million commuters save five minutes a day. In each case, a few people bear a serious harm so that a great many others each get a tiny benefit. (4) If cross-person aggregation is illegitimate, then these trades are illegitimate too, and the right thing to do is let the fevers run and leave the road as it was. (5) That is not merely implausible; it is unlivable — no school, hospital, or government could operate under it. (6) So (1) is false, and my brother-in-law wins: enough specks really do outweigh the torture.

I can see three escape routes and I don't like any of them. I could reject (2) and say aggregation is fine for the small stuff but not when a single person's suffering is this severe — but then I can't explain where the line is or why it sits where it does, and it smells like deciding the conclusion first and inventing the principle afterward. I could attack the move from (3) to (4) by insisting that the vaccine and road cases differ in kind from the torture case — something about risk versus certainty, or about a policy anyone would have accepted in advance versus one person being singled out — but I would want to know whether that difference is real or just a story I'm telling myself to keep my intuitions from fighting. Or I could bite the bullet on (5), which I am more tempted to do than I'd like to admit. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked so safe when I first wrote it down, and what rejecting it costs me — because as things stand, either I'm a monster or most of public life is, and I would like to know which.
