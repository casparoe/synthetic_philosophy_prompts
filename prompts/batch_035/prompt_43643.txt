i run a small reading group of non-philosophers (retired folks mostly, an ex-judge, a couple engineers) and our next topic is why promises actually bind. my plan is not to lecture them about theories but to sort them by their gut reactions to concrete cases, then tell them afterwards which position they ended up in. so what i need from you is a decision tree, not an essay.

build it like this. roughly eight to ten branch nodes, each phrased as a case with two or three answers a normal person could give without knowing any theory names. the cases should be the ones that actually split the field, something like: a promise made to a dying friend who will never find out whether you kept it (no chance of future reliance); a promise kept so secret the promisee never even develops an expectation; a promise extracted by coercion; two castaways on an island with no society around them trying to work out whether "i promise" means anything; an insincere promise where the promisor never intended to follow through. you pick the exact set, but every node has to be answerable from the case alone, no "do you think promises are a social construct?" style questions that just ask for the label.

the leaves should be the real positions: the Hume/Rawls style view that the obligation is created by a social practice of promising, Scanlon-type views where what matters is the promisee's expectation or assurance, will-based views where the promisor binds themselves by invoking their own will, and the deflationary option that there is no special promissory duty over and above ordinary harms like letting people down. include that deflationary leaf even if its hard to place.

two more things. flag any pair of positions the tree cant actually seperate (my guess is the practice view and the expectation view will collapse on most of my cases, tell me if im right). and note which combinations of answers get no leaf at all, with a sentence on whether that combination is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. keep the language plain enough that my group can do it in ten minutes, typos dont matter to them but jargon does.
