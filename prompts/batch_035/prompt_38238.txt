I teach an intro ethics elective, and next unit we're doing the death penalty -- retribution, deterrence, executing the innocent, the usual papers. Instead of another paper, I want to open with Stephen King's The Green Mile, probably leaning on the 1999 Darabont film since most of them will have seen it or can watch it over a weekend. Read it for me as a work of death penalty philosophy: Cold Mountain's cell block E, Old Sparky, John Coffey wrongly convicted of murdering the two little girls, Wild Bill as the actual killer, the guards who know Coffey is innocent and execute him anyway, Percy botching Del's execution out of spite, and Paul Edgecombe living (for a very, very long time) with what he did.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on and want the reading to take seriously: I suspect the work cheats. In the real innocence debate, the horror is precisely that we never get certainty -- the system runs on evidence, verdicts, and probabilities, and the wrongness of executing an innocent has to be argued under uncertainty. The Green Mile hands the audience metaphysical certainty (healing powers, resurrection of a mouse, we literally watch the truth) while the guards have only strong private conviction, which is closer to real cases. So does the film function as an actual argument against capital punishment, or does it only work because it converts an epistemic problem into a melodrama of OBVIOUS innocence that no real case resembles? And there's a wrinkle underneath: Coffey asks to die, Paul grants it, and the guards' grief suggests the retributive machinery isn't even the villain of the piece -- the villain is Percy. Is the work saying anything about retribution or deterrence at all, or is it entirely a fallibility-and-cruelty story? Deterrence basically never comes up, which is itself interesting given how much of the real debate it occupies.

Give me a reading that runs both directions: what the work shows about executing the innocent that the arguments miss (the intimacy of it, the guards as executioners who must live with it, Paul's punishment being to outlive everyone he loves), and where the work distorts or evades -- including whether Edgecombe's longevity is a genuinely interesting treatment of moral residue, or just King piling on. Be honest about the places where my "it cheats" complaint is too quick. Point me to three or four specific scenes to actually screen in class and what to do with each, and tell me what you'd say the work adds to the death penalty debate that argument alone can't. Please write in American English.
