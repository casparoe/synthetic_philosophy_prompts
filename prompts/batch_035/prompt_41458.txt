So I've been reading about the old Confucian rules for mourning your parents, and there's something that bugs me about them. Quick rundown in case the details matter: a son (originally it was mostly sons) was supposed to wear rough undyed hemp clothes, skip meat, wine, music, and sex, sleep badly, not run his usual affairs, and if he held office he stepped down from it for the whole period. Everyone calls it three-year mourning, though in practice the counting worked out to something like two years and a bit — the sources quibble about the exact number of months. There's also that famous story in the Analects where a student suggests the period is way too long and just a custom, and Confucius answers by pointing out that for your first three years of life you were carried around in your parents' arms, so the mourning period pays that back. And when the student says he'd be comfortable eating good food and wearing fine brocade during the mourning anyway, Confucius basically says fine, do what makes YOU comfortable, you're not feeling the grief — and calls him, harshly, not truly human.

Here's my puzzle. Mencius says our moral feelings are natural sprouts that just need cultivating — grief for a parent is already there, and ritual gives it proper form, like grief is the raw thing and mourning clothes etc. are its expression. Xunzi says almost the opposite: our raw feelings are messy and run to excess, and the ancient sages deliberately invented ritual to trim, pace, and shape them — on his picture, part of the point of the mourning timetable is that it gives your grief a place to stop, so it doesn't destroy you, and a shape to move through, so it doesn't fester.

So which theory of human emotion and ritual does this practice actually presuppose? The expressive elements (coarse clothes, bad sleep, going without — these seem like externalized grief, very Mencian) sit next to the strictly regulated elements (a fixed timetable, graded severity depending on which relative died, rules against remarriage during the period, a defined end date, very Xunzian). Is that a tension the practice can't resolve, or does it only look like one? I'd like to know: (1) what the implicit theory of grief and ritual is that makes the whole package coherent, if there is one; (2) what parts of the practice strictly presuppose Mencius-style naturalism about feeling and what parts strictly presuppose Xunzi-style ritual-as-artifice, vs. which parts are just underdetermined and compatible with either; and (3) what a mourner would actually do differently under each view — like, if we took Mencius seriously, would the fixed end date look like a betrayal of grief, and if we took Xunzi seriously, would the ascetic expressions look less like grief made visible and more like scaffolding? Basically: is this practice secretly committed to one of the two, or is it a clever institution that doesn't need to pick a winner? Feel free to tell me the whole framing is confused too, if you think the Mencius/Xunzi divide doesn't map onto expression vs. regulation as cleanly as I'm assuming.
