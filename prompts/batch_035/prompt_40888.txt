I'm teaching intro ethics again this semester and we just wrapped up the moral conflict unit — Sartre's student is the obvious opener but I want to push past the war stories and onto the actual logical terrain. For the final review session I'd like a "which view of moral dilemmas do you actually hold?" quiz that sorts students by how they react to cases, not by which label they find sympathetic, so no question should just ask "do you think real dilemmas exist?" straight out.

Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. The splitting questions I have in mind are things like: when two obligations conflict and you do the better one, does the other obligation simply disappear (so failing it is now fine), or is there a sense in which you still violated something? Is the residue — the felt need to feel guilty, apologize, make amends — tracking a real leftover obligation, or is it just imperfect human psychology that would ideally be trained away? Does "ought implies can" hold, and if so, does it apply to each obligation separately or only to the disjunction of them? Can a fully informed agent ever be in a state where, all things considered, they ought to do A and, all things considered, they ought to do B, where these can't both be done? And should blamelessness be read as retrospective evidence that the situation wasn't a genuine dilemma in the first place, or can tragic blamelessness be the real thing? Also, if someone thinks apparent dilemmas are always resolvable in principle but sometimes not knowably so by creatures like us, that should come out as its own leaf — the epistemic-dilemmas-only position.

So: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case (the murderer-at-the-door-ish lie, the hospital triage where you can save the five or keep a promise to the one, the person who can only prevent someone else's wrongdoing by violating a duty of their own, that kind of thing), two or three answer options each, phrased so a student can answer without seeing where it leads. Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves — at minimum: the Ross-style view that all conflicts dissolve once you see which duty is more stringent, the strict no-conflict view on which apparent conflicts of obligation are always proof of confusion somewhere, the dilemmist view with residue, the dilemmist view without residue (you do wrong no matter what but there's no leftover ought afterward), and the epistemic-only view, plus whatever else the logical space demands. Mark any unoccupied combination of answers and say whether you think nobody sits there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And please flag explicitly which pairs of leaves the questions can't actually seperate — my TAs will be grading this and I don't want to defend a rubric with fake precision. Keep the cases fresh-ish too, no trolleys if you can help it, my students are completely trolleyed out by this point in the term.
