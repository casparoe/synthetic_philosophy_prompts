The topic is Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and its treatment of epistemic closure. As I understand it from Philosophical Explanations, Nozick holds that you know that p when you believe p, p is true, and your belief tracks the truth of p: if p were true you would believe it, and if p were false you would not. A notorious consequence is that knowledge is not closed under known entailment. I can know that I have hands, and know that having hands entails that I am not a handless brain in a vat, and yet fail to know that I am not a brain in a vat, since if I were one, I would still believe I was not. Critics object that this lets the theory endorse claims that sound absurd on their face (knowing you have hands while refusing to claim you know you are not a vat-brain), and Nozick's response was essentially to accept a restricted closure principle, one limited to cases where you believe the conclusion by the same method by which you believe the premise.

Here is my own thought, which I want the proposal to build around rather than treat as an afterthought: the failures of closure all seem to involve a special kind of conclusion, namely one whose nearest worlds where it is false are skeptical worlds that lie far from actuality. For an ordinary conclusion, the nearest world where it is false is typically no farther away, and often closer, than the nearest world where the premise is false; for such conclusions, tracking should let you know them by deduction just as well as by direct observation. So perhaps the correct principle is neither unrestricted closure nor Nozick's method-based restriction, but something like a distance-limited closure principle: if you know that p, you know that p entails q, and the nearest worlds where q is false are no farther from actuality than the nearest worlds where p is false, then you know that q. My research question is whether this principle can be stated precisely using the machinery of comparative closeness of possible worlds that the tracking theory already relies on, whether it correctly handles the skeptical cases that drive the original objection, and whether it generates counterexamples of its own.
