I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I need help adjudicating a dispute that broke out at our story-planning meeting. We're preparing a feature on famine early-warning systems — the international monitoring networks that track rainfall and crop conditions in food-insecure regions and trigger humanitarian appeals when harvests fail. Two colleagues, Dana and Marcus, got into it over what our framing should be, and I have to referee by our next editorial meeting. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Dana, who covers development economics, opened by saying that building an early-warning system around rainfall and harvest data concedes the wrong picture of famine from the start. That approach treats famine as a natural disaster — not enough food produced — whereas Amartya Sen's work on Bengal in 1943 showed the food was there; people starved because they had lost the ability to command it, because wages failed and prices soared while grain sat in markets. Dana said the deeper lesson is that famines are political events, and that Sen's famous observation that no famine of any significance has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press confirms it: where governments answer to voters and a press can sound the alarm, starvation does not happen.

Marcus, our senior editor, said Dana was overcorrecting. Plenty of famines really do begin as supply collapses, he said: the Irish famine started with an actual potato blight that destroyed the crop, and the Chinese famine of the Great Leap Forward followed genuine shortfalls in 1959. An early-warning system that ignored rainfall and harvest data because "the real story is politics" would be useless — you may as well wait for constitutional reform rather than monitor the weather. Sen's Bengal case, he said, is doing too much work as a generalization from one (admittedly scandalous) episode.

Dana replied that Marcus's two counterexamples prove Dana's point rather than his own. Ireland continued exporting food through the worst of the famine; grain was procured out of Chinese villages while people died there. Whatever the supply shock, the deaths were a matter of distribution and political choice, so even the supply-collapse cases end up as political crimes rather than natural disasters — which is exactly why the natural-disaster framing is not just incomplete but exculpatory, since it locates the cause in the weather rather than in decisions.

Marcus made two points in response. First, he said "crime" was doing suspicious work: a crime needs a criminal and a law, and a government that struggles and fails during a genuine drought — despite competent, good-faith effort — would count as a criminal on Dana's definition, which cheapens the charge until it names nothing. Second, he said Dana's democracy claim is close to unfalsifiable as used: democracies that face famine-scale stresses also tend to be the ones with functioning relief capacity, so we never get to see the test case we'd need, and the claim ends up resting on an absence that may have a humbler explanation than a free press.

Dana's last word was that this is all the more reason early-warning systems should track the right variables: market prices, wages, and whether particular groups can still acquire food — the entitlement side — and not just harvest tonnage. Marcus countered that the operational people will never act on such diffuse indicators, and that a false alarm on a genuine crop failure would be a moral catastrophe of its own; you monitor what you can measure.

I honestly could not tell who was right about which parts, and I don't want our feature's framing to rest on a coin flip. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, in the order above — assess each of Dana's and Marcus's substantive moves as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with brief justification, and identify anything both of them are missing. One constraint: since my piece is for an American magazine, please write your answer in American English. Please end your response with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a one-sentence summary of who is right about which specific moves.
