I'm a grad student and in two weeks I'm presenting the central chapter of my dissertation to my department's political philosophy workshop. The chapter defends what I've been calling a "standby view" of religious arguments in democratic politics: a citizen or official can offer explicitly religious arguments for a coercive policy as long as adequate public reasons for that policy exist somewhere in the wider discourse and are doing the real justificatory work -- the religious arguer doesn't have to supply them, recite them, or even be aware of them. So a bishop can argue for expanded welfare policy in purely theological terms, fine, because there's a solid public case to be had; but a purely religious case for criminalizing blasphemy, where the supposedly public reasons on offer are just post-hoc rationalizations, doesn't pass. The test I propose is roughly "would the policy survive on public reasons alone," and I claim this ends up both more respectful to religious citizens and stricter in practice than a lot of what passes for public reason liberalism, since it doesn't let people launder bad policy behind the mere existence of some thin secular excuse.

Here's the audience. Maybe half the workshop are pretty committed Rawlsians who think anything short of the proviso -- you owe your fellow citizens public reasons for the exercise of coercive power, full stop, from you, in your own mouth -- is a sell-out. One guy is a Habermasian who insists that translating religious into generally accessible reasons isn't an optional courtesy but a structural feature of legitimate lawmaking, and he's told me before that my view treats the public reasons as a "free rider" subsidy. And then there's a natural law theorist who agrees with my conclusion but hates my framework: she thinks "public reasons" is basically a label for reasons secular academics happen to like, and that my standby view smuggles in the same bias while pretending to be neutral, which honestly stings because it's a version of the worry I raise myself against other people. Plus a couple of first-years who'll happily ask whatever the senior faculty are too polite to say out loud.

Can you draft my Q&A prep sheet? I want the objections in rough order of how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each camp would actually phrase them (the Rawlsians will go on about reciprocity and civic respect, the Habermasian about the moral function of law and translation, the natural law person about the stacked deck built into the very idea of the "public"). For each one, give me a two-minute reply I could actually deliver, and be honest about where the reply is soft -- I'd much rather find out now than at the workshop. Also tell me what I should just concede up front; I'm half tempted to concede the epistemic asymmetry point about who gets to decide which reasons count as "adequate." And please figure out the question I'm hoping nobody asks, because I already suspect it's this: whether my view quietly collapses back into the proviso whenever the public reasons are genuinely contested rather than sitting there safely on standby. The welfare example is easy precisely because everyone in the room already agrees with it -- the hard cases are exactly the ones where the standby never shows up, and I don't currently have a good answer to that.
