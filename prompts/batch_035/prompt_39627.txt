I'm helping my aunt draft her commencement address (small college, ~600 grads, plus parents and grandparents in folding chairs who have already sat through two hours of other speeches). She just retired after forty-something years as a theoretical computer scientist — complexity theory specifically. Her whole career, as she puts it, was proving that things can't be done, or more precisely proving that entire strategies for proving things will never work. And here's the problem: she absolutley refuses to say anything she believes is false, and she believes about 90% of normal commencement material is false. If you can dream it you can be it — nope. There's no limit to what you can achieve if you just work hard enough — nope, her field is basically the science of some limits being permanent. But she also doesn't want to give a lecture on futility to 600 twenty-two year olds, and the speech has to actually move people — she cried at her own retirement dinner, she is not a cold fish.

The audience split is tricky too: maybe a quarter of the graduating class is CS, and they'll catch every reference — she wants at least one moment that rewards them specifically — while the rest are families who will not tolerate jargon.

Please write the roughly five-minute speech. Her constraints, as she stated them: (1) nothing she'd consider false — no promises that persistence conquers all; (2) it still has to genuinely inspire, and she insists the honest stuff about limits can be framed so it lands as comfort or even pride rather than doom; (3) one analogy or moment that makes the core idea of P vs NP, or of NP-completeness, or of reductions (dealers choice) land for the grandmothers without making the CS grads cringe; (4) no ending about the future being in your hands, which she considers vacuous. She'd also like a beat somewhere on why spending a lifetime proving that certain things are impossible was, for her, its own kind of love affair with the truth — but keep it from going saccharine, she will die.
