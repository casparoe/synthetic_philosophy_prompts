I've followed the collective-harms and inefficacy literature as it circulates in the rationalist blogosphere for the better part of fifteen years — the threshold models, the expected-value rejoinders, the suspicion that multiplying tiny pivotality probabilities by huge stakes is exactly where expected-value reasoning stops tracking anything real. On the theory, my considered position is mostly nodding along. My verdicts on actual cases are another matter entirely: I cannot make them hang together, and I'd rather find out what they add up to than keep applying the argument of the week to each case afresh. So here are the cases and my gut verdicts. Please tell me what principle, if any, captures them — or, failing that, which of my verdicts has to go.

1. Twice a week I buy a chicken burrito when a vegan option sits on the same menu. Verdict: each purchase is a little bit wrong. Small, but real, and not the kind of thing an offset or an eye-roll at the infinitesimal marginal effect makes go away.

2. Once a year I fly across an ocean for a vacation I would enjoy almost as much at half the distance. Verdict: permissible. I cannot make myself feel bad about it, and I'm not sure I should try — even though the flight plausibly emits more carbon than a year of those burritos.

3. I vote in presidential elections, in a state my candidate carries by fifteen points. Verdict: something close to obligatory. Skipping it would make me a freeloader on everyone else's turnout, even though my vote's pivotality probability rounds to zero.

4. I send forty dollars to a campaign in a swing state I don't live in. Verdict: optional, in the way charity generally is — praiseworthy if I do it, no stain if I don't. This despite the donation arguably buying more expected marginal influence than my safe-state vote, which I treat as near-obligatory.

5. During a drought I water my lawn on my assigned days. Verdict: fine. If the city issues a ban and I water anyway, it becomes wrong immediately — and I don't think the harm got any bigger, because it didn't, at least not through my hose. Something else changed, and I can't say what.

6. I observe a boycott of a company whose labor practices I deplore, and I'd quietly think less of a friend who didn't — even though my purchases are far too small to register in that company's decisions, and I know it, and my compliance has no threshold to cross.

7. Offsetting. I pay for verified carbon removal to cover the annual flight and feel the matter settled. I donate the monetary equivalent of my burrito consumption to a well-run animal charity and feel that nothing whatsoever has been settled — the burritos are still wrong, donation or no donation.

I do have a half-formed principle of my own, and it's failing in public: an individual contribution to a diffuse harm matters only when the harm arrives continuously, drop by drop, with no threshold structure anywhere in the pipeline — emissions, animal purchases, aquifer drawdown — and never when effects are lumpy or pivotality-based, which is supposed to explain why I shrug at vote-pivotality objections and why the drought ban in case 5 can't be about harm at all. But it classifies the flight and the burrito together, and it's silent on why boycott-compliance feels obligatory with no threshold in sight. So test it, repair it, or replace it.

The case I keep going back and forth on: at a party, leftover chicken that would otherwise be thrown out. My eating it causes no further purchase, so there's no drop to add — but it doesn't feel like the act is fully laundered, the way the offset flight was. I want whatever principle you land on to take a stand on that case, and also on this one: should I reallocate my safe-state vote — which costs me an hour and does, by my lights, essentially nothing — into an hour spent persuading a friend in the swing state to vote? If your principle says yes, explain why my verdict on case 3 wasn't really about the vote at all; if it says no, explain what the hour is for. State the principle precisely, show which cases motivate which clauses, and be honest about which of my verdicts you cannot save.
