hey, so I run a philosophy of mind reading group, 8 people, mostly grad students, we meet for about 75 minutes. next session we're discussing the Copycat paper and I want to open with a game instead of a presentation. the thing I want them to actually feel is this: concepts don't have fixed, context-independent content -- when you make an analogy, you quietly let concepts stretch or slip (a letter can become a number, the *rightmost* thing can become the *leftmost* thing, "first" and "last" can turn out to be the same concept seen from different sides), and the stretching isn't noise, it's where the intelligence lives. they should discover that themselves, before anyone names it. so I need you to design the actual activity, and I want you to make real choices and commit to them rather than offering me a menu of options.

here's the shape I have in mind, adjust as needed: you give them a tiny made-up world -- a short line of symbols, say six or eight -- and a transformation rule that gets applied to it. then a second line of symbols that's some systematic variant of the first (reversed order, or the alphabet runs the other way, whatever), and they have to figure out what the analogous transformation does to *that* line. the trick is that the obvious reading of the rule, applied literally, gives something that feels deeply wrong, and the answer that feels right requires letting a concept slip -- the way Copycat's answers to its letter-string puzzles require letting "successor letter" slip into "predecessor letter" once it notices the second string runs backwards. design maybe 3 of these puzzles in increasing order of sneakiness, where the first can be solved mechanically and the later ones can't. work out concrete strings and rules yourself -- actually solve them on paper and give the intended answer for each, plus what the literal answer would have been.

rules and structure: they play solo first, on paper, a few minutes per puzzle, writing not just their answer but what they'd say to someone who insists on the literal reading. then pair them up for the last puzzle where one partner is assigned to defend the literal answer no matter what and the other has to produce the slippery one -- I want the argument for the slip to come out of their mouths, not mine. no real payoffs, the stakes are just that we vote at the end and I read out the split for each puzzle.

then the debrief: help me map moments of the activity onto the idea -- which moment was the pressure toward slipping, which was the equivalent of what Copycat calls the tension between perceptual and conceptual pressures, where the group's answer distribution mirrors the codepression thing (competing pressures that only get resolved once the representation re-describes itself). and be straight with me about two things: (1) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing at least one person will find a third answer nobody intended and I need to know how to handle that without shutting them down; and (2) which feature of the real idea a 75-minute game just cannot reproduce -- I assume something about the gradual, parallel, probabilistic buildup rather than a sudden human leap, but tell me what you actually think the gap is, because I'd rather name it honestly in the debrief than pretend the game is the concept. and one last thing: don't put any direct quotes from the paper in your instructions for me -- paraphrase anything you attribute to it, because I'm going to be quoting it from my own copy during the debrief.
