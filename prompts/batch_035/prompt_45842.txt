At the reception following a workshop on practical reasoning, two of my colleagues fell into an argument about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and by the end I could not confidently say who had the better of it. I will have to teach this material in a few weeks, so I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

The exchange proceeded as follows. The senior colleague, who works mainly in deontic logic, opened with the impossibility argument. A genuine dilemma, he said, would be a situation in which an agent, all things considered, ought to do X, ought to do Y, and cannot do both. That description is incoherent: if she ought to do X and ought to do Y, then she ought to do both; ought implies can; ex hypothesi she cannot do both; so at least one of the two original oughts is no genuine obligation, and the case is not a dilemma but a competition in which one duty wins and the other is overridden — a prima facie duty, in Ross's sense. Tragedy, he granted, but no contradiction.

My other colleague, who works on moral psychology and the emotions, went straight for the aggregation step. The principle that an obligation to do X and an obligation to do Y jointly entail an obligation to do both, she said, looks innocent only when both obligations spring from the same source. Where they have different sources, it fails. She offered the case of someone who promised one friend, long in advance, to serve as a witness at her wedding, and separately promised her father to drive him to and from a surgical procedure that was scheduled — after both promises were made — for the same morning, four hours away, with no possibility of rearrangement and no third party who could substitute. There is, in that situation, no further obligation to keep both promises, and there is no mystery about why not: an obligation to do the impossible is exactly the thing whose existence is in dispute, so it cannot be conjured into existence as an intermediate step.

He replied in two parts. First, even granting the failure of aggregation, he could not see what work the word "genuine" was doing for her. On her own account the agent has, in the end, broken a promise and thereby wronged its recipient — done wrong, though through no fault of her own. He was content to accept that; but wrongdoing without culpability is not a contradiction, and calling such cases "dilemmas" is a taxonomic preference, not a discovery. Second, a parity worry: if obligations from different sources do not aggregate, then neither do they compare — yet it is obviously an open question which promise the agent should keep; so the obligations must be commensurable after all, and commensurability is the beginning of a ranking, and a ranking does not leave two survivors.

She answered that commensurability for the purposes of choice is not an ordering of normative standing. One promise can win the competition for action while the other remains in force, and the residue is what shows it: the agent's guilt, the apology and compensation she owes, and above all the resentment of the friend at whose wedding she was absent, which seems fitting rather than excessive — and fitting resentment, she said, is the response to being wronged, so the wronging must be real. His criterion of "genuine," she concluded, meant "entails a formal contradiction," and that definition begged the question by building a single, consistent, aggregation-closed normative system into the word "ought."

His last word was a dilemma of his own, aimed at her residue. Either the residue is dischargeable — a pro tanto duty to apologize and make amends which, once paid, leaves nothing over, so that the case dissolves into a calculation plus a penalty — or it is not dischargeable, in which case the agent owes something she can never do, and she has smuggled the impossible obligation back onto his side of the ledger. They parted there, each certain the other had changed the subject.

Please adjudicate the exchange round by round: who is right about what at each step, and whether his two-part reply and her two-part reply fare differently. Then tell me whether there is something both of them missed. As you go, indicate how confident you are in each verdict, and at the end single out the one move in the exchange you are least sure how to adjudicate, explaining what makes it difficult.
