I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and for a while now I've found myself committed to a position I later learned has a name: Dennett's heterophenomenology. As I understand it, the idea is that the science of consciousness should treat subjects' first-person reports the way I would treat log output from a system I'm reverse-engineering: as data to be recorded and interpreted, never as a privileged window into the system's internals. When a subject reports that something seems red to them, that report is evidence about their functional state, on par with a user's bug report — sometimes diagnostic, sometimes confabulated, and never authoritative simply because the user is the one making it. The alternative, as Dennett seems to see it, is granting first-person reports a kind of incorrigible authority that no other science grants the objects it studies, and he treats that as a methodological betrayal rather than a reasonable caution. That alternative strikes me as exactly the mistake of trusting a user's own theory of why the app crashes instead of instrumenting the code, so I've adopted the method wholesale.

Now tell me what would count against this position — not a defense of it, but a map of its exposure. What observations, experimental results, or conceptual cases, if they obtained, would show that treating reports as mere third-person data loses something essential about consciousness, and which of these are live possibilities rather than philosophical fantasy? Please sort the defeaters by severity, from ones a committed heterophenomenologist should themselves concede as counting against the method, through ones they would reinterpret away, down to ones they would flatly dismiss, and say where you draw those lines. Along the way I want you to address three things in particular.

First, cases where a subject's report appears to carry information that no third-person measurement could in principle capture. If such cases exist, is that fatal to the method, or merely a reason to widen what gets counted as data? Second, the charge that the method quietly assumes what is at issue by deciding in advance that nothing about consciousness could ever outrun the behavioral evidence. Is that a fair charge of question-begging, and if so, does it defeat the method or only limit its scope? Third, whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability by Dennett's insistence that the method is neutral between real and illusory phenomenology. If every apparent counterexample can be redescribed as the subject misdescribing their own internal states, what, if anything, does the method still risk — and is having no risks an acceptable state for a methodological stance of this kind, or a sign it has stopped asserting anything?

Finally, tell me what this method predicts that its rivals don't. Where, in actual practice, would a heterophenomenological consciousness science and a first-person-authoritative one diverge — in the experiments they run, the data they collect, the theories they'd accept or reject? If the honest answer is that they would never diverge in practice, say so plainly, and tell me whether that would make the whole dispute merely verbal, and which side, if either, should then be bothered by that. I care less about the history of the debate than about an honest assessment of how exposed my new commitment actually is.
