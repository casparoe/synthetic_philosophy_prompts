I'm TAing a phil of mind class and could use help grading a homework answer. The question I gave the students was this:

"Suppose each of your neurons is replaced, one at a time, with a silicon chip that replicates the neuron's input-output behavior, including response timing, so that your overall functional organization stays fixed. Explain Chalmers's fading qualia thought experiment: what hypothesis is it meant to rule out, and how does the argument go? Then assess the following claim: gradual replacement is genuinely identity-preserving in a way that destructive scan-and-destroy uploading is not, so the philosophical worries about uploading simply don't arise for it."

One student answered as follows:

"Fading qualia is Chalmers's argument for functionalism. The setup is that your neurons get swapped out for silicon chips one at a time, with each chip doing exactly what the neuron did. If functionalism is false, your qualia might slowly fade as the chips take over, until by the end you have no experience at all. Chalmers says this can't happen, because you would notice your experience fading and say so, but since the chips preserve function your reports stay exactly the same as before — so you'd be saying 'everything seems normal' while actually going blank. That's a contradiction, so functionalism is true and gradual replacement preserves consciousness. He also has a 'dancing qualia' variant where qualia flicker on and off gradually as the replacements accumulate.

On the second part: gradual replacement clearly avoids the teletransporter problem. In scan-and-destroy you die and a mere copy is made, but in gradual replacement there is causal continuity, each stage is psychologically continuous with the one before, and as Parfit showed, psychological continuity with the right kind of causal connection is what personal identity over time consists in. So gradual replacement preserves identity by definition, and there's no extra fact of the matter being papered over. The only remaining worry would be if the chips were imperfect, but that's an engineering problem, not a philosophical one — it has nothing to do with the fading qualia argument."
