I host a small podcast on Indian philosophy — audience is mostly grad students plus the occasional very patient undergrad — and next week's guest is a professor who's spent a good chunk of her career defending the old Nyāya line that upamāna, knowledge through analogy, is a genuine, independent pramāṇa on par with perception, inference, and testimony, not something that can be boiled down to those three. Draft me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions for her. I want them to actually press on the content, not just get a restatement of the doctrine.

A few specifics I want covered. At least one question on the stock example: the person who's never seen a gavaya, has been told it resembles a cow, and then on first spotting one knows that this is the sort of animal the word gavaya applies to — ask her why this couldn't just be a straightforward inference, and since she'll presumably reply that there's no vyāpti, no universal rule connecting looking-like-a-cow with being-called-a-gavaya, include a follow-up on why that gap can't be closed by just adding testimonial premises to the syllogism. Also one question on the reductionist complaint that the whole pramāṇa-counting fight is basically bookkeeping — whether the disagreement between her view and the Buddhist reductionists is substantive or merely about how we file things — with a follow-up asking what actually hangs on it in practice, say for a child learning the meanings of kind-words, or for how you'd diagnose someone who consistently fails to generalize from examples. And at least one question on the fuzzy border between upamāna and śabda, since if she leans too hard on testimony doing the heavy lifting, her opponents can just fold analogy into testimony and declare victory.

One more thing: she's a great talker and will happily narrate the whole history from the Nyāya-sūtra commentaries onward if I let her, so phrase the questions tightly enough that a ten-minute digression would obviously not be an answer to them. And please end with a question about what evidence or case study could in principle count against treating analogy as an independent source of knowledge — I'm tired of interviews where every view turns out to be conveniently unfalsifiable.
