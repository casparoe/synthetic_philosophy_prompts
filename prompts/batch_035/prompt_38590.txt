My father died three weeks ago, and I have been asked to give the eulogy. He was born in Kaifeng and spent most of his life in Montreal as a machinist, and he was raised in a household where the old Confucian expectations were never discussed as philosophy; they were simply how one carried oneself. He deferred to his own mother until her death, never took the trips my mother wanted because they conflicted with obligations he had never exactly chosen, and arranged his entire life around roles he never stopped to evaluate. I am the family member who left — I teach Anglophone ethics at a university here — and for twenty years I pressed the argument that he had traded his individuality for his obligations. I told him more than once that there was a real person buried somewhere under all that filial duty, and that he owed it to himself to find him. He never argued back, which was, I now suspect, its own kind of answer.

Since his death I have been reading what is called Confucian role ethics — the Ames and Rosemont line that there is no self prior to one's relations, that my father was not a person who happened to occupy the roles of son, husband, father, and neighbor but was constituted by how he carried them, so that stripping the roles away to reveal the man underneath would leave nothing to reveal. Whether or not that is ultimately true, it has quietly ruined every eulogy template I know how to write, because the standard eulogy in my tradition is built on precisely the move role ethics calls a mistake: the search for the essential him beneath the duties, the individual behind the relations. And I am not willing to stand up and eulogize a self he never claimed to have. But I am also not willing to pretend I always admired him, because I did not, and some of my grief is an argument we never finished.

So here is the audience, and the difficulty it creates: my mother, who loved him and spent forty years resenting the primacy his own mother held; my sister, who stayed in Montreal and shares the burden of his way of being family, and hears my philosophical regret as an accusation; his grandchildren, raised here, for whom saying he did his duty sounds like faint praise, an apologia for self-erasure; and a handful of relatives and old neighbors for whom duty is simply what a man is, and who would hear any Western tribute to his authenticity as a stranger's speech. Write the eulogy. It must honor him in role-ethical terms — show what fathering, husbanding, sonship, and neighboring looked like when he did them, rather than gesturing at a personality behind them — without sounding to the grandchildren like an apologia, or to the traditionalists like a Western tribute dressed in borrowed decoration. It must be honest about my unfinished argument with him without becoming my own confession, and it must not lecture: nobody in that room wants Confucian role ethics explained to them, so the idea has to live in how the speech is put together rather than in anything the speech says about itself. Five minutes when spoken, plain enough that my mother can follow every sentence of it.
