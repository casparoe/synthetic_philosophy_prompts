I'm drafting a paper arguing that the doctrine of organic unities has only ever been load-tested in one direction. Moore, in Principia Ethica (1903), held that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts, and in the book's final chapter he ranks the appreciation of beautiful things and personal affection as the two great intrinsic goods — but, so far as I can find, every illustration of organic unity he gives runs the same way: the parts are worth little, the whole considerably more. Nowhere does he confront the question whether two genuine goods can combine to the detriment of the whole. I need a thought experiment that forces that question.

Construct the case to this specification: an ordinary, recognizable human life, with no exotic psychology and no science fiction; a deep friendship that remains throughout a genuine instance of Moorean personal affection — mutual, and directed, as Moore insists such affection must be, at qualities that really are there in the friend — so that the friendship is an intrinsic good at every stage; an equally genuine aesthetic life, whose value is not later revealed to have been shallow, so no discovery of badness is allowed; and the quantities held roughly fixed, so the time spent with the friend does not simply subtract from the hours of the aesthetic life, and any loss in value of the whole cannot be explained as subtraction of parts. Yet the life as a whole, once the friendship is added, should be arguablly less good than the sum its parts would suggest. The case must earn this verdict from features a reader can weigh, not stipulate it.

Deliver the response in this order: (1) The scenario — one paragraph, under 150 words. (2) The check — a short paragraph for each theory in play: what a simple summationist (value as the sum of intrinsic goods) says about the life, and what the doctrine of organic unities must say for the case to work, including whether that amounts to extending Moore or revising him. (3) The working feature — the single structural feature of the case doing the work, and what happens if it is removed. (4) The counterattack — the strongest Moorean move against the case, addressing honestly whether my "no bad-making feature anywhere" constraint can be met at all; if not, say which constraint must be relaxed and what the relaxed case shows instead. (5) Fairness — two or three sentences on whether the case generates its verdict or merely stipulates it.
