I am a retired developmental biologist — thirty-odd years working on gene regulatory networks in insect metamorphosis, if that matters — who has spent the last few years reading seriously in philosophy of mind. I have just enrolled part-time in a master's program, and my supervisor has asked me to draft a proposal for my thesis before we commit to a topic. I would like your help turning my half-formed idea into a proper proposal document, and I would rather work through this with someone who will push back than with someone who will nod along.

Please take on the role of an expert philosopher of mind, someone who has supervised many theses on naturalizing content and who is sympathetic to, but not uncritical of, teleosemantics. Before writing anything, I'd like you to think carefully and do some honest brainstorming on paper: sketch the two or three most promising ways my idea could be developed, say which you would recommend and why, and only then produce the proposal. The finished document should be roughly two pages and should include the following, in whatever order serves the argument best:

(1) A precise statement of the thesis question. My starting point is this. Teleosemantics, as I understand it, grounds the content of mental states in biological function: a state represents what it is there, in the evolutionary sense, to do. The standard trouble cases are familiar — the disjunction problem of misrepresentation, swampman-type intuitions, the indeterminacy of what exactly was selected for. But my worry comes from a different direction, and I suspect it is a biologist's worry rather than a philosopher's. In my own field, the function of a gene is rarely a property of the gene considered alone. Knock out a regulatory gene and parallel pathways compensate; the "function" of a component is conferred by its position in a network, and the same component can be co-opted across developmental contexts to do quite different things. Selected-effect accounts of function seem to me to presuppose a kind of trait-level individuation that developmental and systems biology keeps failing to vindicate. My question, roughly: can teleosemantics survive if biological function is taken to be a property of network organization rather than of isolable traits — and if not, is there a version of the naturalizing project that can?

(2) An explanation of why the existing positions leave this open. Cover, at minimum, the etiological and causal-role traditions in the philosophy of function and how each would handle my network worry, and be candid about which parts of my framing a philosopher would consider naive or already answered.

(3) The method: what I would actually do, month by month, over roughly one academic year, given that I have a biologist's training and a philosopher's habits only newly acquired. Be concrete — if part of the project involves constructing worked examples from real developmental systems, say which kinds and what the examples are meant to show.

(4) What a successful outcome looks like, and — this part matters to me — what would count as the project failing. I have watched enough failed experiments to know that a proposal is only honest if it specifies its own failure conditions, and I would rather discover them now than in month ten.

Please do not supply a bibliography or citations; I will locate the primary literature myself with my supervisor. Refer to positions and arguments by name and content, and leave the references to me. I would also appreciate, either as an appendix or a closing note, a frank paragraph on whether this is a thesis-sized problem or a career-sized one, and what you would cut first if it turns out to be the latter.
