My department's philosophy of mind reading group has spent two semesters on the Knobe effect, and two members have been arguing past each other the whole time. One — call her the penetration theorist — holds that the side-effect asymmetry shows that ordinary judgments about whether an agent acted intentionally are directly sensitive to the moral valence of the outcome: evaluation is part of the competence, not noise around it. The other — call him the pragmatics theorist — holds that the asymmetry is generated by conversational factors: harm violates a norm, and a norm violation makes it conversationally relevant why the agent proceeded anyway, which in turn licenses the inference to intention, while mere benefit does not; the concept itself is morally inert. They have finally agreed to write an adversarial-collaboration statement rather than another round of duelling replies. Draft that document for them.

Take the following as stipulated common ground between the two parties, so that neither the drafter nor the reader needs to adjudicate the empirical literature: (1) the basic asymmetry — a chairman-type agent who says he does not care about a side effect, only about profit, is judged to have harmed intentionally far more often than he is judged to have helped intentionally, and this pattern replicates across many vignette contents; (2) the asymmetry is sensitive to how the question is framed and to what the vignette makes salient about the agent's reasons, though it has not been eliminated by any single rewording; (3) parallel asymmetries have been reported for other evaluatively loaded terms, including ascriptions of causation and of certain kinds of knowledge; (4) both parties accept that ordinary concept application can be driven by heuristics without those heuristics being errors.

The document must contain four sections.

Section 1: Numbered agreed claims. Cover the shared empirical picture, any shared metatheory (for example: "the folk concept" need not be a single, internally consistent thing; a pragmatic explanation and a conceptual explanation need not be rivals if they describe different levels), and any concessions each side should make to the other up front — the pragmatist conceding, say, that the pattern is too robust to be dismissed as an experimental artifact; the penetration theorist conceding that not every moral influence on judgment is conceptual penetration.

Section 2: A disagreements table. Rows for at least: what the asymmetry shows about the concept of intentional action; the correct description of what a subject doing the task is actually computing; whether the norm-violation that drives the effect is moral or merely expectation-relative (violating what people tend to do counts, not just what they ought to do); whether the effect generalizes to concepts not tied to agency; and whether penetration, if real, would tell us about the concept's semantics, its acquisition, or its use. Each row gives the penetration theorist's formulation and the pragmatics theorist's formulation — each phrased so its own side would sign it — and each row is labeled substantive, verbal, or empirical.

Section 3: Cruxes. At least three, each with a test, argument, or case both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. At least one crux must center on the following new case, constructed to pull the two views apart. A safety official approves a cost-saving procedure knowing that, as a side effect, it will expose a group of workers to a hazard while also funding a protection that will spare a larger group a greater hazard; she says she cares only about the budget. Whether her decision harmed or helped overall depends on a prior normative judgment about aggregation and about the voluntariness of the risk — one on which our two researchers themselves disagree. The penetration theorist should predict what subjects' intentionality judgments will track and why; the pragmatist likewise. At least one other crux must stipulate a hypothetical empirical result — for instance, a large replication finding the asymmetry wholly absent in a population whose members plainly have the concept of intentional action but whose social norms assign no prescriptive force to the agent's role — and require each side to say in advance whether they would treat it as disconfirming, and of what.

Section 4: A closing verdict on which residual disagreements are merely verbal. In particular: if the concept of intentional action is individuated by its role in the practice of holding responsible, is there still a substantive fact of the matter about whether moral considerations shape the concept itself or only the practice in which it is deployed? Say which parts of the dispute survive that question, and which do not.

Keep the register of a jointly authored methods-adjacent paper: neutral, exact, and free of the word "contamination" unless one side is explicitly allowed to disown the other's usage.
