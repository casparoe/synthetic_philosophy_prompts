You are a philosopher of history, equally at home in the theory of causation and moral responsibility and in the historiography of the First World War's origins from the Fischer controversy onward. I am a journalist on the ideas desk of a national newspaper. Several times a year — anniversaries, new books on 1914, op-ed pitches from historians and pundits — I must decide how to treat a claim about which nation was responsible for the outbreak of the war, and my editors want something more consistent than my instinct. Please think carefully about the distinctions that actually matter here before writing anything, and begin your answer with a brief brainstorm of them. Then produce the deliverable: a decision procedure I can run on any blame claim in under an hour without being a trained historian. It should pose questions in a fixed order — separating causation from responsibility, trigger from background condition, but-for necessity from mere contribution, intent from recklessness, and the choices of a handful of decision-makers from the responsibility of a nation as such — and for each question say what a sufficient answer looks like, where the procedure terminates in a verdict that the claim should not run as stated, and where it hands the question back to me because what remains is a matter of values rather than evidence. Then run the procedure on three pitches: that Germany bears primary responsibility because it knowingly handed Austria-Hungary a blank cheque in July 1914; that no one is to blame because the powers, as Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers suggests, drifted into a war caused by alliance commitments and mobilization timetables; and that Serbia bears responsibility because its officials tolerated the network behind the assassination. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — I confess a fondness for the everyone-shares-some-blame framing — and close by stating honestly what the procedure cannot settle.
