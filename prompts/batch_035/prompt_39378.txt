I've been reading rationalist blogs and the Effective Altruism Forum for the better part of a decade, and I've noticed that discussions of our obligations to future people tend to collapse into slogan-swapping — 'the future is vast', 'equal moral worth regardless of birth date' — without anyone spelling out what their actual verdicts on concrete cases commit them to. So I've written down mine, on cases in the intergenerational-climate neighbourhood, and I'd like your help extracting the principle behind them, if there is one.

My verdicts: a policy that delivers a large present convenience while killing a billion people in 2200 is about as wrong as one that delivers the same convenience by killing a billion people today; I'm not moved by the fact that the victims in 2200 will exist only because of the emitting lifestyle that harms them — creating someone as one of the harmed doesn't cancel the harm; I think we're permitted to give some priority to the present global poor over the future global poor, even though I'd reject giving priority to compatriots over foreigners on grounds of mere distance; I accept discounting for uncertainty (humanity might not be around, or the projections might be off), but pure time discounting — counting a life in 2200 for intrinsically less than a life today just because it is later — strikes me as indefensible; yet when I imagine a choice between £100 of benefit now and £120 of benefit in 2200 for the very same sort of recipient, I find I care noticeably less about the £120, and I can't fully persuade myself this is only uncertainty; and I think a present generation that is itself badly off — poor, and bearing heavy adaptation costs — has weaker climate obligations than a rich present generation, even though the victims of the emissions are the same future people in both scenarios.

I can see at least two candidate rules that might fit some of this: one on which obligations to future people are grounded in their interests, counted without pure time discounting but moderated by uncertainty and by how well-off the duty-bearer is; and another on which the strength of the duty varies with how much the duty-bearer benefits from creating the risk. They diverge on at least one of my verdicts, and possibly more. So: is there a single principle that captures the pattern, or the smallest set of principles that does? Where my verdicts conflict, I'd like you to say which ones should be overturned rather than rescued with ad hoc epicycles. Please also tell me what the resulting account predicts for two cases I haven't decided: a rich generation asked to forgo a modest gain to prevent a small but certain harm to a very large number of people ten thousand years hence; and a very poor generation asked to bear substantial costs now to benefit future people who, on credible projections, will be much richer than they are.
