My population ethics reading group has been stuck in the same spot for almost a year and I'd like a diagnosis of the situation rather than another survey of the views. Here's where we are, so you can see the shape of the deadlock.

The total view says a world's value is just the sum of everyone's wellbeing, so a world with trillions of lives barely worth living can come out better than a world of ten billion excellent lives — the repugnant conclusion. About half the group simply cannot accept that. But every alternative they bring in seems to walk straight into some other bullet. Averaging instead of summing avoids repugnancy but then says adding happy people to a very happy world makes things worse, which is also nuts. Critical-level views, where only wellbeing above some fixed threshold counts positively, avoid that one but run into what Arrhenius called the sadistic conclusion: they can say it's better to add some genuinely miserable lives than to add lives that are positive but below the threshold, since the miserable ones at least don't drag down the sum above the line the way the sub-threshold happy ones do. And there are these impossibility results floating around showing that basically no axiology can satisfy the full set of intuitions everybody keeps citing, which one member of our group takes to mean the whole project of ranking worlds is broken.

We've cycled through a bunch of theories about why we never move. One guy thinks the thought experiments are underspecified: nobody actually knows what a life barely worth living is like, so our intuitions are tracking whatever we privately imagined, and the "disagreement" is partly an artifact of filling in the case differently. Someone else thinks it's two different questions wearing one name — the totalists are answering something like "which world would an impartial benefactor choose to bring about" while the anti-totalists are answering "which world is better for the people in it," and you can hold the same view on each once you see they come apart. A third person says no, it's just bedrock: intuitions about aggregation differ the way tastes do, there's nothing to argue FROM, and the decades of counterexample ping-pong is just each side paying to avoid the bullet they hate most. And the impossibility-theorems guy says that since no view can satisfy all our intuitions, the honest conclusion is that the question "which possible world is better" is ill-posed and we should stop asking it.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Substantive or verbal or two-questions-in-one? Is there a hidden question doing work that nobody's making explicit, or is the person who says we're just comparing unarguable gut reactions right? I'd especially like you to take the impossibility-theorems argument seriously — does it show the question is ill-posed, or only that something has to give, without telling us what? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress here, or make an honest case that we should expect none and stop meeting.
