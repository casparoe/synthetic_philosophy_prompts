I've been running my local effective altruity student group's cause-selection process for the past year, and we basically live and die by the importance-tractability-neglectedness framework: score each cause roughly as the product of the scale of the problem, how solvable it is per unit of effort, and how much attention and money it's already getting. I've internalized this so thoroughly that I now get genuinely annoyed when someone pitches a cause without doing the three-factor breakdown first. But lately I've been having doubts, and I want to know what would actually count against the framework itself -- not against particular sloppy applications of it.

The things that bug me: a friend who studies development economics pointed out that the multiplicative form means a cause that's mediocre on two dimensions but terrible on none will beat a cause that's spectacular on scale but merely middling on tractability, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a bug (pandemic preparedness and existential risk seem like cases where it gives the wrong answer) or a feature. Also, I keep running into the worry that neglectedness is doing double duty -- if returns to effort are diminishing, then tractability per unit of effort already depends on how much effort has already gone in, so multiplying by a separate neglectedness term partially counts the same thing twice. And then there's the deeper problem that the framework feels unfalsifiable: whenever it crowns a winner people dislike, they just say the neglectedness score was miscalibrated, or that tractability should have been about marginal rather than average impact, or that some factor was missing entirely (coordination with other funders? feedback loops?), and the framework absorbs every correction without ever being at risk.

So here's what I want: what would genuinely count against ITN as the right way, even at a rough heuristic level, to prioritize causes? Which observations, arguments, or formal results should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward? What does it actually predict that rival approaches (intuitive weighing of pros and cons, cost-effectiveness analysis of specific interventions, a "hits-based" portfolio view) don't, so that evidence could in principle arbitrate between them? I especially want the things the framework's own defenders would concede as counting against it, not just what outside critics say. And finally, be honest about whether it's been qualified so much that it no longer risks anything -- and if so, whether that even matters for a rough heuristic of this kind, or whether an unfalsifiable framework that nonetheless organizes your thinking well is perfectly fine.
