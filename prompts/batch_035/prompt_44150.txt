Among philosophers of probability, the dispute between precise and imprecise probabilists about whether rational credence must be a single number has been running since the early nineties, and I have come to suspect it is not really a disagreement about probability at all. Consider how the two sides actually argue. The precise camp leans on accuracy-dominance reasoning: if your credal state is not a probability function, or is a whole set of them, then there is some alternative state that is more accurate in every possible world, and a state that is dominated in every world cannot be rational. They add a practical complaint: when the members of your representor disagree, the imprecise theory has no determinate recommendation to give, so it is silent exactly where a theory of decision is supposed to speak. The imprecise camp replies by pointing at the Ellsberg urns: ordinary subjects decline bets on which they have no information relevant to settling the odds, and they seem right to do so, which suggests rationality permits intervals rather than points. They add the charge that a single number manufactures false precision, an idealization that misdescribes the very agents the theory is for. Each side then concedes the mathematics of the other's examples and goes on as before. When dilation comes up -- the phenomenon where learning shared evidence makes the imprecise theorist's intervals spread while the precise theorist's posteriors converge -- the precise camp calls the spreading irrational and the imprecise camp calls the complaint a misunderstanding of what the intervals represent, and neither budges.

My working hypothesis is that the stall comes from neither side's formal claims, which are mostly right, but from a hidden disagreement about what rationality is a theory of: the precise camp reads 'rational' as a demand on ideal agents whose opinions must be complete, the imprecise camp reads it as advice for agents whose evidence genuinely underdetermines their attitudes -- and beneath that, perhaps, a covert dispute about whether there is a unique rational response to any body of evidence. Is this diagnosis right, or is there a genuine formal or empirical question still doing the work? I want your reasons drawn from what each side actually says, not a survey of the positions, and an honest account of what would count as progress here -- a distinction, a theorem, a stipulation about the term 'rational', a change of question -- or an argument that no progress should be expected, and why.
