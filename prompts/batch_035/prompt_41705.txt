I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long-form essay about the epistemic standing of fundamental physics — in particular, whether the field's decades-long investment in string theory, a framework with no distinctive experimental predictions, amounts to a change in what it means to do science. For background I have been reading a recent popular-science book on the state of physics, and it condenses the entire controversy into one paragraph that I am tempted to use as the frame for my piece. Before I do, I want it audited, because my instinct is that it smooths over distinctions the participants themselves would fight about. Here is the passage, as close to verbatim as my notes have it:

"String theory has never produced a single prediction that experiment could test, so on Karl Popper's criterion it is not science at all. Some theorists, led by the philosopher Richard Dawid, reply that a theory can be confirmed without any experiments, on the strength of considerations that are ultimately aesthetic: the fact that no one has found an alternative in forty years, and the unexpected internal coherence of the mathematics. On this view, the failure of two generations of physicists to come up with any other consistent theory of quantum gravity is itself evidence that string theory is true, in the way a detective may become confident about the only suspect who fits the evidence. The multiverse sharpens the problem: string theory's solutions describe a vast landscape of possible universes, ours among them, and since no observation of the others is possible even in principle, the theory can be neither confirmed nor refuted. That is why a growing number of physicists now concede that, for the first time in its history, science may have to give up testability as its criterion of demarcation."

Take this passage apart for me, claim by claim. For each part, tell me what Dawid and working string theorists would accept, what they would contest, and what is in fact the critics' case stated as if it were neutral background. I am particularly unsure about three junctions. First, whether "confirmed without experiments on aesthetic grounds" is a fair rendering of what Dawid calls non-empirical confirmation, and what he would say distinguishes his criteria from appeals to beauty. Second, whether the no-alternatives inference is being reported in the strength its proponents actually claim for it, or in a stronger one. Third, whether the landscape of string solutions and the unobservability of other universes play the same epistemic role the passage assigns them, or whether conflating them is itself part of the controversy. Finally, assess the closing sentence: does anything established in the earlier clauses actually support the claim that science may have to abandon testability, or is that a leap beyond the passage's own content? I want to come away knowing which sentences I could print with confidence, which need qualification, and which I should cut.
