ok so I'm a phd student in sts / history of science, and I'm writing a chapter about how credit for discoveries gets handed out, and something keeps bugging me and I want it straightened out philosophically rather than historically.

The practice: the Nobel statutes cap each science prize at three laureates, it can't be given posthumously, and — apart from Peace — never to an organization. Meanwhile basically everyone in my field takes it as a starting point that discoveries are collective and, in a bunch of famous cases, basically ripe on the tree: calculus, natural selection (darwin AND wallace, and wallace nearly scooped him), the neutron, loads of others. So here's my question: what theory of scientific discovery does the three-person cap actually presuppose? Is it quietly committed to a great-man view, where a discovery is a private mental act by one identifiable genius and the rest of the community is just transmission? Or could an inevitabilist — someone who thinks the thing would have been found within a few years no matter what — still get behind the cap, maybe on the grounds that picking the first finders has some other point (incentives? narrative?)? And is the practice even consistent with itself, given that the same institution loves to invoke giant collaborations in its own storytelling — the gravitational wave detection had a paper with a thousand-some authors — but then hands the medal to three guys and drops the other nine hundred and ninety-seven?

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of discovery, tell me which rival views the practice actually rules out versus which ones it's compatible with, flag where it's underdetermined (I suspect several theories would license it, but I want to know which), and then tell me what would have to change if we took the collective/inevitabilist view seriously — the cap, the no-posthumous rule, the whole idea of a discovery prize? Please don't let the answer bottom out at "it's just tradition" — tradition is a fact about the practice, not a theory of it.
