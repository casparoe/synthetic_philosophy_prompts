So I'm a science and ideas journalist — normally I'm writing about preprints and physics funding — but my editor has me on a piece about the whole statues-and-school-names debate, the Jefferson/Churchill/Columbus cluster, and one phrase keeps landing in my inbox from readers: "he was a man of his time." It gets deployed to shut criticism down, but when I push back on it I notice people mean very different things by it, and I suspect some of the readings are trivially true (and therefore do no work at all), some are substantive and maybe defensible, and some are just plain false. Before I write this up I want the phrase actually pinned down rather than vibes, because right now the debate is everyone talking past each other.

So here's the ask: take "he was a man of his time" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making as a numbered menu. I'll pre-seed some hunches — there seems to be a spread from something like "everyone's moral views are shaped by their era" (but that's true of the abolitionists too, so how could it excuse anyone specifically?), through epistemic claims about whether he could realistically have known better given what was circulating at the time, comparative "better than average for his century" grading, something about blame being pointless when the target is dead, and maybe a fully relativist reading where right and wrong are indexed to the era itself. For each reading tell me: is it plausible, and — the key thing — does it actually support what people deploying the phrase want, which is that the criticism is misplaced or should stop? Then flag which reading is doing the real work when someone uses it on, say, a letters page defending Churchill's name staying on something, because my hunch is its a bait and switch between the trivial version and a stronger one, and I'd like to be able to name the switch in print.

Keep it usable offline — no bibliography, no reading list, my fact-checker handles sourcing later. Each reading should get a short label I can reuse in the article. And at the very end, on its own line, I need a fixed machine-readable verdict: \answer{N} where N is the number of the reading that, in your judgement, is the one actually doing the work in real-world deployments of the phrase.
