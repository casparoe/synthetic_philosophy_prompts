I run an undergrad seminar on existential risk and longtermism, around 15 students, one 90-minute session. Next week's topic is the hinge of history hypothesis — the idea that we might be living at the most influential moment in history, where the choices we make now echo through basically the entrie future. I want an in-class activity where they run into the pull of that idea and the reasons to distrust it at the same time, without me naming anything up front.

Here's the mechanism I've been kicking around — improve it or replace it wholesale if you've got something better. Split the class into "centuries": say five groups, each one a later generation. Every century gets a fixed pot of points (welfare, flourishing, whatever you want to call it) and a menu of choices: consume the points now, invest them in institutions or safety tech that carries forward and compunds, or spend them on values lock-in that constrains what later centuries are even allowed to choose. Later centuries just play with whatever world the earlier ones left them. What I want is for the early groups to viscerally feel their leverage, and for the late groups to viscerally feel locked in — and then, in the debrief, for someone to notice the uncomfortable thing: the early centuries felt special only because they were early, not because anything about their era was actually special. That seems like exactly the trap at the heart of the hinge of history debate (Bostrom-style urgency about our moment vs. MacAskill-style skepticism that maybe every generation thinks this), and I want the game to manufacture that confusion honestly rather than me just asserting it.

So give me: full rules, a payoff scheme (no real money, points are fine), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes including how long each century deliberates, and whatever materials I can prep the night before with a printer and some index cards. Then a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the real distinctions — being early vs. being at a genuine hinge, whether influence over the future compounds or decays as time goes on, the time of perils idea — matching each moment in the game to the standard argument it's a stand-in for, but without pretending to exact citations.

Two more things. First, predict what will go wrong the first time I run it. My guess is that century one makes some reckless or just dumb choice and everyone downstream is stuck with it, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a bug or the entire point — tell me which, and if it's a bug, how to fix it without sanding off the lesson. Second, be honest about which part of the actual hinge of history debate this game cannot reproduce no matter how I tune the rules, the part I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. My suspicion is it's something like the observation-selection worry — the future centuries that got locked out of existence never show up to complain, and a classroom game can't make the absent players felt — but I'd like your take on whether that's right or whether there's a worse gap I'm not seeing.
