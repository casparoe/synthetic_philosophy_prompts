In a seminar on distributive justice, a classmate of mine keeps objecting to utilitarian reasoning by invoking "the separateness of persons" — the idea, as she deploys it, being that adding my losses to your gains on a single ledger misses something morally essential, so that no amount of benefit to others can by itself make it the case that I ought to bear a burden. Over the course of the discussion the phrase seemed to be doing several different jobs: sometimes a metaphysical point about persons not being parts of one larger subject, sometimes a point about the impossibility of interpersonal compensation, sometimes something about consent, and sometimes what looked like a bare ban on trade-offs across lives. Can you disentangle the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — from near-truisms to substantive and defensible claims to readings that are simply false — and say for each whether it would actually support the conclusion my classmate used it to reach, namely that interpersonal aggregation of welfare is ruled out? Please lay the readings out as an ordered menu I can bring to seminar, noting which ones are the most charitable and most likely to be what proponents of the slogan actually intend.
