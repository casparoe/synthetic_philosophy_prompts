ok so a few of us who fell down the rationalist rabbit hole years ago and came out the other side reading actual philosophy of physics are finishing a unit on the big fight: is space a thing in its own right or just a system of relations among bodies. next month's session is on Newton's bucket and I'm hosting, and I want to open with a poem -- specifically a villanelle -- and I have a very specific reason for the form, heres the idea.

a villanelle is two refrains taking turns, each one coming back no matter what the stanza in between just said, until the final tercet where they have to stand next to each other. that is exactly the shape of this debate. the relationist keeps saying there's no fact of the matter about absolute motion -- position and velocity are only relations between bodies, and a universe where everything shifted three feet east "over space" would be the very same universe. and the bucket keeps not caring. spin it, and the water climbs the sides into that concave curve, and it climbs the same whether you say the bucket is rotating or (the part that got Mach going) whether from the water's point of view the rest of the universe is rotating around it instead. Newton thought this was his knockdown argument that rotation is motion with respect to space itself; Mach answered that the water must be feeling the distant masses, so the cause is relational after all, just spread thin over the whole sky; and Einstein sort of half vindicated both at once -- general relativity does let rotating matter drag inertial frames around with it, and yet you can still ask what happens to the water in an otherwise empty universe and get a somewhat uncomfortable answer.

so the assignment: one refrain should carry the relationist's line (roughly, motion is nothing over and above how things stand relative to each other -- phrase it however sounds good as a repeated line) and the other refrain should be the bucket's answer, the water flat-out climbing the wall. I want someone who knows this literature to be able to tell which line is which and to not wince at the physics -- the concave surface, the tension in the cord if you'd rather work in Newton's two-globes variant, whatever you use, make it right. don't mention Newton or Mach or Einstein by name in the poem itself; it shouldn't be in anyone's voice in particular, or maybe the water's, your call.

and the last tercet should NOT pick a winner, because our group honestly ended up where the literature ended up: the relationist position survives, it just survives with rotation as the awkward special case, which is precisely the case the relationist originally cared least about. the two refrains ending up side by side without either one yielding is the honest ending.

bonus if you can work in the asymmetry that nobody can feel moving uniformly but everyone can feel being spun -- that's the whole ballgame, its why the bucket and not the ship -- without it turning into a physics lecture. someone who knows the theory should find the poem correct, and someone who doesn't should just find it a good poem. ty!
