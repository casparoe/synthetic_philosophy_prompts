i've basically accepted the beef-over-chicken argument and I keep making it to friends: per calorie, chicken is way worse for animals, because you're talking about 200+ broiler lives per cow-equivalent of meat, broiler conditions are about as bad as it gets, and beef cattle at least spend a chunk of their lives outdoors. so switching from chicken to beef (holding total meat constant) should reduce suffering a lot, and "swap your chicken for beef" might be the most effective animal advocacy you can do in one sentence.

but lately i worry the argument quietly assumes crop cultivation and wild animal suffering just don't count, or count for almost nothing. the obvious problem is feed conversion: beef needs several times more feed per calorie than chicken, so per burger you're driving a lot more soy and corn cultivation, and if tilling and harvesting kills a meaningful number of field animals, maybe chicken wins once you add those in. the obvious comeback is that a lot of cattle feed is forage on land that would be grass anyway and humans can't eat it, so the relevant number is tilled-crop feed per calorie -- but honestly i have no idea how that shakes out, and there's a further layer about whether pasture is better or worse for wild animals (insects, etc) than cropland in the first place.

so tell me what would actually count against the beef-over-chicken position. i want the full map of its exposure: which empirical facts, if they came out one way, would sink it outright; which would just soften it; which commitments a defender should concede as real vulnerabilities vs dismiss. and be honest about how much of this is even knowable -- is the position hostage to numbers nobody has (field deaths per hectare, the moral weight of a mouse vs a broiler chicken), and if so, is that fatal to making food choices this way, or is expected-value-with-wide-error-bars just the correct response anyway?
