Our school's philosophy society has spent the past year arguing about whether pleasure is one thing, and our faculty advisor has suggested we try an adversarial collaboration. One faction — the common-quality party — holds that all pleasures share a single felt quality of pleasantness: the pleasure of cold water after a long run, the pleasure of finally understanding a proof, and the pleasure of an evening with friends are alike in the way every shade of red is alike, by instantiating one and the same detectable quality. The other faction — the diversity party — holds that these states have nothing phenomenologically in common: they are as unlike as the activities that produce them, and the word "pleasant" merely marks that the person favours the state at the time, so whatever unifies pleasure is an attitude, not a shared sensation. Please draft the joint statement our two parties should be able to sign. It should contain: a numbered section of claims both parties accept, including about which examples started our dispute, about what introspection can and cannot settle here, and about which forms of the view that pleasure is the only thing good in itself live or die with this question; a table of our remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes, that is, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us; and, for each crux, a test, comparison case, or thought experiment both parties agree bears on it, with each party's prediction written down in advance. One crux we suspect matters: since we cannot feel two very different pleasures simultaneously, and memory of felt quality is unreliable, it may be impossible in principle to tell whether the supposed common quality is absent or merely undetectable — please record both parties' predictions about what follows from this. Finally, if you judge that any part of our dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why.
