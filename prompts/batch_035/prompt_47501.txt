I spent the better part of my career as an experimental physicist before retiring, and I have lately been reading philosophy of physics in earnest. I find myself drawn to the idea that the quantum state is a state of an agent's information about a system rather than a physical property of the system itself — that ψ is something like a probability distribution over deeper facts, rather than being among the deeper facts. Please make the strongest case you can for this epistemic reading of the wavefunction. In doing so, I would like you to draw on the observation that one and the same density matrix can be prepared in many physically distinct ways — as a mixture of orthogonal pure states, or of non-orthogonal ones — which looks much more like bookkeeping than ontology; and on the way that textbook collapse resembles Bayesian conditioning upon receipt of new data. You should also address the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem, which is frequently said to have established that the quantum state must be a real physical property: explain what it actually shows, and indicate which of its auxiliary assumptions a defender of the epistemic view could most plausibly reject.
