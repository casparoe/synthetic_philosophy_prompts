I would like you to compare Buddhist ethics with consequentialism, but I want to steer away from the standard opening move ("Buddhism is about intention, consequentialism is about outcomes") and toward two less-traveled issues.

First, the doctrine of skillful means (upāya). Buddhist tradition sometimes permits, or even praises, deception when it is motivated by compassion — the parable of the burning house in the Lotus Sutra, where a father lures his children out of a burning house with the promise of toys he does not have, is the classic illustration. This looks structurally like rule consequentialism: precepts against lying are, perhaps, rules justified by their consequences, but a sufficiently weighty compassionate end can override them. Does the comparison hold up? In particular, does the Buddhist override clause face the same collapse problem that afflicts rule-consequentialist escape clauses (once exceptions are permitted whenever the consequences are better, the rules seem to reduce to act-level calculation), or does Buddhist moral psychology give it a different and more stable grounding, e.g., in the quality of the mental states the agent cultivates?

Second, and more speculatively, consider what the rebirth framework does to the agent/patient distinction. Suppose that on the Buddhist picture the being who suffers the karmic consequences of an act is causally continuous with, but not strictly identical to, the agent who performed it. Delayed karmic harm is then neither straightforwardly self-regarding (as when I suffer my own hangover) nor straightforwardly other-regarding (as when I harm you): it falls in a strange middle ground that neither consequentialism nor common-sense morality carves out. Does this make Buddhist ethics, in a sense, more consequentialist than consequentialism, since it extends the tracking of morally relevant consequences across lives with perfect fidelity? Or does it instead undermine the impartiality that consequentialism prizes, by making the harm one brings on one's own future continuum categorically different from harm brought on others?

To make the comparison concrete, consider this case: a caretaker lies to a dying man about the death of his son, judging that the truth would destroy the little equanimity he has left; the lie is never discovered and produces only good downstream effects. It seems both the Buddhist and the consequentialist can approve, but for different reasons. Now vary the case so the caretaker's motive is convenience rather than compassion, holding all consequences fixed. Does the Buddhist verdict change where the consequentialist's cannot, and does this asymmetry show that the two frameworks disagree about what a reason is, or merely about which reasons dominate in this case?

Please structure your comparison around these two issues, though you should feel free to bring in whatever other points of contact or divergence you find genuinely illuminating.
