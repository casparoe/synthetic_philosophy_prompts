I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. My route into this question was sideways: I train machine-learning models for a living, and I keep arguing with a colleague about whether the internal features a trained network builds — things nobody explicitly designed — are about their inputs in any interesting sense, or merely correlated with them. Someone told me the nearest thing to a serious literature here is teleosemantics, the project of explaining how brain states get their aboutness using nothing but facts about natural selection and function. Rather than dive straight into the individual theories, I'd like to do what I do before choosing an implementation at work: write the spec first. Please write the desiderata — what any adequate naturalized theory of mental content has to deliver, what phenomena it has to explain, which edge cases it has to survive, what distinctions it has to preserve — with the motivating case or consideration stated alongside each requirement.

Here are the things I already suspect belong on the list, so you can tell me whether I've got them right, which are prejudices rather than requirements, and what I've left off:

- Error without malfunction. The classic case is a frog snapping at a small dark dot moving across its visual field when most such dots in the frog's environment are not flies. The theory must let the very same internal state count as being about flies while most of its tokens are false, and it must do this without treating the frog as defective — the mechanism is doing exactly what it was built to do. To me this is the core acceptance test: if a theory can't pass it, it isn't a theory of content at all, just a theory of covariance.
- Content without history. Suppose something molecule-for-molecule identical to a healthy organism came into existence by sheer accident — the usual story involves a lightning strike in a swamp. Intuitively its internal states mean what its twin's mean and guide behavior identically; a theory that says they mean nothing seems to have missed something, yet a theory that says they mean something appears to have abandoned selection history, which was supposed to be doing all the work. I want to know whether this case is a genuine requirement or a philosopher's edge case a theory is allowed to fail.
- Repurposed mechanisms. A mechanism selected for one job and later co-opted for another — the way feathers were, if I remember correctly, initially selected for insulation and only later put to use for flight. If a detection mechanism was selected to track one narrow class of things and is now used for a broader class, is its content fixed at selection, updated at repurposing, or somehow spread across the transition? It seems to me a good theory should handle mid-lineage refactors without an implausible discontinuity, but I can also see an argument that the selection facts simply underdetermine the answer here.
- Learned content. The models I train acquire their features over the course of a single training run, not through anything resembling species-level selection. If individual learning can fix content, the theory needs a second, non-evolutionary mechanism for doing so; if it can't, the theory is implausibly restricted, since plenty of my own beliefs have content traceable to my own learning rather than to my genes. Should the desiderata demand a unified treatment of innate and acquired content, or is a two-part story acceptable?

Beyond the list itself, please flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. My own guess is that error-without-malfunction sits in tension with restricting the theory to selection-history facts, since the selected function of the frog's snap is presumably something like secure-small-dark-moving-things — a description under which the frog is never wrong. Tell me whether that tension is real or an artifact of how I've set the problem up, and identify which single requirement does the most work to eliminate candidate theories. Close with a brief verdict — a few sentences each — on how the three or four main approaches in this literature (I've encountered the names Dretske, Millikan, Neander, and Papineau) fare against the list, but don't go deeper than that on any particular theory. The criteria are where I want the real thinking; the scorecard is just a sanity check.
