I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in ethics and the history of Hellenistic and Buddhist thought, and adjudicate a dispute for me. Please think carefully and rigorously; it may help to begin by briefly brainstorming the dialectical space before committing to verdicts.

Two friends of mine, Elena and Marcus, both philosophy-literate amateurs, have been arguing for weeks about what Elena calls tranquilism: the view that the only thing that ultimately matters in a life is the absence of craving and suffering, and that everything we call positive value is either instrumental to securing that absence or an outright confusion. The position obviously has echoes of Epicurus, of the Buddhist diagnosis of craving as the root of suffering, and of Schopenhauer, but I want you to evaluate the argument as actually made, not its historical pedigree.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went as follows.

(1) Elena opened with a phenomenological claim: every pleasure is the removal of a lack. Eating relieves hunger, achievement relieves self-doubt and restlessness, music relieves boredom and tension — so there is nothing to value beyond the restoration of tranquility, and a life full of intense satisfactions is really just a life full of prior discomforts that needed canceling. The best life is one that doesn't run up these debts in the first place.

(2) Marcus replied that this is empirically revisionary: the felt joy of watching your child take her first steps, or of finally understanding a proof, does not present itself as relief from anything — it presents itself as gaining something. He accused Elena of a false dichotomy: she assumes every state is either the presence of an agitation or the removal of one, when most positive states seem to be neither.

(3) Elena responded that the gain Marcus describes is anticipatory: the joy of achievement is largely the release of tension, plus the prospect of no longer having to crave this particular thing. She then made a move she claimed strengthened her position: tranquilism is not crude negative hedonism. The target is the absence of craving, not merely the absence of suffering, and the two can come apart — someone can be free of suffering yet saturated with craving, and that is a defect in them even if they are never frustrated. Conversely, someone can have desires without craving, enjoy things without needing them, and that is the tranquil ideal, not a blank sedation.

(4) Marcus then offered a reductio. If nothing but the absence of craving and suffering matters, then non-existence is perfect: a nonexistent person has neither. So tranquilism seems to imply that never being born is the best outcome, or at least that no existence can be better than non-existence — and Elena, he noted, was sounding uncomfortably close to a wholesale rejection of the value of having children, of ambitious projects, of any of the striving that gives lives their shape. Elena replied, invoking in paraphrase the old Epicurean point that death is nothing to us, that comparisons with non-existence are category mistakes: non-existence is not a state anyone is in, so it cannot be better or worse for anyone. Within the class of lives, the tranquil sage's life is good. Marcus pressed: she cannot have it both ways — if the only value is the absence of craving and suffering, and non-existence contains both absences, then either tranquilism ranks non-existence at the top or it is secretly relying on some positive good, like engagement or awareness, that its official theory denies exists.

(5) Elena closed with a thought experiment meant to make the position attractive rather than merely austere. Imagine two pills. Pill A permanently dissolves all craving while leaving you awake, perceptive, and capable of enjoying things you no longer need; Pill B leaves your cravings intact and intensifies both them and their satisfactions. She claimed any clear-eyed person prefers Pill A, and that the very fact that Pill B sounds hollow — a life of escalating itches and escalating scratches — shows tranquilism captures our considered judgments better than Marcus lets on. Marcus said Pill A describes someone serenely drifting through the world without caring about anything, and that caring is itself part of what he values — love is worth its exposure to grief, ambition worth its risk of failure, and a life inoculated against both has lost something, not gained something. Elena answered that this conflates the objects of craving with craving itself: love without neediness, commitment without desperation, are precisely what Pill A preserves. What it removes is only the pathology.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered moves (including Elena's replies within them), say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and justify each rating. Then tell me who is more right overall; identify the strongest point on each side that the other side never adequately answered; and say whether you think there is a principled middle position between them, or whether the disagreement ultimately bottoms out in a clash of basic intuitions that argument cannot reach.
