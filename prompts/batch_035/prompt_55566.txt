My friend has walked me into a corner with the absent-minded driver and I need help finding the exit. In case you don't have the details memorized (I think it's originally Piccione and Rubinstein from the 90s, but honestly the attribution doesn't matter, here's the whole setup): a driver is heading home along a road with two identical intersections. If he exits at the first one he ends up in a terrible neighborhood, payoff 0. If he continues at the first he reaches the second; exiting there gets him home, payoff 4; continuing there puts him on a loop back to where he started, payoff 1. The catch: he's so absent-minded that when he's AT an intersection, he cannot remember whether he's already passed one. He can, however, follow a plan, and he can flip a coin.

His arguement, numbered: (1) Rational choice at a moment is fixed by the agent's information at that moment — if you can't distinguish two situations, the rational thing is the same in both. (2) The driver can't distinguish the intersections, so his choice is the same at both: it's really one decision, made once, not two. (3) If it's one decision, then when he settles on "continue with probability p," that single settling covers both intersections. There's no second roll of the dice — the deliberation he's doing now is the only deliberation there is, so whatever randomness he builds in gets drawn exactly once. (4) So under continue-probability p, with probability p he continues at the first AND at the second (payoff 1), and with probability 1-p he exits at the first (payoff 0): expected payoff p. (5) So the best he can rationally do is continue for sure, for a payoff of 1. All the standard advice about randomizing — continue with probability 2/3 for an expected 4/3 — is incoherent, since 4/3 requires independent draws at the two intersections, which requires two decisions, which contradicts (2). (6) Generalizing: randomization can only help you if you can re-run it per occasion, and you can only re-run per occasion if you can tell the occasions apart. So no absent-minded agent can ever gain from randomizing.

I want to keep (1), and (2) looks fine to me. My instinct is that the slide is somewhere in (3)-(4) — "one decision" quietly becoming "one draw" — but I can't make it precise, and he has a comeback ready: if the dice get re-rolled at the second intersection, who re-rolls them, given that there's only one decision? He also has a bonus point for when I try to diagnose it: the driver's own probability of being at the first rather than the second intersection depends on the continue-probability he chooses, so the action-time calculation is self-referential and decision theory is broken here anyway. And (6) smells wrong to me independently: I can picture a player with total amnesia who has to play matching pennies every round, situations completely indistinguishable each time, and randomizing fresh each round seems obviously right and obviously available to them. If his argument were sound it would convict that player too, which is why I think it proves too much — I just can't say exactly where it goes wrong.
