Novelist here. My next book is about two physicists in a decades-long rivalry, and while taking notes on how real scientists talk about their theories I've noticed my own gut reactions are all over the place. I want to pin down the principle behind them, because right now I can't tell if my gut is consistent or just mood. (A friend who reads philosophy of science muttered something about "degenerating research programmes" at me, but I don't want jargon, I want the actual rule.)

My verdicts. A theory whose failed prediction gets patched, where the patch then gets independently confirmed and the theory goes on to predict something new that comes true: good science, clearly. Astronomers inventing an unseen planet to explain Uranus's wobbly orbit: I'm fine with that — and here's the thing, I'm fine with it even though the same guy later pulled the same move to explain Mercury (the planet that never turned up). I can't bring myself to say whether inventing a planet was good science depends on how the dice landed. Darwin, who mostly organized facts everybody already knew rather than making risky predictions: great science anyway, even though by my other standards he'd flunk. A friend of mine in nutrition research who adds a new fudge factor everytime a prediction misses: eye-roll, that's not science anymore. And I notice I'll accept "give us five years, the theory is young" from a five-year-old theory when I'd never take it from a fifty-year-old one.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? Tell me what it says about a case I haven't mentioned, and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save. And if you think there's just no principle in there and my gut is incoherent, say that plainly instead of building something clever to flatter me.
