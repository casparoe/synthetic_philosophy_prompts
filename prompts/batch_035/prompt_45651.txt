I spent eleven years as a market maker / prop trader, mostly options and stat arb, so my native vocabulary is stuff like static vs dynamic replication, convexity, arbitrage bounds, quote sizes, adverse selection, marking to market, the idea that prices aren't beliefs until someone stands behind them with size. I've tried to read the philosophy literature on Dutch book arguments a couple times and honestly bounced off the framing, but from what I can gather the rough claims are: (1) if your degrees of belief don't satisfy the probability axioms, a bookie can construct a set of bets you'll accept where you lose money in every possible world, so you're irrational; (2) there's a further argument that if you don't update by conditionalization, a similar thing happens across time -- the bookie gets you at t0 and again at t1, and you're guaranteed to lose overall; (3) some people claim this "proves" you should be a Bayesian, others reply that the whole thing is rigged because it assumes you'll take any bet at your stated odds, or that willingness to bet at all shows your credences, or that the bookie somehow knows your quotes and you don't know theirs, etc.

Restate that whole debate -- both the static and the time-dependent versions -- in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. I want it in terms of things like: what it means for me to publish two-sided quotes, when a set of quotes admits static arbitrage against me, what a locking portfolio is, the difference between being arbitraged once and being systematically arbitraged, what dynamic replication does and doesn't guarantee, and whether "guaranteed loss" is even the right scare-word for someone whose whole career is about managing distributions of P&L rather than worst cases. Translate the philosophers' objections too -- the ones about betting willingness, about the bookie's knowledge, about whether the diachronic argument is even the same kind of argument as the synchronic one.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report.
