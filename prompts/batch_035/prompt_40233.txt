In my metaphysics teaching and in a couple of papers I've defended a particular account of causation by omission, and I have begun to wonder how much exposure it actually has. The position has three components, and I'll state them as precisely as I can.

First, absences are genuine causes, but not in the way events are: strictly speaking, no absence is a relatum of the fundamental causal relation. When we say the gardener's failure to water killed the flowers, what is fundamentally the case is a pair of facts about situations: in the actual situation the flowers die, and in the nearest situation in which the gardener waters, they survive — with that second fact underwritten by ordinary positive causal processes running in the contrast situation (water reaching the roots, uptake, turgor maintained in the cells). Causation by omission thus has a two-step structure: genuine positive causation in the contrast scenario, plus an actual-world difference-making fact. Second, the account is irreducibly contrastive: "the gardener's failure caused the death" is elliptical until a comparison class is fixed, and that is why the Queen of England's failure to water the flowers does not count as a cause of their death, even though a similarity-based counterfactual runs through her as well. Third — and this is where I have grown uneasy — the comparison class is constrained by normality: by what is supposed to happen, by who bears which responsibility. I have told myself this constraint is benign, a special case of the general context-sensitivity of causal explanation rather than a smuggling of the normative into the causal facts themselves.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure: not a survey of the literature, but the defeaters, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward. You should not feel bound by my list, but here are the pressure points that trouble me most. (i) Double prevention: the hostage survives because the bodyguard deflects the shot, and it is easy to construct chains in which the relevant preventing is itself accomplished by an omission; such cases seem to demand a contrast scenario that itself contains causation by omission, which looks regressive on my two-step analysis, and I have never found my own replies to this fully satisfying. (ii) The collapse worry: since I analyze the causal contribution of an absence in terms of difference-making relative to a contrast, what stops the analysis from generalizing to all causation, event-causation included — in which case the event/absence asymmetry asserted by my first component evaporates, and with it much of what made the view seem worth defending rather than a notational variant of a familiar counterfactual account. (iii) The normativity worry: if the normality constraint is doing the real work in excluding the Queen, have I thereby conceded that absences, strictly, cause nothing, and that what is true in the neighborhood is only that we explain through them — with the causal facts hostage to facts about what ought to happen? (iv) The idle-metaphysics worry: my claim that no absence is ever a relatum of the fundamental relation looks insulated by construction, since absences have no location of their own, only shadow locations borrowed from the events that would have occurred; I would like to know what, if anything, could in principle show this wrong.

For each defeater, tell me which a committed defender of the position would concede as genuinely counting against it, and which she would dismiss, and say where you think I am most likely deceiving myself about the view's resilience. Finally, give me your honest assessment of whether the position has, over the years of qualifications and epicycles, been insulated into something that no observation or argument could threaten — and whether, for a metaphysical claim of this kind, such insulation is a defect or simply the price of the genre.
