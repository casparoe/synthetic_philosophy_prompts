Build me a thought experiment where Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge has to deny that knowledge is closed under known entailment, but I want it done without the usual textbook crutches, because I'm testing whether the failure of closure is a deep structural fact about sensitivity or just an artifact of how the famous examples are set up.

Quick setup so we're on the same page (tell me if you'd frame it differently, but I dont think it matters much for the task): on the tracking view you know p iff you believe p, p is true, and (sensitivity) in the nearest worlds where p is false you no longer believe p, and (adherence) in the nearest p-worlds you believe p the same way. Closure fails when you know p and know that p entails q, but fail the sensitivity test for q -- standardly because the nearest not-q worlds aren't the nearest not-p worlds, so tracking p's truth doesn't help you track q.

The standard demonstrations all lean on one of two crutches. Either there's a concealment structure (the grandma whose family hides bad news from her, so in the nearest worlds where her grandson is dead she still believes he's fine), or the conclusion q is a disjunction you never actually entertained (the thermometer reads hot, therefore it's hot or the thermometer's broken). I want neither.

Specs:

1. Ordinary setting -- a workplace, a kitchen, a commute, a hobby. No demons, no brains in vats, no fake barn country, no lotteries, no conspiracies of any kind. Nothing weird has to have happened for the case to run.

2. The subject must genuinely know p, and must come to believe q by consciously deducing it from p, on the spot, in at most two or three steps that a normally attentive person could perform. They can't have noticed q independently, and they can't be sloppy about the deduction.

3. The subject's failure of sensitivity with respect to q has to come from something mundane about how the belief in q is held -- habit, distraction, overconfidence in a routine -- rather than from a skeptical hypothesis or someone lying to them.

4. This is the hard part: q must not be a disjunction, and it must be a substantive fact about the ordinary world, not a "p or else things are weird" gerrymander. The not-q worlds that defeat sensitivity have to be ordinary worlds that are close to actuality for an ordinary reason.

After you present the scenario, do the check: state the belief, the method, the nearest not-p worlds, the nearest not-q worlds, and show exactly why tracking grants knowledge of p but withholds knowledge of q. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and stress-test it: if a tracking theorist replies that your subject just formed the belief in q sloppily and that the same sloppiness would undermine the belief in p on that same method, does the case survive? Also, one honest note on whether Nozick's own patch -- counting the deduction itself as a new belief-forming method so that q can inherit p's tracking -- actually rescues closure here or just re-describes the failure. And if you find that spec 4 simply can't be met without smuggling the grandma structure back in under a different description, say so and explain why that keeps happening -- I'd take that as an answer too, and honestly a pretty interesting one.
