I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a piece on what a completed neuroscience might still leave out. The spine of the piece is a contrast between two cases. The first is Frank Jackson's Mary: the scientist who knows every physical fact about color vision, stated in the vocabulary of a completed physics and neuroscience, but who has never had a color experience because she has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room; the question is what happens on the day she walks out and sees red for the first time. The second case is real: clinical trials are under way for gene therapy in achromatopsia, the congenital condition in which people lack functioning cone cells and see no color, and some patients — mostly young children — are acquiring color vision for the first time. When I recently interviewed a neuroscientist working on one of these therapies, he put his confidence at about nineteen to one that Mary gains nothing on leaving the room that is not already fixed in her wiring; in his telling, the seeing just is the wiring doing its work, and the therapy patients are Mary with an address. My own gut number is lower, maybe a coin flip, but I would rather print a reasoned figure than a gut one.

So I am asking you for a number I can defend to my editor. State your credence in the following precisely formulated proposition: that Mary, on her first experience of red, acquires a piece of knowledge-that she did not previously possess — where knowledge-that is meant to exclude two things, the mere gain of an ability such as recognizing, imagining, or remembering red, and bare acquaintance that never rises to a belief or a claim. Then show me the derivation rather than the conclusion alone. At minimum, decompose into: the probability that the phenomenal character of experience is nonphysical; the probability that it is physical but that some physical facts are accessible to a person only through having the experience, never through description; the probability that the two exclusions above can be made to do clean work rather than smuggling the verdict in by definition, since if knowledge-that is defined so that only the physical descriptions count, physicalism wins by fiat; and any further terms you think the decomposition needs, named and justified by you. Give each term a number with one line of reasoning, show how they combine into your headline figure, and tell me which term that figure is most sensitive to. After that, two more things. First, given that the neuroscientist sits at roughly 0.95 that nothing new is learned, identify which term in the decomposition he is most plausibly voting against — is he denying the possibility of experience-only access, disputing that anything counts as knowledge-that, or disputing the taxonomy itself? Second, tell me what the therapy patients would have to say, or conspicuously fail to say, about their first experiences of color that should move my number in one direction or the other, since I have access to their reported reactions and intend to close the column with them. I plan to print the decomposition itself, under my byline, so please do not give me a paragraph about how it all depends on one's theory of consciousness; I need a figure, its parts, and the weak joint marked.
