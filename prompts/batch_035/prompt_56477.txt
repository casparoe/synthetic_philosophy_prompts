so I've been reading LW and the alignment-adjacent blogosphere since like 2014, and one of the ideas that actually stuck with me is what people there call the strategy-stealing assumption -- the hope that if an AI system gives its operators a decisive strategic advantage, that advantage is "stealable" in the sense that a more careful, more altruistic coalition could have taken the same play and gotten the same benefits. The assumption basically underwrites a lot of moderate optimism: you don't need to solve all of philosophy of alignment up front if the good guys can just copy whatever the winning strategy turns out to be. It fails when a capability is asymmetric -- when it helps whoever gets it first in a way that can't just be copied, because it burns the commons, or because its value is positional (only worth something while others lack it), or because it depends on a resource base only one side has.

Here's my situation. I'm not at a frontier lab, I'm at a mid-sized shop doing capability evals -- the kind of place that ships models to enterprise customers and does safety reviews that are... let's say advisory more than binding, though lately we've gotten enough regulator attention that our sign-offs matter more than they used to. My actual job, the boring part, is going through capability reports from the training teams every week and deciding what needs deeper evals before release. Each report is a model-card-style writeup: what the model can now do, rough benchmarks, internal anecdotes. I get maybe an hour per capability. I've realized that the question I keep circling -- "is this dangerous" -- is way too vague to be answerable in an hour, but "is this stealable, and if not why not" is actually the question I care about, and it's more tractable. If a capability mostly just creates knowledge anyone can copy, that's a very different situation than one whose benefits are locked to whoever deploys it first.

So: please turn the strategy-stealing idea into an actual decision procedure I can run on one of these capability reports in an hour. What I want is:

- A set of questions to ask in order, where each one either passes the capability along, kills it (or rather, escalates it), or splits it into a "stealable part" and an "unstealable part" that get different treatment. It should be written so a colleague with no rationalist-adjacent background can run it without me explaining what "positional" means, so bake the concepts into the questions themselves.
- One thing I keep chewing on: I suspect stealability isn't one thing. There's at least a difference between capabilities whose benefit is copyable knowledge (someone writes it up, anyone uses it), capabilities whose benefit is positional (first mover takes the prize, second mover gets nothing), and capabilities whose benefit presupposes a resource base (you can only benefit if you already have the compute / data / personnel / legal cover that one side has and the other doesn't). If you think that triage is right, build the procedure around it; if you think it's wrong or needs different cuts, say so and use yours instead. Don't just take my framing because I handed it to you.
- The points where the procedure has to give up and hand me a judgment call, marked honestly, with a note on what kind of judgment it is (empirical guess vs values call) and who at the org should be making it rather than me.
- Where the procedure will misfire -- the cases where it says "stealable, ship it" and that's the wrong answer anyway, and vice versa. I specifically worry about capabilities that look like plain knowledge production but whose value degrades as more people have the knowledge, if that makes sense -- that feels like it quietly breaks the knowledge/positional split.
- Then run it on three capabilities from recent reports, as worked examples: (1) a model that's gotten weirdly good at novel protein design, good enough that our pharma customers are excited and our bio people are uneasy; (2) a model that predicts an individual human's responses and next actions disturbingly well after ingesting a few thousand of their messages, which a big customer wants for a consumer mental-wellness product; (3) a change to our serving infra that lets you spin up thousands of cheap parallel research copies of the model -- the model itself isn't smarter, it's just that quantity now has a quality all its own, and our biggest customer has far more compute than any academic or nonprofit coalition ever will. For at least one of these the procedure should give an answer I'm going to hate, because I already know which product exec will be in my office about it -- tell me which one and why, don't soften it.

If some step needs a number I won't have in an hour (like "how much does this capability's value decay once published"), tell me what to do when the number is unavailable rather than pretending the step is runnable. And keep it honest about the biggest weakness of the whole approach: that "would the good guys have gotten the same benefit" smuggles in a claim about who the good guys are, and my org is not exactly a neutral referee on that question.
