I write novels — the quiet kind, where the big things happen offstage between chapters — and I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole that I suspect is my problem and not my characters'. A friend reccomended some stuff on causation by omission, the debate about whether an absence can literally be a cause. Their stock example seems to be the gardener who doesn't water the plants and they die: did the not-watering cause the death, and how does something that didn't even happen do any causing? And there's apparently a worry about counterfactuals going haywire, since if the gardener hadn't not-watered, maybe it would've rained, or the neighbor would've done it — so why does the gardener's absence get to be THE cause while all the other non-waterers and near-misses don't?

Here's the thing: my entire craft is built out of exactly this material. The Sherlock trick where you solve the case by noticing what the dog did not do in the nighttime. Chekhov's gun run in reverse, a gun established early precisely so its never firing means something. Whole chapters that only work because a character does not make a phone call. My editor will say "nothing happens in this scene" when structurally the scene is where a decision not to act happens — the omission is the beat.

So: restate the main positions in the philosophers' debate (whatever camps there are — the ones who say absences are real causes, the ones who say it's all disguised talk about present events, the ones who punt to something about defaults or norms, and anything else that's actually load-bearing) in the vocabulary I work in: setups and payoffs, on-page versus off-page, negative space, subtext, implied backstory, the beat sheet. Do it as faithfully as you can, not as a cute analogy.

Then the residue report, which is what I really want: which parts of their debate map cleanly onto stuff I do, which parts I'd have to distort my vocabulary to fit, and which parts have no expression at all in narrative terms — nothing I could even gesture at from inside the workshop. And then your verdict: is the residue there because narrative causation genuinely is causation by omission half the time and we've been doing it fine without the metaphysics, or because the philosophers are worried about something we can't see from where we sit? I ask for a practical reason: the novel I'm drafting now pivots entirely on a letter that never arrives, and I want to know whether their worry — the one about an absence having a thousand ways of not happening, so the counterfactuals come apart — is a problem I need to solve in how I structure the plot, or whether readers' brains just handle this in a way the philosophers' puzzle never touches. If you think it's the second, explain what the reader's brain is doing that their analysis isn't.
