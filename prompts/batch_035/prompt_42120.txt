My friend and I spent most of last Saturday evening on the old question of why there is something rather than nothing, and we ended exactly where we began, which now strikes me as the more interesting puzzle. To make the stall concrete: I kept pushing the explanatory demand — if the universe is contingent, its existence contrasts with a genuine alternative, the empty world, and where a real contrast exists there is at least a prima facie question why one side of it is actual. He had three counters, each a familiar move in the literature. First, the deflationary one: every explanation operates within reality, citing laws, states, or probabilities, so an explanation of reality as a whole would have to cite something, which makes the demand incoherent rather than merely unmet. Second, a probabilistic argument in the spirit of van Inwagen's, meant to show that among the possible worlds there is exactly one in which nothing exists and infinitely many in which something does, so the empty world has probability zero and there is no residual fact to explain. Third, the Grünbaum-style complaint that the question smuggles in a default: treating nothing as the natural condition and something as the deviation reverses the burden of proof, and no principle of parsimony ever said that a hypothesis must also explain why there is a world for it to be a hypothesis about.

The stall itself came from something narrower, though. At some point my friend claimed that the empty world is simpler and so should be expected absent a reason, and I accused him of treating nothing as though it were a competing hypothesis with a higher prior — as though "there is nothing" were a theory doing less explanatory work, when really it is the absence of any theory, with nothing to be simple. He replied that the empty world is still a way things could have been, and that modal status alone is enough to make it an alternative that needs weighting; I answered that a way things could have been is not automatically an outcome that could obtain under some probability distribution, so his prior talk was unearned on both sides of the ledger. We also went a round on the selection effect: I argued that the question can only ever be asked in worlds where something exists, so nothing could never have been asked about, and he responded that this makes the question look worse rather than dissolved, since it shows the explanatory demand can never be silenced from the inside. By the end I noticed we weren't disagreeing about cosmology, or even really about possibility, but I couldn't say what we were disagreeing about, and he flatly denied that our dispute was merely verbal — which is my working hypothesis, offered as nothing more than that.

So here is what I want from you, and I want it as a diagnosis rather than a verdict on the question itself. Taking the positions as I've described them — the contrastive explanatory demand on one side, and the incoherence, probability-zero, and no-default responses on the other — tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: a hidden substantive question about the modal status of the empty world, a clash over when a demand for explanation is legitimate, two questions (one about explanation, one about metaphysical priority) being run under a single name, or something else entirely. Ground the diagnosis in what the moves actually presuppose rather than in labels for them, and say explicitly which, if either, side is begging a question the other side can legitimately refuse to grant. Then tell me honestly whether anything could count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction between explanation and contrastive explanation, a worked-out measure over possible worlds that settles whether the empty world can carry probability at all, an argument that one side is answering a question nobody asked — or whether this is a dispute whose perfect stability is itself the thing to be explained, and why. Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter to a philosophically literate friend: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
