So my first question is how to parse this. Is it a single conditional whose antecedent is a giant conjunction of virtues (each necessary, the whole set jointly sufficient for manhood), or a series of separate conditionals each individually sufficient ("If you can dream—and not make dreams your master ... you'll be a Man")? Second, and here is where the course material enters: Lewis's triviality results are standardly taken to motivate the Adams-style view that indicative conditionals are not truth-apt — that their assertibility is governed by the conditional probability of consequent given antecedent, rather than by the truth value of some compound proposition. Does the poem behave like an Adams conditional or like a truth-apt assertion? My instinct is that nobody reads it as a prediction ("given these traits, the probability that you'll be a Man is high"); it reads instead as a claim of sufficiency — meet these conditions and you will, by that very fact, have become a man. Is that a semantic or analytic connection between antecedent and consequent, in which case the probabilistic apparatus may simply be the wrong tool? Or is there still something to say about what the poem presupposes about the probability of the antecedent, given that the speaker never asserts the addressee will fulfill any of the conditions?

Third, the final stanza bothers me in an interesting way. The last antecedent is "If you can fill the unforgiving minute / With sixty seconds' worth of distance run", and the consequent then splits into two parts joined by "And—which is more—": "Yours is the Earth and everything that's in it" and "you'll be a Man, my son!" If the second is more than the first, as "which is more" signals, what does that ranking tell us about whether the poem's conditionals are descriptive, normative, or in some sense performative — a father conferring a status rather than forecasting one?
