I teach an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology and am writing the take-home final. Please draft one essay question on Jeffrey conditionalization and uncertain evidence, together with a model answer of roughly 650 words and a marking scheme worth 25 marks.

The question must be built around the following scenario, which you should state in the question itself: Mara has credence 0.5 that it will rain overnight. Her barometer, which she is 70% confident is working properly, reads rain; taking this as uncertain evidence, she Jeffrey-conditions on the partition {rain, no rain}, moving her rain credence to 0.7. The next evening she is again 70% confident the barometer is working, and it again reads rain. Applying Jeffrey's rule a second time leaves her credence exactly where it was: once she has adopted 0.7 on that partition, a second update with the same input is vacuous.

The question should press students on whether this idempotence is a virtue or a defect. It seems right that identical evidence should not be double-counted; yet a second, apparently independent confirmation from the same source arguably should push her above 0.7, and Hartry Field's reparameterization of Jeffrey's rule — under which the effect of an update depends on how antecedently probable the evidence proposition was — was in part motivated by the thought that Jeffrey conditioning is too conservative in just this kind of case. Students should have to say what a "count it twice" policy commits them to, whether the right diagnosis turns on how the two evenings' inputs are individuated, and what each verdict implies for Jeffrey's radical probabilism: the idea that there is no privileged class of certain evidence and that any input the agent can rate with a probability is admissible. A student who merely recites the statement of Jeffrey's rule and re-derives the 0.7 should not be able to pass.

The marking scheme should contain four or five must-hit points with marks assigned, a one-line note on the standard way students get each wrong, and a paragraph separating an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves only the A answer makes.

Because the question forces a verdict on a contested matter, close the marking scheme with a short note (about 100 words) on which of your must-hit points you are least confident in: which one rests on a contestable reading of Field's motivation or of what radical probabilism actually requires, and what a reasonable examiner could say to show that it should be weighted differently or dropped. Answers adopting the compounding policy should not lose marks if they defend it well; reflect that in the scheme.
