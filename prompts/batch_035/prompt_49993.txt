I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of science seminar on theories of causation, and we've just moved from counterfactual theories to interventionism. There's an asymmetry between the two theory-types that I keep stumbling over, and I'd like it explained carefully.

Here is my understanding of the setup. On the simple counterfactual theory, c counts as a cause of e when, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. This has notorious trouble with preemption-style cases: if the first rock shatters the bottle, and the backup thrower would have thrown only had the first thrower held back, then in the nearest world where the first rock isn't thrown the bottle still shatters, and the actual cause comes out as no cause at all. The interventionist fix, as I understand it, is to replace the "what would have happened otherwise" test with a manipulation test: c is a cause of e when an intervention that changes c — where the intervention severs c from its other causes and forbids backtracking — would change e. Because interventions hold the backup thrower's behavior fixed rather than letting it covary, the bottle breaks exactly when the first rock is thrown, and the preemption case comes out right. So far, so good.

What I cannot see is why the same machinery doesn't fail in symmetric overdetermination: two marksmen fire simultaneously, either bullet alone would be fatal, both hit. Intervene on marksman A's shot — severing it from its causes, holding B's shot fixed — and the victim dies anyway. So neither shot individually makes a difference under intervention, exactly as neither does under the plain counterfactual test. Yet the intuition that both shots are causes is at least as firm as the intuition the interventionist vindicated in the preemption case. So, three questions: first, is it accurate that interventionism handles late preemption but not symmetric overdetermination, or have I misdescribed what an intervention is doing somewhere along the way? Second, what, if anything, does the interventionist have to say about the overdetermination case — I have heard it suggested that the conjunction of the two shots can count as a cause even though neither conjunct does, and I would like to know whether that response works or merely relocates the problem to the question of why the conjunction is a cause when its parts are not. Third, and more generally, does this tell us something about the relationship between the two theories — that the interventionist advantage over counterfactual dependence is real but local, or that difference-making under intervention was never going to capture actual causation on its own? Please be precise about the role of "holding fixed" in each case, since I suspect my confusion is located there.
