At a student union meeting last week, a motion to withdraw a speaking invitation from a controversial pundit was defended almost entirely with a single sentence, delivered as if it settled the matter: "a tolerant society must be intolerant of intolerance." The pundit in question holds views many members find bigoted, but nobody claimed he incites violence or heads a movement — the argument was that the views themselves, expressed on our platform, are the intolerance the sentence licenses us to refuse. I gather the line is a popular compression of a passage in Karl Popper's The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945), where Popper writes of intolerant movements that they refuse rational argument and "teach them to answer arguments by the use of their fists or pistols" — but the sentence as it was used in our meeting has come loose from that context, and I want to know what it actually claims before I decide how to have voted.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be expressing, ordered from weakest to strongest. I'd like the menu to include at least: (i) the truism that no principle is exceptionless, or that tolerating a view does not oblige me to host, fund, or applaud it; (ii) a reciprocity reading, on which tolerance is owed only to those who practice it in return; (iii) an ecological or empirical reading, on which tolerant majorities are reliably outcompeted or destroyed by intolerant ones unless they actively suppress them; (iv) something close to Popper's own scope, restricted to movements that have abandoned argument and begun organizing for force; (v) the Rawlsian fallback, on which the intolerant are to be tolerated up until they concretely endanger the institutions of liberty, and constrained only then; and (vi) whatever stronger reading would license refusing a platform to a nonviolent, unorganized speaker purely for holding offensive views. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or too indeterminate to do any work.

Then answer the question the meeting actually turned on: which of these readings, if any, delivers the conclusion the sentence was used to support — that withdrawing the invitation was a matter of principle rather than taste? Please be explicit about where the real action is. My own suspicion is that it sits in the question of who classifies a view as "intolerance" and by what procedure: on some readings the sentence hands whoever already holds power a standing license to suppress whatever they choose to label intolerant, which makes the slogan a tool of exactly the thing it warns against. If some readings collapse under self-application — intolerance of intolerance looking, to its target, indistinguishable from intolerance — say so and say which readings survive it. Close with a verdict: which reading did my fellow student most plausibly intend, and did it support the motion?
