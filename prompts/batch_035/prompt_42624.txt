I've been reading the evolution-of-cooperation literature lately — Axelrod, tit-for-tat, shadow of the future, all that — and I want to stress-test it against a case I suspect doesn't fit neatly: the Cod Wars. Quick facts in case they're fuzzy: three rounds (late 50s, early 70s, mid 70s) of a dispute between Iceland and the UK over fishing limits. Iceland kept unilateraly extending its exclusive fishing zone — 4 miles, then 12, then 50, then 200 — and UK trawlers kept fishing inside the new lines with Royal Navy vessels riding escort (officially "fishery protection," since the British weren't calling it a war). The Icelandic coast guard's signature move was cutting trawlers' warps, and Iceland's trump card was threatening to close the NATO base at Keflavík, which eventually got the US to lean on London until the UK folded and accepted the 200-mile zone in 1976.

So here's the ask: analyze the whole thing as an iterated prisoner's dilemma. Who are the players, what are the moves, what are the payoffs, and over what time horizon — including how the "shadow of the future" actually operated round by round. But be honest about where the mapping breaks, because I suspect it breaks fast. Iceland's "defections" (sailing out to cut warps) look more like punishment of defection than defection itself; the payoffs were set as much by third parties — NATO, the Americans, and the cod stocks themselves — as by the two players on paper; and mutual overfishing was closer to a commons collapse than a mutual-defection equilibrium. Is this even a PD, or is it closer to chicken, or a common-pool resource game, or some ugly hybrid? Don't force it into one box if it isn't.

And then the part I actually care about: what does the episode say about the evolution-of-cooperation story, where cooperation is supposed to emerge from repeated interaction and the long shadow of the future? The "cooperation" here — the 1976 settlement and really the whole modern 200-mile regime — seems to have been imposed from the outside, by a player with leverage that had nothing to do with fish, rather than evolved out of iteration between the fishermen themselves. Does that still count as cooperation evolving, or is it evidence that once the stakes are big enough and asymmetric enough you just need an enforcer, and the folk-theorem story is beside the point? I'd also like your take on whether Iceland's side of it changes the analysis — a tiny state with essentially one industry, for whom the game was existential, playing against a great power for whom it was a rounding error. Does the PD framework even have anything to say about bargaining positions that lopsided, or does it quietly assume the payoffs are symmetric enough that the players are interchangeable?
