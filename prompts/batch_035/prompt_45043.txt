I run a small HCI lab that studies presence in VR — the feeling of "being there" — and I'm trying to redo how we analyze our interview data, and I want to steal a method from philosophy of mind that seems oddly applicable: Dennett's heterophenomenology. The idea as I understand it: you treat what a subject says about their experience like a text, maybe like a novel, and you first construct the world the text describes — the subject's heterophenomenological world — without prejudging whether it's a faithful report of inner events, and only then decide, at the theory level, what in that world corresponds to anything real. The reports are data, but they're data about what the person is inclined to say, not a window directly onto consciousness.

What I want from you is a step-by-step analysis procedure that my two grad students can actually run on a stack of interview transcripts, in an afternoon each, without any philosophy background. Something like: what you do with the transcript first, when you're allowed to start interpreting, how you separate "what the subject said" from "what we think caused them to say it," how you record both without letting one contaminate the other, and how you handle the moment where you have to choose between interpretations. Be explicit about where the procedure forces you to write down a judgment call as a judgment call rather than laundering it as a coding decision, because our reviewers keep asking us exactly that.

Then run the procedure on two hard cases from our actual data (I'm paraphrasing):

1. A subject who gives an extremely vivid report — "it felt like the floor dropped away, I was physically standing on the platform, my stomach dropped" — but whose behavioral data (postural sway, task performance, break-off latency) shows basically nothing, nothing different from a control condition.
2. The reverse: a subject who insists the whole thing did nothing for her, "it was just a screen on my face, honestly I don't get the hype," but who spent forty minutes in a session budgeted for fifteen and refused to skip the last environment, and whose heart rate tracked the designed drops.

Also tell me where this method breaks. My own worry: Dennett's approach is famously deflationary, and my co-investigator thinks if we follow it strictly we end up explaining away presence instead of studying it — like the subject's own testimony about what happened to them gets downgraded from the thing we're studying to just another output to be explained. Is there a version of the procedure where that line is drawn differently, and where does the procedure have to just hand the decision to the analyst? And if there's a case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like (e.g., "your vivid report subject is not a presence case at all, reclassify her"), I want that flagged rather than softened. Keep it practical — I'm not trying to write a philosophy paper, I'm trying to stop my lab from doing naive introspection-report averaging and calling it science.
