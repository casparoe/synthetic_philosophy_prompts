here's my situation. part of my job is blurbing other people's books, and part of it is reading what critics say about mine. and I've noticed something odd about what I do and don't take from other people's aesthetic judgments. when my editor tells me a chapter is beautiful, I believe her instantly — I'll even tell people "apparently that chapter's the good one" — but I don't *feel* the chapter as beautiful until I go back and see it myself, and sometimes I go back and just don't. meanwhile when a friend tells me her co-worker was treated unfairly, I seem to get the moral fact just fine secondhand. I'm mad on her behalf, I act on it, no felt gap. so deference works in one domain and feels hollow in the other, and I gather philosophers have noticed this — the aesthetic testimony problem, and the roughly parallel worry about moral deference.

what I want is a comparison of those two ideas: the problem of aesthetic testimony and the problem of moral testimony. where do the puzzles actually run parallel, where do they genuinely come apart, and what does the comparison do to the candidate explanations? like, the obvious move is "aesthetic judgment needs acquaintance, moral judgment doesn't" — but is that even true, since plenty of moral cases need you to have seen the situation too? and the "you can use testimony to find the value but not to appreciate it" line — does the moral side have an analogue, or does it break down there? I want you to figure out whether the asymmetry is in the kind of fact, the kind of knowing, or the kind of thing we do with it once we know it.

and here's the part I actually care about. I have my own half-baked theory and I want you to test it against both, as a third thing in the comparison. I call it the rehearsal theory (in my notes it's got a worse name, don't ask): appreciation is a bit like a small performance you put on for yourself — you have to do something with the object, dwell in it, and the pleasure and the warrant are both in the doing. testimony hands you the review of a show you never gave. moral knowledge is different because it's not a performance at all, it's more like a verdict — you can inherit a verdict, you can't inherit a rehearsal. so my theory predicts the gap I feel. but I want you to tell me honestly whether it predicts *too much* — because there are cases where borrowed aesthetic judgment seems fine, like when I take a friend's word that some painter is worth the museum trip, and I don't feel any fraudulence about that, I just go. if my theory can't distinguish the blurbs-that-work from the blurbs-that-don't, it's junk and you should say so. don't be polite about it, be polite about my novels if you must, not my theory.
