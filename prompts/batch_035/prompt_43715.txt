I'm writing a dissertation chapter on Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal, and in particular on the received diagnosis that the paradox is an artifact of meddlesome preferences — rankings over what happens inside someone else's protected sphere — so that a social choice rule could keep both the Pareto principle and minimal liberalism if only those preferences were laundered out of the aggregation, the move usually connected to Dworkin's distinction between personal and external preferences. I suspect the diagnosis is wrong, because the theorem never consults the moral valence of a preference, only its direction. What I need is a thought experiment that makes that vivid.

Construct a case in which the Sen cycle is generated entirely by benevolent, other-regarding preferences — worry, deference, protectiveness — with no nosiness, contempt, or sadism anywhere in either person's ranking. Requirements:

1. Two agents, an ordinary setting (neighbors, colleagues, housemates; no exotica), with each agent decisive over at least one pair of outcomes differing only within their own protected sphere, so that minimal liberalism applies naturally. State explicitly what plays the role of the social choice rule — a shared norm, a pact, a house policy, a third party's ruling — so the social ranking is not a philosopher's abstraction.

2. Keep the outcome space finite and small, four outcomes if possible, and give each agent's complete preference ordering as a table. Then verify the cycle step by step: which edge of the cycle comes from which agent's decisiveness over their own sphere, which edges come from Pareto, and how the edges close. Every edge must fall out of the stated rankings; do not stipulate any verdict. For each preference in the table, note whether a hostile reader could read it as covertly meddlesome, and defend or amend it if so. If you conclude that pure benevolence cannot close the cycle, say so plainly, identify exactly which kind of self-regard you had to admit and how much, and build the minimal version that works.

Once the case works, put it through the game-form reformulation of rights — the move, usually credited to Gärdenfors and Sugden, of recasting rights as protections over each person's choice of action rather than over final outcomes. Specify the strategies, find the Nash equilibria of the resulting game, and tell me whether the pathology is dissolved or has merely migrated from social choice theory into game theory. My suspicion is that in a case like this the game has a Pareto-dominated equilibrium, so the paradox comes back wearing different formal clothes, but check rather than assume.

Finally, the audit: which single feature of the case is doing the work (my guess is that each agent's ranking within their own sphere is conditional on what happens in the other's, so the spheres interlock, but verify this against your construction), what happens to the cycle if that feature is deleted, and how a defender of outcome-based rights — someone who thinks a right protects the enjoyment of a thing, not merely the act of choosing it — would attack your case. I want to present this at a seminar without it being dismantled in the first ten minutes.
