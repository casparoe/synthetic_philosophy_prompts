I'm a third-year undergrad taking a comparative politics course on democratic erosion (we did a unit on Hungary and Poland, and the seminar paper topic is the US), and I also put out a weekly two-page digest for my university's politics society called "is this backsliding?" — me and one other student go through the week's news, pick four to six items, and label each one backsliding / not backsliding / gray area, with a couple sentences of justification. It's gotten contentious. Last term my co-editor labeled a judicial overhaul in one country as backsliding and half the society said that's just ordinary politics and the label was partisan; someone else got grief for NOT labeling something an incumbent did to the electoral commission. Our lecturer keeps using the phrase executive aggrandizement and the distinction between formal and informal channels of erosion, but that doesn't help us on a Thursday night with a deadline.

So: write me a decision procedure the two of us can actually run in about fifteen minutes per news item. It should (a) give a fixed sequence of questions, each with what counts as a sufficient answer; (b) have explicit stop points — where an item gets a clear not-backsliding, a clear backsliding, or goes into a call-a-meeting bucket because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts; (c) be built on the real conceptual distinctions from the literature — tilting the playing field versus ordinary policy disagreement, legal/formal changes versus informal capture (appointments, pressure, selective enforcement), unilateral action versus changes passed through the legislature with opposition input, reversibility, and who the change disadvantages going into the next election — but usable by someone with zero background reading; and (d) adress the partisan-label worry head on: some test for whether our label is tracking the action or just tracking who did it, like asking whether we'd label the same action the same way if the other side did it — and be honest about where that test breaks down.

Then run the procedure on three items so we can see it work: (1) a governing party that legally has appointment powers replaces the members of the electoral commission with loyalists a year before an election; (2) an opposition-leaning constitutional court strikes down a batch of the government's flagship laws, and the government responds by openly declining to comply and daring the court to enforce it; (3) a government lowers the voting age to sixteen, which the polls suggest would help it electorally. And include at least one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'm broadly in favor of expanding the franchise, so if the procedure puts item 3 in the gray-area bucket rather than the clean one, say that plainly instead of softening it.

Finally, tell us what the procedure cannot do: which cases are genuinely judgment calls no checklist can settle, and where our own politics will leak into the labels no matter what safeguards we build in.
