I serve as secretary of a residents' association — forty households, none of us academics — and last month the chair asked me to work out how we should choose among three proposed redesigns of our shared courtyard. My plan was the obvious one: each household submits a ranking of the three designs, we combine the ballots into a single association ranking, and the top-ranked design gets built. While reading up on voting methods, I came across a theorem of the economist Kenneth Arrow from the 1950s, and it has left me with an argument I can neither accept nor refute. I would be grateful for help finding where it goes wrong.

First, the theorem, as I understand it. When there are three or more options on the table, there is no method whatsoever for turning a set of individual ranked ballots into a single group ranking that satisfies all four of the following. (i) Universality: the method must deliver a group ranking whatever pattern of ballots is submitted. (ii) Non-dictatorship: there must be no single voter whose ranking automatically becomes the group's ranking no matter what anyone else submits. (iii) Unanimity: if every voter ranks one option above another, the group ranking must place the first above the second. (iv) Independence: the group's ranking of any two options must depend only on how voters rank those two options against each other, and not at all on where voters place any other option.

Now the argument.

(1) Whatever method we use to combine our ballots — the one I planned, or any other — must fail at least one of the four conditions. This is simply the theorem.

(2) Each of the four conditions is a minimal requirement of fairness. A method that fails universality breaks down on some ballots; one that fails non-dictatorship lets one member overrule the other thirty-nine; one that fails unanimity can place an option below another that every voter placed above it; one that fails independence lets the association's verdict between two designs depend on whether a third design nobody wants is also on the ballot.

(3) So whatever method we use, the resulting association ranking will be unfair in one of these ways.

(4) An unfair ranking has no authority. It does not bind the members, because it does not reflect the association's will — only an accident of the procedure or one person's will.

(5) Nothing in the argument turns on our association in particular. It applies to every body that settles matters by combining individual rankings of three or more options: our committee, the city council, parliament.

(6) Therefore no outcome of any such body has authority; each is no better than a procedure that openly ignores the members' views.

(7) And if no outcome has authority, honesty requires dropping the pretense. We should stop collecting rankings and simply draw the winning design out of a hat: a lottery at least treats the three designs equally and claims no legitimacy it lacks.

I cannot accept (7), nor the part of (6) about parliament — it seems obvious to me that our city council's decisions do bind us. Yet I cannot locate the faulty step; each one looks harmless to me in isolation. If I have to give something up, I would rather keep (1), since the theorem is a theorem, and I would prefer not to concede (2) either, since the four conditions still strike me as the bare minimum. Please tell me which step to reject, why that step looked safe, and what rejecting it costs.
