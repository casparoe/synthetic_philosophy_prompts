I'm taking an upper-level epistemology course, and we've just finished the unit on epistemic regress. My professor set up the dialectic in the usual way: the regress of justification appears to force a choice between foundationalism (some beliefs are self-warranting and can bear weight without support), coherentism (justification is mutual support among beliefs, looping rather than linear), and infinitism (every reason requires a further reason, without terminus). In presenting coherentism she leaned heavily on Neurath's boat — the image of sailors who must rebuild their ship while at sea, plank by plank, never able to pull into drydock and start over from the keel — and it clearly charmed most of the room.

Here's my problem. My study group is split on how much work that picture actually does. One friend says the analogy is doing all the arguing: the boat feels cozy, but the sailors can drift in circles forever, and nothing in the picture guarantees they are headed anywhere true rather than merely staying afloat. Another insists it straightforwardly refutes foundationalism, since a boat rebuilt in drydock is precisely what we cannot have — we are always already at sea with a stock of beliefs we did not choose and cannot suspend. And a third raised a point that has stuck with me: historically, every actual boat was first built on land, so perhaps the picture quietly concedes the foundationalist's claim about the origin of a belief system even if it is right about its maintenance. Our term essay asks whether Neurath's boat is a genuine argument for coherentism or merely an illustration of it, and I want to commit to a defensible line before the deadline.

So I'd like a real audit of the analogy. Please set out what corresponds to what — the sea, the planks, the drydock, the storm, the destination, the question of whether the crew is many or one — and then identify where the mapping breaks down. Which breakdowns matter for the epistemological debate, and which are incidental noise? In particular: can a modest foundationalist, one who wants only a keel rather than a full drydock, happily accept the boat picture, so that the image underdetermines the choice between the two views it is supposed to adjudicate? And does the picture even engage infinitism, or does it treat the demand that every reason have a reason as something the sailors can simply refuse? If you think there is a better picture — an analogy that would adjudicate rather than merely soothe — I would like to hear it, along with an honest account of where that one fails too.
