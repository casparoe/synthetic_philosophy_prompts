I run a small satirical philosophy blog — the readers are grad students and people who actually plowed through the Hong editions for fun, so the jokes need to earn their keep. You should write as a scholar of nineteenth-century Continental thought who also has a sideline writing corporate parody. And please think carefully and do some brainstorming before you commit to jokes: list 3 or 4 directions the piece could take, pick the best one, then write.

The piece: a parody of a corporate membership terms-of-service agreement issued by "The Public" — that is, the entity Kierkegaard goes on about in *The Present Age*: the great abstraction everyone belongs to and no one is, which exists only because the press gives it a body. The conceit is that The Public has decided to formalize itself with a customer agreement. Formatted as numbered clauses, roughly 700-800 words, deadpan legalese throughout — no winking at the reader.

The humor has to come from actually getting Kierkegaard right, so a specialist laughs instead of winceing. Some of the material I'd love to see worked in (you don't need every single one):

- Membership terms: you cannot sign up, cannot resign, and the agreement cannot be predicated of any individual member. Yet everyone is enrolled at birth.
- A liability clause establishing that nothing The Public says is anyone's responsibility, since no individual ever said it. All opinions are held "on behalf of The Public."
- The press as the corporate body / official organ: clauses governing subscriptions, where the subscribers are in effect the thing they are reading.
- Envy set up as a renewable energy source or internal currency — leveling described in the language of "community building" or "equity initiatives" where the equity is horizontal: nobody gets to be taller than anyone else.
- A "passion clause" prohibiting passionate commitment, dramatic gestures, and leaps, with grandfathered exceptions for genuinely revolutionary ages (which, per the agreement, expired).
- The Public's phantom status: it has opinions, appetites, and curiosity (it shows up for executions) but no body, no address, and no one to serve papers on.
- Somewhere in the fine print, a quietly ominous clause about "that single individual" who attempts to exit, and what the agreement says happens to them — Kierkegaard's counter-ideal should surface only as a rejected liability or a banned activity.

Don't quote Kierkegaard verbatim anywhere — paraphrase in legalese instead. And keep it a parody of the agreement, not of Kierkegaard: he's the diagnostician, The Public is the client that wrote this thing.

After the piece, add a short note (150 words or so) doing two things: say how confident you are in the Kierkegaard content, and single out the point you're least sure of — for example whether hanging so much of the leveling story on envy vs. on the press itself is a fair reading, or whether the "single individual" material belongs more to the later religious writing than to *The Present Age*, and flag any place where the piece leans on a contested interpretation or takes a liberty for the sake of a joke.
