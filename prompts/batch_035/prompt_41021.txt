My cousin Elena has pain asymbolia — she feels pain, can rate its intensity like anyone else, and pulls her hand off a hot pan with perfectly normal speed, but the sensation simply doesn't bother her. She's been known to chat amiably with the phlebotomist while having a needle sunk into her arm, and once laughed through a wasp sting because, as she put it, the buzzing feeling in her thumb tickled her. At Thanksgiving this somehow detonated a two-hour war between my aunts, both retired philosophy professors, and I've been trying to reconstruct it for a family newsletter piece (long story). Aunt Rosa thinks pain's badness lives entirely in the phenomenal character of the sensation: if you have an experience with all the qualitative feel of pain, something intrinsically bad has happened to you, full stop. On her view Elena's case just means the sensory-discriminative component is intact while some downstream affective system got scrambled, which tells us nothing about what pain essentially is. Aunt Marge thinks this is absurd and leans the other way: pain's badness is not in the raw feel at all but in the aversion, the distress, the imperative aspect — on one view she likes (imperativism, I think it's called), pain's content is something like a command to stop what you're doing and get away, and Elena's system issues commands that never land anywhere.

Please write the dialogue between Rosa and Marge. Real back-and-forth — each aunt should respond to the other's actual points, not deliver prepared monologues. Some moves I remember and want included: Marge pressing Rosa with the observation that Rosa apparently has to say Elena's dentist-drill sensation is exactly as bad for Elena as mine would be for me, which Marge finds bizzare; Rosa replying that Marge's view collapses pain's badness into mere dislike, so that a person who trained herself not to mind pain (a stoic, or one of those meditators who claims to observe pain without suffering) would thereby have nothing bad happening to them, which Rosa can't accept; the two of them circling the question of whether Elena's sensation even deserves the word pain anymore, since Marge's view might commit her to saying Elena has literally never been in pain, which Elena would presumably find hilarious; and a stretch where they argue about a hypothetical asymbolia pill you could take before a root canal, with Rosa asking whether the pill made you better off or merely suppressed your ability to notice a harm that was fully present. If it helps, morphine patients who report the pain is still there but that they don't mind it can come up too.

Constraints: neither aunt is a strawman, and neither one gets to win by talking past the other's strongest point. Elena herself should pipe up at least once, ideally with something that quietly unsettles both of them. End it with each aunt refining her position slightly rather than one converting — the family agreed afterwards that the argument got genuinely better over the course of the evening, and I'd like the dialogue to show that. Maybe 800-1200 words, conversational register, no lecture-y paragraphs.
