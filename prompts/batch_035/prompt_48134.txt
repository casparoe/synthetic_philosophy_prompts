I teach an intro epistemology section, and we just finished two weeks on dream skepticism. The students read Malcolm's old line that dream reports can't be reports of experience because there's no criterion for what someone experienced while asleep — everything we call the dream is fixed by what the sleeper says on waking — and then Dennett's comeback that dreams are real events of the brain but our access to them is backwards and retrospective: on his picture the dream is composed and implanted as a memory in real time during waking, or on one of his variants drafted beforehand, like a cassette slapped into the machine at wake-up so the sleeper "remembers" a dream that was never run through as an experience. The students got the slogans but I could tell half of them couldn't actually locate themselves in the debate — they'd say "yeah, dreams are experiences" while also agreeing that the dream is whatever the waking report makes it, without noticing the tension.

So I want a diagnostic quiz. Please write, as a professional philosopher of mind would, a "which view of dreaming do you actually hold?" instrument: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case the positions genuinely divide on, with two or three answer options each, and a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto at least these positions — the received/experiential view (dreams are experiences had during sleep, reported afterwards by memory), Malcolmian conceptual denial (the very idea of unverifiable dream experiences is confused; the dream just is the waking report embedded in its circumstances), Dennett's real-time composition view where the dream is authored at wake-up, and his pre-drafted variant where both a dream and a memory of it are prepared in advance — plus whatever further leaves you think the logical space forces, such as intermediate positions that keep dreams as experiences but deny the natural assignment of time-stamps to them, or purely methodological views on which the whole dispute is empirically underdetermined. The point is that students get sorted by what they say about cases, not by which famous name they like, so no question may just ask "do you agree with Malcolm?" or "are dreams experiences?" — that's the trap I fell into in class.

For the cases, I'd like you to draw on the thought experiments the debate actually runs on: a sleeper woken mid-REM who reports a dream whose events, had it really been experienced in real time, would have taken far longer than the interval between sleep onset and waking; lucid dreams where the dreamer seems to know she's dreaming and even to signal with pre-arranged eye movements; a dream with a beginning that covertly explains a noise that the dreamer couldn't have anticipated, like an alarm-clock klaxon built into the dream's plot; the possibility of a dream experienced but completely forgotten, or of a fabricated report that convinces the dreamer he had a dream he didn't; and whether "I am asleep" uttered while genuinely asleep could ever express knowledge. Include a couple of trick cases where the obvious answer doesn't map onto the position students think they hold. I also vaguely remember that Dennett's paper flirted with tying dreams to whatever psychophysiology predicts on waking — the idea being that the content is whatever would explain the sleeper's disposition to report it — but don't lean on my memory for details; just make sure no leaf requires distinguishing more Dennettian sub-positions than the cases can actually separate, and flag explicitly any pair of leaves your quiz cannot cleanly pull apart. End the scoring key with one or two sentences of honest diagnosis about which combinations of answers don't correspond to any stable view and why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored). Take a moment to brainstorm the logical space before writing the questions, and phrase everything so a student with no philosophy background beyond my course could answer it.
