For forty years I was a physical chemist — mostly spectroscopy, latterly simulation work — and since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind. The question of whether the brain is a computer strikes me as the one where my scientific training and my philosophical reading pull apart, so I want to do this the way I would have done it in the lab: fix my data first, then ask what law fits them.

My data are my own verdicts about which systems compute. I offer them as honestly as I can:

1. A pocket calculator summing two numbers: computes, unambiguously.
2. A slide rule multiplying two numbers: computes, I think, though nothing in it is discrete and no part of it represents a number rather than a logarithm of a number.
3. An analogue computer of the sort I used in graduate school — banks of operational amplifiers wired to integrate a differential equation, read off on an oscilloscope: computes, I feel quite sure, and this one matters to me.
4. The solar system: the planets obey the equations, and their configuration at any later time is exactly the solution, but I do not think it computes anything. It just moves.
5. The old line about a suitably contrived mapping under which an ordinary wall implements a word processor: does not compute, obviously. Description is not computation.
6. An ant's wandering trail which, under a generous formal description, implements a small finite automaton: does not compute.
7. The brain: I lean toward saying it computes. But I want that verdict to fall out of whatever principle captures the others, not to be smuggled in because I am fond of it.
8. A plant whose leaves track the sun across the sky, anticipating and correcting for the sun's apparent motion: I go back and forth on this one daily.

I can see at least one apparent inconsistency myself. The analogue computer is a continuous dynamical system whose evolution is described by differential equations; so, on the same footing, is the solar system. Why does one compute and the other merely happen? And case 5 suggests the difference cannot lie in the physical description being ours, since the calculator's description is ours too.

What I would like from you: a single principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically; its verdict on each of my eight cases; an explicit statement of which verdict it cannot accommodate if it cannot accommodate them all; and its predictions for cases I have not listed — say, the head-direction cells in a rat's brain, a protein folding to its native conformation, and a hurricane as against a numerical simulation of that same hurricane. Please also tell me which of my verdicts is most plausibly sentiment masquerading as data.

And one request about the framing itself: if you think my data are contaminated at the source — if there is no coherent principle because "computation" names several different concepts and my cases quietly mix them — say so plainly and tell me which cases belong to which concept. I would rather have my experimental design criticized than have you manufacture a unifying law to accommodate me. I am not looking for references; I am looking for the analysis.
