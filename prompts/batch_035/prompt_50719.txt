I chair the benefits policy committee at a mid-sized logistics company, about 900 employees, non-union. Our 401(k) participation has been stuck around 60%, concentrated among younger and lower-paid staff, and the CFO is pushing a package to fix it: auto-enrollment at a 6% default contribution with 1% annual escalation, a target-date default fund picked by age, and — this is the part that started the fight — the vendor's "engagement kit" for the annual enrollment email, which puts the opt-out link at the bottom in small print and adds a line about how much money in matching funds the average non-participant leaves on the table each year. One of our committee members, who teaches ethics courses at the community college on the side, says the whole package is manipulation dressed up as help: defaults don't give people reasons, they exploit inertia, and an employee whose "choice" is really just never getting around to opting out hasn't consented to anything in any meaningful sense. Another member counters that there's no neutral option to fall back on — the current default of zero contribution is itself a choice we made, just a lazy one, and as long as opting out stays cheap and easy the thing stays a nudge rather than a shove. I got tasked with writing up where the committee should land and I keep going back and forth, so I'd like you to draft the decision memo I can circulate before the next meeting.

It needs a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and it should actually engage the manipulation-vs-paternalism question rather than just citing participation statistics. In particular I want it to take seriously whether the autonomy of a choice made under a sticky default is damaged in a way that a choice made after a plainly persuasive email is not, since the ethics guy insists there's a real difference there (something about one route going through your reasoning and the other route going around it) and honestly I half agree with him even though I support the policy. Also worth addressing: whether the pieces of the package differ from each other morally — the default rate, the default fund, the small-print opt-out formatting, the "money on the table" line — or whether it's all just one thing and we're kidding ourselves carving it up. If your recommendation is approve with conditions, be concrete about what the conditions should be and why those conditions answer the objection rather than just papering over it.

Please write it as continuous prose only, no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables — the way an actual memo email reads — and use American English throughout. It should be something the dissenting member could read and grudgingly respect even if he still votes no, which is the standard I care about most here.
