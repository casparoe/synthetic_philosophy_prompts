Write an essay, of roughly 2,000 to 2,500 words, on whether the relation better-than, as it applies to the comparison of lives, is transitive. You must reach a definite conclusion and defend it; an essay that ends by declaring the question irresolvable will not do, and neither will anodyne remarks about how difficult the issue is.

The essay should be organised around spectrum arguments, which have the following shape. One constructs a sequence of lives, w₁ through wₙ, such that each life in the sequence appears no worse — indeed marginally better — than the one before it, while wₙ is plainly worse than w₁. The most familiar versions trade length of life against quality: the first life is very long but saturated with a low-grade unpleasantness, the last is very short but of excellent quality, and each intermediate step shortens the life slightly while improving it slightly. Since transitivity of better-than, together with the judgement that each step is an improvement, entails that the last is better than the first — which is exactly what seems incredible — something must give. Your first task is to state the argument as a numbered set of premises and then identify precisely which premise carries the argumentative weight, and which premise a reasonable person should reject.

In carrying this out, I want you to take seriously the most natural reply on behalf of transitivity: that the argument works only because better-than is context-sensitive, so that in the pairwise judgements the comparison class shifts from step to step, and the transitivity failure is an artefact of equivocation rather than a discovery about value. Your second task is to assess this reply carefully, and in particular to avoid the two standard ways of botching it: dismissing it as a verbal trick without argument, and accepting it as a verbal trick without noticing that it needs an account of what the context-invariant relation is, and of why that relation should be the one that matters for choice.

Third, consider a feature of these arguments that the literature tends to pass over: whether our judgements about the steps depend on how the spectrum is presented. It is one thing to be shown w₇ and w₈ in isolation and asked which is better; it is another to see the entire sequence laid out together, where the direction of travel is visible and one is effectively asked to endorse a long slide. Work out what it would mean for the argument if these two presentations pulled in different directions, and which of the two sorts of judgement, if either, deserves to be treated as the evidence about betterness. I am not presupposing an answer here; I want to see the issue handled, not mentioned.

Fourth, address the structural consequence usually pressed by defenders of transitivity: if better-than is not transitive, then for some option sets there may be no such thing as the best option — no maximal element — and practical choice among the options on a spectrum becomes unstable, or vulnerable to cycles in which each option is better than some other all the way around. Decide whether this is a decisive cost of giving up transitivity, a merely formal inconvenience, or in fact a faithful description of certain real choice situations; and say which.

I would accept a range of conclusions, among them: that the spectrum arguments fail and transitivity survives intact; that transitivity fails for comparisons of lives but holds elsewhere; that it fails only where the goods being traded off are of incommensurable kinds; or some position you construct yourself. What I will not accept is a verdict reached before the arguments have been given a fair run, or a verdict whose defence ignores the strongest case for the other side.

You may refer to positions and arguments by name or by description, but no bibliography is wanted and no citations should be invented; I will supply references myself. Write for a reader who knows some philosophy but has not encountered this debate, in a register that is serious without being pompous.
