I'm a science correspondent preparing a feature on the epistemology of string theory, and I keep hitting a wall trying to make the "no-alternatives" argument vivid for readers. As I understand it, the argument — associated with the philosopher Richard Dawid — is that a theory's being the only workable candidate physicists have found, despite many talented people trying, raises its probability of being empirically adequate, in roughly the way a failed search for additional suspects raises confidence in the prime suspect. My worry is that this analogy smuggles in a premise about the search itself: a detective knows roughly what counts as a suspect, whereas we arguably do not know what would even count as a serious alternative to string theory, since the standards for seriousness are set by the very community that works on it. Could you walk me through (a) how a defender of the no-alternatives argument would formalize the Bayesian step — what exactly does the failure to find alternatives count as evidence about, and through what prior? — and (b) whether my circularity objection can be met, for instance by distinguishing research areas where the search space is genuinely constrained by known facts from those where the absence of alternatives reflects little more than funding and fashion? I would also appreciate a short verdict on whether the argument transfers to the case I want to close the piece with: the multiverse "prediction" that the cosmological constant should be small but nonzero. Please pitch it so I can use it in the article: technically serious, but with the logic laid out in plain steps rather than formalism.
