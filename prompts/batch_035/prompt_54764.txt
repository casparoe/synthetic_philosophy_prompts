I am a novelist at work on a book set in Skokie, Illinois, in 1977 — the year a small neo-Nazi group, the National Socialist Party of America, announced that it would march through a village that was home to a significant number of Jewish residents, many of them survivors of the camps. As I understand the record, the village first obtained an injunction barring the marchers from wearing Nazi uniforms or displaying swastikas, and then passed three ordinances — one prohibiting military-style uniforms at demonstrations, one prohibiting the distribution of materials promoting hatred, and one requiring a $350,000 insurance bond before any demonstration could be held — while the ACLU, represented by a young Jewish civil-rights attorney named David Goldberger, took the Nazis' case up through the courts; the Supreme Court in June 1977 ordered that the appeal be heard immediately, the federal courts eventually struck the ordinances down, and the march through Skokie never took place, though the ACLU paid for its stand in resignations and cancelled memberships. I could invent my way past the legal history, but I don't want to. My novel is narrated by a survivor who runs a bakery on Oakton Street and whose son, to her quiet horror, has joined the ACLU's defense committee, and the book turns on a question I cannot settle by intuition alone: was the village's response — turning the machinery of law against the intolerant — an instance of what Popper warned against, or of what he prescribed?

I would like a disciplined analysis of the Skokie affair through the lens of Popper's paradox of tolerance, run in both directions. By the theory's lights: who counts as the intolerant party, what counts as suppression, and does the principle actually reach this case? The reason I press this is that Popper's own footnote, as I read it, says we should claim the right to suppress them "if necessary even by force; for it may easily turn out that they are not prepared to meet us on the level of rational argument, but begin by denouncing all argument; they may forbid their followers to listen to rational argument, because it is deceptive, and teach them to answer arguments by the use of their fists or pistols." The NSPA in 1977 had answered no argument with fists; it had applied for a permit. So one live possibility is that Popper's paradox, on its own terms, is silent about speech-only cases, and that everything since said in its name on the internet has been smuggled in from elsewhere. Another is Rawls's narrower license — that the liberties of the intolerant may be restricted only when the tolerant "sincerely and with reason" believe their own security and the institutions of liberty to be in danger — in which case Skokie becomes a test of whether a village of survivors could hold that belief reasonably rather than only traumatically. And I want to know whether the ordinances and injunction are categorically different, by the theory's own lights, from the community's non-legal refusals — the counter-demonstrations, the refusals to do business — or whether the theory cannot tell state suppression from social refusal at all.

Finally, tell me what the episode does to the theory, because the idea organizing my novel needs to be stress-tested. That idea is that tolerance is less a virtue than a treaty: a reciprocal arrangement one is bound to honor only toward those who honor it in return, so that withdrawing tolerance from the intolerant is not a paradox or a self-contradiction but simply the revocation of a standing offer. If that reading is right, the village board did something categorically different from what a government banning a merely disliked but tolerant sect would do. If it is wrong — if tolerance is a unilateral virtue that condemns the withdrawer regardless of the other party's conduct — then the paradox returns in full force, and the Skokie litigation becomes a much lonelier affair than either side admitted at the time. Give me your honest verdict, including a frank statement of where the theory refuses to speak: my ending depends on which of the three readings — Popper's, Rawls's, or the treaty view — best survives contact with this case.
