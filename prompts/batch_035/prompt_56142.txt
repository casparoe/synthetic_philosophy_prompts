I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and I've run into the same sentence twice in a week, doing what I suspect is very different work each time. At a departmental memorial for a colleague who died suddenly and, by any human accounting, pointlessly, the university chaplain closed with "God works in mysterious ways" — delivered, as far as I could tell, as an expression of grief and epistemic humility. Three days later, in our seminar on theodicy, a classmate used the same sentence to wave off Rowe's evidential argument: when pressed on the fawn's suffering in the forest, he said that if we can't discern a reason for it, that's just God working in mysterious ways — as though our inability to see the point were itself evidence that there is one. I have the sense that the chaplain's use and my classmate's use are not the same claim at all, and that my classmate's use quietly smuggles in a premise.

Please disentangle the precise claims the slogan could be making. Lay them out as a menu of readings, from the trivially true to the genuinely contentious, and for each reading tell me: (a) whether it is plausible on its own; (b) whether it would support the specific use my classmate put it to, namely blocking the inference from "we cannot see any justifying reason for this horror" to "there is probably no justifying reason"; and (c) whether, if the reading is strong enough to do that work, it also cuts against other things my classmate is confident about — for instance his confident claims about what God wills in history, or his reliance on natural theology. I'd like to see, among other readings, ones that treat the line as mere platitude about finite minds, ones that amount to an actual theodicy or a mere defense, and ones that amount to substantive skeptical theism about the range of goods and evils we might be ignorant of. Please also flag which readings are consistent with the way the chaplain used the sentence at the memorial, since I want to be fair to both speakers when I raise this at the next seminar.

One more constraint: keep it to something I can distribute as a two-page handout, so order the readings by how much philosophical weight they can bear, and end with a short paragraph stating which single reading my classmate would have to defend for his move to be legitimate, and what it costs him.
