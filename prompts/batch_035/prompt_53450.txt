Quick bit of background: I'm in an amateur history group that meets in the back of a pub, mostly retired people plus a few of us who just like arguing. Last week the topic was who to blame for the First World War, and two of our members got into it properly — not shouty, but neither would give an inch. I'm the one writing the group's notes this month, so I could genuinely use some adjudication rather than my usual muddling through.

It started when Dave brought up Fritz Fischer, the German historian who caused all that trouble back in the sixties by arguing that Imperial Germany deliberately started the war as a grab for world power. Dave's opening case: Fischer dug up the September Programme of 1914, where the chancellor's people set out plans to annex chunks of Belgium and France and organise Europe around Berlin just weeks into the war, and that sort of thing doesn't come from nowhere — it shows what they'd wanted all along. Add the famous war council meeting the Kaiser chaired in December 1912, where war against the Franco-Russian alliance was openly discussed, and the blank cheque Germany handed Austria-Hungary in July 1914 — unconditional backing whatever Vienna did — which is what turned a Balkan bust-up into a world war. So Germany carries the principal blame, and Dave reckons the people who wrote the war guilt clause into Versailles basically had the right idea, whatever their motives.

Eena rolled her eyes and said this is Fischer-worship decades out of date. The 1912 meeting didn't actually decide anything, and the September Programme was a wish-list drafted at the moment it looked like the war in the west had been won — a shopping list written after you think you've won the lottery isn't evidence you bought the ticket on purpose. As for the blank cheque, every great power has it's own dodgy documents if you go digging — Russian mobilisation plans, the French and their revanche, the British knowing perfectly well what their naval buildup meant to the Germans. She said the sleepwalkers view (the Christopher Clark book everyone quotes at each other) showed you can build a Germany-centric story or a shared-responsibility story from the same facts, and she knows which one she finds more honest. Also, the war guilt clause was written by the victors to justify reparations, which makes it a seperate argument, not evidence of anything.

Dave said fine, forget intentions, forget whether the Kaiser secretly wanted war since 1912 — the counterfactual still does the work. If any single government could have kept this a local conflict by behaving differently in July, it was Germany's: they could have reined in Vienna, and instead they pushed at every stage. You don't need to prove premeditation to blame someone, any more than you need to prove a driver meant to crash.

Elena's answer to that was the bit that left me genuinely unsure. She said even if you granted the whole causal case, blaming a nation is a category confusion. A nation doesn't have intentions; the Kaiser and a handful of ministers and generals did. The German public had about as much say over the blank cheque as she had over her last phone bill, so what does it even mean to say Germany was responsible? Either you blame the actual decision-makers and name them, or you say the whole thing came down to alliance systems and mobilisation timetables and everybody's imperial baggage — in which case blame dissolves and nobody's to blame, and she said she was quite fine with that.

Dave's last word was that this is convenient hand-waving. We happily hold nations responsible for things all the time — we expect national apologies, we think countries can owe debts for what earlier governments did — and if structural explanations always dissolve blame then no country is ever responsible for anything, which is absurd. And anyway, he said, the crowds in Berlin were reportedly euphoric in August 1914, it's not as if the public was dragged in kicking and screaming. At that point the chair called time and we all went up to the bar.

Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right about what — including where both of them are being sloppy — and then say who, if anyone, comes out more right overall? Please be upfront about how confident you are in each of your judgements, and single out the point you're least sure about. I also know this is a topic where the historiography has gone back and forth, so if part of their argument turns on a factual claim that historians genuinely dispute, please say that rather than picking a side and pretending it's settled.
