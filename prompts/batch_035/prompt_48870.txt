You are a philosopher of economics with expertise in both Keynes's Treatise on Probability and his later writings on radical uncertainty, and in the epistemology of forecasting institutions. Since 1996 the Bank of England has published its inflation forecast as a fan chart — a full probability distribution over future outcomes, with calibrated bands around a central path — and most major central banks have followed suit. Treasuries, meanwhile, embed central-bank or official forecasts in fiscal rules that mechanically trigger when the central projection of debt or growth crosses a threshold, and bank regulators require capital adequacy to be demonstrated against a small set of named, narrative stress scenarios fixed years in advance.

Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this bundle of practices. Does the fan chart presuppose a view of probability that Keynes's early, logical-relation account would underwrite, or does it fit a Bayesian or a frequentist view just as well — that is, is the practice underdetermined among rival theories of probability? Which parts of the bundle are compatible with Keynes's 1937 claim that about the big drivers of the economy "there is no scientific basis on which to form any calculable probability whatever. We simply do not know," and which parts quietly rule it out? Is the apparent tension between fan charts (uncertainty treated as measurable risk) and narrative stress tests (uncertainty implicitly conceded to be unquantifiable) a genuine inconsistency in the implicit theory, or can a single coherent view of uncertainty license both? Finally, say what in these institutions would have to change — and what, if anything, would remain usable of a forecast-triggered fiscal rule in particular — if radical uncertainty were taken as the default rather than the exception.

Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorm of candidate reconstructions before committing to one. State how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
