I'm a master's student in philosophy and public policy, applying for a three-month visiting fellowship at an AI-governance research institute. I have a half-formed idea that came out of a seminar disagreement I couldn't settle, and I'd like you to turn it into the proposal.

The idea: everyone in AI safety talks about the "alignment tax" — the performance gap between a model built under safety constraints (refusals, oversight, honest reporting) and the same model built without them — and then slides immediately into saying the tax makes aligned AI "uncompetitive". But competitiveness is comparative: your lab loses ground only if the tax you pay exceeds what your rivals pay, or exceeds your lead over them. So the real question may not be the size of the tax but the structure of the race it induces. My suspicion is that the race is not always the prisoner's dilemma it's usually assumed to be, where any lab that unilaterally "pays" for safety just hands the market to a faster, less scrupulous rival. In some parameter ranges it looks more like a stag hunt: mutual restraint is stable and profitable, and the danger comes only from a single defector. Which game you're in plausibly depends on where the tax sits relative to the first-mover advantage of scaling first with fewer scruples — and that distinction matters, because it splits the policy options into two families that are usually confused: shrink the tax (better oversight, cheaper interpretability) versus change the game (coordination agreements, liability rules, compute governance that raises the cost of being the unsafe first mover). I want the proposal to make the game-structure question precise enough to have an answer, without pretending I'll have empirical data on real labs.
