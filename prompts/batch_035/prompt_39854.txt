I'm teaching an intro political philosophy seminar (20 students, 75 minutes) and I want an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between the American Dream's "success is to be celebrated" norm and the Scandinavian Jantelagen / Australian tall poppy syndrome "success is to be quietly resented and cut down" norm. Not as a lecture topic -- I want them to run into the norm from the inside.

Here's roughly the shape I have in mind, but feel free to improve on it: some kind of multi-round game where students can either play it safe or "stand out" for a shot at bigger points, and where the other players can each pay a small cost to "cut down" a stander-outer -- enough cuts and the stander-outer loses the round's gains and the cutters split a modest pool. So standing out is collectively good (it grows the total pie somehow) but individually risky, and cutting is individually tempting but shrinks the pie. No real money, just points that I convert to something silly like candy or picking the break-time music.

A few things I want designed into it deliberately, and I'd like your take on how to do them well:

1. Success should sometimes come from a visible lottery and sometimes from visible effort/skill (maybe a trivial puzzle vs. a coin flip), so we can see in the data whether people cut down luck-success and effort-success differently. I suspect this matters -- part of me thinks the American Dream norm secretly depends on everyone believing success was earned, and tall poppy cutting might really be punishment for perceived unearned status rather than envy of success per se. Design the rounds so we can actually tell those apart.

2. Somewhere in the middle, some groups should find out that a couple of "stand outs" from earlier rounds were rigged by me from the start (confederates, or quietly boosted scores). I want to see whether the norm flips retroactively -- do they cut more, or do they feel like the whole game's legitimacy collapsed?

3. A debrief that maps specific game moments onto the real cultural norms: what in the game corresponds to the social enforcement part of Jantelagen (it's not a law, it's your neighbors), what corresponds to the American Dream's narrative function, and where the mapping is fake. I'm worried the game measures envy, not norm enforcement, and I'd like the debrief to at least name that distinction even if the game can't cleanly separate them.

So: full writeup. Rules, materials, exact point values (and the reasoning behind the numbers -- I want standing out to be a genuinely hard call, not obviously right or obviously stupid), a round-by-round timeline, a facilitator script for the rigged reveal, the debrief with the questions that actually land, and an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (socially, not just logistically -- I'm guessing at least one group figures out the confederates early and the whole thing goes meta).

One more ask: after you've got the design, tell me where you'd bet your own reasoning is weakest here. Like, which design choice are you least confident actually produces the effect I'm after, and what would you expect to see in the room if you're wrong -- and what specific outcome in the debrief data would make you say the payoff numbers need to be redone? I don't want a design I can't debug.
