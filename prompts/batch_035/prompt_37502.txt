i'm a PhD student in political theory, and my reading group had a proper argument last week about Weil's 1943 essay on abolishing political parties that nobody could settle. Maybe you can. The two people arguing, Maya and Tomas (both also grad students), went roughly like this. Maya said Weil's argument proves too much: her standard for when a collectivity is allowed to discipline its members' thinking — organized around a definite external object, limited in scope, no money changing hands — would condemn basically every modern institution that develops any esprit de corps, from research labs to the bar association, and Weil never explains why abolishing parties but sparing those is anything but arbitrary. Tomas said it proves too little instead: her whole mechanism is the party as a machine that hands members ready-made phrases and punishes them for stepping out of line, but in loosely organized systems like the American one, with primaries and weak whips, nobody is handed a line to toe — the formal mechanism she targets barely exists there, so the essay is really about the disciplined, cadre-style parties of interwar Europe. Maya came back that this misses that parties manufacture the discipline themselves: partisan identity does the work whips used to do, and voters now engage in exactly the motivated cognition Weil describes, so the mechanism survived, it just migrated from the machine to the membership. Tomas replied that if the problem has migrated to the identities, her remedy is useless — you can abolish organizations, you can't abolish the identities they leave behind — so the essay would have to become a critique of partisanship rather than of parties, which is a different and probably impossible project. Maya then made what I thought was her best move: a single-issue party, say a pensioners' party that contests elections on one issue only, seems to satisfy her criteria as stated — defined by its object, limited in scope — which suggests the criteria, not party-hood as such, do all the work, and the call to abolish *all* parties overshoots her own standard. Tomas's answer: the moment such a party contests an election it has to have a view on everything, since whoever wins governs everything — the electoral mechanism drags every party toward totality regardless of its platform — so the abolitionism doesn't overshoot, it's just tacitly aimed at the combination of parties with electoral competition, and Weil should have said so. Can you adjudicate this move by move? Say who's right about which specific point, where each of them slides from something Weil actually commits to to something they're reading into her, and whether there's a reading of the essay on which both the "proves too much" and the "proves too little" complaints are correct at once. And if Tomas's last move works, tell me whether it makes the essay better or worse as a piece of political philosophy.
