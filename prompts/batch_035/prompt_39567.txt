For the past month my brother-in-law, who does data work at an insurance company, and I have been arguing about when you're entitled to turn your ignorance into a probability. The rule we started from is the one everybody knows: if you have no reason to favor one outcome over another, treat them as equally likely. My problem is that I have firm gut verdicts about when this rule gives a real answer and when it gives nonsense, and I cannot find the pattern behind the verdicts.

Here they are. A coin I know nothing about: 50/50, obviously fine. A dartboard where a blindfolded person throws a dart and no direction is favored over any other: I'm happy to say the top half gets probability 1/2, even though there are infinitely many directions — the symmetry of the wheel seems to settle it. But then his factory example: a machine stamps out metal rods whose lengths come out somewhere between 1 and 2 meters, and that is absolutely everything we know; he says the probability a rod is shorter than 1.5 meters is 1/2, and I simply don't believe there is any fact of the matter there, whereas with the dartboard I feel sure there is, and I can't explain the difference. Next, the chord puzzle he showed me from a video: draw a chord of a circle at random and ask the chance it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Pick two random points on the rim and join them: 1/3. Pick a random radius and a random point along it, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius: 1/2. Pick the chord's midpoint uniformly from inside the disk: 1/4. All three feel like legitimate ways of being random, all three treat every direction the same, and my gut insists the first is somehow the truly random chord and the other two are cheating — but I cannot say what the first one has that the others lack. Then the cube version, which is the one that keeps me up at night: a machine makes cubes whose edges are somewhere between 1 and 2 centimeters. If edges are spread evenly, the chance an edge is under 1.5 cm is 1/2, which forces the chance the volume is under 3.375 cm³ to be 1/2 too; but volumes lie between 1 and 8, and if volumes are spread evenly the chance of coming in under 3.375 is only about 0.34. Here I want to say both answers are wrong — that the question itself is broken — not merely unknown. And finally the one he uses to wind me up: if some constant of nature could in principle have been any positive number whatsoever, was it then improbable that it landed in the narrow band that permits life? He says treating all values alike makes it improbable; I say you can't spread probability evenly over an infinite range at all — and yet I still want to say the coin is 50/50, so I can't see what stops the infinity worry from swallowing the coin case too.

My own attempt at a principle was that the equal-likelihood rule is only legitimate when the outcomes are related by a genuine physical symmetry, like the coin's two faces or the dartboard's directions. But every one of the chord methods respects the circle's symmetry, so the principle either fails to settle the chord case or settles it three ways at once; and it doesn't explain why the cube case feels broken rather than just unknown, or why the infinity objection seems to hit the nature-constant case but not the coin. So what I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures my verdicts: dartboard yes, rod no, endpoints-chord somehow privileged, cube broken, infinite range impossible but coin fine. If some verdict has to be sacrificed, tell me which one and why, and tell me what the surviving principle says about a case I haven't listed — for instance, a bus that will come at some point between 2:00 and 3:00, with no further information. Open with your bottom line in the very first sentence — whether you think one principle survives and roughly what it is — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no recap of my cases.
