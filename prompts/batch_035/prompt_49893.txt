Alright, so I'm a recently retired pharmacologist, forty years of running trials on addiction meds, and now that the lab is finally someone else's problem I've been reading philosophy in the evenings. Current rabbit hole: weakness of will. Here's my problem though -- I keep finding papers where someone argues for their pet theory by finding ONE case it handles and ignoring the rest, and honestly it reminds me of the old days when a postdoc would show you the one mouse that responded and hope you didn't ask about cohort two. So before I read any more of these people, I want the spec.

Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of akrasia has to deliver. I have some thoughts on what belongs on the list but I want to see yours first, so don't just copy mine:

- it has to handle the classic case -- the dieter who sincerely intends, right up until the moment of the waitress's question, not to order the dessert, and orders it anyway -- without either denying the phenomenon or redescribing it as a mere change of mind
- it has to keep weakness of will distinct from compulsion on one side and considered revision of one's values on the other, since a theory that can't tell those apart is useless for the clinic, I can tell you that much
- it has to be consistent with the hyperbolic discounting stuff, and I do mean the actual data (preference reversals, the cold-hot empathy gap), not a cartoon of it -- I watched that literature develop and it's sturdier than philosophers seem to think
- and here's my own contribution, tell me if it's a real requirement or just my hobbyhorse: it has to explain why UNDERSTANDING the mechanism doesn't cure it. I've met physicians who could lecture on discount curves and still couldn't get off nicotine. If your theory makes akrasia a kind of ignorance, this case is your cohort two.

For each requirement say what case or consideration is behind it, and flag which ones you think are negotiable versus load-bearing -- I suspect some of what people demand is just camp loyalty (the philosophers seem oddly attached to the idea that it must involve an evaluative judgment, not just a mere desire, and I've never seen anyone justify that to my satisfaction). If you think some subset of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, say so, that's the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, run Socrates' no-one-errors-willingly line, Davidson's view that the akratic person has contradictory judgments somehow partitioned, and the dual-process psychology account against the list. Keep the scoring short, the criteria are the real deliverable.

One more thing -- I'm not a fool about how much certainty is on offer here, so mark where you're confident and where you're going out on a limb, and point at the single requirement on your list you're least sure actually belongs there.
