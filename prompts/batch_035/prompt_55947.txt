I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, writing a thesis chapter on the scope of distributive justice, and I've hit a pattern I want to understand rather than simply dismiss. At our departmental seminar on global justice, a discussant closed off a Singer-style argument about duties to distant strangers by remarking, as though it were decisive, that charity begins at home. Everyone nodded; nobody spelt out what the proverb was actually supposed to establish. The same line resurfaced at a conference panel a few weeks later, this time aimed not at individual giving but at the level of the state, where it was deployed against increasing the aid budget at the expense of domestic welfare spending — as if the proverb settled a question about public finance rather than about personal generosity. So the one-liner appears to be doing quite different jobs on different occasions, all while sounding like a single piece of wisdom.

What I'd like is a careful disambiguation. As far as I can tell, the sentence could be advancing at least four distinct claims: (i) a platitude about the psychology or efficacy of giving — that beneficence is easier, cheaper, or more visible when exercised nearby; (ii) a claim about the structure of duties — that positive duties of beneficence are discretionary in a way that does not extend across borders, or that they are indexed to some relation rather than to need as such; (iii) an associative-obligations claim — that compatriots have special claims on one another, something like familial partiality scaled up; and (iv) a claim about what the state, as opposed to individuals, may or must do with collectively raised resources. Some of these strike me as true but incapable of supporting the conclusion the discussant wanted; others seem substantive and genuinely contested; and I suspect at least one is false as a generalisation. Please lay the candidate readings out as a menu rather than picking one and glossing the rest — for each, say whether it is trivial, defensible but irrelevant to the cosmopolitan argument, or actually doing the work of refuting it — and then indicate which reading the seminar and the panel were most probably relying on. I'd also like your view on whether the word 'begins' itself commits anyone to anything: as far as I can see, the proverb's wording leaves open where charity ends, so a reading that only supports prioritising compatriots over strangers needs a further premise to become a reading about refusing the strangers' claims altogether.

Finally, where a given reading acquires its anti-cosmopolitan force only when supplemented — for instance by a premise that the compatriot relation is non-arbitrary in the way the family relation arguably is, or that obligations of justice arise only within schemes of coercion or cooperation — please make that hidden premise explicit rather than letting the proverb carry it silently. My worry is that the phrase owes its dialectical power precisely to oscillating between the readings: it sounds homely and uncontroversial as a platitude while being cashed out, at the moment of use, as a contentious thesis. If you think that diagnosis is wrong, or that some further reading belongs on the menu, say so and say why.
