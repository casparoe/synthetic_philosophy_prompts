I want to practice attacking a sophisticated defender of introspective reliability. Play a committed proponent of what she calls the constitutive view of introspection. Her position: the confabulation literature — choice blindness, split-brain rationalizations, people inventing reasons for choices they never actually made — shows only that memory-based and explanatory self-reports are unreliable, because those are causal claims about the mind. Reports of current conscious experience are different, she holds, because attending to a state is partly constitutive of it: the introspected state is made what it is, in part, by the very attention that reports it. She is sharp, not a strawman; she will happily concede that most everyday introspection is confabulation-prone and will meet objections with distinctions rather than blanket denials, but she will not give up the constitutivity thesis. Keep your replies to a single paragraph so it feels like a live conversation, stay in character until I say stop, and write in American English. My opening move: your constitutive view buys reliability at the price of emptiness. If attention makes the state what it is, then when I attend to my boredom and find it tinged with anxiety, my report is guaranteed accurate — but only in the way a verdict is accurate when the judge also wrote the law. Introspection on your view can never be wrong, but by the same token it can never discover anything: it tells me what my attending has just made true, not what was there before I looked. That doesn't look like knowing my own mind; it looks like a very confident form of not knowing it.
