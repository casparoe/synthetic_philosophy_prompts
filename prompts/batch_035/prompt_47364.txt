our reading group just worked through a chunk of Elster on precommitment (Ulysses and the Sirens) and it somehow turned into a three hour argument about the US Senate. Specifically the nuclear option episodes: in 2013 the Democrats, fed up with republican blockades of Obama's nominees (the fight over filling the DC circuit especially), changed the rules by simple majority vote so that most executive and judicial nominees only needed 51 votes instead of 60; then in 2017 the Republicans did the same for supreme court nominations to get Gorsuch confirmed, after having refused to even hold a hearing on Garland in 2016. The way people talk about this, the filibuster/cloture tradition was a kind of Ulysses contract — the Senate tying itself to the mast so that fleeting majorities can't just govern like a parliament — and the nuclear options were the ropes coming off one limb at a time.

Analyze the two episodes as a case study in Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment. I want an actual mapping, not vibes: who is Ulysses here, who are the sailors, what plays the role of the mast, the ropes, and the siren song each majority claimed to be resisting. Then the disanalogies, and I especially want you to take these seriously: Ulysses binds himself against his own known future weakness, whereas the cloture rule binds a majority made up largely of *different people* than whoever will later want to be unbound — so is this precommitment at all, or is it one coalition entrenching itself against its successors? And crucially, the crew on the ship could not untie the knots while Ulysses begged, while the nuclear option shows the Senate's knots were always untieable by the very procedure they were supposed to restrain. Where does that leave the self-binding framing — was the precommitment imperfect from the start in Elster's sense, and does anything without external enforcement (courts, article V, an unamendable provision) really deserve the name?

Then run it the other direction: what do these episodes show about precommitment theory itself? Half my group says it's actually a vindication — the predictable unraveling proves the majorities' temptation was real and only genuinely constitutionalized checks can do the work, so the theory correctly predicted that a re-tieable constraint would fail. The other half says it shows the whole self-binding metaphor was smuggling in a collective agent that doesn't exist: what we actually have is sequential bargaining between ever-changing coalitions, where "binding the future" just means the current minority betting the rules will protect them later, which is exactly why both parties only respected the filibuster when they feared the alternative. Say which reading the episode supports, or whether it reveals a category the theory lacks. And if you think a different framing altogether (the Senate as a repeated game, norms as equilibria that no one defects from until the discount factor shifts) explains what happened better than the Ulysses picture, I'd genuinely like to hear it.
