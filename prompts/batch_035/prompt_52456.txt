My cousin is in law school and she was telling me about the duress defense and it's been rattling around my head for weeks. The way she explained it: if someone holds a gun to your head and says rob this bank or die, and you rob the bank, the law can treat you as less guilty or not guilty at all, because you were forced. Fine so far. But then it gets weird. The excuse only works if the threat was of death or really serious injury, so a guy who steals because he'll otherwise lose his job and his kids will starve doesn't get it. And there's this test about whether a person of "reasonable firmness" would have resisted, so apparently if you're cowardly you're out of luck even though you were just as coerced as a brave person would've been. And the part that really got me: apparently in English law duress is not a defense to murder at all, so the person under the most extreme pressure of all gets the least protection from the excuse.

What I keep wondering is what this whole setup assumes about free will or blame in the first place. It looks like it's built on the idea that you're only responsible when you had a real choice, that the coercer is the true author of the crime and you were just his instrument. But that can't be the whole story or the guy stealing to feed his kids would be excused too, since he also kind of had no acceptable alternative. And the murder rule seems to point the other way entirely, like blame there depends on what happened to the victim rather than on how much choice the defendant had, which is the opposite logic from the rest of the doctrine. So the pieces feel like they're pulling in different directions and I want to know if the doctrine actually rests on one coherent picture of when someone is responsible, or several mashed together, or maybe on no picture at all and just on judges' gut feelings about who deserves pity.

So that's my question really. Lay out what the duress defense implicitly believes about moral responsibility, as best you can reconstruct it from the rules themselves. Tell me which parts of the doctrine are actually forced by that underlying picture and which parts it could drop or change while keeping the picture intact. And then tell me what the doctrine would have to look like instead if the law committed fully to the no-choice idea, and whether that version would be a better law or a nightmare. I've got no background in any of this so don't assume I know the jargon, just explain it as you go.
