So I've been reading LessWrong since the Overcoming Bias days, ended up going into effective altruism-adjacent work, and now irony of ironies I'm on the grantmaking panel of a mid-sized AI safety fund (roughly $3M/year, we mostly do interp and governance stuff). Which brings me to the fight I need your help settling: we have an applicant, someone with a decent track record in decision theory, who wants about $180k over two years to build what he calls "acausal coordination protocols" for AI labs. The idea, as best I can reconstruct it, is a template for public commitments by which a lab binds itself in advance to cooperate on safety measures (sharing dangerous capability evals, jointly pausing if either party crosses some threshold) with any other lab it can verify has adopted the same commitment — no contract, no enforcement mechanism, just matching decision procedures. His pitch is that if two labs run similar enough decision-making, the choice to cooperate is itself evidence the other lab cooperates, Newcomb-style, so the trade is real even though there's no causal link between the two decisions. Think Parfit's hitchhiker paying the driver, or Hofstadter's superrationality, or one-boxing generalized: cooperate because your cooperation and theirs are two outputs of structurally similar deliberations.

Our panel is split roughly down the middle and its getting spicy. The pro side says this is the single most fundable thing we've seen all cycle: the world badly needs cooperation between AI labs, contracts can't be enforced fast enough, and if you take functional decision theory even slightly seriously you should expect the payoff from correlated cooperation to dwarf the grant money — plus a lab that publicly adopts this kind of precommitment is more trustworthy to everyone, so its not like the money only pays off in weird possible worlds. The anti side says the grant is just a decision theorist dressing up stuff ordinary game theory already handles with iterated interactions, reputation, and verifiable commitments, that "acausal" trade with agents you'll never causally interact with is where the reasoning gets speculative, and that we'd basically be funding a LessWord... you know, LessWrong jargon, the kind of thing that sounds rigorous to people who already one-box.

I've been tasked with drafting the decision memo that circulates before Friday — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and I want it to be a real memo the panel could vote on, not a philosophy seminar transcript. Concrete recommendation, reasons the applicant could actually respond to, maybe suggested conditions (like milestones, or a first deliverable that tests the idea on the causal part before the acausal part). But also do right by whichever side loses: give the strongest opposing consideration its honest due rather than burying it. You can assume the panel knows the basic Newcomb/prisoner's dilemma/superrationality background, so don't re-explain that from scratch, but keep it grounded enough that the skeptical half doesn't roll their eyes. Thanks in advance!
