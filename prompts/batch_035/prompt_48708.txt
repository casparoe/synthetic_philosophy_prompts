I'm a postdoc co-teaching a graduate seminar on rational choice under ambiguity, and I've been assigned the centerpiece question for our take-home final. The course covered the standard motivations for credal sets, the Ellsberg paradox, and three decision rules in detail: Γ-maximin, Levi's E-admissibility, and Walley's Maximality. In past years the exam question was essentially the Ellsberg urn relabeled, and our strongest students still answered by reciting the standard dialectic — ambiguity aversion, the sure-thing principle, a gesture at "the market-for-uncertainty literature" — without ever doing any actual reasoning. So this year I've built a concrete numerical problem in which the three rules genuinely diverge, and I want the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

Here is the case I want embedded in the question. The Agency for Inland Waters must pick one intervention for Lake Averni. Two consulting labs disagree about the cause of the degradation: the credal set is the convex hull of P1 and P2, where P1(s1) = 0.2 and P2(s1) = 0.8, with s1 = "nutrient-driven" and s2 = "toxin-driven." The options and utilities (in units the agency treats as interpersonally comparable utiles) are: dredging, worth 10 if s1 and 0 if s2; activated-carbon filtration, worth 0 if s1 and 10 if s2; and a hedged combined intervention worth exactly 4 in either state. Part (a) asks the student to determine, with computations, what each of the three rules recommends. Part (b) presents an objection from the agency's deputy director: the hedged option is the only one that doesn't gamble on which lab is right, so any rule that permits choosing an act justified only by a single probability in the set — one the agency has no way to single out — is licensing arbitrariness. Part (c) adds a proposed monitoring study whose evidential import is itself contested: under P1 a positive reading is certain given s1 and impossible given s2, while under P2 the likelihoods are reversed. A board member protests that a rational agent cannot learn to know less, so the study must be worthless or the framework broken; the student must compute the posterior interval for s1 given a positive reading, assess the complaint, and say whether any rule's recommendation actually changes. The fictional objections are invented by me, so the model answer should engage them on the merits rather than hunt for a canonical rebuttal.

Please deliver three things. First, the exam question exactly as students will see it, under 400 words, assuming the three rules were defined in lecture so the question text should not restate their definitions. Second, a model answer of 700–900 words that shows the expected-utility computations explicitly and defends its normative verdicts. Third, a marking scheme worth 25 marks total, formatted as a table with exactly these four columns: "Must-hit move" (the specific content, not a topic label), "Marks," "What separates an A from a B here," and "Predictable error that loses marks" — with one row per part (a), (b), and (c) plus at least two cross-cutting rows. Finally, append a JSON object with three fields: "crux" (one sentence naming the single move that separates students who understand from students who memorized), "part_a_key" (an object mapping "gamma_maximin," "e_admissible," and "maximality" each to the set of options that rule recommends), and "posterior_interval" (the interval for P(s1) after a positive reading, as a two-element array). Students will have 90 minutes for this question; calibrate the marking scheme accordingly.
