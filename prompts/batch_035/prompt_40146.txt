I am a recently retired atmospheric chemist — forty years of air-quality modeling, mostly the mundane business of quantifying how much combustion does to lungs — and I have lately been reading philosophy of risk and environmental ethics in an effort to work out whether my old discipline's habits of thought survive contact with the precautionary principle. I would like your help on a case that has come to strike me as the sharpest test of that principle I know of, and I ask you to analyze it thoroughly rather than survey it. Please also hold me to the usual standards of clarity we expected in journal referee reports: define your terms, and say plainly when something does not follow.

The case is Germany's nuclear phase-out after Fukushima. The salient facts, which I take to be common knowledge: in March 2011, after the tsunami and meltdowns in Japan, the German government imposed an immediate moratorium and shut down the oldest reactors, and by that June the Bundestag had voted to close all nuclear plants by the end of 2022 — a deadline ultimately met in April 2023, with the last three plants granted only a brief crisis-driven extension. Germany has no tsunamis and no comparable design-basis threat; the lost generation was substantially covered, over the following decade, by coal and lignite — the dirtiest fuels I ever had the misfortune to model — alongside imported gas and rapidly growing renewables.

The lens is the precautionary principle, in both its weak and its strong forms. By the weak form I mean the modest claim that absence of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason to postpone measures that prevent serious or irreversible harm; by the strong form I mean the further claims that the burden of proof shifts to the proponent of a risky activity, and that prohibited-by-default is the sensible posture toward activities whose worst outcomes are catastrophic. My instinct, from a career spent around Neyman and Pearson, is to recast the dispute in my own idiom: if the null hypothesis is that the activity is safe, the principle looks like a rule that sets the tolerance for false alarms at something near zero while saying nothing at all about the rate of missed dangers from the precaution itself, and a loss function that simply omits those harms — the deaths and disease caused by the replacement fuel, the climate cost of the foregone low-carbon generation — is not a loss function I would have dared submit. But I am aware that philosophers have refinements of this principle I may not know, and I want your judgment, not my prejudices confirmed.

So: analyze the phase-out as a decision made under the precautionary principle. What does each version of the principle actually prescribe here, and can any version order the choice at all, given that the live alternative was not inaction but coal — a risk-versus-risk comparison the principle seems structurally ill-equipped to make? Where is the principle silent, and where does it misdescribe what the actors thought they were doing? And what does the episode show about the principle itself — a vindication, an anomaly, or a demonstration that it was never meant for trade-offs like this one?

I want a committed answer. Take a definite position on which reading of the precautionary principle is the most defensible, state plainly whether that reading vindicates or condemns the German decision, and either endorse or amend my statistical framing of where the principle goes wrong — with reasons, not with a menu of options left for me to choose among. If you find the episode refutes something I have said, say so directly; I would rather be corrected than flattered.
