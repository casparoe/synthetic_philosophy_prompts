I'm a junior and I co-lead the philosophy club at my school, which is a small group of about ten students who meet for half an hour at lunch every other week. For an upcoming session I've been assigned to present the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, the view that reality is many-sided and that any single statement about it captures only part of the truth, along with its companion doctrine of sevenfold predication, according to which a claim is to be prefixed with something like "from one standpoint" and can be affirmed, denied, both, or neither in various combinations. Most of the group has never heard of Jainism and none of them has studied Indian philosophy.

I don't want you to explain the doctrine to me; I've read enough to feel I understand it myself. What I need is a plan for the session. Specifically:

1. Dependency structure: in what order the pieces must come so that the doctrine feels like a natural response to a real problem rather than a piece of trivia? Does the idea of standpoints have to come before the sevenfold scheme, or the other way around, and what breaks if I get the order wrong? And does anything need to be in place even before that — some prior sense of what it would mean for a description to be incomplete?

2. The carrying example: the parable of the blind men and the elephant is the obvious one, but I'm suspicious of it, partly because it seems to suggest there is a sighted person who sees the whole elephant and gets it right, which is exactly the kind of privileged standpoint the doctrine seems to deny. Should I use it anyway and repair it, or is there a better case to build the whole session on? Whatever you recommend, tell me what the example must have in it and what it must not smuggle in.

3. Misconceptions: I can already guess my clubmates will conclude either that the doctrine is just relativism ("everyone's opinion is equally valid"), or that it's self-refuting ("if every statement is partial, so is that one, so why believe it?"), or that it forbids anyone from ever asserting anything flat-out. Are these the right three to expect, in what order will they surface, and at what point in the sequence should each be headed off rather than allowed to derail the session?

4. A check for understanding: one question or task I could pose in the last five minutes that would show me whether they've actually grasped the doctrine rather than just found it charming, and what a good versus a merely compliant answer would look like.

5. What to leave out: given the audience and the thirty minutes, what parts of the tradition should I openly decline to cover, and say I'm declining, so the session doesn't bloat?

Please be concrete about the sequencing and the reasoning behind it; I care less about the content of the doctrine than about the order of ideas, because that's what I keep getting wrong when I try to teach things.
