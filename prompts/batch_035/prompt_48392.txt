I run a small evening discussion group at my local community center — about fourteen adults, mostly retirees plus a few people from my poker home game — and we meet for ninety minutes every other week. None of us has an academic background; I certainly don't. For the past two years I have been thinking on my own about how one should size bets. It started when I noticed that the loosest player at our poker nights had both the biggest winning nights and the most nights he ended unable to cover the taxi home, and it slowly dawned on me that the right size for a favorable bet might depend less on how favorable it is than on whether you get to bet again tomorrow. Working this out alone led me to the Kelly criterion — betting a fixed fraction of whatever you currently have, which I have come to understand as maximizing how fast your bankroll compounds rather than how large it might be on average. I gather this connects to something researchers call geometric rationality, though I have only ever read summaries, never the underlying papers.

Three things I have worked out by myself, which I would like an activity to make people feel rather than be told, and which I would also like the activity to test, since I do not fully trust my own arithmetic:

1. On a coin that lands heads 60% of the time at even money, the Kelly bet is 20% of one's bankroll. I found this surprisingly small, and the stranger part is that betting 100% has a higher expected bankroll than any other strategy, and yet betting 100% guarantees you finish with nothing if you keep playing. It seems to me that expected value measures the wrong thing the moment continued play is possible.

2. My group has a ritual of stating a confidence before a big sports bet and then betting the average. But when I simulate two people — one at 90% and one at 30% — making small Kelly-sized trades with each other until neither wants to trade further, they settle at a price that is not the 60% average but roughly two-thirds, which is further from a coin flip than the average is. It is as though the price that forms between two people does not average their probabilities but averages their odds, so to speak. I do not trust my simulation and would like to see whether real participants settle there.

3. A committee that votes its average confidence and then bets on that average is exploitable by its own members: everyone who disagreed with the average will want to bet against it, and the money should flow until the price moves somewhere no member ever voted for.

Design me the activity. I need rules, materials, a payoff scheme using raffle tickets redeemable for a decent bottle of wine at the end of the term (no money changing hands), and a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, all simple enough that novices can play without my explaining probability theory beforehand. The participants should run into the ruin of betting everything, and into the gap between the voted average and the traded price, before I say a word about compounding or logarithms. Then give me a debrief, in plain language, that maps what actually happened onto the three observations above. Finally, tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which parts of the idea no ninety-minute game with fourteen people can possibly reproduce — my own suspicion is that the whole point lives in the long run, and we only have ninety minutes.
