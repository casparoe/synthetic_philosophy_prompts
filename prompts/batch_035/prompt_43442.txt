I run a weekly evening seminar at an adult-education institute on the long history of arguments for toleration. The audience is mixed and non-specialist: retirees, a dentist, two sixth-formers, an engineer who reads philosophy for pleasure; none has any Latin, and their appetite reliably exceeds their background. The coming session is on Montaigne's essay 'Of Cannibals' (1580), Book I, Chapter XXX of the Essays, in the old public-domain English translation. I want a plan for the evening rather than a content summary.

I intend to open by putting the essay's first anecdote on the whiteboard, exactly as the class will meet it. When King Pyrrhus, invading Italy, had surveyed the order of the Roman army sent out against him, he is made to say: "I know not," said he, "what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations) "these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it." I chose it because it does something the students will not expect: the word 'barbarian' enters the essay as a piece of Greek parochialism, before anyone has eaten anyone. But I do not trust my instinct for the rest of the session, which is to race to the punchline everyone half-remembers — that Europeans torture and burn men alive, so that the cannibals' roast is the milder barbarity — because stated that baldly it lands as a slogan, not an argument.

So plan the ninety minutes for me. What has to be established, and in what order, before the relativist claim can be more than applause? Which single case should carry the whole session, and what must that case have and lack? The misconceptions I anticipate: that Montaigne is offering a noble savage, so that the Tupinamba are being praised as better people rather than as less corrupted; that the essay licenses 'any practice is fine anywhere', which the students will voice as 'so who's to say?'; and that 'barbarism' simply means cruelty, when the Pyrrhus anecdote has defined it as whatever the Greeks were not. Tell me where in the sequence each should be headed off, and what closing question would show that they have understood the argument rather than merely enjoyed the provocation. Finally, tell me honestly what to leave out at this level and say so: I assume the reliability of Montaigne's informant belongs out, and so does the essay's later afterlife — but correct me if either belongs in.
