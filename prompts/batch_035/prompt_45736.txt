The single most durable import from academic philosophy into the rationalist blogosphere, in my experience, is the veil of ignorance, but almost never in Rawls's own form. What circulates instead is a lottery framing: choosing principles of justice is like choosing the rules of a draw in which you will be randomly assigned a life, with no advance knowledge of which one. The reasoning then runs along expected-value lines: if you might end up as anyone, the rational move is to pick the principles that maximize the expected quality of the ticket you'll draw, which lands you somewhere near average utilitarianism -- which is, conveniently, where many of the bloggers using it already wanted to land. I've seen this deployed to support utilitarian aggregation against Rawls's difference principle (with the aside that maximin is just irrational risk aversion), to argue for global health interventions over near-term conveniences, and occasionally, by extending the ticket pool to include non-human animals, to argue for prioritizing factory-farming reform over nearly everything else. Each of these arguments treats the veil as a genuine gamble with a genuine gambler: a persisting "you" who will be handed one of the tickets, and who therefore has ex ante preferences over ticket distributions.

I have two worries I'd like help untangling. The first is that Rawls seems to have deliberately blocked the lottery reading: he denies that the parties behind the veil have any probability assignments to work with, and he says the original position is a device of representation rather than an actual choice situation someone is in. The second is deeper: if the veil strips away your conception of the good, your talents, your attachments, and your identity, it is not clear there is anyone left to draw a ticket, or anything it means to say you *might be* the person born into the worst-off caste rather than merely that you *are* that person, full stop. The lottery framing quietly restores a bare subject -- something like a soul queuing for reincarnation -- precisely where Rawls's construction is meant to have subtracted it. On the other hand, Harsanyi built essentially the lottery reading into a formal argument for utilitarianism that many economists still take seriously, so I can't just dismiss the gambler as a philosophical error.

So here is what I'd like. Audit the analogy as it is actually used: give me the explicit correspondence table (what plays the role of the ticket, the draw, the betting scheme, the stake, the house), then the places where the mapping breaks. In particular, tell me which disanalogies matter for the conclusions the bloggers want and which are merely cosmetic -- I suspect dropping the lexical priority of basic liberties and substituting Rawls's primary goods for utility units do some quiet work, but I'm not confident which way. Then tell me whether the utilitarian conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: is the lottery version a legitimate sharpening of the veil that reveals what Rawls's construction can't deliver, or was the expected-value machinery carrying the conclusion from the start? And finally, does extending the ticket pool to animals -- the version I find most persuasive in practice -- strengthen the analogy by making the ticket pool honest about who's actually at risk, or break it by making the persisting-subject assumption even harder to swallow?
