I'm halfway through a master's in archives and records management, and we keep circling something I can't put my finger on. Every national archive faces the appraisel problem: you cannot keep everything, so someone decides which records have "enduring value." And when you look at what actually survives from, say, the last two centuries, the pattern is overwhelming — cabinet minutes, land registries, court files, tax rolls, personnel dossiers, the paperwork of ministries, corporations, and prominent families. Ordinary people enter the archive almost entirely at the points where they touched an institution: arrested, taxed, admitted, deported, naturalized, hospitalized. The archivists I've met are all openly progressive about this and agree the record is skewed; the profession even funds oral history projects and community archives to "document the undocumented." But the daily practice of appraisal doesn't change much, and I've started wondering what it is that the practice itself is assuming.

Here's the tension as I see it. In class we were taught two justifications for keeping what we keep, and they don't sit together. One is something like: the archive is the organic residue of activity — records exist because some administration needed them in order to do its work, and the archivist's job is to preserve that residue, not to construct a picture of society; what never generated paperwork, in a sense, isn't ours to manufacture. The other is: archives are society's memory, and a memory that contains only the powerful is a false memory, so we have a duty to actively fill the silences. If you take the first view seriously, the current pattern of what survives isn't a bias to be corrected but almost a definition — the past that counts is the past that functioned administratively. If you take the second seriously, appraisal as practiced is failing at its own task, and the oral history add-ons are a fig leaf. My supervisor says both are "heuristics" and I shouldn't worry about it, but that feels like a dodge.

Please act as a philosopher of history who has spent real time around archival theory, and think carefully before answering — maybe start by brainstorming what the practice of appraisal, as distinct from what archivists say about it, actually requires in order to make sense. What theory of evidence, or of what a historical fact is, does the surviving pattern presuppose? Does the residue view really license keeping what we keep, or is there a slide somewhere between "the records reflect what institutions did" and "the institutions' records are what matter"? And is the memory view actually incompatible with the pattern, or could a defender say the silences are themselves evidential — that the absence of documentation about certain lives is itself a fact about power that a later historian can read off the archive? Basically: reconstruct what current appraisal practice presupposes, tell me which of the rival theories it strictly needs versus which it merely fits well with, and say what would actually have to change in the practice — not just in the rhetoric — if the presupposed view were replaced by the other one. And if you think my framing of the two views is itself too tidy, say so and give me a better map.
