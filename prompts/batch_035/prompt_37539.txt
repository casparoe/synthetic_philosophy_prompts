I teach a 200-level ethics seminar (16 students, one 75-minute session) and next week we hit suffering-focused ethics. Design an in-class activity where students run a "world shop": they get handed one pairwise choice between possible worlds at a time, each step offering a huge gain in total happiness for the addition of a tiny bit of suffering, so that if they follow their own choices consistently they end up preferring a world with no beings at all over a world with astronomical amounts of happiness plus a single pinprick of suffering — and if they refuse, they break their own chain somewhere and have to say where. I don't want the terms "negative utilitarianism" or "pinprick argument" said out loud until the debrief; the sequence itself should build the dilemma. Give me the actual worlds in order (concrete numbers of happy vs suffering lives), voting rules, a timeline, and a debrief mapping each choice onto the step it represents — the aggregation step, the lexical-priority step — plus where each step can be resisted and what resisting it costs. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce, e.g. that real negative utilitarians just bite the benevolent world-exploder bullet and no undergrad will. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (someone googles it on their phone, someone refuses to vote, etc.). End with a one-sentence summary of the activity as the very last line.
