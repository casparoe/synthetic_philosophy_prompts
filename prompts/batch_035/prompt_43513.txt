I direct a memory-care unit in a residential facility, and my staff — mostly nursing aides and activities coordinators — face what our dementia-care training materials call "therapeutic fibbing" a dozen times a shift: a resident asks when her husband is coming, and he died three years ago; another insists she must leave to cook dinner for children who are now middle-aged; the standard guidance is to enter the resident's reality and say whatever keeps the peace. Some of my staff are religious and have begun refusing to follow that guidance, citing Augustine's teaching that every lie is a sin, and I receive complaints from families on both sides — some insisting we never "correct" their mother, others appalled that we would "deceive" her. I've been asked to produce a decision procedure grounded in Augustine's position that my staff can actually run in the thirty seconds before a resident becomes agitated.

Please build that procedure. It should be an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a point at which the procedure returns a definite "no" (the utterance is a lie and may not be spoken), a point at which it returns a "permitted" (silence, redirection, ambiguity, a consoling truth), and points at which it stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than application. It must be usable by someone with no philosophy training and no time for theory — a card they could keep in a pocket.

The conceptual work is in preserving the distinctions Augustine actually draws while discarding the apparatus. His definition of a lie — asserting to another something contrary to what one holds in one's own mind, with the intention that the hearer believe it — implies that concealment, silence, evasion, and even misleading-but-true statements are not lies, and that benevolent motive makes no difference: his catalogue of lies told in jest, to please, and to help are all still lies. Build the procedure around the three elements of that definition: what the staff member believes, what the utterance asserts, what the staff member intends the resident to come to believe. Then confront the genuinely hard questions rather than papering over them. Whether redirection that trades on a resident's inability to track time counts as intending to deceive even when no uttered sentence is false. Whether agreeing with, nodding along to, or simply failing to correct a resident's false belief is assertion at all. And whether a statement can be a lie when the resident can no longer form new beliefs — Augustine's definition presupposes a hearer who can be deceived, and it is not obvious our most impaired residents satisfy that presupposition.

Then run the procedure on three cases, at least one of which should end where the staff member or the family will not like the result:

1. A resident, still early enough in her dementia to form beliefs and act on them, asks every afternoon whether her son has called; her son has died, and each time she is told she grieves as though for the first time, forgets within the hour, and asks again. Her family has asked us to say he called and is doing well.
2. A resident insists he must leave the unit to pick up his children from school and becomes physically agitated at the locked door. A colleague proposes telling him that the school phoned — the children are staying with grandparents tonight — which we know dissolves the agitation every time it is used.
3. A resident far past forming new beliefs asks where her mother is. There is no way to tell whether answering produces any belief in her at all, but she visibly calms when someone answers her.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do. Say where the Augustinian framework misfires for this population — it assumes truth-telling serves a rational hearer, which is precisely what dementia erodes. Say where staff whose moral or religious commitments differ from Augustine's will refuse to follow it, and what the procedure should do about conscientious refusal — hand-off points, documentation, escalation to family — without pretending that the disagreement over therapeutic fibbing is a factual dispute that further procedure could settle.
