A former colleague of mine who took a mathematical logic course years ago heard I'd been reading about the incompleteness theorems and gave me what he called the real story. I want to know how much of it I can trust. Roughly what he said:

"Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent formal system is incomplete: it contains statements that can neither be proved nor refuted within it. It isn't that we haven't found a proof yet — no proof exists and none ever will, even though we can see the statements are true. The theorem only bites once the system can do enough arithmetic: Presburger arithmetic, which has addition but not multiplication, is complete and decidable, so it's really multiplication that introduces incompleteness. Notice the contrast with logic itself, by the way — Gödel also proved that first-order logic is complete, every logical truth is provable, so the trouble comes from arithmetic, not from deduction. The second theorem is even stronger: no consistent system can prove its own consistency. So arithmetic cannot vouch for itself, and any system strong enough to vouch for it would need vouching for in turn, which means consistency is ultimately something we just have to take on faith. Now the philosophical payoff: we can see that the Gödel sentence is true even though no machine grinding through the system's rules can ever prove it. So the mind outruns any formal system — Gödel proved that human reasoning cannot be mechanized. And it doesn't stop at mathematics: any theoretical edifice rich enough to contain arithmetic — a fundamental physics, even a body of law — will harbor truths it can never establish, so the dream of a completed science is incoherent."

Since he actually studied this and I haven't, I'm inclined to accept the parts about arithmetic. But the jump from unprovable sentences to minds defeating machines sounded like it was doing a lot of work, and so did the closing claim about science. Please go through his account and separate what a logician would accept as stated, what counts as a harmless simplification, and what genuinely misrepresents the theorems — including anything he garbles at the very start that he quietly fixes later. Then tell me whether his conclusion about minds follows even from the accurate parts, whether the faith point about consistency is as strong as he makes it sound, and whether anything true in the summary supports the claim about a completed science being impossible.
