I want to work through a question in the ethics and metaphysics of value — whether comparing historical atrocities is a legitimate intellectual operation or itself an act of diminishment — but through a real episode rather than in the abstract, because in the abstract the debate just reenacts its own stalemate. One side says comparison is simply rational: to refuse to order the Gulag and Auschwitz on a single scale is intellectual dishonesty, an abdication of the historian's job. The other side says the comparison is performative: placing them on one scale is already to relativize, to tell Germans that their crime was, at worst, an answer to a crime. I would like an analysis of an episode in which these two positions actually collided, run through the contemporary philosophy of value comparability, and I would like it to be honest about where the philosophy helps and where it is silent.

The episode is the West German Historikerstreit of 1986–87. To fix the salient facts, since the analysis should not turn on contested details: in an essay in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung of 6 June 1986, titled "Vergangenheit, die nicht vergehen wollen will" by the historian Ernst Nolte, Nolte argued that the Bolshevik terror — the Cheka's early massacres, the Gulag as a "class genocide" — preceded the Nazi genocide, that the Nazi genocide was in significant part a fear-driven, imitative response to that precedent, and that the prevailing German guilt-discourse suppressed this causal history. Jürgen Habermas, opening the counterattack in July 1986, charged that whatever the historiography came to, the public function of the comparison was relativization: it invited Germans to understand their own past as, at worst, a defensive echo of a still worse one. The exchange widened into a national quarrel over the "singularization" of the Holocaust, with most of the philosophical heat, on both sides, radiating from the question of what it means to place two atrocities in one explanatory frame.

Here is the lens I want applied, and a hypothesis to test against it. The lens comes from the literature on value comparability: Joseph Raz's account of incommensurability, where options cannot be ordered by a common measure without a rational basis being lost; Ruth Chang's work on parity, according to which items can be comparable — related by neither better, nor worse, nor equal — and comparison can still be rationally principled; and Calabresi and Bobbitt's treatment of tragic choices, where an institution must rank incommensurables and the tragedy lies in the ranking's being both necessary and unspeakable. The hypothesis I want tested is one I have not seen stated, and it is the constructive part of the task: that comparisons of atrocities are not one operation but at least three, performed simultaneously and often confusedly — a forensic function (which events explain which), a justificatory function (who owes whom what, whose guilt is diminished or aggravated), and a memorial function (how a community holds its dead, and whose dead). The hypothesis is that incommensurability is not a property of the atrocities themselves but of particular functions performed on them: that some functions tolerate ranking and others do not, and that disputes like the Historikerstreit are at bottom disputes over which function a given sentence was performing.

So the analysis should do the following, in this order. First, a disciplined mapping: state precisely which comparisons were actually made in the exchange, by whom, and in which of the three functions; and reconstruct, using the lens, Habermas's strongest objection — roughly, that Nolte's ostensibly forensic comparison covertly performed justificatory work — before asking whether that objection can be stated without the lens, and whether the lens sharpens or merely relabels it. Second, an honest account of where the lens breaks: Chang's parity is defined for options an agent must choose between, and Calabresi and Bobbitt's tragic choices are made by institutions under scarcity; but in 1986 nobody was choosing between the Gulag and Auschwitz, and the fight was not over a ranking to be acted on but over what may be said. Is the philosophy of comparability, built for choice situations, simply a category error when applied to a speech situation — or can it be extended, and if so by what right and at what cost? Third, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the philosophy? In particular, whether the hypothesis survives — whether the episode confirms that the quarrel was over functions rather than over the acts — and what it suggests about the suspicion that claims of incommensurability in public life do political work rather than metaphysical work. Finally, as a check on the hypothesis's reach, extend it briefly to a second, structurally different episode: the cancelled Smithsonian exhibition on the Enola Gay in the mid-1990s, where the disputed comparison was between the casualties of the atomic bombings and the projected casualties of a land invasion of Japan — a forensic comparison performed in an unmistakably justificatory register, contested almost entirely on the numbers rather than on whether the comparison should be made at all. If the function hypothesis is right, it should predict which part of each dispute became the actual fight; say whether it does.

Two constraints on the response. Where the philosophy of comparability is silent, say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak; and distinguish throughout between what the lens genuinely explains about the episode and what it merely redescribes. At the end, state how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the single point in the analysis about which you are least sure, together with what would make you more sure of it.
