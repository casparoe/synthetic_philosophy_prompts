I'm a retired physicist; for most of my career I worked in a metrology and instrument-calibration group, and a recurring duty was helping younger engineers turn vague ignorance into numbers. The house style, inherited from nobody in particular, was: if all you know is that a quantity lies between two bounds, call it uniform on the interval and propagate. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of probability, and I've discovered that this habit sits on top of something much murkier than we ever admitted. The old "principle of indifference" — assign equal probabilities when you have no reason to prefer one value over another — turns out to depend on what quantity you spread your ignorance over: uniform in a resistance is not uniform in conductance, uniform in a radius is not uniform in the area, and there's the notorious business with the random chord of a circle where equally natural ways of being "random" give different probabilities for the same event. I've also gathered that there are ideas floating around for repairing this — invariance under reparameterization, transformation groups, picking the parameterization with the most natural symmetry — but I've never seen any of it written for someone with ten minutes and a specification sheet rather than an afternoon and a treatise.

What I'd like from you is a decision procedure my former colleagues could actually follow before invoking uniform-in-the-interval. Concretely: an ordered list of questions to ask about a given problem — things like whether the bounds are hard physical limits or just spec-sheet convention, whether there's a natural reparameterization, whether anyone downstream of the number will make a decision whose cost is asymmetric — with, for each question, what counts as a clean answer, where a clean answer of the wrong kind should stop the procedure and send them back for more data rather than to a prior, and where the question is a genuine judgment call that no procedure can absorb (please mark those honestly rather than laundering them as steps). It should handle the hard cases explicitly: the situation where two competent engineers parameterize the same unknown differently and arrive at different failure probabilities, and the situation where indifference is being asked to do the work of a physical model because nobody measured anything. Then run the procedure on two worked examples: an unknown resistance known only from manufacturer tolerance bounds, where the choice between uniform-in-R and uniform-in-1/R changes the answer; and a shaft diameter known only within a machining tolerance, where what the customer actually cares about is the cross-sectional area. I'd especially like one of the worked examples to end somewhere the engineer won't like — where the honest output of the procedure is "your stated interval does not determine a probability, and pretending otherwise is the mistake" — and a closing statement of what the procedure cannot do, i.e., whether any such checklist can actually dissolve the reparameterization problem or only tell you when you're about to step in it.
