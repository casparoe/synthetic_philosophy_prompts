Write me a dialogue (maybe 600-800 words) between two AI safety researchers who disagree about whether "heuristic arguments" can be formalised into something we'd actually trust. One of them, Maya, wants to build a rigorous theory of arguments that fall short of proof but are more principled than gut feeling. Her favourite examples: we're all convinced a well-designed pseudorandom generator's output looks random in practice, and that there's no clever shortcut for inverting some hash function, but nobody can prove either of those things. Her flagship idea is the presumption of independence: when you've looked hard for correlations between two things and found no reason to expect any, it should be legitimate — and formalisable — to provisionally treat them as independent, even though proving independence is hopeless. Her pitch for alignment: black-box testing of a big model can only catch failures on inputs you actually try, but a good heuristic argument could rule out whole classes of failures on inputs nobody will ever find, which matters when your adversary is smarter than you. The other, Theo, thinks this is wishful thinking: the whole alignment problem is that an adversarial model is better at finding counterexamples than we are at ruling them out, and "we searched for trouble and didn't find any" is precisely the complacency alignment was supposed to cure — worse, if the heuristic argument itself has a hole, it just gives you false confidence with a mathematical veneer. Both characters should be genuinely smart and engage with each others actual points rather than talking past each other — e.g. Theo should press on what exactly distinguishes a "formalised heuristic argument" from just a very careful empirical generalisation, and Maya should have a real answer (maybe something about how a heuristic argument, unlike a pile of test results, is at least exposed to being argued with and found wanting in advance). Write it in British English, please.
