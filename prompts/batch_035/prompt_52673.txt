You are a senior philosopher working in value theory and decision theory, with an unusual second specialization in the semantics of gradable adjectives and comparatives — the kind of person who is equally at home in an ethics seminar and a linguistics workshop. Please think carefully and at length about what follows; before committing to an answer, briefly brainstorm the full space of possible diagnoses, including ones you will ultimately reject, and say why you reject them.

The dispute I want diagnosed concerns the transitivity of better than. Since the late 1990s, philosophers like Stuart Rachels, Alastair Norcross, and above all Larry Temkin have deployed spectrum arguments: a long series of outcomes, each differing from its neighbor by some minute amount — a barely perceptible reduction in pain traded against a barely perceptible loss of some other good, or a tiny gain in welfare traded against a tiny loss of equality — such that each step seems at least as good as, or better than, the last, while the endpoints differ enormously. If the adjacent comparisons hold, better than cannot be transitive. The pro-transitivity side responds that a nontransitive better than would leave "best option" undefined, would break the machinery of choice that consequentialist ethics presupposes, and that our apparent judgments of "no worse" along the spectrum are better explained as failures of discrimination between adjacent options — a psychophysical artifact — than as genuine verdicts of betterness. Temkin's own later position concedes context-dependence: perhaps better than fragments, so that transitivity holds within a context but not across them. And that, roughly, is where the literature sits.

My reading group has been circling this for over a year, and we have stopped moving. Everyone accepts the cases as described; everyone can recite the rejoinders and the rejoinders to the rejoinders; nobody changes their mind about whether the spectrum arguments refute transitivity. What I want from you is not another pass through the arguments but a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Take seriously, among others, these hypotheses:

1. The dispute is verbal: better than is doing double duty between an overall evaluative relation on whole outcomes and something closer to a purely formal comparative, and the two sides tacitly fix different relations.
2. The dispute is substantive but intuition-driven: each side holds bedrock commitments — about parity and comparability, or about whether better than must behave like taller than — that no argument reaches.
3. The sides are answering different questions under one heading: one camp is doing the semantics of comparatives, where transitivity is close to a logical truth; the other is doing axiology and decision theory, where the real question is whether the ordering that rational choice requires to be transitive is the same relation the spectrum arguments target — and whether money-pump-style pressures on preference even transfer to betterness.
4. The question is ill-posed because better than is interest-relative or context-sensitive, so there is no single relation to ask the transitivity question about.

Here is my own tentative view, which I would like you to assess rather than accept: the spectrum arguments are Sorites-shaped — a long chain of indiscriminable or barely discriminable differences between widely separated endpoints — and I suspect the debate has stalled because it inherits, unnoticed, the structure of the stalled debate over vague predicates, where each side's favorite response to the sorites paradox simply gets reimported into the axiology as a response to the spectrum. If that is right, the impasse is not a sign that anyone has made a mistake; it is a sign that the two debates are the same debate wearing different vocabulary.

Your deliverable: a reasoned diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, factual, standards-of-evidence, different-questions-under-one-name, or bedrock intuition — supported by what each side actually says rather than by strawmen; an assessment of my Sorites-inheritance hypothesis, including whether it survives the disanalogy that better than, unlike bald, is supposed to figure in rational choice; and a candid statement of what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result about what nontransitive betterness does to maximization, empirical work on magnitude discrimination, or a principled change of subject. If you think no progress should be expected, say so and explain why the debate nonetheless remains worth having, if it does. Do not simply survey the literature; I can read. I want the anatomy of the impasse.
