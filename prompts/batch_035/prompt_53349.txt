I host a "one movie, one thinker" film night, and this month is Midnight in Paris paired with Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, the one where he splits our relation to the past into monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. Help me prep the discussion. My first instinct was to read Gil as pure antiquarian history — reverent preservation, everything old is automatically good — but a friend in the group says he's really doing monumental history, treating Hemingway and Fitzgerald as heroic exemplars to imitate, which Nietzsche actually approves of in certain doses so long as it inspires new deeds rather than paralyzing them. Which reading survives the whole film, and does Gil move between them? Second thing: the scene where Adriana would rather stay in the Belle Époque, and Gil realizes every era dreams of some other era — is that basically Nietzsche's diagnosis that an excess of history makes life sick, or is the film making a claim Nietzsche would reject (like some golden ages really were better)? Third: the ending, Gil walking off in the rain with the girl from the record shop, choosing the present — is that meant to be the "unhistorical" power to forget that Nietzsche says we need just as much as memory, and is that even achievable for someone who has literally time-traveled into his fantasy? Last, and I want the group to be honest about this: where does the film resist the whole framing? It seems to luxuriate in its own 1920s fantasy as much as Gil does, which muddies any claim that it critiques nostalgia. I want a reading that takes both the film and the essay seriously, not just a scavanger hunt for parallels.
