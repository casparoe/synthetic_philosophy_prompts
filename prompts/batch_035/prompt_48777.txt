I teach a third-year seminar in formal epistemology and game theory, and this week we worked through the muddy children puzzle as our introduction to common knowledge. You know the setup: k children have mud on their foreheads, each child can see the others but not themselves, and the father announces, in front of everyone, that at least one child is muddy, repeating each round that anyone who knows their own status should step forward. The standard result is that all k muddy children leave together on round k, and the standard explanation is that the father's public announcement converts mutual knowledge into common knowledge. In seminar, one of my sharper students pressed an objection I'd like to think through more carefully. She conceded that in the k = 1 case the announcement does genuine work, since the lone muddy child can see no one else with mud and only the announcement lets her infer her own status. But for k ≥ 2, she argued, the announcement is informationally vacuous: every child can already see another muddy child, so every child already knows that at least one child is muddy, and everyone knows that everyone knows this, too. From this she concluded that the round-by-round induction cannot be driven by the announcement, that the textbook's invocation of "common knowledge" is doing no real explanatory work beyond "everyone knows," and that the puzzle is best understood as an artifact of an artificial timing convention rather than a lesson about the epistemic power of public announcements.

I suspect she is right that something is off in the popular tellings, which conflate the announcement's content with its publicity, but wrong about the conclusion. My instinct is that the announcement matters not because of what it says but because it establishes, at every finite depth of iteration, that everyone knows that everyone knows that... at least one child is muddy — a property that fails, for k ≥ 2, if the children merely observe the world without any public signal. Still, I'd like a careful, self-contained treatment that I could adapt into a handout, ideally tracing an explicit case (k = 2 or k = 3) round by round and saying precisely which level of the knowledge hierarchy becomes newly available to which child, and when. I'd also welcome a note on whether her point has any residual force — for instance, whether some formulations of the puzzle get away with weaker assumptions than full common knowledge.
