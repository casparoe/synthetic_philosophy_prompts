Just so you know where I'm coming from: I work adjacent to the laws of armed conflict world (policy side, not a lawyer), and for years now I've watched the same fight play out between the philosophers and the more traditional just-war people, and it never goes anywhere. The question is the moral equality of combatants — whether soldiers fighting on the unjust side of a war are morally on par with soldiers on the just side, so that both sides' fighters may be targeted and may kill. The revisionists, roughly the Jeff McMahan line, say obviously not: your liability to be killed doesn't come from wearing a uniform or from following lawful orders, it comes from what you're morally responsible for, so a soldier fighting an unjust war can't justly kill the soldiers defending against him, even when everything he does is perfectly legal under the laws of war. The traditionalists, going back to Walzer's framing, say whatever the deep morality says, the convention that combatants are legitimate targets regardless of their side's cause is the only workable rule — you discriminate between combatants and civilians, and that's it — and that if you tell soldiers to settle the justice of their own war before fighting, you either get conscientious objectors everywhere or, more likely, each side remains fully convinced it's the just one and now feels licensed by morality rather than just law, which is worse.

And then it just repeats. The revisionists say you're confusing the law of war with its morality; I can grant the law is good law and still say the unjust soldier acts wrongly. The traditionalists answer, fine, but then your deep morality has no grip on anyone — nobody can reliably know which side is just in the fog, and if morality and law come apart this far, lecturing soldiers about the morality can only make things worse. The revisionists reply that truth matters and that we don't usually say a widespread moral mistake is harmless just because it's widespread. And around we go. I've seen this exact loop at conferences, in journals, over drinks. As far as I can tell nobody has budged in maybe two decades.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis because I could be totally wrong: the two sides are answering different questions and each is right within its frame. The revisionists are doing fundamental ethics — what makes a person liable to lethal harm at all. The traditionalists are doing institutional design — what rule, given how wars actually start and how soldiers and populations actually reason, produces the least atrocity. If that's right, the stall isn't stubbornness; it's that each side takes the other's question to be an evasion of its own. But I'm not fully sold on my own diagnosis, because the two questions do interfere. Whether an unjust-side soldier can be prosecuted for the murder of just-side soldiers seems like a place where deep morality and institutional rules have to meet, and neither camp has a comfortable answer there — the traditionalists have to punish someone they think did wrong only in a Pickwickian sense, and the revisionists have to admit that courts should sometimes punish killings they hold weren't wrong, or that were, and they don't love either horn.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right about moral equality. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal? Substantive but about standards — about what counts as a moral reason in this domain, where the traditionalists treat institutional facts (being in an army, the war being public) as generating permissions and the revisionists refuse? Two projects under one heading, per my hypothesis, and if so why does the interference case not stay neatly separable? Or is it bedrock intuitions all the way down — roughly, that participation in a wrongful collective enterprise can make you liable to be killed even if you're individually as blameless as the conscript next to you, which the traditionalists deny and the revisionists assert? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a test case, a change in the question — or give me an honest argument that this impasse should be expected to never move.
