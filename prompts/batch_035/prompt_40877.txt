I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book that's told entirely from inside one character's head, and the whole architecture of the thing leans on her decisions mattering — chapter to chapter, her reasons are what move the plot. My partner (a neuroscientist, annoying in the specific way you'd expect) keeps telling me the mind is just software running on the brain's hardware, and that this is supposed to be good news for me: software states cause things at the software level all the time, nobody thinks a running program is a ghost pushing the processor around. So her jealousy can be real and do actual work even though every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, because "her jealousy made her lock the door" is just the higher-level description of what the hardware was doing anyway.

Here's what bugs me. The underlying worry, as I understand it from her, goes something like: if the neurons fire because of other neurons, then by the time you get up to the jealousy, the causal work is already finished — stacking the mental state on top either does nothing extra or double-counts the same event. The software analogy is supposed to answer that. But I can't tell if it answers the worry or just hides it. When people say the program "caused" the printer to jam, that sounds fine — but is that causal talk doing real work, or is it a convenient shorthand someone could always rewrite in hardware terms without losing anything? And if it's always rewritable, then maybe my plot only really happens at the neuron level, and at the level where I actually write — where reasons and decisions live — it's all just narration laid over the machine, epiphenomenal as anything. Which would honestly be a bummer for the book.

So: audit the analogy. How far does it hold? And I want it in structured form because I'm going to tape it above my desk:

First a mapping table with columns — [computer side, mind/brain side, where the mapping holds, where it strains]. Software, hardware, running, executing, "the program caused the crash," the whole works: tell me what corresponds to what.

Then a JSON object with these fields:
- "disanalogies": array of objects, each with "what_breaks" (string), "why_it_breaks" (string), "matters_for_my_question" (boolean — my question is whether character-level reasons do causal work, not whether brains are literally computers), and "if_true_costs_the_conclusion" (string, can be short).
- "smuggled_premises": array of strings — things the analogy quietly imports that the brain case lacks, e.g. that software is designed, or that you can open the case and point at the parts.
- "verdict": one of "holds", "holds_with_repairs", "fails", "inconclusive", plus a one-sentence "verdict_reason".
- "better_analogy": a string — what a novelist should keep in her head instead, and what adopting it costs.
- "one_line_for_the_novel": a single sentence I can actually put in a character's mouth or a narrator's aside without it sounding like a lecture.

Around the structured stuff, whatever prose you need. Two things I especially want flagged honestly: whether the analogy is doing the same job when engineers use it and when philosophers of mind use it (I suspect those are different jobs, and alot of people slide between them), and whether the conclusion my partner wanted — jealousy really causes the locked door — was riding on the analogy the whole time or survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the whole thing turns on some deeper distinction I've barely gestured at — say, between the mental being identical to the physical versus just correlated with it, or between one event under two descriptions versus two events overdetermining an outcome — definately lay that out, because I suspect that's where the real action is and I only half know it.
