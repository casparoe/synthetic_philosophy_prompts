I need a thought experiment built to order for the debate over where the badness of pain resides, and I want it to use pain asymbolia together with its mirror image. Asymbolia, as described in the clinical literature: pain felt at full intensity — located, describable, rated high on the pain scale — with no distress, no wincing, no avoidance. Your case must also feature the reverse dissociation, which I will call aversive anesthesia: a subject gripped by the full aversive face of pain — the suffering, the urge to escape, the collapse of attention onto the body — while stipulated to have no distinctive sensory quality at all, no localized feel, nothing to point at or rate.

Specifications:

1. One paragraph of scenario. Both sides must be clinically recognizable in spirit: describe the subjects' reports and behavior rather than the mechanism, and stipulate phenomenology freely, but never stipulate verdicts — those must be derived from the theories.
2. The subjects must be otherwise unharmed and have no further stake (no tissue damage, no lost opportunities, no fear of what the pain means), so the only deviations from a healthy control are the pain components themselves.
3. Walk through all four cells of the design — normal pain, asymbolia, aversive anesthesia, and neither component — under three theories: (a) a hedonist for whom badness rides on felt quality; (b) an attitudinal theorist for whom pain is bad in virtue of being disliked; (c) an imperativist for whom pain is a command issued to the body, bad when it demands what cannot be done. On the question of which dissociated subject is worse off, the three theories must give pairwise distinct verdicts, and a theory may deliver the verdict that a given cell is not really pain at all — but you must argue for that, not assume it. Note that the case is for a paper arguing that no single component suffices to carry pain's badness, so a design that quietly stacks the deck for one theory fails.
4. Add the trade-off probe: each dissociated subject is offered one-shot conversion into the other's state. Say what each theory predicts they would choose, and be honest about which prediction feels forced.
5. Name the load-bearing feature of the design, say what happens to the verdicts if you remove it (for instance, by granting the mirror subject any localized feel at all), and give the strongest one-paragraph attack on the case from whichever theory fares worst.

Close with a single line of the form Verdict: X, where X is at most ten words naming the component of pain the trade-off probe most favors as the bearer of its badness, or the single word symmetric if the case genuinely supports no ordering.
