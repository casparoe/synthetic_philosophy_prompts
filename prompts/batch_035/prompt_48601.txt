I'm taking an undergraduate metaphysics course, and we've just finished the unit on material constitution: the statue and the clay, Lumpl and Goliath, the question of whether there are two objects in the case where a sculptor's lump is shaped into a statue that could not survive being squashed even though the clay could. I came out of the readings sympathetic to the "coincident objects" view — two things sharing all their matter and differing in persistence conditions — mainly because of the modal argument: if the statue and the clay were one thing, that thing would both survive and not survive squashing, which looks like a contradiction.

At office hours my TA pushed back with an analogy I haven't been able to shake. A fist, she said, is not an object over and above the hand. When I clench, no new entity pops into existence alongside my fingers; when I relax, nothing perishes. "Fist" is just the hand under a posture or phase, and the fact that the hand survives opening while "the fist" does not is not evidence of two objects — the "does not survive" is a fact about a way of describing, not about a thing. She then applied this across the board: the statue is the clay under the statue phase, the way a caterpillar is a phase of the animal, so the modal argument is a trick of language and there are no coincident objects. The step where she got most animated was the extension to persons: the popular modeling of persons on statues — you are one thing, your body another, related by constitution — inherits the same defect, because on her view persons would be fist-like phases of animals rather than further entities, and arguments for a distinct person-over-and-above-the-animal are just the statue argument reheated.

I'd like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the underlying views reached by other means. Please give me a correspondence table first: hand, fist, clenching, opening, and anything else that's needed, mapped onto clay, statue, sculpting, and squashing. Then the places where the mapping breaks, sorted into disanalogies that matter for her two intended uses — defeating the modal argument for coincident objects, and then extending the result to persons — and ones that don't. Two candidate breaks I'd especially like your judgment on: whether fists have too shallow a life (no aesthetic properties, no purchase price, no place in art history) for the comparison to carry the statue's sortal-relative features, and the temporal point that the clay plausibly existed on a shelf before any sculptor began, whereas a hand never precedes all its fists in that way — a fist looks like something the hand does, while a statue looks like something the clay becomes. Finally, tell me whether her conclusion survives once the breakdowns are on the table, and here I want you to commit: say which parts of her position you would accept and which you would reject, with reasons, rather than laying out the live options for me to choose among. And if you think some other pairing — a wave and the water, a song and a performance, whatever you judge best — does the work she wanted done, say so and explain what it gets right that the fist does not.
