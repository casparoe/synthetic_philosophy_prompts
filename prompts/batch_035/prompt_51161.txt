I've been reading — and occasionally arguing in — the same fight about aggregating tiny harms for well over a decade now, going back to the original thought experiment that pits one instance of a grave harm against a truly astronomical number of trivial nuisances (3^^^3 of them, in the canonical version). Team Multiplication holds that any nonzero badness, repeated enough times, must eventually outweigh any fixed severe harm, and that refusing to say so is scope insensitivity. Team Threshold holds that there is something deeply wrong with trading a grave harm for any number of dust specks, and that this judgment is bedrock, not a rounding error in our moral arithmetic. Over the years the standard moves have all been run: variants where the tiny harms are spread across enormous lifetimes or duplicated populations; the accusation that one side can't do arithmetic and the other that it has let arithmetic do ethics; appeals to what a benevolent bystander with the choice would actually do. Nothing has moved. Each side can restate the other's arguments to the other's satisfaction, and the arguments simply don't bite.

I want a diagnosis of the stall rather than another round of it. My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to take seriously but not feel bound to endorse, is that the two sides are implicitly answering different questions — one about the structure of an impersonal ordering over outcomes, the other about what an agent may permissibly do to one individual for the sake of others — and that the thought experiment conflates them by putting a chooser in front of the ledger. But I can equally see it as a genuine disagreement about whether intuitions formed at numbers we cannot even represent carry any evidential weight, or even as a partly verbal dispute about whether the word 'worse' is being used to rank outcome-totals or to render a verdict on a choice. Which of these diagnoses (or some fourth one) best fits what the participants actually say to each other, and what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a decomposed variant of the experiment that separates the ranking question from the choice question, a formal result about which aggregation rules the threshold side can consistently accept, an agreed distinction in terms, or nothing at all? I'd rather have a committed diagnosis with reasons than a tour of the options, and please keep your answer under 450 words.
