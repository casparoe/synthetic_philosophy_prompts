Hey! So I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, the kind of reader who buys pop physics books but drops them around chapter four. Once a year I give a talk at a local science festival, 30 minutes plus Q&A, no slides, just me and a whiteboard. This year my editor talked me into doing Boltzmann brains, of all things, and now I have to figure out how to actually pull that off in front of a curious lay audience.

Don't explain Boltzmann brains to me — I know the material cold, that's my job. What I need is a plan for the talk:

- The order the pieces have to go in. The whole thing rests on probability plus thermal fluctuations, and if I sequence it wrong it comes out as magic ("sometimes brains just appear!") instead of the mundane-but-terrifying statistical claim it actually is.
- The one concrete example that carries the whole talk. I've been mulling the usual suspects — shuffled decks, monkeys at typewriters — but they feel stale, and worse, I think they mislead in a specific way: they make the rare event look easy-ish to get rather than merely possible-in-principle. If you know a better candidate, tell me what it has to have and what it must NOT suggest.
- The misconceptions the audience will walk in with or form, and where exactly in the sequence I should defuse each. I'm expecting "so nothing is real, we're in the Matrix" vibes, people conflating this with Descartes' demon or plain solipsism, and — the one that worries me most — people hearing "you might be a brain that fluctuated into existence five minutes ago complete with false memories" as a live hypothesis about THEM personally, rather than an absurdly improbable scenario that the physics nonetheless seems to predict.
- The self-undermining bit. I want to land the point that if most observers like me are Boltzmann brains, then the evidence that got me to the physics is itself probably fake, so the reasoning eats its own tail. I think that's the actual payoff of the talk, but I can't decide where it goes in the sequence — before the misconceptions, after them, as the closer?
- Some check I can use during Q&A to tell whether a person actually got the idea versus just got spooked/entertained by it.
- What I should flat-out refuse to cover at this level — the measure problem in cosmology? the actual numbers for fluctuation probablities? which cosmologies make the whole thing worse? — and how to say "not today" gracefully from a stage.

No math beyond "big numbers are big." And please don't just hand me a generic talk outline — I want the dependency structure and the misconception map, the stuff that requires knowing how this idea gets misunderstood, not just knowing the idea.
