I am a professor of political philosophy preparing a ninety-minute session on the boundary problem of democratic theory — the question of who is entitled to belong to the demos before any vote can be taken — for an interdisciplinary research seminar whose members are economists and legal scholars rather than philosophers. They are sharp but impatient with what they will take to be merely definitional hand-wringing, and several of them will assume the answer is obvious: the demos is whoever holds citizenship of the state in question, and the interesting questions start only after that is settled. I do not want the concept explained to me here; I want a plan for the explanation itself.

Please set out the order in which the components should be introduced and why — in particular, whether the circularity worry (that any procedure for deciding who gets to decide would presuppose a demos that has already been fixed, so that the group cannot simply vote itself into existence) must be established before the candidate principles are reviewed, or whether it lands harder afterwards — and then assess the candidate carrying example against alternatives. My working idea is a referendum in a town on whether to license a smelter upwind of a neighbouring town, whose residents breathe the result but cast no ballot; I would want to know what such a case must have and must lack in order to keep both the all-subjected and all-affected principles under pressure, and why narrower examples (gerrymandering, disenfranchised felons) would collapse the session into something adjacent but distinct. I would also like a misconception map: the confusions my audience will predictably bring or acquire — that the boundary problem is really about immigration, that the all-affected principle is refuted simply by the observation that almost everyone is affected by almost everything, that the all-subjected principle handles the smelter case, that cosmopolitanism dissolves rather than relocates the boundary — with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than left to fester until questions. Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level (I suspect the standing of future people and of non-human animals is the right candidate for exclusion, but argue the point), and give me one closing question whose answer would reveal whether they have grasped the problem itself rather than merely grown fond of one of its putative solutions. Please write your response in British English.
