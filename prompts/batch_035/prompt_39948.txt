I'm a graduate student in a program that straddles machine learning and ethics, and my reading group has spent the semester on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness. A few weeks ago I assembled the following argument out of things people in that literature actually say, and I now can't decide whether it's a serious result or a trick. I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher of science who works on the formal foundations of machine learning, and analyze it carefully: before committing to an answer, brainstorm internally — list every step that could conceivably be challenged, test each challenge against what the step actually asserts, and only then write up your diagnosis. Please reason at length; a quick verdict would be useless to me.

The argument, as I've formalized it:

(1) Any acceptable risk score must be calibrated within each socially salient group: among members of a group who receive a given score, the proportion who actually go on to have the outcome must equal the score. A score that systematically overstates risk for one group is either a standing misrepresentation of that group or useless as a guide to action; either way it fails the most basic condition on an instrument being used to sort people.

(2) Any acceptable use of a risk score must distribute its errors evenly across groups: false positives and false negatives at comparable rates for each group. It is the errors that do the harm — detention for the wrongly flagged, withheld release or credit for the wrongly cleared — so a system whose errors fall disproportionately on one group treats the members of that group unjustly, whatever the calibration of the underlying score.

(3) Two well-known results — Chouldechova's 2017 paper and the Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan ITCS 2017 paper — show that when base rates of the outcome differ between two groups, no score can be calibrated within each group while also equalizing error rates across them, unless the score is a perfect predictor within one of the groups. No real predictor of recidivism, default, or readmission is perfect within any group, and none ever will be.

(4) Base rates of virtually every outcome of consequence differ across the groups about which decisions are made — this is close to definitional for the groups that matter enough to argue about.

(5) Therefore, in every domain where risk scores are actually used, every possible predictor is unfair to some group: either miscalibrated for that group, or error-imbalanced against it. This covers logistic regression, gradient boosting, a committee of human experts, and a judge's unaided intuition alike, since the theorem in (3) applies to any predictor whatever.

(6) Since no method of prediction can even in principle avoid unfairness, the practice of predicting risk about persons is intrinsically unfair, and those domains ought to abandon it — not by substituting human judgment for algorithmic scores (human judgment is a predictor too, and (5) already covers it), but by ceasing to score people at all and deciding by group-blind rules that make no use of risk.

(7) But (6) seems to prove too much. It condemns as unjust and to-be-abolished not just COMPAS-style sentencing tools but emergency triage, sepsis early warning, epidemiological screening — every practice that sorts people by predicted outcome. And it implies that the pre-algorithmic world of judges and loan officers judging by feel was exactly as unfair as the worst model. If everything is unfair, "unfair" has stopped doing contrastive work, which makes me suspect the word means something different in (5) than it did in (2) — though I can't locate the slippage.

Two members of my group have offered rival diagnoses, and I can't adjudicate between them. One says the mistake is in the statement of (1) and (2) as individually necessary conditions: the theorem in (3) shows only that (1) and (2) cannot be jointly satisfied, from which it does not follow that every predictor violates one of them and is therefore unfair — failing one member of an unsatisfiable conjunction is not yet unfairness, since fairness may require satisfying one of them rather than both. The other says the real problem is later: in (6), where "no score satisfies both criteria" is quietly converted into "the practice is intrinsically unfair," an extra normative premise about what fairness requires of a practice sneaks in. I have a nagging feeling these are partly the same point, but I can't say why without begging the question.

So, please tell me: which premise or step, if any, should I give up? Where exactly does the argument go wrong, or is it valid and the conclusion merely hard to live with? For the step you diagnose, explain why it looked fine when I wrote it — what made it seem compulsory? Then price the alternatives: if I reject that step, what do I lose, and what would I have to accept? And tell me, separately, whether either of my groupmates' diagnoses survives your analysis, whether they are in fact the same point, and — if the argument does go wrong — whether a repaired version of it could still deliver some weaker conclusion worth taking seriously. For the record, the step I'd most like to keep is (1); calibration looks non-negotiable to me. I'm prepared to give up (6) if I must, but I want to give it up for a principled reason, not because its conclusion is embarrassing.
