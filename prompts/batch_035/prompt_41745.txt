I'm a graduate student in bioethics writing a thesis chapter on the concept of mental disorder, and the seminar discussion around it has stalled in a way I'd like your help diagnosing. The debate I mean is the one Thomas Szasz started in the early 1960s with The Myth of Mental Illness and never really closed: Szasz held that disease, strictly speaking, requires a bodily lesion or a demonstrable physical pathology, so that calling depression or schizophrenia illnesses is a category mistake — a metaphor that has hardened into an apparent literal truth — and that what psychiatry treats are better described as problems in living, conflicts that are moral and social rather than medical. His critics responded on at least three fronts that I can see. Some argued the concept of disease doesn't require a lesion at all — it's a family-resemblance concept organized around dysfunction and maladaptation, on which epilepsy or migraine would have failed the lesion test for much of history — while others, following Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis, tried to reconstruct disorder as failed mental function plus harm to the person. A second group largely conceded the conceptual point but denied it mattered, insisting that whether or not these conditions are diseases, they cause suffering and respond to treatment, so medicine is the right home for them. And a third group engaged Szasz on the ethics: his deepest objection was always to coercion — involuntary commitment, the insanity defense, psychiatry as an arm of the state — and here some critics who share his anti-coercition views reject his claim that the coercive practice is grounded in the conceptual error. What I find striking is that after sixty years of this, nobody has moved. Psychiatry as an institution mostly stopped engaging, the DSM grew regardless, and the argument simply re-erupts, with the same scripts, every time a diagnostic manual adds grief or internet addiction.

Please take the role of a philosopher of medicine and psychiatry who has watched this literature for decades, and think carefully before answering — I'd genuinely prefer you brainstorm the possible diagnoses first, then commit. My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis: this is not one stalled debate but three or four bundled together — a semantic dispute about the extension of the word disease, a normative dispute about the legitimacy of state power exercised through medicine, and a sociological dispute about professional authority — and the parties systematically equivocate, treating a win on their home front as a win in the whole war. Szasz seems to think establishing the semantic point (no lesion, no disease) automatically establishes the institutional one; some of his critics commit the mirror-image error of thinking that because involuntary commitment is sometimes justified, the conditions treated must therefore be diseases. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a genuinely substantive disagreement hiding underneath, say about whether the semantic question is even decidable, or about whether our concept of disease ought to be descriptive or revisionary? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a stipulative reconvention of the term, empirical findings like robust biomarkers for depression (though I suspect those wouldn't settle the semantic question — convince me or correct me), or nothing at all, in which case explain why the impasse is structural. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions, and not a reading list.
