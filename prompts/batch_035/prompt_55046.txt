I teach an upper-level seminar on theories of justice, 18 students, one 80-minute session. They've already read the equality-of-what material (Sen's lecture, the Dworkin auction / envy test papers, a bit of Nussbaum), and frankly they can recite the "welfare vs. resources vs. capabilities" taxonomy without it touching anything. So I want to ambush them instead of lecturing them again. Design me a game where the question ambushes them.

Rough idea of what I'm after, but you work out the details: participants are citizens of a mini-society, they recieve some in-game currency, and there's a shop where they buy things like mobility, pleasant afternoons, achievements, good health. Secret condition cards dealt at the start mean the same goods cost different amounts for different people -- one student's card makes movement cost a fortune (disability analogue), one makes everything taste cheap (easily pleased), one has cultivated a taste for champagne so their pleasant afternoons cost triple, whatever you think works. Then the twist: at least two redistribution votes during the game, where the group has to choose a principle -- equal currency, equal stuff bought, equal satisfaction at the end, something else they invent -- before the consequences of each one play out. I want them to physically experience that "equal" keeps breaking in different places depending on what you equalize, and that fixing the last round's unfairness creates the next round's one. The expensive tastes problem, the disability problem, and the contented-poor problem should all show up as things that happen to them, not as bullet points.

Constraints: no real money (chocolate or lottery tickets toward the participation grade or something else you think actually motivates students), everything runnable by one facilitator with paper cards, and no lecture up front -- they shouldn't hear any theory names until the debrief. The debrief should map the specific moments of the game onto the specific positions (which redistribution vote corresponds to which metric, and where the game forced someone to make the interpersonal comparison that the theory says is hard). Also tell me honestly what the game can't reproduce -- I assume interpersonal utility comparisons and the whole responsibility-for-your-preferences thing will be papered over somehow, and I'd rather know where I'm hand-waving -- and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: whoever gets the expensive-tastes card plays it for laughs and derails round two, but you tell me what you actually expect and how to handle it).

One more thing: I'd like at least one participant's situation to make equal-welfare look monstrous, not just unequal-resources look unfair -- the leveling down / contented peasant intuition should also arrive as something that happens, not as a footnote. Keep the whole thing inside 80 minutes with a realistic minute-by-minute breakdown, and assume 2 or 3 students will try to game whatever rules you write. That's fine, but tell me whether to let them.
