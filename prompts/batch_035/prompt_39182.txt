I direct a small graduate program in philosophy of science with an emphasis on the life sciences, and I've just been given funding for three postdoctoral fellowships of two years each, plus a twice-yearly workshop series, all devoted to the debate over biological function — the standing dispute between etiological (selected-effect) accounts, on which a trait's function is what it was selected for, and causal-role accounts, on which a trait's function is its contribution to some system-level capacity we care about, along with the various organizational and dispositional hybrids proposed as reconciliations. My job over the next month is to set the program's research agenda, and I don't want to spend it rehashing the classic statements of the two camps; I want to know which open questions in and around this debate are actually worth three postdocs' time. A few candidate directions occur to me, though I'm not wedded to any: whether the selected-effect framework can be stretched to cover functions fixed by only a few hundred generations of laboratory selection, or by cultural rather than genetic inheritance; whether the function labels assigned in genome-annotation and systems-biology work tacitly presuppose a causal-role analysis, and whether the tacit presupposition causes real trouble; whether organizational accounts based on self-maintaining systems earn their keep outside of origins-of-life and metabolism-first contexts; whether the medical notion of organ dysfunction genuinely requires etiological function rather than merely statistical or design-like notions; and what to say about synthetic and engineered organisms, where there is a design history but no selection history. There may well be better candidates I'm not seeing.

So, taking the role of a senior philosopher of biology who has followed this literature for decades and also sits on enough hiring committees to know what makes a postdoc project viable: think carefully about your criteria before committing — I'd want you to weigh how many other debates hang on each question (disease and malfunction, artifact function, function pluralism, the teleosemantics of mental content), how tractable each is for purely conceptual work as opposed to empirical work our fellows can't do, and how neglected each is relative to its importance — and also tell me which of the alleged implications of the debate are, in your judgment, overrated or simply don't follow. Please rank exactly five open questions for this program, most defensible priority first. Give me exactly five numbered items, each one to two sentences long: name the question, state where it ranks, and give the single strongest reason for that placement, including one clause on what would demote it. No introduction, no conclusion, no reading list or citations — just the five items.
