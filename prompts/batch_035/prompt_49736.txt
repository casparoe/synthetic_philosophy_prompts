I'm giving the toast at my PhD advisor's retirement dinner, and I'd like your help writing it. For forty years she has worked on one side of the debate over knowing how and knowing that: against the intellectualist mainstream, she has insisted that her knowing how to teach a seminar, to ski, to read a room full of tired graduate students was never a body of propositions she could have written down and handed to someone else — that the difference between the two kinds of knowledge is real, and that collapsing it misses something about what a life of acquired skill amounts to. Roughly half the room will be philosophers who know the literature and will catch every allusion; the other half will be family, former students from other fields, and neighbours who have never heard of any of it. Write a five-minute toast that works for both halves.

There is one constraint I care about most of all, and it is philosophical rather than stylistic: the toast must not commit the error she spent her career diagnosing. If you summarize her life as a list of things she believed — a sequence of positions she endorsed, as though the person were exhausted by her propositional attitudes — you will have praised her in exactly the currency she argued was the wrong one for understanding skilled people. So the speech should spend at least as much time on what she could do, and on how she got better at doing it over the decades, as on what she held true; her commitments may appear, but as things she did something with, not as the substance of the tribute. Relatedly, she has told me she hates being spoken about as if she were already a chapter in a textbook, so no framing of her as a representative of a position.

Beyond that: include one moment the philosophers in the room will recognize as a jab at the idea that knowing how to do something just is knowing some fact about how to do it, phrased so that everyone else hears it as an ordinary compliment — she would like it to land as affection rather than as a lecture, and the family should never feel they are being educated at a party. Avoid jargon, avoid dead languages, and do not end with a summary of her views. End instead with something she knows how to do — something the closing lines of the toast can point at but not paraphrase, so that the last thing the room hears is an instance of the distinction she made her life's work, whether or not anyone notices.
