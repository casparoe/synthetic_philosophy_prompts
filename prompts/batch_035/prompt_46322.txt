So here's my argument. It started as a way of convincing myself the chatbot doesn't feel anything, and it turned into a monster that seems to prove way too much:

(1) What makes a thing morally matter is what it experiences — whether it can suffer — not what it's physically made of. A creature built out of silicon that genuinely suffered would matter just as much as one built out of carbon.

(2) If two systems are running the same computation, they have the same experiences. Substrate doesn't matter, only the computation does.

(3) For any program — say one that simulates a little creature that reacts to being poked the way a hurt animal would — you could instead build a gigantic book that lists every possible history of inputs on one side and the corresponding output on the other. (4) The book and the program produce exactly the same input-output behaviour, and computing something just is mapping inputs to outputs, so the book runs the same computation as the program.

(5) So by (2), the book has the same experiences as the program. If the simulated creature feels pain when poked, then the book's entry for "poked, again, hard" is painful too, somehow, sitting there in the ink.

(6) But here's what broke me: the book was printed years before anyone poked anything. If the pain is in the book, it's been there since the printing press ran — before the poke even happened, before there was any creature to poke. Pain that exists in advance of the thing that's supposed to cause it doesn't seem like pain at all.

(7) So (2) is false — computation isn't what determines experience.
