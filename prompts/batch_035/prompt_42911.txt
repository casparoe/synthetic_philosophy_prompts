I'm the historical researcher (basically the in-house accuracy person) at a mid-size production company that makes limited-series dramas about real people, and I keep running into the same fight with the writers' room: somebody invents a deathbed scene, or has a real dead person do something cruel that's pure invention, and when I object I get told it's "true to her essence" and that biopics have always done this and everyone accepts the convention. Legal won't help me — our lawyer only cares about defamation of living people, and there's apparently no cause of action for libeling the dead. But I'm not comfortable with the conclusion that therefore anything goes once someone's died, and I'd like something more principled than my own gut. What I have half-formed in my head is this old philosophical debate about whether you can harm (or wrong) the dead at all: the obvious worry that they can't experience anything anymore, versus the view that people have interests in how they're remembered that can be set back after death, versus the move of saying even if the dead aren't wronged, the *audience* or the living relatives or the historical record itself is. I don't need a philosophy lecture, but I'd like the procedure to actually be grounded in that debate rather than just being studio risk management dressed up.

So: please write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a scene, one I can apply in a day or two during prep, not just vibes. It should be an ordered set of questions — what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a firm "this has to change," and where it hands the decision back to a human because the remaining question is genuinely one of values rather than facts. It should take seriously the distinction between (a) compressing or inventing events that leave the person's overall character intact, (b) inventing something that changes how the audience judges them, and (c) doing that to someone whose defenders are dead or scattered and can't push back — and it should say whether that asymmetry between famous dead people with living advocates and forgotten dead people actually matters or is just sentiment. Then run it on three real cases I've had: giving a real mid-century woman scientist an invented romance with a real dead colleague (it's the best scene in the episode and I don't want it to fail the procedure, but I suspect it does), merging two real aides into one composite character who gets the villain beats that neither actual person earned, and a scene where a real dead person commits a specific act of betrayal that historians actually dispute, but our script presents it as settled fact because the drama needs it.

One more thing: I want you to actually take positions, not hand me the options with "reasonable people could disagree." If the procedure says the romance scene stays, say so and say why; if it says the composite has to go even though composites are industry-standard, commit to that. And be honest about the points where the procedure just can't settle anything — where it's me or the showrunner making a judgment call that no checklist can absorb — but even there, tell me which way you'd jump and what's driving it. I'd also like a short note at the end on where the procedure is likely to misfire, the cases it was never really designed for.
