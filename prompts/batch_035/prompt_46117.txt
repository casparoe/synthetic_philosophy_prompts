I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how US federal courts decide what counts as "real" science in the courtroom. Ever since the Daubert decision in 1993, trial judges are supposed to act as gatekeepers for expert testimony, and the factors they're told to weigh are roughly: is the theory or technique testable, has it been peer reviewed and published, does it have a known error rate, and is it generally accepted in the relevant field. What I keep tripping over is that these factors seem to point in different directions. Testability sounds like Popper's falsificationism straight up -- a theory is scientific only if it could, in principle, be proven wrong. But peer review and general acceptance are basically sociological criteria: they ask what the scientific community happens to believe, which is exactly what a Popperian would say shouldn't settle anything, since a whole field can be organized around an unfalsifiable dogma. And the track record backs up the worry -- for decades courts kept letting in bite-mark analysis and other pattern-matching forensics, presumably because they were "generally accepted" and had been peer reviewed in a loose sense, while those same fields had barely any error-rate data at all. So my question for you: what view of science -- what demarcation criterion -- does the Daubert framework actually presuppose, if any? Is it a watered-down falsificationism, a "trust the community" view, an engineer's reliability view, or an incoherent mix? Can you reconstruct whatever implicit philosophy of science is holding it together, tell me which parts of the practice strictly depend on that philosophy and which parts are just compatible with several, and give me some sense of what courtroom gatekeeping would look like if courts were honest about adopting a purely Popperian test -- or a purely sociological one -- instead of the blend they've got now? Also happy to hear whether you think the blend is actually more defensible than either pure view, which is a suspicion I can't quite shake but can't argue for either.
