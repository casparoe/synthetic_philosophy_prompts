I'm starting a paper on the moral equality of combatants and want the specification fixed before I argue for any position: what must a theory of the ethics of killing in war deliver to count as adequate? Fix the purpose two ways, since adequacy is relative to it — a theory good enough to ground what soldiers may be blamed and punished for, and a theory good enough for the individual conscript deciding whether to fight at all. Give me the requirements list: the cases it must get right (unjust combatants who fight strictly by the rules; conscripts with no meaningful choice; civilians who voted for the war and now assemble its munitions; the just soldier ordered to do something noxious that would shorten the war), the distinctions it must respect or explicitly refuse to collapse (combatant/civilian vs. liable/not liable; doing vs. allowing; individual vs. collective responsibility for a state's war), and the constraints it cannot contradict (consistency with domestic self-defense, no verdict that makes every war unjust by fiat, some answer to why the conscript who fights and the taxpayer who funds are treated differently). With each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp. Most importantly, tell me whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect liability-based individualism, robust civilian immunity, and the datum that ordinary unjust soldiers are not murderers cannot all be true together, and if so the list itself is the paper. Then one short paragraph on how the traditionalist picture (Walzer), McMahan's revisionism, and a conventionalist defense of the war convention score against it; I care about the criteria, not the scorecard. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble, no restating my question. And if the two purposes I've fixed yield lists no single theory can serve, or if my framing smuggles in an answer — say, by presupposing that adequacy for blame is the primary purpose — say so plainly rather than reconciling them politely.
