I'm preparing a discussion paper for a seminar on moral luck, and instead of the usual philosophers' hypotheticals I want to work through a real historical case: Giuseppe Zangara's attempt on Franklin Roosevelt's life in Miami in February 1933. The salient facts as I understand them — please take them as given rather than fact-checking them: Roosevelt, then president-elect, had just given an impromptu speech from the back of an open car in Bayfront Park; Zangara, an unemployed Italian immigrant bricklayer with what seems to have been a diffuse hatred of rulers and the wealthy, fired five shots with a cheap revolver; five people were hit, none of them Roosevelt; among them was Chicago's mayor Anton Cermak, who died of his wounds some weeks later; Zangara pleaded guilty and was executed within about five weeks of the shooting. He was apparently indifferent about which prominent person he hit — at trial he presented his grievance as being against presidents and the powerful in general, not against Roosevelt personally.

I'd like an analysis of this episode through the classic moral luck framework of Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams. Please map the case carefully. Which of Nagel's four kinds of luck are present, and where? Resultant luck looks obvious — the same firing produces deaths versus wounds depending on the gun, the crowd, and chance — but say something about why this episode makes resultant luck unusually clean: the agent's intention and action are held fixed across the outcomes, and the identity of the victim looks almost like a lottery draw. Circumstantial luck is there too: had the attempt come two weeks later, the post-inauguration Secret Service cordon would have been different; had it come two weeks earlier, Roosevelt would not yet have been president-elect at all. Then the harder questions. First, does the legal system's treatment of Zangara embarrass the control principle — the principle that people are properly blamed only for what is within their control? He was executed either way, but the charge, the historical label, and the weight of the stigma differ enormously between "murderer of a mayor" and "assassin of a president-elect," and the difference turns on nothing he controlled. Second, the consequentialist dimension: had one of the stray bullets killed Roosevelt instead, John Nance Garner would plausibly have become president in the depths of the Depression, with the New Deal undone before it began — so the stakes of the very same gunshot vary by orders of magnitude with pure luck. Does that make Zangara more blameworthy, less blameworthy, or neither, and what should each answer say about the value of outcome-based desert?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory of moral luck itself? My own suspicion is that it's a cleaner confirmation of the puzzle than the standard two-driver example, because Zangara's indifference to which ruler he killed flattens out the intention differences that usually contaminate real cases. But I can see the opposite argument too: precisely because his intention was so uniform and his aim so poor, his killing of Cermak is close to pure resultant luck, and if our intuitions still treat the death as his doing, that may be revealing something about the difference between an agent's responsibility for an act and for an outcome, or between causal contribution and luck. Please be disciplined about where the framework is silent — for instance, about the questions surrounding his mental state, the speed of his trial, or whether he understood himself as killing a particular person at all — and say so plainly rather than forcing the theory to speak where it cannot.
