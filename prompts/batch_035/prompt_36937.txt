I run a discussion section for a graduate seminar on the ethics of idealizing away people's stated preferences, and I want a decision-tree handout that sorts students (and the authors we read) by what they actually believe, not by which label they self-apply. The tree should start from the shared starting point that an agent — a benevolent AI, a paternalistic state, a trustee deciding for a temporarily incompetent patient — should act on what people would want under some improvement or idealization of their current selves, rather than on their present, possibly confused preferences. Each branch node should be a question answerable without knowing any position's name, and the nodes should carve the logical space: whose preferences are in scope (one person, a bounded group, everyone); what the idealization consists of (full information, calm reflection, logical coherence, moral improvement, or some conjunction); whether the process is substantive (it changes what you want) or merely procedural (it only removes errors in getting what you already want); how conflict among idealized preferences is handled (aggregation, veto, further idealization, or declaring some conflicts beyond the theory); and whether the output is a set of preferences to be satisfied or a judgment to be deferred to. Leaves should include coherent extrapolated volition as originally proposed for AI alignment, full-information or ideal-advisor accounts of a person's good, preference-satisfaction theories of well-being that idealize only minimally, and at least two leaves you construct from the tree itself rather than from the literature, marked as such. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and note at least one unoccupied region of the space — a consistent combination of node answers that no one, to your knowledge, defends — with a sentence on whether that gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Eight to twelve leaves, phrased so that a student answering the nodes honestly ends up somewhere that surprises them.
