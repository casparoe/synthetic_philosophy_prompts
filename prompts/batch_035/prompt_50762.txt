So I'm in a situation that's been bugging me for weeks. I volunteer with this campus group that restores little urban wetlands — mostly ripping out invasive plants, replanting natives, trying to get amphibians to come back to this one drainage basin behind the chemistry building. I'm also taking a conservation biology course this semester, so I actually know some of the ecology involved, and I love this stuff. Anyway, over fall break my cousin (he's super into effective altruism, reads all the forums) told me that what I'm doing might be bad, net. His argument: a healthy wetland supports an absolutely enormous number of small animals — insects, tadpoles, minnows — and the overwhelming majority of them die young, painfully, mostly by being eaten or starving. He says a single healthy marsh might contain tens of thousands of individual vertebrates and millions of insects at any moment, with most offspring dying within days of hatching, so if you count each individual's experiences equally, more habitat means more total suffering, and a parking lot is arguably an improvement. He also pointed out that the ones who survive to adulthood still live with parasites, injuries, fear of predation, and cold. So my "restoration" is, on his accounting, basically a suffering factory.

I don't think I fully buy it, but I couldn't give him a real answer, just a lot of "but wetlands are beautiful" and "biodiversity matters," which he correctly pointed out is question-begging — it assumes the value without showing why it outweighs the suffering. Since then I keep going back and forth. Part of me wants to say that death in a wetland isn't bad for the animal the way it is for us, or that the ecosystem as a whole has some kind of positive welfare that isn't just the sum of individual moments, but I honestly don't know if I believe that or just want it to be true. And there's a darker thought I can't shake: if he's even roughly right about the numbers, does it matter whether I like wetlands?

So here's what I want from you: tell me whether I should keep volunteering or not, and actually commit to an answer rather than giving me "reasonable people disagree." Give me your best overall verdict on whether creating/restoring habitat like this plausibly increases or decreases net suffering, with your actual reasoning, and be upfront about which parts of your argument are solid and which parts you're taking on faith. If you think the whole framework he's using is broken somewhere — like the way you count minds, or whether early death is a harm, or whether you can sum welfare across a pond — say where it breaks and what you'd put in its place, not just that it's "contested."
