I spent most of my career in condensed-matter physics before retiring, and I have to confess that the philosophers' attitude toward the Church-Turing thesis strikes me as hand-wringing over something that, in my old line of work, we would long since have called settled. As I understand the history, Church, Turing, Kleene, and Post each proposed a precise definition of effective calculability in the 1930s; these definitions were then proven mathematically equivalent; and every alternative model proposed since — register machines, cellular automata, quantum circuits, even the idealizations behind analog chaos-computation proposals — has turned out to compute exactly the same class of functions, give or take polynomial overhead. By any standard I ever applied at the bench, a hypothesis that survives nearly a century of adversarial attempts to break it counts as confirmed. Yet the philosophers insist the thesis is neither provable nor empirically testable, and the field seems split between those who say Penrose's Gödelian arguments about human mathematical understanding refute the thesis outright and those who say those arguments are simply beside the point. So I have three questions. First, why do mathematicians refuse to call the thesis a theorem when the equivalence proofs are on the books — what exactly is supposed to remain unproven once Turing machines and lambda calculus and partial recursive functions have been shown coextensive? Second, does quantum computation give us any genuine reason to suspect that some physically calculable function lies outside the Turing-computable class, or does the speedup story leave the thesis untouched? Third, and this is what I most want to know, what would even count as a falsifier: what observation or experimental result — say, a physical system whose input-output behavior demonstrably outran every Turing machine — would force you to concede that the thesis has genuine empirical content that could have gone false? Please also tell me where in your own answer you think your reasoning is most vulnerable, and what argument or finding would move you to the opposite verdict on whether the thesis really occupies the peculiar epistemic status philosophers claim for it, since I am well aware that I may simply be failing to see where my laboratory habits about confirmation mislead me when applied to a definitional question.
