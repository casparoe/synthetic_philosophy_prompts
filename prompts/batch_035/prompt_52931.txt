I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, and I'd like a reading of Orwell's 1984 through a question that puzzles me partly because of my day job. In the systems I build, the record is the source of truth: if the database says an event happened, it happened, and anything in memory — caches, replicas, a stale node's state — is expected to defer to the log, and letting memory overwrite the record is what we'd call corruption. Orwell, it struck me on a recent rereading, builds an entire society on precisely the reverse policy: the record is continuously rewritten, and individual and communal memory are required to fall in line, with the mismatch itself made criminal.

Please read the novel through the lens of collective memory versus history — when a community's memory and the record conflict, which should give way? I'd want the reading anchored in specific scenes rather than slogans: Winston's job at the Ministry of Truth, where 'correction' means the record always wins; the moment where he once held in his hands a photograph proving the confessions of Jones, Aaronson, and Rutherford were false, and later finds himself doubting his memory of it; his diary line early in the book — "If there is hope," he had written, "it lies in the proles" — which seems to locate historical truth in a class with memory but no records; and his conversation with the old man in the pub, who remembers the pre-Revolution world but can produce nothing usable from it, only fragments about beer and top hats.

My questions are these. Does the novel take a position on which should give way when memory and record conflict, or does it show that the question is badly framed — that memory without records cannot function as evidence, and records without a community to remember them are just paper? And does my engineering analogy do any real work — the regime as a system that keeps rewriting its own log — or does it break down in ways worth spelling out, for instance that in my systems the log-writer is assumed trustworthy and external to the data, which is exactly what the novel puts in doubt? I'd like a reading that is faithful to the novel's actual scenes and honest about where it resists a tidy interpretation. Since neither of us can consult the text, please paraphrase scenes rather than attempting verbatim quotation.
