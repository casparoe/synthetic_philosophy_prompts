My flatmate thinks he's sunk the entire fine-tuning debate with one observation about likelihoods, and I can't find the exit.

(1) If an observation O is much more probable given hypothesis H than given not-H, then O is evidence for H. That's just the likelihood idea, and nobody in the Bayesian tradition disputes it. (2) Let O be: the physical constants fall in the narrow life-permitting range instead of anywhere else in the much larger space. On design, O is reasonably probable — a designer who wanted observers would tune things roughly like this. On single-universe chancy physics, O is astronomically improbable. (3) So fine-tuning is evidence for design. That's the standard setup, fine so far.

(4) But notice nothing in the reasoning mentioned any specific designer. Consider H*: a commitee of seventeen omnipotent beings tuned the constants by majority vote, they happened to land on exactly these values, and they've agreed to hold them fixed for the next 10^40 years. On H*, the probability of O is 1 — strictly better than generic design gives you. (5) So by (1), O is evidence for H*, and in fact stronger evidence than for plain design. (6) And you can build a hypothesis like this for any observation at all: for whatever happens, there's a bespoke hypothesis on which it had probability 1, so by (1) everything is evidence for some gerrymanderd story. (7) If confirmation worked like that it would be useless — every observation confirms some tailor-made hypothesis, and there's no filter anywhere in steps (1)-(6) that ranks plain design over the committee. (8) Therefore (1) is false: a high likelihood alone can't make an observation evidence for a hypothesis. His moral: fine-tuning was never evidence for design, and the whole debate runs on a broken notion of confirmation.

I don't want to accept (8), because I rely on likelihood reasoning everywhere else and I don't see how it could be right there but wrong here.
