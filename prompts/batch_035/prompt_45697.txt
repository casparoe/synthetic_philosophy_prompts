I'm giving a talk next month at an interdisciplinary workshop — roughly half epistemologists, half people from HCI/ML who work on conversational agents and get visibly impatient when someone starts using virtue-epistemology vocabulary. My thesis, in brief: the "sycophancy" problem in AI assistants (the assistant that folds the moment a user pushes back on a correct answer) is the mirror image of the old dogmatism paradox. The paradox, as I'll present it: if a subject knows that p, she's supposedly within her epistemic rights to dismiss any apparent counterevidence to p without investigating it — which makes it look like a knower could never rationally give up what she knows in the face of new evidence. Flip the polarity and you get the sycophantic assistant: a system trained to treat every user disagreement as automatic counterevidence becomes structurally unable to stand behind anything it "knows," which is just as epistemically broken as the dogmatist is. My claim is that the standard moves in that literature — the Sosa-style idea that counterevidence only gets a grip when you don't already properly know, relevant-alternatives restrictions on what needs checking, Harman's old point that knowledge matters because it licenses relying on p in reasoning and action — each correspond to a different design policy for when an assistant should hold firm against a user, and each policy fails somewhere. The relevant-alternatives one, for example, seems to license flat-out ignoring the user on well-worn topics while giving no guidance at all on novel ones. I want to argue that the line between stubborness and spine can't be drawn purely inside epistemology — it needs facts about how reliable this particular user is as an information channel in this context, which I expect the epistemologists in the room will hate.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet for this. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this particular audience — the epistemologists will press on whether I've understood the Sosa/Harman dialectic correctly or just rebranded their sixty-year-old debate with a chatbot sticker on it, and the ML people will press on whether "knowledge" even applies to a system whose confidence is miscalibrated and who has no stakes — ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that subgroup would actually phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Also flag the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. I suspect it's the one about whether my "user as unreliable channel" move just collapses into ordinary Bayesian conditioning, in which case all the dogmatism-paradox machinery does no real work in my talk — but tell me if you think there's a worse one lurking.
