I teach a first-year seminar on ancient philosophy — fifteen students, ninety minutes for a special session, and no alcohol permitted on campus — and I want an in-class activity built around a contrast we'll have just read: the Republic forbids the guardians wine on the ground that their self-control must never be put at risk, while the Laws reverses course and has the Athenian Stranger propose that the city deliberately use wine, at supervised drinking parties run by a sober chairman, as an instrument for revealing and training character — the theory being that drunkenness is a cheap, reversible way to see who is genuinely brave, modest, and gentle underneath, and that a soul never observed in its fearless, loosened state is a soul the city knows nothing about. I want the students to run into this contrast themselves before I name it. Please take the role of an experienced designer of experiential pedagogy for philosophy courses: begin with a few minutes of brainstorming possible designs, tell me which you would reject and why, and then give me the full plan — rules, roles, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and exactly what participants are told at each stage, noting that they may not be told the point of any of it up front. The disinhibition has to come from something other than alcohol: improv tasks, singing, playful mockery, awkward personal questions, small consensual dares — some mechanism that genuinely lowers inhibition and risks real, if mild, embarrassment while staying safe and legal. Structure it in two phases: the group first operates under strict ceremonial formality, and then under a licensed loosening, with one student cast as the sober symposiarch, empowered to regulate the escalation and even expel a participant, while the others either perform or observe and take notes on changes in one another's character — vanity, irritability, generosity, meanness — feeding into a closing exercise in which small groups draft laws about which classmates they would trust with which civic offices, partly on the strength of what they witnessed.

Then design the debrief so it maps specific moments from the activity onto the parts of the view: the Republic's implicit insistence that if training is good enough there is nothing worth testing, versus the Laws' insistence that a virtue never exposed to risk may be nothing at all; the idea of the loosening agent as a drug that educates or corrupts depending entirely on the regime governing it; and the odd detail that in the Laws it is the oldest citizens, long past any call to courage, who are licensed to sing and drink. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students faking disinhibition, the symposiarch getting drunk on authority, someone ending up genuinely humiliated — and how to handle each case, and be frank about the feature of Plato's idea that no such activity can reproduce: his claim that repeated, controlled exposure trains the disposition rather than merely displaying it, which I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. Since they will have read the Republic but not yet the Laws, also advise me on how much of the Laws' position I should hold back until afterwards, and on how to keep the closing law-drafting exercise from sliding into students simply ranking each other's popularity.
