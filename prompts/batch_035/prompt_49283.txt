I'm preparing the closing session of a seminar on just war theory that pairs historical sources with modern cases, and this week the source is Thomas Aquinas on the killing of rulers. As I read him, he distinguishes, roughly, between a tyrant by usurpation — someone who seizes a power he has no right to — and a tyrant by oppression, a legitimate holder of power who rules against the common good, and while he grants that slaying a tyrant can be licit in principle, he insists the judgment belongs to public authority rather than private individuals, and he attaches a heavy prudential warning that such attempts usually provoke worse disorder. His own examples, though, are simple ones: bad kings and seized thrones. My session is meant to stress-test that framework on political situations with no thirteenth-century analogue, and I want the students to see how the theory behaves at its edges rather than in the cases it was built for.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion, each item one or two sentences long. The five items are fixed: (1) a leader who took office through elections that were formally free but held amid pervasive bribery and capture of the press, and who then uses entirely lawful instruments — appointments, prosecutions, emergency powers on the books — to hollow out the courts: is he a tyrant by usurpation or by oppression in Aquinas's scheme, and does that classification change whether killing him is permissible? (2) a foreign state's intelligence service, rather than any domestic body, being the only actor with the capacity and will to act: can it ever hold the public authority Aquinas demands, or does that requirement categorically exclude outsiders, whatever their motives? (3) a precision strike on the tyrant in a city center, where he reliably appears surrounded by aides and bystanders: apply the double-effect reasoning as Aquinas would and state what it yields about the strike's permissibility. (4) the prudential caution about provoking worse disorder: in his hands is this a rebuttable presumption or something close to an absolute practical bar, and where would he land in a state with resilient institutions and a lawful designated successor? (5) a tyrant who is broadly beloved, under whom the polity is prosperous and stable, but who oppresses only a small minority: does his account turn on the common good being harmed overall, or on any group of the community being denied its share of it, and what follows for this case?

Throughout, I want committed reconstruction, not surveys of the possibilities: for each item give the answer Aquinas would most plausibly give, along with the single strongest reason for it, and where you have to extrapolate beyond what his texts settle, still commit to one reading rather than laying out alternatives for the students to choose between. If a commitment depends on a contestable interpretation of him, flag that within the same sentence or two — but flag it and commit anyway.
