I sit on the grants committee of a mid-sized private research foundation that disperses roughly $15 million a year in small awards, most under $200,000, across the life sciences. Our new director is an enthusiast for futarchy — Robin Hanson's scheme in which prediction markets settle factual questions while trustee and committee deliberation settles the value questions — and she has persuaded the board to consider a two-year pilot. As she describes it: for each proposal, alongside the usual peer review, we would open an internal market on whether the project, if funded, would hit a designated measurable target within two years (a follow-on grant, a registered trial, an independently verified benchmark result). The markets would run on a logarithmic market scoring rule so that the foundation subsidizes liquidity; every staff member and committee member would receive a capped annual token budget so that no one can dominate prices; and program officers sponsoring the work would be allowed to trade, the theory being that they hold exactly the local knowledge the mechanism is meant to harvest. Wherever a market's closing price placed a proposal's success probability more than twenty points below the committee's sealed median estimate, the proposal would be declined or returned regardless of review scores. The director's flagship argument is that peer review is where prestige bias and motivated reasoning do their worst, and that forcing people to back their opinions with scarce tokens is the cheapest discipline we will ever buy.

Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before the March meeting — recommend adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions, with reasons the director could actually respond to, and a candid statement of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses. In drafting it, please take seriously at least three points the pilot's boosters have not thought through. First, the choice of measurable target looks like a technicality but does all the values work: which outcomes count as success is precisely the question futarchy assigns to non-market judgment, so the pilot relocates our hardest problem rather than solving it, and grantees will optimize whatever metric we pick. Second, our traders are not the profit-seeking speculators the mechanism assumes: they are employees with career stakes, friendships, and sincere commitments to particular research programs, holding small budgets in thin subsidized markets — conditions under which prices are cheap to move for reasons unrelated to accuracy, and in which the profit available to a correcting trader is too small to attract one. Third, whatever you recommend, the memo should specify what would count, two years from now, as evidence that the recommendation was right, so that it functions as a self-assessing document rather than a one-off policy memo.
