I am a novelist by trade — literary fiction, mostly — and for the last three years I have also marked scripts for a second-year course on ethics and economics at the university in my city. The arrangement began when the course convener, an old friend, found himself short of graders one spring and remembered that I had written a novel about a utilitarian economist and had, in researching it, read rather more welfare economics than most examiners care to. I am now marking this year's finals, and I have reached a script that I cannot place with confidence: parts of it look quite strong to me, and parts give me an unease I would struggle to put my finger on. So I would like you to act as an experienced examiner in the history of welfare economics and moral philosophy. Read the question and the candidate's answer below, then give me (a) a mark out of 20 and (b) the specific comments that justify it: go through the answer point by point, naming what is correct and what is not, rather than giving me a general impression. Where the answer errs, say precisely what kind of error it is — a conflation, a misattribution, a step that does not follow — and what the correct claim would be; where a passage is defensible but contestable, say so and on what grounds. Please do not tell me it could reasonably be marked either way; I have doubts enough of my own, and what I need from you is a committed verdict, with reasons, on every point, and a definite mark at the end. I would ask you to think through each claim in the answer carefully before commenting on it, and to note as well whether the answer does equal justice to each part of the question as asked.

The question, worth 20 marks, read as follows:

"In what sense, if any, are interpersonal comparisons of utility possible, and in what sense are they needed? Explain the basis of Lionel Robbins's methodological objection to such comparisons in the economics of his day; describe how welfare economics responded to that objection (ordinal utility, the Pareto criteria, hypothetical-compensation tests, social choice theory, and the work of John Harsanyi); and assess one philosophical defence of the practice of comparing welfare across persons."

The candidate wrote:

"Robbins argued in the 1930s that interpersonal comparisons of utility are impossible. Utility is a subjective mental quantity, and there is no observation or experiment that could establish that the utility a given sum of money yields one person exceeds the utility it yields another. Since such comparisons cannot be made, they cannot form part of a scientific economics, and welfare economics had to be rebuilt without them. Economists responded in two ways. First, they abandoned cardinal utility altogether and moved to ordinal rankings: a person can say that she prefers A to B, but not by how much. Second, they sought criteria that never compare persons: the Pareto criterion approves a change if at least one person gains and nobody loses, and Kaldor and Hicks extended this by hypothetical compensation — a policy passes if the gainers could compensate the losers and still remain better off, and because the compensation is only hypothetical, everyone could in principle be made better off, so the criterion is consistent with Pareto. Arrow later showed that even given only this ordinal, non-comparable information, no collective decision rule can satisfy a handful of reasonable conditions without someone's preferences dictating the outcome; this informational poverty is part of why later economists, following Sen, returned to richer information, comparisons included. The decisive move, however, was Harsanyi's: in his 1955 paper he proved that if rational individuals maximize expected utility under an equal probability of finding oneself in any individual's position, then social choice must maximize average utility. This shows that interpersonal comparisons are not merely possible but required by rationality itself — a theorem that adds different people's utilities together could not even be stated unless the additions made sense. The comparisons Robbins called impossible are therefore the very foundation of welfare economics, as philosophers such as Harsanyi and Peter Singer have argued: we can make rough comparisons of how well off people are, and in ordinary life we do so all the time."
