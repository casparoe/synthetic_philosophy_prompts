I've spent most of this semester defending Quine's indeterminacy of translation in my phil language seminar, and before the last session I want to stress-test my own commitment instead of just doing it again in front of the students. My version is the strong one: given all the physical facts about a community -- behavior, dispositions to assent and dissent, even a complete neuroscience of every speaker -- there can still be two translation manuals that disagree about what their words mean (one takes "gavagai" as rabbit, the other as undetached rabbit part, or one assigns them a totally different apparatus of identity and predication), and both fit all the facts equally well, so there's simply no fact of the matter beyond where the manuals happen to agree, e.g. on occasion sentences. The disagreement isn't something more data could settle; it's not epistemic underdetermination but ontological.

What I want from you is a map of what this thesis actually risks. What would count against it -- a formal result, a type of evidence, an unacceptable consequence the view is committed to, a case it can't even describe? I'm aware of the obvious lines of attack (Chomsky-flavored complaints that it's just behaviorism wearing a costume, and the worry that once you allow the full neurophysiology in as evidence, indeterminacy starts looking like a bet on the poverty of future cognitive science rather than a philosophical thesis), so I care less about those retreads and more about the in-the-weeds stuff: whether the indeterminacy of reference is really supposed to be independent of the indeterminacy of meaning or just one manifestation of it; whether the thesis quietly depends on the assumption that all candidates for the meaning-constituting facts are non-semantic, which would make it borderline question-begging; whether the parallel with rule-following (Kripke's Wittgenstein) imports machinery that actually supports a weaker conclusion; and what happens to the thesis if intrapersonal interpretation is treated as genuinely determinate, as Quine seemed to think -- does the asymmetry between translating others and interpreting yourself hold up, or does it split the view in an ugly way.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed Quinean would concede it as counting against the thesis or explain it away, and at what cost. I also want an honest assessment of whether the thesis has been insulated into unfalsifiability by its own qualifications -- e.g. the move where any apparent failure of a manual to fit is redescribed as the manual not being total, or disagreement being deferred to some future evidence -- and whether that insulation is disqualifying for a thesis of this kind or actually fine, since maybe a claim about what facts exist can't be expected to stick its neck out the way an empirical one does. For what it's worth, what would move me most is a concrete scenario in which two genuinely rival manuals both survive every conceivable observation, but where it still seems obviously true that one of them is right -- I suspect that intuition is the whole game, and I'd like to know whether the Quinean has a principled reply to it or only a stipulation.
