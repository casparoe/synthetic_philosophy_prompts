For the past four years my closest friend and I have kept a running file of what we call standing cases: scenarios in which one person blames another, and the question is not whether the blame is accurate but whether the blamer is entitled to make it at all. We each record a verdict before comparing notes, and hers are strikingly stable — which makes me suspect they are generated by a small number of tacit principles. But she has read no philosophy and refuses to theorize, so the only way to learn her rule is to extrapolate from her record. Here is that record. A mutual acquaintance who has cheated on his wife for years, while shrugging off his own infidelities as complicated, delivered a savage condemnation of a friend's affair: no standing. A former colleague who embezzled in her twenties, was never caught, but later repaid the money with interest, apologized, and now describes her own past conduct with genuine disgust, condemned a peer caught embezzling: standing intact. A woman whose family wealth traces to her grandfather's ruthless labor practices blames a friend for buying from exploitative brands: standing intact. A man who gossips cruelly and constantly, and knows it, condemns a friend for plagiarizing an essay: standing intact. A distant acquaintance, on no personal terms with me or my mother, overheard me speak to her with real impatience and upbraided me for ingratitude: no standing — and when I suggested he was merely rude, she insisted the point was standing, that the blame was not his to make. A man who has never reproached anything graver than a cold dinner erupts in fury at a colleague's mildly teasing joke at his own expense: standing intact, though his sense of proportion is defective — the failing lies elsewhere. A stranger in a long taxi queue condemns another man who pushes to the front: standing intact. Three new cases have now come up. First: a man is still having his affair, but he is unsparing about his own conduct — he calls himself exactly what he would call anyone else who cheats — and with equal heat he condemns a close friend's newly revealed affair. Second: a manager has quietly padded her own expense claims for a decade, waving it off as something everybody does, and she now condemns, with full severity, a friend caught shoplifting goods of comparable value. Third: an old college friend of a husband, no longer in contact with either spouse, learns independently of the wife's affair and condemns her, to her face, at a dinner party. Predict her verdict on each of the three. Then reconstruct the candidate principles that could generate all seven of her earlier verdicts — there is more than one, and part of the work is saying precisely where they come apart, including how broadly each reads the notion of the same fault and what exactly her acquaintance verdicts are responding to. Commit to your best reading of her rather than surveying the options: tell me which principle she is actually running, why it fits better than its rivals, and what each rival would predict where they diverge. Finally, design the single further case that would most cleanly separate the leading candidates — the one whose verdict would tell me, in one stroke, which rule she is obeying. I am not interested in a taxonomy that ends in it depends; I want definite predictions with reasons.
