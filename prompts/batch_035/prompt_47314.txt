My department runs a game theory reading group, and a colleague has walked me into an argument about Shubik's dollar auction that I can't get out of. In case the setup matters for what follows: a dollar is auctioned to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder also pays their final bid and receives nothing, and the auction ends only when one of the two remaining bidders declines to raise.

His argument:

(1) Rationality consists in choosing, at each point, the act with the better expected outcome from where you stand.
(2) Suppose I have bid 45 cents and my rival has just bid 50. If I quit now, I am out 45 cents for certain.
(3) If I bid 55 instead, there is a real chance the auction ends there and I finish up 45 cents, and my further exposure is only the 10-cent increment I have just added; even if the auction continues, my position is scarcely worse than it was.
(4) A certain loss of 45 cents is worse than a small gamble that offers a roughly comparable chance of a gain, so (2) and (3) together make bidding again the rational move.
(5) Nothing in that reasoning turns on the particular numbers: whatever my current bid and whatever the increment, the same comparison comes out in favor of bidding. So a rational bidder in a dollar auction never stops bidding.
(6) Two rational agents who never stop bidding will transfer unbounded sums to the auctioneer for a single dollar, and a conception of rationality under which rational agents predictably do that is not a conception of rationality at all.
(7) Therefore (1) is false, and there is no such thing as choosing rationally from where you stand.

He takes the moral to be that rationality is somehow perspective-dependent or self-undermining. I have three specific worries and no resolution of them. First, (2) smells like sunk-cost reasoning — the 45 cents is gone whether or not I bid again — but when I redo the comparison in properly forward-looking terms, the gamble still looks attractive whenever the chance of the rival quitting exceeds the tiny increment at stake, so I cannot tell whether the flaw lies in the accounting or in the stopping rule, or in neither. Second, (5) ought to be blocked by the fact that the rival runs the same argument in reverse, since the rival's willingness to quit is exactly what my calculation takes as given; whether that reflection yields an equilibrium, an infinite regress, or an immediate bid of a full dollar is beyond me. Third, real escalations — the situations this game is supposed to caricature, protracted conflicts, projects kept alive long past the point of sense — do not look like anyone making one clean mistake; they look like chains of individually defensible decisions, which is what frightens me about the possibility that the argument is simply valid.

So: where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does? Name the step or the inferential slide, explain why it looked fine, and tell me what it costs to reject it. I would rather keep (1) than abandon the idea of rational choice altogether, but I want to know what keeping it buys and what it doesn't. Finally, please keep separate what is genuinely settled in the analysis of this game and of all-pay auctions and wars of attrition generally from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — I have heard knowledgeable people state, with equal confidence, that rational players never bid at all and that rational play involves bidding up to the full dollar, and I do not want a contested reading dressed up as the textbook result.
