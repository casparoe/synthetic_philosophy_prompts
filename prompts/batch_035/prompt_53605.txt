I spent close to forty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and I have lately been filling my time with moral and political philosophy, working through the theories of punishment. The textbook opposition is clear enough to me on paper: retributivism holds that punishment is justified as an intrinsically deserved response to past wrongdoing, while consequentialism justifies it by its future effects — deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation. My own instinct, trained by decades of reasoning about measurable outcomes, is toward the consequentialist side, and I suspect that instinct is too quick. What I find hardest is applying these frameworks to a case in which punishment was deliberately withheld in exchange for something else, and I would like your help analyzing one such episode: the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission.

For orientation, here are the salient facts as I understand them. The Commission was established by legislation in 1995, after the end of apartheid, and chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, with hearings beginning in 1996. It offered individual perpetrators of politically motivated crimes amnesty from both criminal and civil liability, but only conditionally: the applicant had to make full public disclosure of the relevant facts, the act had to have had a political objective, and it had to have been proportionate to that objective. Both sides of the conflict fell within its jurisdiction — the apartheid security forces and the liberation movements alike — and victims gave testimony in hearings of their own. The explicit rationale was that without the amnesty incentive there would have been no peaceful transition at all, and that the public record of testimony was itself a good the country needed.

Treat this, please, as a case study that runs in both directions. First map each theory onto the Commission: what exactly does the consequentialist count in the ledger, and does the design — trading prosecution for disclosure — follow from that accounting? What does the retributivist make of it: is amnesty-for-truth simply the denial of deserved suffering, or can public confession and the acknowledgement of victims carry the expressive weight that retributivists ordinarily assign to punishment itself? Then tell me where each theory is silent or actively misdescribes the episode — for instance, whether either framework, taken strictly, has anything to say about the intrinsic value of the testimony, or about the awkward asymmetry that the worst offenders had the strongest incentive to apply. And finally, what does the episode show about the theories themselves: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case they never anticipated? Where a theory has nothing to say, say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak — I would rather have an honest account of the limits of the mapping than a tidy synthesis, and I would especially welcome it if you told me where my consequentialist instinct is leading me astray.
