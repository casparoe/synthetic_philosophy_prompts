At my university's debating society we recently argued about whether to refuse a platform to a student group that campaigns for the legal exclusion of a particular ethnic minority. The case for refusal was made, as usual, by appeal to Popper's paradox of tolerance: a society that tolerates everything, including movements dedicated to destroying tolerance, will find tolerance itself eliminated, so a tolerant society must be prepared not to tolerate the intolerant — in the last resort, Popper allowed, even by force, though he clearly wanted that held in reserve. That much I know; it's the standard move and I don't need it re-explained. What interested me was what came next. Our proposer went analogical: a tolerant society, he said, is like a living body. The body tolerates its own cells and does not fight them; but it has an immune system, and the immune system does not sit down and debate with a pathogen — it recognizes it and destroys it. A body that insisted on giving every invader a hearing would not be tolerant, it would be dead; tolerance of one's own parts presupposes a working immune system that keeps the organism alive. So the refusal of a platform is not an exception to tolerance but its enabling condition. A respondent then countered that the whole comparison is dehumanizing: the moment you call your political opponents a pathogen, you have already decided they are not interlocutors but foreign matter to be eliminated, and everything from censorship to worse has quietly been licensed in the choice of metaphor.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a repetition of either speech. Lay out the mapping explicitly: in the proposed correspondence, what plays the role of the organism, which parts are the tolerant cells, what is the immune system (the no-platforming committee? the criminal law? public opinion? — the analogy is silent here and I'd like to know whether that silence is fatal), what counts as the pathogen, and whether there is any room in it for things like inflammation, autoimmune disease, or fever, i.e. the costs and misfirings of the response itself. Then give me the places the mapping breaks, and tell me which of the breaks matter for the use the analogy was actually put to. My suspicion is that the load-bearing disanalogies are these: an immune system classifies without judgment, by mechanisms that did not themselves deliberate, whereas a society must decide — through fallible, contestable, sometimes partisan institutions — who is to count as a pathogen, and that classification is precisely what is in dispute; a pathogen has no claim to be heard, while citizens, even intolerant ones, ordinarily do; and the body's response is proportionate by nature, whereas institutional suppression can overshoot and, once built, does not stay aimed only at the original pathogen — the autoimmune worry. But I'd like you to test that suspicion rather than confirm it, and to note any breaks I have missed that are more damaging, or any that look like breaks but are actually harmless for this use.

Finally, the verdict. Does the conclusion the analogy was carrying — that refusing the platform is not a restriction of tolerance but the immune response tolerance requires — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Or does it depend on exactly the features of the body that a society lacks: automatic, incorruptible recognition of the dangerous and automatic confinement of the response to them? And if the body/immune-system picture fails as an argument, say whether some repaired version of it still teaches us something about the paradox of tolerance — for instance, by making vivid the risk that the machinery of intolerance-suppression becomes self-sustaining — or whether a different analogy entirely (a fire brigade, an immune-compromised patient, a club with rules of admission) would serve the debate better, and what it would change.
