The question should be pitched at an upper-level seminar audience, answerable in about sixty minutes by hand, and designed so that it cannot be answered by summarizing the literature. It should be built around a proposal that keeps coming up in our study group. We noticed that David Lewis's 1976 triviality proof in the Philosophical Review, on probabilities of conditionals and conditional probabilities, seems to require that the Equation — the thesis that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability — holds for every probability function defined over the algebra, and one of us thinks that is the weak point. His suggested fix: perhaps the Equation is only ever meant to hold for the credence function of a single, ideally rational agent at a time — the probabilities the agent actually assigns — rather than for arbitrary functions over the same algebra. Since the proof quantifies over probability functions generally, it would then simply fail to apply. The exam question should ask the student to evaluate this escape: reconstruct the proof carefully enough to say exactly where it needs probabilities beyond the agent's own, and then determine whether the escape works or collapses.

You may refer to positions and arguments by name where that helps, but the question must not ask for a bibliography, reading list, or citations, since we will be writing this by hand in a seminar room.
