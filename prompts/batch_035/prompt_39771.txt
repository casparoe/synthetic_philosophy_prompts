I sit on the editorial standards committee at a public broadcaster, and we have to rule on a docudrama that's already been shot and paid for. It's about a real woman who died in 1978 — call her by her actual role: a mid-century labor organizer whose strike leadership is well documented. The problem is what the writers did around the documented core. They invented a whole romantic subplot with a composite character (a company spy who's actually three real men merged), and they wrote a scene where she, on the night of a picket-line beating, decides not to warn a fellow organizer because she's demoralized — there's zero evidence this happened and her surviving letters suggest she did try to get word to people that night. The scene is genuinely good television, honestly, and it's doing obvious thematic work (the cost of leadership etc). One committee member wants it cut entirely, another thinks we're being precious because "the dead can't be defamed, that's why libel dies with the person," and a third keeps saying the emotional truth justifies the invention.

I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Draft it for me — it should recommend a concrete course of action (air as-is, cut specific elements, air with conditions like disclaimers or a framing device, or shelve it), with a time frame we can actually enforce. Some things I want it to actually grapple with rather than hand-wave:

- whether the dead have interests that can be set back at all (the usual worry being that you can't harm someone who no longer exists), and if they do, whether an invented act of cowardice is the kind of thing that sets them back versus, say, an invented romance
- the difference between composite/condensed characters (which basically every docudrama uses) and the invention of a specific morally-charged act, if there is a principled difference
- the fact that her granddaughter was consulted and signed off on the whole thing — does living-family endorsement speak for the dead, and does that change when the family is wrong about what she'd have wanted?
- the "emotional truth" defense: whether there's a version of it that isn't just "the invented scene was more dramatically convenient than any true one"

The strongest consideration on whatever side I end up losing should get a fair shake in the memo — the other committee members will read it, so I don't want a strawman. Keep it to something they can read in ten minutes. And end the memo with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase naming the recommendation, so I can pull the bottom line out at a glance. Also — if you spot a typo in the circulated version, that's mine, ignore it.
