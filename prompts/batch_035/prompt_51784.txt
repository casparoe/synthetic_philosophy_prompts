I'm a philosophy grad student who spent last semester sitting in on a behavioral economics course out of curiosity, and I keep running into the same fight whenever the two groups end up in the same room: whether hyperbolic discounting "solves" akrasia. The economists' side of it, roughly: with hyperbolic rather than exponential discount curves you get systematic preference reversals — a far-off bigger reward can be ranked above a sooner smaller one, but as the smaller one gets close, its value spikes and it wins. So at the moment of action the smoker genuinely ranks the cigarette above the health payoff. There's even a literature that goes further, treating the agent as a sequence of temporally distinct selves whose interests conflict, and willpower as something like strategic bargaining between them. Akrasia, on this picture, is just dynamically inconsistent preference — an ordinary, well-understood feature of the curves, not a paradox about the will.

The philosophers, meanwhile, insist this at best explains the pattern of choices and doesn't touch the actual puzzle, which goes back to Socrates pressing Protagoras on how someone can, at the moment of acting, still be able to avow that all things considered the other option is worse, avow it sincerely, and act anyway. The discounting story explains why the near-term option wins the bidding, but the puzzle is about how the evaluative judgment survives intact while being motivationally inert, and a value curve over goods doesn't seem to have anywhere to put that. There was a move a while back to reframe weakness of will as abandoning a resolution rather than acting against one's better judgment, and the philosophers liked that, but the economists just shrugged — resolutions, they said, are also just preferences with their own time constants, so the same model swallows them too. When someone brought up the neuroimaging work where limbic regions light up disproportionately for sooner rewards, one of the philosophers called it a relabeling, not an explanation, and that's kind of where it died. This has been going in circles for years as far as I can tell, and it doesn't look like anyone has moved.

My own working theory, and I offer it as just a guess: the two camps mean different things by "irrational." For the economists it's a formal property — you're irrational when your preferences are inconsistent across times in a particular way. For the philosophers it's a property of agency — you fail to be governed by your own reflective assessment of what you have most reason to do. If that's right, the hyperbolic model solves their problem and was never aimed at ours, and the whole argument is two questions traveling under one name. But I'm honestly not sure, because the philosophers also keep claiming the economic model makes some kind of factual error — that it can't distinguish weakness of will from compulsion, say, or that sincere avowal at the moment of action doesn't really fit the preference-reversal story — and if any of those complaints is right, it's a substantive disagreement after all, not a verbal one. So: diagnose this for me. Is it verbal, is it two different questions under one heading, is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, or is it a genuine substantive dispute that's just hard? And whatever your diagnosis is, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction someone should draw, an experiment, a reformulation of the question — or whether I should just accept that this particular argument runs forever and find something better to do with my Tuesdays.
