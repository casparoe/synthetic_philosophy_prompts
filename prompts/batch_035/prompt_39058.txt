In debates about sentencing, people constantly lean on the idea that prison is how an offender pays a debt to society. In my criminal justice seminar, we heard it used for three different conclusions at once: that sentences must be proportionate to the crime, that a sentence should not be cut short for good behavior, and that someone who has served their time deserves a clean slate — the debt is discharged and no further handicap is warranted. My professor treats the debt picture as philosophically serious; a friend who works in sentencing policy thinks it's a folk metaphor that collapses on inspection: there is no identifiable creditor, nothing is actually paid to anyone, debts are ordinarily incurred voluntarily while crimes are not, debts can be transferred or settled for cash or paid by someone else, and creditors can simply forgive. Can you lay out the correspondence table — who or what plays the creditor, the principal, the interest, the repayment, the default, the discharge — and then sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the three conclusions and those that don't? In particular, I'd like to know whether the same analogy can really carry both the no-early-release conclusion and the clean-slate conclusion, or whether the debt picture pulls in opposite directions there. And if the metaphor doesn't survive, does the retributivist proportionality it was supposed to support survive without it, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time?
