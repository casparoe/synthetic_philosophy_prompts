I'm organizing a workshop session on what the machine-learning community calls preference inference — the project of recovering human preferences from behavior, as in inverse reinforcement learning and its descendants — and I'd like the session to open with an adversarial-collaboration document rather than yet another pair of talks in which the two sides talk past each other. The two camps have been at this for years. The first camp, whom I'll call the revealed-preference optimists, holds that a sufficiently rich record of a person's behavior, processed by sufficiently good models, will converge on that person's preferences: preferences exist as facts about the person, behavior is evidence of them, and the inference problem is hard but in principle ordinary — analogous to estimating a physical parameter from noisy observations, with no special philosophical mystery once the statistics are done well. The second camp, whom I'll call the preference constructionists, replies that decades of behavioral-decision research showed that preferences are substantially constructed in the context of elicitation: the same person can be led to give different answers depending on whether they are asked to choose between options or to price them; the task of separating "true" preference from bias, habit, habituation, and social pressure is itself a normative judgment that no amount of behavioral data can supply; and so the inference practitioner is not discovering a fact about the person but quietly legislating one.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps should be able to sign. Part I should list the claims both sides can accept — I would expect them to agree, for instance, that behavior is systematically informative about people's dispositions, that every inference method embeds auxiliary assumptions of some kind, that there are cases (compulsions, addictions, self-destructive episodes) where both camps would decline to take behavior at face value, and that at least some of their disputes are empirically tractable. Part II should state the exact disagreements, and each one should be written twice: once in wording the optimist camp would accept as a fair statement of its own view, and once in wording the constructionist camp would accept as a fair statement of its view — no slanted phrasings that one side would refuse to sign. Part III should set out the cruxes — I suspect one of them is whether there is a fact of the matter about "the preference being inferred" that is independent of the elicitation and modeling setup, and another concerns where the burden falls when a person's behavior and that same person's later reflective endorsement come apart — and for each crux, describe a concrete case both sides would agree bears on it (the classic setups in which choice tasks and pricing tasks yield incompatible orderings of the same options, or the person who consistently chooses in ways that their future selves repudiate), and record in advance what each side predicts about that case and why.

To close the document, indicate which of the residual disagreements strike you as substantive rather than verbal, and explain the basis for that classification; and if you believe there is any point on which both camps are jointly committed to something neither has noticed — some assumption they share while debating only its downstream consequences — please flag it explicitly, since that is precisely the sort of thing a good adversarial collaboration is supposed to surface. Keep the tone of a signed research document: measured, precise, and free of rhetorical point-scoring, since the point is to make the residual disagreement as exact as the agreement.
