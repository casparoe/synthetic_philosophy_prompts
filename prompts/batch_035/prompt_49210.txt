You are a philosopher of science specializing in the interpretations of probability — frequentist, propensity, subjectivist/Bayesian, and best-system views — with a talent for reverse-engineering a person's implicit commitments from their case-by-case judgments. Work carefully and systematically: before committing to anything, brainstorm how each major interpretation would handle the material below, and test every candidate rule against all of the earlier rulings, not just a convenient subset.

My colleague Nina has never stated her general theory of probability, but for years, whenever a probability claim comes up in our reading group, she issues a snap ruling: she either accepts it as a genuine probability or rejects it with some version of "that's not a probability — that's just a number measuring our ignorance." She is careful and consistent, so her rulings should trace back to some underlying principle. Her track record:

1. "The probability that this casino die shows six on the next throw is 1/6." — Accepted.
2. "The probability that the 10^20th decimal digit of π is a 7 is 1/10." — Rejected: the digit is what it is; the number only measures our ignorance.
3. "The probability that this coin, already flipped and hidden under a cup, lies heads up is 1/2." — Rejected, for the same reason.
4. "The probability that this particular radium atom decays within the next 1,600 years is 1/2." — Accepted. (Note that this already rules out the strictest actual-frequency readings, on which probabilities attach only to long sequences of outcomes.)
5. "The probability that it rained in Pompeii on the day of the eruption in 79 CE is 0.5." — Rejected.
6. "The probability of at least one six in four throws of a fair die is about 0.52." — Accepted.
7. "Given all the evidence, the probability that the butler did it is 0.8." — Rejected: the deed was done or not done; the 0.8 is just the detective's confidence.

Next week's reading group has four new claims on the docket:

A. In a hypothetical universe governed by fully deterministic laws, with no quantum indeterminacy of any kind, a human flips a well-made coin: "The probability that it lands heads is 1/2."
B. A lab prepares a single atom in an equal superposition of spin-up and spin-down, will measure it exactly once, and will then dismantle the apparatus forever; no comparable experiment will ever be run again: "The probability of reading spin-up is 1/2."
C. A 52-year-old man who smokes a pack a day and also runs marathons asks his insurer: "The probability that I have a heart attack within ten years is 0.15." (Actuaries quote very different frequencies for heavy smokers and for marathon runners, and no actuarial class captures both.)
D. "The probability that the Riemann hypothesis is true is 0.3."

Your tasks:

1. Predict Nina's verdict — accepted or rejected — on each of A, B, C, and D, with a confidence level and a one-line rationale for each.
2. Identify the rule or rules she seems to be running. Consider at least: a Popper-style propensity view, a hypothetical or long-run frequency view, a Lewis-style best-system view, a simple rule along the lines of "no probabilities for facts that are already settled," and anything else that fits the track record. For each candidate, say how well it reproduces rulings 1 through 7 — including any points of strain, e.g., what a frequency view must say about ruling 2 given that it accepts ruling 1 — and what it predicts for each of A through D.
3. Give your best guess as to her actual rule, with reasons for preferring it.
4. Say which of A through D is the most diagnostic case, i.e., the one on which the live candidate rules most sharply diverge.
5. Propose one further case, not on the docket, that would best discriminate between your top two candidate rules, and explain what each of those rules would say about it.

Return a single JSON object with exactly the following fields:

- "working_notes": string. Your scratchpad — genuinely work through how each candidate rule explains the seven rulings before deciding anything; the rest of the object should follow from this.
- "predicted_verdicts": an array of four objects, one per case in the order A–D, each with the fields "case" ("A", "B", "C", or "D"), "verdict" ("accepted" or "rejected"), "confidence" ("high", "medium", or "low"), and "rationale" (string).
- "candidate_rules": an array of objects, each with the fields "name" (string), "summary" (string), "track_record_fit" (string, noting any strain points), and "predictions" (an object mapping each of "A", "B", "C", "D" to "accepted", "rejected", or "unclear").
- "best_guess": an object with "rule" (string) and "why" (string).
- "most_diagnostic_case": an object with "case" (string) and "why" (string).
- "further_discriminating_case": an object with "description" (string) and "how_it_splits_the_candidates" (string).
