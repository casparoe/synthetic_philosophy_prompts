I'm a high school junior taking AP Physics and an elective on philosophy of science, and last week two members of my lab group got into an argument that I, as the third member, was asked to referee — and honestly couldn't. I'd like you to take on the role of a philosopher of science, think through each step of their exchange carefully before writing, and adjudicate it move by move. As you do, please clearly separate what is settled among people who actually study this topic from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested position as if it were the consensus of the field.

The argument began over our pendulum lab. We had plotted the square of the period against string length and got a mostly straight line with some scatter. Maya fitted a line through the points and declared the relationship confirmed. Dev, partly to be provocative, then drew a degree-eight polynomial that passed exactly through every point, outliers included, and asked why her straight line was better than his curve. Maya said the data by themselves cannot decide: any finite set of points is compatible with infinitely many curves, so preferring the line reflects an extra taste for simplicity, which is a value, not an observation. Dev replied that this proves too little: the two curves disagree about what the next measurement would show, so further evidence can discriminate between them, and it was precisely that kind of disagreement that eventually let astronomers settle heliocentrism against geocentrism, even though for a long time both could accommodate the observations of their day.

Maya then made the point I found strongest: even when a prediction fails, you never know which assumption to blame — the theory itself, the stopwatch's calibration, the assumption that the string is massless and the amplitude small — so no hypothesis is ever tested in isolation, and the evidence cannot tell you where the fault lies. Dev answered that if that were the whole story, science could never reach agreement at all, yet it demonstrably does — sometimes quickly, as when general relativity accounted for the anomalous behavior of Mercury's orbit that Newtonian gravity had struggled with. Maya replied that agreement shows scientists share training and values, not that the evidence forced their hand. Dev said that lands her in anything-goes relativism, where no experiment ever rules anything out, which she presumably does not accept. Please rate each of these moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Along the way, I'd like you to address three things specifically: whether Maya is relying on a strong version of the underdetermination thesis that most philosophers of science would reject, or a weak version they would accept; whether Dev's appeal to further evidence genuinely answers her, or merely pushes the problem one step back; and whether Maya's final claim that scientific agreement is sociological rather than evidential is a respectable position or a concession too far. Please end with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, who was right about what.
