I've been staring at a version of the argument from illusion and I can't locate the bad step, though the conclusion seems to prove far too much. It runs like this. (1) In a perfect hallucination of a red cube, the subject's experience is just like a veridical seeing of a red cube considered from the inside, so the subject can never tell the cases apart by reflection on the experience alone. (2) The intrinsic character of the hallucination can be fully described without mentioning any red cube or, indeed, any external world at all. (3) If a hallucination and a veridical experience are alike in every intrinsic respect, then the veridical experience can also be fully characterized without mentioning any external object. (4) So even in veridical perception, the immediate object of awareness is not the physical cube. (5) The same reasoning applies to any state with accuracy conditions: a veridical memory of a birthday is intrinsically indistinguishable from a confabulated one, and a true belief from a delusion sharing all its internal features. (6) Therefore no mental state whatsoever — no perception, memory, or belief — is directly about the external world; we only ever encounter inner items from which we infer the world's existence, if we can infer it at all. Step (4) is the familiar conclusion about perception, but (6) about all intentional states seems to show that even this very thought isn't about anything. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd most like to keep (2), since the hallucination really does seem inwardly exhausted by how it is for the subject. But rejecting (3) looks like disjunctivism, and I've heard the worry that if perception gets the disjunctivist treatment, memory and belief will have to get it too — and it's not clear that can be made coherent. Please separate what is settled among philosophers of perception from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether the traditional argument turns on an equivocation between an object of awareness and a way of characterizing an experience — and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
