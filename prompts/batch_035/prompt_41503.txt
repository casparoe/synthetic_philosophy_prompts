I'm on my town's charter review committee and I've realised my instincts about who should get a vote are kind of all over the place, so I want to figure out if there's actually a principle underneath them. Here are my gut calls, going case by case.

Permanent residents vote, obviously. Tourists passing through don't. Second-home owners who pay property tax but are only there six weeks a year: no, even though they're taxed — no representation for them, sorry. Undocumented immigrants who've lived and worked in town for a decade: yes, definitely. Prisoners from our town incarcerated two hundred miles away: yes. Commuters who work in town five days a week but sleep somewhere else: no, zoning here isn't really theirs to decide. Sixteen-year-olds who'll live with the consequences longer than anyone: I keep flip-flopping. And citizens of the country who've lived abroad for twenty years should still get to vote in national elections — I'm confident about that one, mail-in ballots and all.

Oh and I've heard some places actually let businesses vote in local elections, which strikes me as completely wrong, but I can't fully say why, since I did just say taxpayers without residency should maybe get... hmm, no, I said they shouldn't. Fine.

My tentative principle is something like "you belong to the vote if you're affected by the decision," but that can't be right, because the commuters and the second-home owners are clearly affected by, say, a big zoning change, and I still want to say no to them. Meanwhile the prisoners are barely affected day-to-day and I say yes. So maybe it's about being subject to the laws? But then tourists are subject to our laws while they're here, and that's absurd.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close to it? If there are a couple of candidate principles that each fit some of my verdicts, tell me what they are and what each one says about the new cases coming up on our committee's agenda — a regional referendum on a lithium mine where the mine's water usage would hit farmers in a whole different county, and whether to let the residents of a long-term care facility in town who have advanced dementia cast ballots with a family member assisting. And be honest with me about which of my verdicts has to go if you can't save them all — I'd rather know now than mid-meeting.
