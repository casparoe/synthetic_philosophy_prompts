I am a high school senior writing my IB Extended Essay in history on the debate over the economic and political legacy of British rule in India. I have been reading Niall Ferguson's Empire: How Britain Made the Modern World alongside Shashi Tharoor's Inglorious Empire: What the British Did to India, and I have noticed something that my textbooks never mention: although the two authors reach opposite verdicts, they often argue in the same way — each asks what India would have looked like without Britain, and each builds a balance sheet of credits and debits that is supposed to settle the case. I would like you to compare the two books at the level of their argumentative structure rather than their lists of facts. Specifically, please address three questions. First, what baseline does each author assume for a British-free India? Ferguson tends to treat the most plausible alternative as fragmentation or domination by a harsher power, while Tharoor assumes a continuation of the comparatively prosperous, commercially advanced economy that existed before conquest; how much of the disagreement between them can be explained by this difference in the counterfactual world each is comparing against? Second, taking the railways as a test case, compare how each author decides what counts as a benefit of empire. Ferguson treats infrastructure, trade integration, and institutions as items on the credit side, whereas Tharoor argues that railways built for extraction, financed by Indian taxpayers, staffed by Britons, and plausibly buildable without conquest do not belong on the credit side at all — the question is not only what was built but for whom, at whose expense, and whether the same thing would have been built anyway. Which account of what makes something a benefit is more defensible, and can the two accounts be reconciled? Third, my supervisor raised an objection that I want to evaluate: she suggested that the whole balance-sheet genre may be incoherent, because it aggregates gains to some Indians with losses to others — prosperity for some, famines and deindustrialization for others — as though one column could simply cancel the other, when there may be no meaningful way to sum these across millions of people. Does this aggregation problem undermine Ferguson more than Tharoor, both equally, or neither? Does either author have the resources to answer it, or would answering it require abandoning the balance-sheet format altogether? I would appreciate a reasoned comparative analysis rather than a summary of either book, and if you think both authors share a flaw that neither sees, please say so explicitly and explain what a better framing of the question would look like.
