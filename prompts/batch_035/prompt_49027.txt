So in Australia you get fined if you don't turn up to vote in federal elections, and for the House of Representatives you have to number every single box on the ballot — if you just put a 1 next to your favorite and leave the rest blank, your ballot is invalid and gets tossed. So the state compels you to rank every candidate on the list, including candidates you actively despise, or ones you've literally never heard of. And at the same time the ballot is completely secret. Some Australian states actually use optional preferential voting instead, where you can stop numbering whenever you want, which makes the contrast nicely concrete.

Something about this combo bugs me and I want the implicit theory dragged out. Normally the idea of the government forcing you to produce political speech is horrifying — compelled speech is one of the worst things a liberal state can do. Here the state fines you for declining to speak, and dictates the form the speech must take (a complete ranking, no abstentions partway down, no "none of the above"), yet apparently the secrecy is supposed to dissolve the compelled-speech problem. Does it? Also: what if I genuinely have no preference between the last five candidates? The law doesn't care; I have to manufacture an ordering among people I'm indifferent between (and plenty of people just number straight down the ballot, which the system happily counts as my sincere will). So what view of voting does all this presuppose — that voting is a duty like jury service rather than a right you exercise at discretion, that preferences exist and merely need to be elicited, that the vote is an input to a counting procedure rather than an expression at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, say which rival theories the practice quietly rules out versus which several it's compatible with, and tell me what would actually have to change if we instead took the expressive view seriously (or the view that some people reasonably have no preference between the bottom of the ticket).

One more thing: at the end, tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to fail — which part of the practice, if it worked differently than you assumed, would flip your verdict on what's being presupposed. And is there any argument that would make you reconsider whether the secret ballot really does defuse the compulsion? I'd rather you flag the shakiest link than pretend the whole chain is solid.
