I've just come out of a workshop on entrepreneurial risk where the facilitator repeatedly invoked Machiavelli's notorious image of fortune as a woman who must be beaten and knocked about by whoever wants to keep her down, together with the surrounding argument of chapter 25 of The Prince: fortune as a destructive river that can be contained by dikes and embankments, fortune governing half of our actions while leaving the other half to us, fortune favouring the young and impetuous over the cautious. The lesson drawn was blunt: under uncertainty, boldness is rational and hedging is for the timid. I suspect the slogan is doing less work than he thinks, but I can't yet say what work it does. Please disentangle the distinct precise theses on offer — that exogenous shocks dominate some fixed fraction of outcomes; that preparation converts unmanageable chance into manageable risk; that impetuousness changes how fortune behaves toward you rather than merely which occasions you meet; that fortune systematically rewards a certain temperament; and the gendered claim itself, on which the whole figure arguably rests. For each thesis, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false, and whether it actually supports the facilitator's conclusion or cuts against it. Then answer two structural questions: where does virtù sit once fortune governs half of our actions — is it the remaining half's entire content, or a disposition that operates on fortune's half too? And which element is doing the real work — would the argument survive if the woman were dropped and only the flood remained, given that Machiavelli seems to offer the flood as the sober analysis and the woman as the justification for preferring boldness? I'd like the readings as an ordered menu I could bring back to the next session, with a verdict on each.
