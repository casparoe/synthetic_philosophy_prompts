My close friend is a moral psychologist by training, and over the years she has issued a running set of verdicts on whether particular people "have a reason" to do something — a reason that applies to that agent, as opposed to a reason the rest of us have to make them do it. Her guiding line, as she puts it, is that a reason has to connect somehow to what the person already cares about, or could come to care about through their own reflection and experience; a reason that floats entirely free of that she calls an empty claim, "external" in the pejorative sense she picked up from a graduate seminar on Williams. Her verdicts so far: a smoker who every January half-means to quit has a reason to quit, though a weak one; a man who wants to reconcile with his estranged brother only because he falsely believes the brother regrets the feud has no reason to write to him — once the false belief is corrected, the desire dies, and with it the reason; a woman who is indifferent to music but curious about new experiences generally, and who we can be confident would love chamber music if she went once, has a reason to go; and a thoroughly cruel man — indifferent to argument, to evidence, and to anyone's suffering, and such that no amount of reflection or correct information would move him — has no reason whatsoever to stop, a claim she says is not so much false as unintelligible.

Two new cases are likely to come up at our next dinner, and I'd like to walk in with a sense of where she'll land. The first: a thirty-five-year-old who finds children tedious and has never felt a flicker of desire for one, but who, on the pattern of very similar people, would — if she had a child — come to care for it devotedly, would call it the most important thing in her life, and would never regret it. Does she now have a reason to have a child, or to take any of the costly, hard-to-reverse steps that lead there? The second: the cruel man from before is offered a pill that would make him kind; the man he would become would embrace his new motives and never look back. Does he have a reason to take it?

What I'd like from you is, first, a prediction of her verdict on each case, but more importantly a reconstruction of the rule she is implicitly running. Her track record seems to me consistent with at least two principles: one on which a reason reaches any value the person would come to hold and would not regret, and one on which a reason must be reachable by reflection or inexpensive experiment from concerns the person already has — so that new values count only when there is a toehold of some existing concern and a cheap way to test it. Set out the candidate rules you think fit her record, including any I have missed, say what each predicts for the two new cases, give your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and propose one further case that would best divide the rules. Throughout, please say how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
