My university's philosophy society runs a podcast listening group, and this week's episode was a history-of-philosophy survey of logical positivism. The host spent the final ten minutes on A. J. Ayer and life after death, and it was the part everyone wanted to discuss afterwards — but I suspect that for dramatic effect some of it got streamlined at the expense of accuracy, and I'd like to know what to actually take away. Here is the gist of the segment as best I can reconstruct it: 'In Language, Truth and Logic, published when he was in his mid-twenties, Ayer proved that all religious language is literally meaningless — not false, but gibberish, no more apt for truth or falsehood than random noise. That included talk of an afterlife: to say the soul survives death is strictly nonsense, so Ayer had settled the question before he was thirty. Half a century later, in 1988, he choked on smoked salmon while hospitalized with pneumonia, his heart stopped for four minutes, and he was revived. Afterwards he published an article describing a vivid experience of a painful red light responsible for the government of the universe and two ministers in charge of space, which he came to interpret as evidence that consciousness can exist apart from the brain. He conceded that his verification principle had been refuted by his own experience, admitted the events had "slightly weakened" his conviction that his genuine death would be the end of him, and died the following year with a convert's open mind about what he had seen while clinically dead. So the arch-positivist who declared God-talk meaningless ended his life unable to explain away the one datum that mattered.'

Something about this feels off to me, though I don't know Ayer's corpus well enough to pinpoint where. I do know the concession quote is authentic, since it's widely reprinted, but the full sentence ends with him saying he continues to hope that death really is the end — which doesn't sound like a man inching toward belief. I've also heard that Ayer held religious utterances, while not factual statements, could still express feelings and attitudes, which doesn't square with 'gibberish, no better than random noise.' And I have a vague memory that he publicly expressed dissatisfaction with the verification principle decades before 1988, which would make the host's 'refuted by his own experience' framing look anachronistic.

Could you go through the segment claim by claim and tell me what's accurate, what's a standard oversimplification, and what Ayer himself explicitly denied? In particular: whether he ever claimed to have *proven* anything in Language, Truth and Logic, or whether 'proved' papers over how that book actually argued; what his considered position on the meaningfulness of survival-talk actually was, and whether it shifted over his career; what he really concluded from the near-death experience in the article itself; and whether the closing line — that he was 'unable to explain away' what he saw — is a fair reading of that article or a misreading that ignores his own stated interpretation. Where the host's framing is right in substance but misleading in presentation, I'd like to know how a defender of Ayer would reframe it; and where it's just wrong, I'd like to understand what the correct version is.
