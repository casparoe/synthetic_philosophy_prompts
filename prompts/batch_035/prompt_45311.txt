I am the clinical ethics consultant at a mid-sized hospital, and our ethics committee keeps getting referred the same kind of case: a written advance directive that stands in some tension with what the patient, now cognitively impaired, says or does in the present — and we have no settled procedure that tells the attending team what to do next. The disagreements are currently resolved by whoever in the room is most forceful, which is worse than either of the outcomes being fought over. The committee has asked me to draft a decision procedure they can actually run in a conference room in under an hour, and I want it grounded in the philosophical debate about whether and why an earlier self has authority over a later one — not just in hospital custom.

As its first move, the procedure should distinguish between two readings of a directive that our ad hoc discussions blur together: the directive as an exercise of authority (the earlier self issuing binding instructions, Ulysses ordering himself bound to the mast) and the directive as evidence (a fallible record of what this person valued, defeasible by fresher evidence). Behind that distinction sit the questions we keep circling: Dworkin's contrast between critical interests in the shape of a life as a whole and experiential interests in present satisfaction, and which of them the directive protects; the objection that the person in the bed now has interests of her own that the earlier self cannot claim to speak for merely by having once been the same person; and the countervailing worry that if the earlier self binds the later one too tightly, we treat the patient as a means to the closure of a biography she no longer knows she has.

What I need:

1. A stepwise procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and, at every step, which of four doors the answer opens — follow the directive, follow the present patient, hand the decision to the surrogate, or declare the question one of values that the committee must decide and cannot derive. It must contain a diagnostic step for whether the directive is about the intervention it names or the condition it feared: a clause refusing a feeding tube, written by someone who chiefly dreaded being a burden on her children, may or may not apply to a patient who is untroubled, unbothered, and happily unaware of being a burden, and our teams cannot currently tell which reading to use.

2. A rule for the hardest class of conflict: directives written many years earlier, by a person now substantially different in values and temperament. One committee member proposes a decay rule — the directive loses authority in proportion to documented change in the person's character — and I want the procedure to adopt it, reject it, or replace it with something workable, with reasons a non-philosopher can follow.

3. Worked applications to three cases, at least one of which must end somewhere I won't like:

(a) Mrs. A, moderate dementia, wrote a directive eight years ago refusing artificial nutrition should she become unable to recognize her family. She now fails to recognize them on most days but not all, is cheerful, eats willingly when food is offered by hand, and has begun saying that she wants to keep going, at moments the nurses dispute are lucid. Her daughter wants the directive honored the moment swallowing fails; her son points at the cheerfulness and the phrase about recognizing family, arguing it was a proxy for something else.

(b) Mr. B, a retired professor, severe dementia, untroubled and affable. His directive says no heroics, nothing that prolongs the dying. He develops a treatable infection; antibiotics would likely restore him to his current pleasant state, which is not a state he would recognize as his life's work but is a state he enjoys. He cannot discuss it. The family splits between reading the directive as his final judgment on his life as a whole and reading it as a fear of intensive care that this scenario never touches.

(c) The case I am hoping the procedure never reaches but suspect it will: a patient who, while competent, wrote into his directive an instruction that it be honored even against his own later objections, and who now, moderately impaired and distraught, is asking that everything be done. The procedure must say whether an earlier self can pre-commit against its own future voice, and where, if anywhere, that stops being autonomy and becomes the past person wielding the present one.

4. A closing statement of the procedure's limits: the kinds of cases it should refuse to settle, and the points at which it must openly hand the decision to the human beings in the room, marked as judgment rather than dressed up as the output of the steps.

Constraints: the procedure must be usable by committee members with no philosophical training; every philosophical term it employs must be explained in a single line the first time it appears; and it should not lean on any statute, case, or jurisdiction, since its job is to structure deliberation, not replace the lawyers.
