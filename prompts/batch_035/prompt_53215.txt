Some background: I had two friends over for dinner a while back, Dan and Maya, and they got into a long argument about whether the world is actually becoming less violent. Dan had been reading one of those big statistical-history books arguing that violence of basically every kind has been declining for centuries, and he's a total convert. Maya thinks the whole genre is an exercise in storytelling with charts. I just watched, and honestly I couldn't referee it, so I'm going to give you the exchange as it happened and I'd like you to go through it move by move and say who's right about what. Not just who wins overall — each step.

(1) Dan opened with the trend: measured per capita, deaths from war and homicide are way down compared with most of recorded history, and even the twentieth century, which everyone thinks of as the worst ever, is mild compared to typical stretches of the past once you divide by how many people were alive. So the world is getting less violent. Period.

(2) Maya said per capita is doing sneaky work there. In absolute numbers, the twentieth century was the bloodiest stretch of human history by a huge margin. If what you care about is the total amount of violence and suffering in the world — which is what "is the world becoming less violent" should mean, she thinks — then absolute numbers are what count, and dividing by population is a way of making a enormous [sic] amount of killing look like progress just because the population grew faster than the body count. A bigger world can hold more violence at a lower rate, she said, and she doesn't see why the second thing should console anyone.

(3) Dan answered that per capita is the right measure because it's the risk faced by a random individual — that's what "how violent is the world" means for anybody actually living in it. Absolute totals have the opposite problem: they'd make a tiny, ultra-violent ancient society look peaceful just because there weren't many people around to kill. He also pointed out that by absolute numbers, a future world of ten billion people with occasional small wars would count as more violent than a medieval world where a fifth of men died at someone's hand, which he said is obviously wrong.

(4) Maya said fine, but even granting the per capita trend, the category is cooked. When violence gets counted narrowly — bodies with wounds, an identifiable person doing the harm — the past looks terrible. But widen the lens to what people sometimes call structural violence: deaths from deprivation, from sanctions, from preventable disease that policy could have averted, the slow stuff that doesn't leave a corpse anyone counts. Count that way, and the ledger can flip, or at least stop looking so rosy. And the choice between narrow and wide counting isn't neutral, she said — the narrow count happens to suit the person whose thesis is that things have gotten better. The definition is doing the work, not the data.

(5) Dan's reply was that if you widen the category like that, the word stops meaning anything. Structural violence is a metaphor, and if every preventable death counts as violence, then every society that has ever existed was close to maximally violent, since some people always die of causes the society could have prevented with more effort or resources. You need an identifiable agent doing the harming to someone; harm that floats around with no one committing it isn't violence, however tragic.

(6) Maya called that the value judgment smuggled in exactly where she said it would be: requiring an agent means state violence that operates through hunger rather than bullets gets a discount, which is very convenient for states and for optimists. And then she made what I thought was her strongest point, though I'm not sure: she said that even setting the definition fight aside, no trend claim can avoid aggregating different kinds of violence into one quantity — you have to trade war deaths against domestic abuse against torture against famine deaths, and some weighting has to decide how many of one equals one of the other. So "the world is less violent, period" is never a pure empirical fact; it's a fact under a weighting, and the weighting is a moral judgment. There's no fact of the matter until you've made it.

They went one more round, sort of converging, and this is where I got really lost. Maya conceded that per capita rates of interpersonal violence have probably fallen, if you trust the historical reconstructions. Dan conceded that his headline claim is really "less violent as measured by wound-caused deaths per person per year, under a narrow definition, weighted somehow." But then they disagreed about whether that convergence leaves Dan's original thesis standing or hollowed out. Dan said a true trend under any reasonable measure is still a true trend. Maya said a claim that only survives after you pick the measure, the definition, and the weights isn't really an empirical discovery, it's a moral outlook wearing a lab coat. And they left it there.

So, please: adjudicate each of the six moves plus that final round — rate each as clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and justify the rating. Say who's right about the per capita vs. absolute question in particular, because that one seems like it might have an actual principled answer. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. And one more thing, which I'd like you to take seriously: tell me what would change your adjudication — which specific argument or piece of evidence, if it existed or if Maya/Dan had made it, would flip your verdict on any of the steps — and point to where in your own reasoning you think you're most likely to have gone wrong. I'd rather you flag the shaky spots yourself than hand me a confident-looking scorecard.
