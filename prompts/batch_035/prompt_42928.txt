I got appointed to my city's little-known "naming review" committee (we mostly handle petitions to rename streets and public buildings, and there's more of those than you'd think) and it's forced me to actually figure out what I believe about commemoration instead of just having vibes. So here are my gut verdicts, and I want to know if there's a principle underneath them or whether I'm just doing motivated reasoning case by case. Confederate general on a courthouse square, erected in the 1920s: take it down, obviously. Same general's grave marker in a battlefield cemetery: leave it alone, it's a grave. Street named after a slaveholding founding father: keep it. Street named after a now-forgotten state politician who was a big eugenics booster, renamed by the council in the 90s: that renaming was fine, even good. A statue of a Union general who later presided over massacres of Native villages: I'd keep that one, which I admit sits weird next to my Confederate verdicts. And a building named for a living donor who turned out to have built his fortune on fraud: strip the name. Same donor dead for forty years: I'd honestly leave it, I think, or at least I wouldn't fight it.

My tentative principle was something like: a commemoration should track the achievement it was erected for, unless the achievement was itself a vehicle for the bad stuff or the honoring was done in bad faith (i.e., the 1920s courthouse statue wasn't really about the general's "military virtue," it was a message to Black residents). That gets me through most of the above, but it strains on the eugenics politician, since the street probably wasn't named in bad faith and eugenics wasn't his "achievement." And I have no idea what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a beloved local doctor from the 1950s with a park named after her, who genuinely did save the town during a polio outbreak, and who we now know from letters was a committed segregationist who quietly blocked integrated housing for years. Achieved good, privately pursued bad, and the park was named for the good.

So: is there a principle that captures all or most of my verdicts, stated precisely enough to actually hand to a renaming commission? What does it predict for cases I haven't listed, like a donor whose name is on a hospital wing and whose company later poisons a river (but he's dead and the wing's already been renamed once), or streets named for the losing side in some other country's civil war, or the fact that we keep statues of ancient philosophers with views we'd now consider monstrous? And be honest about which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn -- I suspect the Union general one and the dead-donor one are the load-bearing inconsistencies, but maybe you see a different seam. Feel free to propose more than one candidate principle if no single one works, just tell me which clause each case is supposed to motivate.
