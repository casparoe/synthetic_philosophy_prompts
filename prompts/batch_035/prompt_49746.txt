A member of my ethics reading group has an argument that he claims reduces consequentialism to silence about everyday choices, and a week of arguing with him has left me unable to say exactly where it slips. It runs:

(1) For a consequentialist, whether an act is permissible depends only on the comparative value of its total consequences, however far in the future those consequences fall.
(2) For ordinary acts — a donation to malaria relief, a career switch, telling a hard truth to a friend — the effects we can actually discern run out after a few years; beyond that, any act can be traced to arbitrarily large goods and arbitrarily large harms, and nothing in our evidence marks one act's causal future as better than another's.
(3) When our evidence about the value of two acts' consequences is symmetric in this way, the expected difference in value between them is zero.
(4) So for ordinary acts the expected difference in value is zero, and there is no consequentialist reason to prefer one to another: none to give rather than keep, none to tell the hard truth rather than the kind one.
(5) A theory of right and wrong on which we have no reason to prefer telling a friend a hard truth over flattering him is not a viable theory.
(6) So consequentialism is not a viable theory of right and wrong.

I feel the trouble is between (2) and (3): "we cannot tell which future is better" and "the expected difference is zero" are not the same claim, and I suspect (3) converts an epistemic blank into a substantive assignment of zero. But I cannot see how to reject (3) without simply asserting that our evidence is asymmetric wherever it matters, which looks question-begging. The friend who proposed the argument says it is valid and that the real exit is (2): he thinks we can discern more than the argument allows, at least for some acts. I don't find that convincing for acts with long shadows.
