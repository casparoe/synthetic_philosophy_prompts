Last weekend at a family dinner, my two cousins got into an argument about whether a person could have come from a different sperm and egg, and it went on for four rounds before they gave up and asked me to settle it. I couldn't, so I'm bringing it here.

It started when Marcus mentioned reading somewhere that "you could not have been born from a different egg — if a different egg of your mother's had been fertilized, that would have been someone else, and you would simply never have existed." Dana objected: the person conceived instead would have had the same parents, the same upbringing, the same name and life, sitting in her chair today — and she said that person just would be her, slightly different, the way she'd have been different had she grown up in another city.

Marcus answered with a case: suppose two of their mother's eggs had instead been fertilized that month, producing two children, not one. Both can't be Dana, so at most one of them could be — and nothing about them would pick out which — so neither is. The particular egg and sperm that actually met matter, not just the recipe.

Dana replied that the recipe is all there is: a different sperm from the same father would have carried nearly the same DNA, so the resulting person would be nearly identical to her in every respect, and she said there's no further fact of the matter beyond that — Marcus keeps pointing at the original cells as if they have some special status, but they're just one pair among millions of near-duplicates that could have done the same job.

Marcus then tried an analogy from the other direction: if he and his wife, who already have a son, made a new embryo from a different egg and sperm of theirs, nobody would say that future child *is* their son in another possible world — it would plainly be a different child, even with the same parents. Dana came back that this smuggles in his conclusion: the analogy assumes what's at issue, namely whether "same parents plus similar genes plus similar life" suffices for being the same person, and she thinks it does — while his view has the strange result that whether she is Dana hangs on which microscopic cell won a race decades ago, a fact no one could ever know or check, which she found too arbitrary to be the ground of identity.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please go through the exchange move by move: say who is right at each step and why, note where either of them assumes what needs to be shown, and if there's something important both are missing, tell me what it is and whose position it damages more.
