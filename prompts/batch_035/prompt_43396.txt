I'm a PhD student in comparative politics, dissertation on state violence, and I've gotten myself tangled in what is basically the Browning/Goldhagen debate but not really about Germany — it's about my own gut verdicts on perpetrator cases, which I've slowly realized don't follow any principle I can actually state. So here are the verdicts I keep landing on. Reserve Police Battalion 101, the middle-aged German reserve policemen shooting civilians face to face in Poland, commander apparently distressed, an offer to step out on the table and a modest number taking it: situation, obviously — conformity, deference, the momentum of the day, no deep antisemitism needed. Eichmann, who never pulled a trigger: also situation, careerism inside a bureaucracy. My Lai: situation, young draftees, panic, command pressure. The Stalinist executioners and interrogators killing mostly their own co-nationals on quotas: situation again, careerism plus state demands. But the Einsatzgruppen leadership — the educated, ideologically articulate men further up the chain: genuine belief, and I'd bet on it. And Rwanda, where ordinary Hutu villagers killed neighbors with machetes: here my gut flips to ideology and culture, the radio propaganda, the dehumanizing vocabulary, something deeper in the water — even though structurally Rwanda looks a lot like Battalion 101, ordinary men killing face to face, and some Hutu sheltered people or refused without being shot for it. So my two strongest verdicts are near-doppelganger cases and I give them opposite explanations, and I can't tell if I'm tracking a real variable or just how much I've read about each one.

I've tried to build a principle myself and my current candidate is something like: the situational explanation is the right one when refusal was safe and some people refused — the refusers are the control group, and where you have them, you don't need ideology to explain the killers. But this immediately messes up my Rwanda verdict, because refusal there was often survivable too, and by my own principle Rwanda should come out situational, which I keep resisting without being able to say why. Maybe the real variable is whether the regime is conscripting reluctant manpower or mobilizing a population that already wants to participate — conscription vs mobilization — but I can't decide which side of that line the Stalinist terror falls on, and I suspect I just have the line because it flatters the verdicts I already had. So what I want is this: take my verdicts seriously as data, tell me whether there's a principle (situational vs ideological explanation, some third thing, or a mix with a rule for the mix) that captures all or most of them, and be honest about which of my verdicts has to go if a cleaner principle does. Then run the principle on a couple of cases I haven't listed and tell me what it predicts — the Western kids who volunteered to join ISIS from comfortable lives is one I'm curious about, and pogroms that happened in some towns but not neighboring ones is another. And if the upshot is that the situational/ideological dichotomy itself is doing damage, say that and tell me what the better cut is, because I have a feeling the dichotomy is why my verdicts keep sliding around.
