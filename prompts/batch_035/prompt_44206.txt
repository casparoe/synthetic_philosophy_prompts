I'm teaching a college seminar on perpetrator studies, built around the Browning/Goldhagen debate about Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the men who carried out the shootings in occupied Poland. There are 14 students, one 75-minute session, and I don't want another discussion section where everyone just recaps the two books. Design an in-class simulation where students run into the explanatory problem themselves before I name it for them.

Here's roughly what I have in mind, but you should improve on it. I want a sanitized, clearly fictional analogue — do NOT have students roleplay shooting anybody, that would be grotesque. Something like a mid-level bureaucracy in a fictional authoritarian state, where teams process paperwork that, in stages, they slowly realise is relocations of a stigmatized minority, with euphemisms on all the forms and a supervisor offering each person an easy, no-consequences opt-out early on. Rules, roles, materials, a timeline of stages, and some mechanism where I can track who keeps going, who asks questions, and who quietly defects without ever saying anything. No real money payoffs obviously, but there need to be some stakes or status costs so the choices aren't free.

The key thing is the debrief. It should map specific moments in the simulation onto the actual dispute: Browning's conformity/obedience story (peers, not orders, doing most of the work), Goldhagen's claim that neither mechanism explains much and shared eliminationist ideology does, and the fact that most of what we know about the battalion comes from postwar testimony from men defending themselves decades later — which is a problem my simulation also has, since I'd basically be asking participants to self-report afterwards.

Also, please be frank about what a 75-minute classroom exercise cannot reproduce — decades of ambient hateful culture, actual fear of the regime, real career stakes — and how that gap predictably biases students toward situationism and against Goldhagen, and what I can say to correct for it. And a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
