Next month, as part of a campus interview in intellectual history, I present a chapter of my dissertation before a department heavy with postcolonial scholars, a historian of science who works on measurement and standardization, and one analytic philosopher of history. My thesis is deliberately double-edged: the Enlightenment's stadial theory of social development — the "four stages" schema running from hunting through pastoralism and agriculture to commerce, elaborated by figures like Turgot, Smith, and Condorcet — plainly did ideological work for empire, but it also replaced a picture in which non-Europeans belonged to separate, biblically fixed kinds with one in which all societies occupy a single human history governed by learnable causes. I call this the "inclusion problem": the framework that ranked the colonized also, for the first time, made them comparable to Europe on shared terms, and my argument is that both facts must be held at once. Rank-in-time is a subtler domination than rank-in-kind, and I don't think the two readings can be cleanly separated into the good one and the bad one. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do, phrased in their vocabulary (epistemic violence, legibility, the civilizing mission, the dialectic of Enlightenment), each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft; tell me what to concede before I am forced to; and identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — which objection is most dangerous and whether my thesis survives it — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
