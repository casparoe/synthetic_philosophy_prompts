I'm a PhD student working on imprecise probabilities — credal sets, dilation, the usual — and each spring I edit the satirical end-of-term issue of our department's graduate blog. This year's theme is "epistemic advice columns." Please write the centerpiece piece: an agony-aunt column of roughly 700 words, in the voice of an utterly committed imprecise probabilist, titled something like "Ask Your Credal Set" (you may adjust the title, but the persona should be a warm, folksy advice columnist whose epistemology is a set of probability functions and who has never in her life given a point estimate).

The column should answer three or four reader letters of your own invention. Two letters should be imported from the technical literature in disguise — for example, a reader torn between two Ellsberg-style urns who can't understand why he prefers betting on either urn over betting on a known mixture, and a reader whose partner demands they "just say a number" about whether they see a future together — and the others can be everyday quandards (a job offer, whether to carry an umbrella) that the columnist refuses to resolve with a sharp probability.

The humor has to come from getting the view right rather than caricaturing it, so that someone who actually works on imprecise probabilities laughs rather than winces. In particular: the columnist's refusal to give numbers should be grounded in real motivations for imprecise credences (ambiguity aversion, severe uncertainty, the failure of sharp priors to represent incomplete evidence), her answers should deploy real machinery correctly — dilation (where learning a seemingly irrelevant fact widens your interval), the behavior of credal sets under conditioning, and at least one decision rule for acting on a set of probabilities, such as maximin or E-admissibility, deployed with its actual trade-offs — and where the standard objections to imprecise probabilism exist (indecision at the crucial moment, the awkwardness of never being able to say a simple number, the worry that one sharp prior is hiding inside the set all along), she should acknowledge them the way a seasoned columnist acknowledges her critics: serenely, without conceding anything.

Please include at least one recurring feature of the columnist's voice that becomes funnier with repetition — a sign-off, a recurring disclaimer, a standing offer to sharpen a reader's credences for a fee, or something of that sort — and make sure the irony stays legible without winking: a reader unfamiliar with the literature should still understand that the joke is the refusal to commit, and a reader who knows the literature should be able to tell the columnist is being written with affection rather than contempt.
