I'm a third-year student facilitating a weekly discussion section for an upper-level seminar on consequentialism — about fourteen students, one 50-minute session. We've just finished classical utilitarianism, and next week's topic is whether pleasure and pain are commensurable: whether any finite quantity of pleasure can genuinely compensate for a given quantity of suffering, or whether compensation fails somewhere. I want an in-class activity where they feel where tradeoffs stop being available, instead of being told where philosophers think they stop.

Design a structured game with the following elements. It should use abstract units rather than real stakes — no money, no actual discomfort — but the units can't simply be fungible tokens with a posted exchange rate, since that would smuggle in the commensurability assumption we're interrogating; tell me how you'd handle that artifact. The game should proceed through a sequence of forced allocations, starting with cases where trading pains against pleasures seems obviously fine (a mild annoyance against a large pleasure) and escalating toward cases where the same exchange rate produces results the players will balk at, including something structurally like the reductio often aimed at negative utilitarianism: a world of trivial but universal suffering versus a world where that suffering is removed at the cost of everyone losing all further happiness. Somewhere in the sequence, the allocation should be other-regarding — the group, by majority or by posted rate, must impose a pain on one or a few members for the pleasure of the rest — so that participants confront whether their threshold is a personal preference or something they'll enforce on others. Participants should record or revise their own exchange rates across rounds, and I want to see how you'd collect the revisions so the debrief can chart where the rates broke.

They should not hear the terms "negative utilitarianism," "lexical priority," or "commensurability" until the debrief; define for me how you'd introduce strict lexical orderings, lexical thresholds, and continuous scales only afterwards, mapping each to specific rounds. Please also include: a minute-by-minute timeline, a list of materials, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of the debate no classroom game can reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
