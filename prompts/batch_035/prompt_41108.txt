I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice and collective action (14 students, one 75-minute session). Design an in-class simulation of a Luring Lottery: the prize goes to a randomly drawn entrant, but is divided among all entrants, so the unique Nash equilibrium is for everyone to enter, while the collectively best outcome is that exactly one person enters. Participants should make real choices under these incentives across several rounds — include a round in which participants may post public non-binding commitments before choosing, and a final round in which a small entry fee or side-payment scheme is introduced — so the class can see for themselves whether pre-play communication, commitment, or incentive tinkering can get a group of individually rational agents to the cooperative outcome, and where each device breaks down. The debrief should map what happened onto the distinction between Nash reasoning and superrational reasoning (choosing as one member of a class of similarly-situated choosers), and should press on whether superrationality is a form of disguised altruism or a genuine refinement of rationality. Keep the payoffs in points or tokens rather than money, give a minute-by-minute timeline, and be candid about the respects in which a classroom game cannot reproduce the one-shot, genuinely uncommunicative conditions under which the dilemma is supposed to bite — and what the facilitator must say to bridge that gap.
