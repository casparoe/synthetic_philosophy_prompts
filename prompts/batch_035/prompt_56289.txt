I'm the half-time archivist (and only paid staff member) at a county historical society, working with a rotating group of volunteers. Like most such institutions, our holdings are a monument to accidental accumulation: judges' and mayors' papers, church registers, DAR scrapbooks, photographs of factory openings donated by the companies that owned the factories. Over the past year a few board members and I have been reading and discussing the idea — familiar enough in archival theory, though we're arriving at it late — that an archive isn't a neutral window onto the past but an artifact of decisions: what someone thought to save, what an institution had the standing to deposit, what nobody thought was worth a folder. The silences in our stacks aren't gaps where nothing happened; they were produced, mostly by people who never knew they were producing anything. Tenants' organizing drives, migrant farmworkers' seasons, the middle of the century as experienced by people who didn't own the mill — none of it is here, and the absence itself is a record of the county's power relations, if anyone could read it.

I'd like you to turn this cluster of ideas into an actual appraisal and acquisition procedure my volunteers and I can run whenever someone walks in the door with a donation, which happens a few times a month. The procedure needs to do several things at once: handle the ordinary practical questions (condition, provenance, storage and processing costs, deed of gift) well enough that we don't stop doing those; force an explicit comparison between what's being offered and what our holdings already overrepresent; distinguish the cases where we should simply accept a donation from the cases where accepting it quietly deepens an existing silence; and tell us when the right response is not to wait for donations at all but to go solicit records from communities that have no reason to think a historical society wants their papers. I'd also like a step in which whatever we decide — accept, decline, solicit, or pass with a referral — gets written down as part of the collection-level description, so the archive's own finding aids start documenting what the archive doesn't have and how that came about.

Please then run the procedure on three cases: the papers of a well-loved county judge, offered by her family, filling exactly the niche we already have forty linear feet of; a single banker's box from a defunct furniture plant, mostly payroll and safety-inspection records, saved by someone who worked there; and an approach from a tenants' rights group with twenty years of meeting minutes, flyers, and eviction-defense files, almost entirely digital, held on two members' personal laptops, and accompanied by a wariness about handing materials over to an institution they see as the county's memory-keeper rather than theirs. For each, show where the procedure leads, where it hands off to a judgment call that can't be proceduralized, and flag at least one point where following it faithfully would mean telling our board something they won't want to hear — including, if that's where it leads, that our processing backlog of the already-documented is not the highest priority our budget implies it is. Be candid about where the procedure will misfire: where "documenting the silence" becomes performance, and where a small institution pretending to solve structural problems just burns its volunteer hours.
