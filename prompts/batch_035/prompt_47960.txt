I'm an undergraduate taking an upper-level seminar in contemporary epistemology, and one of our course requirements is that each student propose a candidate essay question for the final exam, along with a model answer and a marking scheme; the instructor will select the best few to appear on the actual exam. My assigned topic is the epistemic regress problem. The instructor has warned us that she will discard any question a candidate could answer by reciting the three standard labels — foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism — plus one memorized sentence about each. She wants the question anchored in a concrete case that forces the answerer to notice something the usual taxonomy tends to obscure.

I'd like the question built around a case of the following kind, which you should develop and refine as you see fit. While hiking, Mara consults a handheld GPS unit, forms the belief that the trail forks in eight hundred metres, and acts on it. Her trust in that unit rests partly on a background belief that satellite navigation generally works; that background belief is itself held mainly on the strength of things she has read in textbooks and news reports whose reliability she has never independently checked; and those sources, if pressed, would ultimately cite instrument readings of the same broad kind as the device at the bottom of Mara's chain. The feature I want the question to exploit is that somewhere up the chain, the justification for trusting the instruments at the bottom runs, in part, back through the reliability of instruments of that very type.

With that in place, please produce the following three deliverables.

First, the exam question itself, roughly 120–150 words, addressed directly to the candidate. It should present the case, then ask whether the standard three-way taxonomy gives Mara's belief a comfortable home, and instruct the candidate to defend a verdict about where, if anywhere, her justification terminates.

Second, a model answer of about 650 words. It should be answerable by a student with nothing but the concepts available from our seminar: propositional versus doxastic justification, the distinction between the order in which beliefs are acquired and the order in which they justify one another, epistemic circularity, and the difference between a chain's stopping for practical purposes and its stopping in principle. It should not lean on named philosophers for more than incidental color, and it should not presuppose any literature the candidate could not be expected to know from the course alone.

Third, a marking scheme out of 20 marks, structured as five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how candidates typically miss each point, and a closing sentence naming the specific difference in the moves made that separates an A answer from a B answer on this question.

Please keep the whole thing polished and self-contained, since the candidates will sit the exam without access to any materials beyond what is described above.
