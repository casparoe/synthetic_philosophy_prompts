So there's this guy at my pub quiz night, very confident, big on history podcasts, who insists the British empire right through 1776 should be understood as "a family business" -- mother country as parent, colonies as the kids, some of them grown up and surly, and the whole imperial project as an attempt to keep the family firm under one roof. He claims Adam Smith would have agreed, and that Smith's real position was basically "keep the family together at all costs, it's the loyalty that pays." I know enough to smell that something's off but not enough to say exactly what, so help me audit it.

Here's what makes it interesting: Smith himself uses the family metaphor in the colonies chapter of the Wealth of Nations. Talking about how the ancient Greeks ran their colonies, he says the mother city considered the colony "an emancipated child over whom she pretended to claim no direct authority or jurisdiction" -- the colony made its own laws, elected its own magistrates, made war and peace, without waiting on the mother city's approval. So the parent/child picture is in the text. The question is what work it does for him, and whether it supports my friend's "keep the family together" conclusion or something close to the opposite.

My sense is Smith deploys the family analogy mostly to make the British case look like the weird one: the Greeks treated colonies as emancipated children, Rome kind of absorbed hers, and the British, three thousand miles away with no hope of governing well from London, tried to keep grown children on an allowance and then wondered why there was a rebellion. And there's a second, competing analogy in the same part of the book that my friend never mentions -- the one where Smith mocks the whole idea of running an empire as a shop, something like founding an empire for the purpose of raising up a captive population of customers, which he says would be absurd in any private business and is absurd in a kingdom too. He's famous for the line about a nation of shopkeepers, though people argue about whether it was a compliment. So on my reading you've got the family analogy (colonies as kids) and the shop analogy (colonies as customers), and Smith thinks the second one, which was the actual policy logic of the mercantile system, is the disaster.

What I want from you: first, the correspondence table for the family-business analogy -- what plays the parent, what plays the grown child, what plays the inheritance, what stage of parenting the American colonies were at by 1776 in Smith's telling. Second, where the analogy breaks -- I assume the emancipation point is the big one, since an emancipated child doesn't owe you anything, but tell me what else (e.g. that the British colonies weren't actually founded by the state the way Greek colonies were sent out by the mother city, more by licensed adventurers and the like). Third, and this is the part I actually care about: Smith apparently took the family logic seriously enough to float a real constitutional proposal for America, something along the lines of bringing the colonies into the parliament so the family becomes one shared household rather than parent-and-subordinates -- and yet the upshot of his argument, especially after the war basically settled the question, seems to be that Britain should let the colonies go as equals and keep trading with them, which is what you do with an emancipated child, not a customer. So was the family analogy carrying the conclusion my friend attributes to Smith the whole time, or was it carrying the exact opposite one?

Be honest if I've got pieces of the story wrong -- I'm reconstructing all this from half-remembered reading and the podcast guy. And if the analogy audit shows the family-business picture can't support "keep it together at all costs," say what it CAN support, if anything, since the Greeks seem to have gotten something out of it that the British didn't.
