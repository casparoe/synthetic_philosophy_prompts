You're a professional philosopher who works on reasons, rationality and the emotions — the kind of person colleagues rope in to referee papers on fittingness at short notice. So take this seriously and give yourself some space to brainstorm before committing; I care about the reasoning at least as much as the verdict.

My colleague Dee and I co-teach a seminar on the emotions, and she has accumulated a running list of rulings about which considerations are genuine reasons for emotions — I gather the literature calls this the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, but you don't need the papers, just her track record. Her verdicts so far:

1. A demon tortures his victims with extraordinary skill. His skill, she says, gives you no reason at all to admire him — not a weak reason, not an outweighed one, none.
2. A joke is genuinely witty but mocks a stutterer. The wit is a real reason to find it amusing, even though the cruelty is a real reason not to laugh — the fittingness of finding it funny and the justification for laughing come apart, and she insists on keeping them apart.
3. A mutual friend offers her a fiver to be amused by his terrible joke. The fiver is no reason to find it funny, though she concedes it might be a reason to fake a chuckle.
4. A spider in her kitchen is harmless but visually indistinguishable from a deadly one — no expert could tell them apart on sight. Fear of it is fitting.
5. Her house is in a floodplain and the forecast puts a fifty-fifty chance on the river bursting its banks tonight. The fear is fitting, and would remain so even if, as luck has it, no flood comes.
6. A student of hers, abused as a child, feels dirty and ashamed. Dee says the shame is unfitting — nothing shaming happened to the student — though it is perfectly understandable and perhaps even useful to her.

When I press her on what unites these, she just points back at the cases and mutters something about there being reasons for the emotion and merely reasons for the state, and refuses to elaborate.

Now the new case. Our retired colleague Herb blew a chunk of his savings on a case of expensive wine and is pouring it at his leaving do. The wine is frankly mediocre — everyone at the table privately thinks so — but Herb is fragile, and his evening (and honestly his sense of a life well spent) will be ruined unless his guests sincerely enjoy it. No question of lying to him; the only way to spare him is to actually take pleasure in the taste.

Here's what I want from you. First, work out the rule or rules Dee seems to be running — I can see at least two or three candidates that fit her track record equally well, so lay them out carefully. Second, tell me what each rule predicts about the wine case. Third, give me your best guess as to what she'll actually say, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her. And finally, propose the further case — one not already on her list — that would best tell the rival rules apart. Please think carefully and go step by step; a colleague's Friday evening may depend on it.
