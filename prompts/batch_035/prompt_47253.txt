My wife and I spent most of a long car ride arguing about whether species are real things or just names we made up, and I'd like someone to go through it and tell me who was right about what, because I genuinely can't sort it out. It started when she said that the whole idea of a species is like the idea of where "bald" begins: nature just gives us populations that shade into one another bit by bit — populations that barely differ, that differ more, that can sometimes still interbreed — and the moment where one species ends and another begins is our line to draw, not nature's. She brought up ring species, where a chain of neighboring populations wraps around a geographic obstacle, each link interbreeding with the next, while the two ends of the chain meet and can't breed at all, and asked which link is "the" species. If the drawing of the line is up to us, she said, then the line is a bookkeeping choice, like deciding Pluto isn't a planet, and it would be confused to fight about it as if a fact were at stake.

I pushed back that species can't be arbitrary in the way words like "bald" are, because when you cross a dog with a cat you get nothing, while dogs crossed with dogs give you dogs — there's a real seam in nature there that we didn't put there. My image was that a species is like a galaxy: the edges are fuzzy and astronomers might argue about whether some outlying star belongs or not, but nobody concludes from the fuzzy edge that there's no such thing as the galaxy, and the stars in it are genuinely held together by something — by gravity, in the galaxy's case. She answered that the comparison collapses exactly where it matters, because gravity is a force acting on every star in the galaxy whether or not any astronomer has ever looked at it, but nothing comparable holds a species together across time — it's just our decision about which populations to lump, which is the choice she was questioning in the first place. I said that there is a galaxy-analogue for living things: interbreeding, gene flow, the actual passing of genes between members of a population generation after generation, which is a physical process running along without us. She then pointed out that bacteria trade genes across what we'd call species lines constantly, so gene flow can't be the gravity, and that ring species show interbreeding itself fails to carve the world cleanly. My last move was that a handful of messy cases don't show that everything is a mess — the fact that some coastlines are so jagged you can't say exactly where the land ends doesn't mean there are no islands, and most of the time a dog and a cat still make nothing.

Neither of us conceded anything, and I honestly don't know which of our moves were any good. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — say who's right at each step and why, flag any points where both of us are talking past each other or smuggling something in, and then say who comes out more right overall? I'd especially like to know whether her "it's a bookkeeping choice" conclusion and my "the messy cases don't spoil the general picture" reply can both be sustained at once, or whether one of them really does undercut the other.
