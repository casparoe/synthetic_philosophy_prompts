For the past two years I've been part of a reading group on the foundations of probability, and one argument in it has stalled so completely that I've begun to suspect the participants are no longer disagreeing about anything that could be settled. The topic is the reference class problem: when we want the probability of some attribute or outcome for a single individual, we must first place that individual in a class of "similar" cases, but the individual belongs to many overlapping classes, and the statistics differ across them. My favorite illustration: estimating the life expectancy of a particular 45-year-old. The mortality table for all 45-year-olds gives one number; refine the class to male, smoker, office worker, with a family history of heart disease, and the number moves each time; and the class defined by everything relevant we know about him contains exactly one member, from which no frequency can be read at all. So which class is the right one, and what fixes that?

Here is where the debate froze. One side, following what I understand to be the traditional Bayesian line, holds that the problem is an artifact of the frequency interpretation of probability and simply dissolves for a Bayesian: an agent should conditionalize on everything it knows, and its probabilities then come from its prior and its evidence, not from matching the individual to a table. The other side concedes that this is right for an idealized agent but insists the problem returns the moment beliefs have to get their numbers from somewhere: a real person forming a view about that 45-year-old consults frequency data, and the frequencies come sorted into some reference class; even self-described Bayesians talk this way when they advise a friend, price an insurance risk, or read an actuarial table. The dissolutionists reply that this is a practical crutch, not part of the theory — the ideal agent needs no class, and the fact that we lean on statistics is a fact about us. The rejoinder to that is where it truly stalls: even if my credences never touch a table, other people have to evaluate them, and to check whether someone's probabilities are any good you compare them against the observed frequencies in some class of cases; choose a different class and you get a different verdict about the same person's calibration. After that, everyone mostly restates their position. I have a hypothesis I want tested rather than comforted: that this is not a substantive disagreement about probability at all, but two projects sharing one word — one group is doing ideal-agent epistemology, where probabilities are a guide for the agent, and the other is doing something like a theory of public or checkable probability, where probabilities summarize the world for purposes of comparison, policy, and trust — and that the reference class problem is real in the second project and absent in the first, so neither side's "we're right" claims ever engage.

Please give me a committed diagnosis, not a survey of positions: say whether my hypothesis is right, and if not, what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, a difference about which cases count as data, a hidden empirical question about whether human credences are stable enough to be assessed without frequencies, or something else — supporting your diagnosis with what each side actually claims, and explicitly noting what each side would have to concede for the other to be vindicated. Then take a definite position on what would count as progress here: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that the impasse is permanent and why. Please write in American English, and don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I'm interested in the diagnosis itself.
