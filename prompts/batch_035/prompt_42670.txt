I'm a philosopher working mostly on action theory, and I've been asked to circulate a short position statement ahead of a conference on agency and rational choice, where the audience will include behavioural economists as well as philosophers. My original training is in the akrasia literature — Davidson, the Socratic denial, the distinction between strict and continent failure — but over the past several years I've become convinced that the empirical work on delay discounting and self-control is not somehow external to the philosophical problem but is in fact the best body of evidence we have about what is actually going on in ordinary failures of will. As a result, my statement has become a hybrid of the traditional package and something closer to the economist's picture, and I want to make sure the hybrid runs before I submit it.

Here is the set of views as I currently hold them. (a) Genuine strict akrasia is possible: an agent can, at the moment of action, judge all-things-considered that abstaining is better, and nonetheless freely perform the act judged worse, so the Socratic view that such cases involve an merely apparent judgement is false. (b) The phenomena ordinarily called weakness of will are best explained by the mechanisms studied in the delay-discounting literature — hyperbolic discount curves, preference reversals as the smaller sooner reward becomes proximate — rather than by anything like a faculty of will that yields to temptation. (c) Hyperbolic discounting is not a straightforward defect; because there is no privileged way of aggregating the preferences of one's temporal selves, a reversal as one reward comes near is not automatically an error, and in at least some cases both orderings are defensible. (d) Akratic action, in the strict sense, is irrational — not merely suboptimal or regrettable, but contrary to reason. (e) Precommitment devices (removing the cigarettes from the house, Ulysses and the mast) do not reduce an agent's autonomy but enhance it, precisely because the akratic agent was never fully in control to begin with. (f) The agent's judgement at the time of action is the judgement that matters for assessing the rationality of what they do then; earlier selves do not get a vote once the moment arrives.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a survey — no history of the problem, please, I'll handle that on the day. Go through the commitments, tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only seem to, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally. In particular, I confess I can't tell whether (b) quietly takes back (a): if the discounting mechanisms explain the phenomena, perhaps the everyday cases are all just preference reversals and the strict cases go unexplained, in which case my concession to the economists is more radical than I intended. And I can't see whether (c) is consistent with the verdict in (d), or with (f) — if there is no fact about what is best all-things-considered, I'm unsure what the irrationality verdict is supposed to be relative to. For each tension you find, say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please write your reply in American English, since the conference is in the States and I'll be reusing portions of it verbatim.
