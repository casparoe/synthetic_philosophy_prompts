I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory, and at last week's reading group two of my fellow doctoral students — Maya, in statistics, and Jonah, in philosophy — fell into a disagreement about imprecise probabilities that I was not able to referee, and would like your help adjudicating.

The occasion was a wager proposed half in jest: £10 at even odds on heads, for a coin to be drawn at random from a box of coins known to have been manufactured with biases spread somewhere between roughly 0.35 and 0.65, with nothing in the setup indicating which coin one will draw. The exchange ran as follows.

(1) Maya opened by insisting that a rational agent must hold a definite credence in heads. If one's attitude towards the proposition is not a single number, she said, then either one is ignorant of one's own mind, or one has no credence at all — and if one has no credence, one cannot evaluate the bet, and should simply decline it. (2) Jonah replied that this demands a precision the evidence does not contain: where the available information is scattered or conflicting, the honest representation of one's attitude is a set or interval of probabilities rather than a fabricated point estimate, and forcing a single number is a kind of self-deception. (3) Maya pressed the practical point: given Jonah's interval, the bet at even odds is favourable on some admissible values of the bias and unfavourable on others, so the theory owes us a verdict. If the verdict is 'consult the midpoint', then a precise credence has been smuggled back in; if the verdict is 'you may take it or decline it', the theory licenses both accepting and refusing the very same wager, which threatens to make probability vacuous as a guide to life. (4) Jonah distinguished recommendation from permissibility: a theory of rationality can leave a choice underdetermined without thereby saying nothing, rather as traffic law permits me either of two routes without recommending either. He added that Maya's demand for a unique verdict in every case is an evidential idealisation — real agents facing real choices are not owed the sharp answers available only when the evidence happens to be sharp. (5) Maya's rejoinder was that permissive theories still owe an account of when options are comparable: if declining heads at even odds is permitted, whereas heads at generous odds must be accepted and heads at stingy odds declined, then the boundary between the permitted and the compulsory is doing all the real work, and Jonah must say where it falls and why. Jonah closed by locating that boundary in maximal choice: an option is permissible just when no probability in the set prefers an alternative to it, and he claimed this is a substantive standard rather than an arbitrary cut.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification. Then indicate who is more right overall, and, if you can, identify what both of them are missing.
