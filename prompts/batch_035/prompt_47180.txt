Last night my closest friend and I spent four hours arguing about Benatar's asymmetry, and we ended exactly where we started, which is what bothers me. Here is where it stalled, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a rerun of the argument.

We both accepted the first two prongs without fuss: when a person exists, their pains are bad and their pleasures are good. The fight was entirely about the second pair — the evaluations of absence. My friend holds that the absence of pain is good even though there is no one around to enjoy that goodness, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived of it. His clinching case was a couple who could conceive a child whose life would contain vastly more suffering than happiness: he insists it is straightforwardly good, not merely neutral, that this child's pains never exist, and that nothing comparable can be said about a couple whose potential child's happiness would have outweighed its pains. I kept pressing the same two objections. First, that praising the absent pain requires a subject of that praise, and there is none — that the third prong is a category error dressed as a value claim. Second, that his move proves either too much or too little: if "good" in the third prong just means "no bads on the books," then the whole asymmetry collapses into a stipulation about which absences we choose to ledger, which is the conclusion smuggling itself in as a premise.

His comeback, which is the part I can't get out of my head, was this: he grants that the third prong cannot be "good for" anyone, and says that is fine — it is impersonally good, the way a world with less agony in it is impersonally better, and only prong four needs the "for someone" reading because deprivation is the one thing that requires a victim. So he ends up with two different senses of evaluation doing different jobs inside one four-cell table, and when I called that equivocation, he called it precision. That is the exact point the four hours died.

So, professional philosopher, think this through carefully before answering — sketch out the possible diagnoses first, then evaluate them. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, turning on whether impersonal and personal value are allowed to be distinct senses of "good," in which case one of us is just defining the terms? Is it substantive but metaethical, about whether welfare-level claims can survive without a subject? Or are we answering different questions under one table — him a question about what worlds are better, me a question about what happens to whom? And here is the thing I most want your judgment on: if the two-senses reading is coherent, does it rescue the asymmetry as a substantive argument for antinatalism, or does it quietly concede that the conclusion only follows for people already sharing a controversial verdict about how to weigh absences — which would make the argument dialectically idle? Finally, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a sharper distinction, a test case that splits the two readings, or is this a bedrock disagreement about whether the absence of agony is a feature of the world that no argument could move?

Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each of your claims, and be explicit about the single point in your diagnosis you are least sure of — I would rather know where your footing is soft than receive a smooth verdict. And do not pad the answer with reading recommendations or name-dropping; argue it from the ideas themselves.
