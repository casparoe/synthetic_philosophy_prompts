I am an analytical chemist, and a case I testified in last month has left me unable to do my job without feeling like a fraud. I was called as an expert witness on a blood sample; the substance was detected, and on cross-examination I was asked whether I knew the substance was present or merely believed it with high confidence, given that my laboratory's own validation studies document a false-positive rate. I answered that I knew it, and the conviction turned partly on that answer. Afterwards, my sister, who did a PhD in epistemology before law, wrote out an argument whose conclusion I find intolerable, and I cannot see my way out of it.

1. One may assert that p only if one knows that p. (Call this the knowledge norm; it seemed the natural thing to appeal to on the stand.)
2. Knowing that p requires more than justified true belief: it requires that one could not easily have been wrong.
3. If one's method for forming the belief that p has a known, nonzero error rate, then one could easily have been wrong: in some nearby possible situation the method runs exactly as it did and produces the same belief while p is false. The size of the error rate is irrelevant to this point; nonzero is enough.
4. My assay has a documented false-positive rate of roughly one in several million. So, by 2 and 3, I do not know that the substance was present.
5. So, by 1, I was not permitted to assert in court that the substance was present; the knowledge norm, taken seriously, forbids the plain form of my testimony.
6. But nothing in the argument is special about chromatography. Every measurement instrument has a documented error rate; so does ordinary perception; so does memory. By parity of reasoning, I may not tell my wife that I parked on level three this morning, and a safety officer may not assert that the fire escape is clear — at most we may assert probabilities or heavily hedged disjunctions.
7. A norm with that consequence is not a norm of assertion but the abolition of assertion. Yet each of 1 through 3 struck me as well-motivated when I first heard them.

Something has to give, but I cannot see what, and the point that troubles me most is this: I am told it is standard to deny that one knows one's lottery ticket will lose, even at odds of one in several million, on exactly the ground in step 3. If a one-in-several-million lottery defeat is enough to destroy knowledge, I cannot find any principled difference between the lottery and my assay — except that everyone agrees my testimony is a paradigm of knowledge and the lottery is a paradigm of its absence. That feels like having the datum decide the case by fiat.

Please tell me which step or inference in the argument you would reject and why. Explain why the step nevertheless looked sound to me, and then price the alternatives: what does each candidate rejection cost, both for my situation as a witness and for the broader positions at stake — the knowledge norm versus its truth- and belief-based rivals, and the treatment of lottery propositions, lotteries being the classic stalking horse here. If you think the argument is valid and the conclusion should simply be swallowed, say so and say what swallowing it comes to in practice. Please answer in British English.
