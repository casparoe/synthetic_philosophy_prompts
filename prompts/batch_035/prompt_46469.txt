So here's my situation: I'm a novelist, mid-way through book five, writing a character who teaches philosophy at a small liberal arts college. Early chapters have her running an intro metaphysics class and I refuse to do the usual movie-version thing where a professor lectures at a whiteboard and everyone gapes — readers can smell a fake classroom scene from a mile away and I won't write one. The session is on what her syllabus calls the special composition question (when do several things add up to one further thing — the two bricks and the one house kind of question), partly because the theme is secretly doing work for the whole novel: her marriage is quietly coming apart and at some point she has to decide whether "the marriage" was ever a thing over and above the two people in it. But in this scene I just need the class itself to be real.

What I want from you is the actual session, designed like a lesson plan she could follow: fifteen sophomores, fifty minutes, bright kids who havent read any of the technical literature yet. Design an activity with rules and some kind of stakes — she's exactly the type to hand out candy or ridiculous fake currency — where students make actual composition judgments about real stuff. I'm imagining she brings in a bag of objects: scattered Lego bricks, a deck of cards stacked just so, a stapler, two chairs shoved together, maybe something gross like her own ponytail. The crucial thing is that the arbitrariness of their own judgments has to hit them during the game, not because she explains it afterwards — I want a stretch where, step by step, nothing intuitive changes at any single step and yet they've crossed from two objects to one (or one to many), and nobody can point to where it happened. Then the debrief, where she maps what just happened onto the standard positions (nihilism, universalism, the restricted views) without having lectured on any of them beforehand.

Two more things and then I'll let you go. First, tell me what would predictably go wrong the first time someone ran this — the ways a room of nineteen-year-olds will route around the point of the exercise if it's badly designed. Second, and this is where you'd genuinely help a novelist out: be honest about which part of the real philosophical problem a classroom game cannot capture. Later in the book a colleague of hers (kind of a jerk, but not wrong about everything) is going to needle her that the cute exercise smuggles in a conventionalist answer — that once you hand out points for composition judgments, you've made it look like composition is just up to us in a way the actual debate never conceded. I need to know whether he's right, so I can decide if she concedes it or bites the bullet. It should all be runnable with junk from a dollar store and no slides — she's fancy about chalk, not equipment.
