In the type-design trade, a "revival" is a new digital typeface presented as a modern instantiation of a historical one — a Garamond or a Baskerville redrawn for today's sizes, screens, and weights. Nobody can copy the original letterforms: the surviving specimens are worn, distorted by ink spread and press pressure, and a revival must in any case redraw the design at new weights, new contrast, new proportions, often for a different medium. Yet designers routinely say of a good revival that it is the same design, and of a bad one that it has lost the design — even though no visible outline in the revival matches any visible outline in the original. At the same time, the same profession treats the exact outlines as what matters when ownership is at stake: a foundry accuses a competitor of stealing a typeface by comparing curves point by point, and in the United States the courts have held that letterform designs themselves are not eligible for copyright (Eltra Corp. v. Ringer, 1978) while the font software that draws them is protected as code — so what the law protects is precisely the file, not the design.

I'd like to know what theory of letterform identity and categorization this pattern of practice presupposes. When designers judge that a revival is still the same Garamond despite wholesale changes to every visible feature, are they presupposing that identity rides on some hidden structural invariant — the underlying stroke skeleton that lettering teachers make students draw before any weight or contrast is added (Gerrit Noordzij built an entire theory of writing around the primacy of the stroke) — or is that too strong, with the practice equally compatible with a family-resemblance view anchored to a few exemplar letterforms? Conversely, when the same community polices knockoffs by comparing outlines, does it presuppose something inconsistent with the revival view, or is it answering a different question — who made this file — under the same word? And how does all this connect to the everyday miracle the letterforms exist for: that a reader recognizes every 'a' as an 'a' across wildly different designs, and even recognizes a single design's 'a' after interpolation to an intermediate weight that was never actually drawn?
