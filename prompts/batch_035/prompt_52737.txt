Two members of my evolutionary anthropology reading group spent most of last week's session arguing about the human chin, and those of us watching couldn't agree on who had the better of it. I'd like a point-by-point adjudication.

Some background for context: humans are, as far as anyone knows, the only primate with a protruding bony chin; Neanderthals, archaic Homo, and the australopiths all lack one. The assigned readings covered both the functional hypotheses about the chin (masticatory stress resistance, sexual selection, effects of changes in hormone levels tied to social restructuring) and the developmental literature on jaw growth. Mara argued the adaptationist line; Devin invoked Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique. The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Mara opened by insisting that a structure this conspicuous could not simply be invisible to selection. Selection is the only naturalistic source of functional organization, she said, so the burden of proof lies with the nonadaptationist: we should begin with adaptive hypotheses and retreat from them only when they fail.

(2) Devin replied that the chin is the classic candidate for a spandrel. As the hominin face shortened over evolutionary time, the tooth row and the alveolar process anchoring the teeth retreated, while the base of the mandible did not shrink correspondingly; the chin is the bony geometry that results when the two growth fields fall out of alignment, sculpted by ordinary processes of bone deposition and resorption. Nothing about the chin itself needed to be selected — it is a byproduct of building a smaller jaw.

(3) Mara's comeback: a byproduct hypothesis is a just-so story in reverse. You can tell an equally unfalsifiable story either way, whereas adaptationist hypotheses at least generate checkable predictions — that the trait should impose costs, show heritable variation, and exhibit sex or population differences tracking its proposed function — which can be tested against mandibular mechanics and the fossil record.

(4) Devin said that was Panglossian bookkeeping. The adaptationist search procedure, he argued, is to generate a plausible benefit, look for confirming evidence, and move on; since every trait has some beneficial consequence or other, the procedure can in principle never return the verdict that a structure is a nonadaptation. A hypothesis that cannot fail explains nothing.

(5) Mara answered that drift and byproduct claims are in no better epistemic shape. Distinguishing drift from selection requires effective population sizes, heritabilities, and selection coefficients — data that are simply unavailable for fossil hominins — so saying that something is a byproduct is at least as much of a free pass as saying it is an adaptation, and the accusation of untestability cuts both ways.

(6) Devin conceded the data point but not the moral. Even if neither side can settle the chin, that is a reason to be more restrained about adaptive storytelling in general, not a reason to keep telling the stories. He added that the dispute is not about whether the chin does anything now — a byproduct can always be co-opted later for some new function — but about whether its origin demands a selection story at all, and about which default a rational inquirer should adopt when the evidence runs out.

At that point they ran out of time and we adjourned without a verdict. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and justify the rating. Then say who comes out ahead overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing — both about the chin specifically and about how the adaptationism dispute is actually resolved in practice, for example by what would have to be true for one side's method to outperform the other's.
