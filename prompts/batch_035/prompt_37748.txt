I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on social epistemology (18 students, 80 minutes), and next week we move from the Mill/Hume-style reductionist view of testimony to Coady's anti-reductionist rejoinder and then Fricker's local reductionism. Design an in-class activity in which students acquire beliefs from deliberately mixed channels — some first-hand observation, some short relay chains of classmates, some unsigned written notes — under a scoring system that pays for accurate beliefs and penalizes false ones, so that they run into the reductionist's challenge in practice rather than on the page: whenever they trust a relayed report, they should be forced to ask how they could non-circularly certify the source's reliability, and discover that any certification route they try runs through further testimony. At some point the cost of checking should exceed the value of the belief, so that students also feel Coady's point that much of our testimonial reliance is rationally non-optional and cannot be earned by prior investigation. Include rules, materials, a timeline, a scoring scheme that doesn't use real money, a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the three positions, an honest note on which part of the debate no classroom game can reproduce (for instance, why testimony is practically inescapable in ordinary life but merely one option among many in a staged exercise), and a list of the two or three things most likely to go wrong on a first run and how to handle them.
