My coworker Elena runs a lunchtime discussion group at the software company where we work, and over the past year she has handed down verdicts on a series of moral-dilemma cases. Her track record so far: (1) the standard trolley switch case — you must divert the trolley, and it is not a real dilemma, just a hard decision; (2) the transplant case, where a surgeon could harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five — she must not, and again no dilemma, since refusing is not wrong even though five people die; (3) two promises of equal importance to two friends that cannot both be kept — no dilemma: you may flip a coin or otherwise pick, and while you owe the disappointed friend an apology, you have done nothing wrong; (4) Sophie's Choice — a genuine dilemma: whichever child she picks, she does something wrong, though the greater wrong is the guard's; (5) Sartre's student torn between joining the Free French and staying with his mother — a genuine dilemma, and in her telling there is no fact about what he ought to do; his commitment itself ends up creating the reason that justifies it. Next week's case: during a ventilator shortage, a nurse must decide which of two patients — identical prognoses, equal claims, no relevant distinguishing facts — gets the last ventilator, knowing the other will die; hospital policy permits but does not require holding a lottery between them. Predict Elena's verdict on this case, identify the rule she seems to be implicitly running, lay out the other rules that would fit her earlier verdicts just as well, explain which of those rules the new case actually divides and how, give your best guess at her answer along with your reasons for preferring that reading, and propose the single further case that would most cleanly discriminate among the candidate rules. Please answer in American English.
