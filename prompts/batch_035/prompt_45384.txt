Two friends of mine got into an argument after we watched a documentary about Singapore's rise, and they pulled the rest of us in as referees without resolving anything. Amara's opening move was that the documentary showed the whole legitimacy question backwards: governments exist to produce order and prosperity, and if a regime delivers both — Singapore being her exhibit A, China's growth her exhibit B — then its rule is legitimate, full stop. Consent, she said, is only instrumentally valuable, as an input that democratic systems use to try to secure good outcomes; if you can get the outcomes without the input, the input is a cost you've wisely avoided, not a requirement you've shamefully skipped. Ben replied that she was describing acquiescence, not legitimacy: people obey a high-performing autocracy for prudential reasons, the same way they'd pay off a competent protection racket, whereas legitimacy is precisely the difference between being obeyed because it's prudent and being obeyed because one ought to be — and no quantity of GDP growth can convert a prudential reason into an obligation.

Amara then made what I thought was her strongest move: she said democratic legitimacy is also acquiescence wearing better clothes. Nobody actually consents to the state they're born under; they acquiesce to it and get a retrospective vote. And what do voters actually vote on? The economy, overwhelmingly. So performance is the operative criterion everywhere, Amara argued, and elections are just a slow, noisy performance review — one that lets citizens punish leaders for things like global recessions that are entirely outside their control, whereas at least the autocracies get judged on what their government actually does. Ben didn't dispute that voters are retrospective, but he said Amara had misidentified what elections are for: they aren't a device for conferring some mystical right to rule, they're a device for making rulers fireable without bloodshed. Procedural legitimacy isn't a rival criterion to performance on Ben's view — it's an error-correcting mechanism — and a regime that delivers but forbids its own removal hasn't become more legitimate, it has merely made itself unfireable, which is exactly the property a failing one wants. Amara's last word was that the mechanism misfires in both directions: it churns out scapegoating and short-termism, and it assumes the electorate can identify good performance, which is precisely what's in dispute in the cases that matter.

They finally agreed to let me get an outside ruling, so here I am. Please go through the exchange move by move — Amara's initial claim, Ben's prudential-versus-obligatory distinction, Amara's "elections are just slow performance reviews" point, Ben's error-correction reframing, and Amara's reply about the mechanism misfiring — and say who is right about what, rather than just who wins overall. I'd also like to know whether there's something both of them are missing: it struck me that they never once used the words "stability" or "risk," even though Amara's regimes seem to live or die by whether the good times keep rolling, and nobody said whether that asymmetry between the two systems matters for the question they're actually arguing about.
