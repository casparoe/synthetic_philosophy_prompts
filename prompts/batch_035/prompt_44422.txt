Please argue for the following position, arguing for it and not against it: the cross-cultural moral psychology literature is usually deployed against moral objectivism — as fuel for the argument from disagreement — but it in fact contains the resources for the objectivist's strongest reply.

The background, for orientation: empirical work on moral judgment has established that Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic (WEIRD) populations are outliers on many measures long treated as the human default — the steepness of the fall in willingness to help strangers as kinship and proximity decline, the insistence on impartial, generalizable principles over context-sensitive role obligations, the treatment of harm and fairness as the whole of morality while loyalty, authority, and sanctity register only faintly. The cross-cultural economic-game studies tell a complementary story: the Machiguenga of the Peruvian Amazon, playing the ultimatum game, offered on average roughly a quarter of the stake and almost never rejected even offers below twenty percent, while proposers in Los Angeles offered nearly half; across the wider set of small-scale societies, offers and punishment patterns tracked local market integration and cooperative ecology rather than any invariant fairness instinct. The standard moral drawn by relativists and constructivists is that what we call moral judgment is a variable local product, and that apparent moral truth is just the consensus of whichever population is doing the judging.

Argue instead that these same findings, properly interpreted, hand the moral objectivist precisely what the argument from disagreement was thought to preclude: a non-question-begging way to rank the conditions under which people form moral judgments.

The argument should be organized around what I will call an instrumental model of institutions. Treat market integration, anonymity, mobility, and contract enforcement not as mere causes of different moralities, but as instruments through which people perceive what morally matters — some functioning like telescopes, some like distorting lenses. On this model, cross-cultural variation is not deep disagreement about moral truth but variation in instrument quality, and disagreement only counts against objectivism when it is unconditionalized, i.e., persists among fully informed, calm, mutually comprehending judges reasoning under conditions we have independent reason to trust. Once we can rank those conditions, the observed variation stops looking like a stalemate between equally positioned observers and starts looking like the expected disagreement among observers using instruments of differing quality.

In developing this, you must do four things. First, say what licenses the instrumental interpretation at all — what distinguishes an institution that improves moral perception from one that merely reshapes preferences, given that both will produce confident, self-seeming judgments. Second, confront the question-begging objection directly: the ranking of institutions looks parasitic on the very WEIRD-inflected values (impartiality, welfare, individual choice) that the institutions themselves promote, so the telescope analogy threatens to smuggle in its conclusion. Third, deal honestly with purity: if institutional modernity predictably erodes sanctity norms, and some of those norms plausibly encode genuine moral knowledge, the instrumental model appears to deliver the wrong verdict — explain why this does not sink the argument, without denying the phenomenon. Fourth, close by stating candidly what the argument does not establish, and where it leaves the objectivist still owing the relativist an answer.

Avoid the following cheap moves: retreating to the observation that all cultures condemn unprovoked harm, as if thin convergence sufficed; counting the cross-cultural presence of the same handful of moral foundations as vindication, when the disagreement is about weighting, not presence; and quietly relabeling a constructivism as a realism. The instrumental model is a substantive, contestable claim about institutions — treat it as something to be defended, not asserted.
