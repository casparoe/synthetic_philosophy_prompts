My university's philosophy society has invited a scholar of Jain philosophy, a committed defender of anekāntavāda, for a recorded interview that I shall be conducting. I come to the topic freshly bruised: last weekend I spent several hours arguing with a friend, a staunch realist, who granted that Jain logic is elegant metaphysical bookkeeping but insisted that the doctrine of many-sidedness amounts, at bottom, to 'a polite way of never being wrong'. By the end he had a challenge I could not meet, and I want to put it to my guest directly. Call it the dispute test: if two parties' claims can both be affirmed 'from some standpoint', then they were never really disagreeing; and if they were genuinely disagreeing — over whether some event occurred, say — anekāntavāda does nothing to resolve it, but merely redescribes the disagreement, after the fact, as a difference of aspect. My questions should force the guest to engage with that, not wave it away.

Please draft nine to eleven interview questions, written in British English. Include at least one each on: (i) whether the claim that all truths are partial is itself partial, or whether the doctrine exempts itself and thereby becomes the one-sided absolute it condemns; (ii) the scope of the syāt operator — whether 'in some respect' applies to mathematics and morals as readily as to descriptions of complex substances; (iii) how the seven forms of predication are meant to distinguish a genuine contradiction from a mere difference of standpoint; and at least one that operationalises my friend's dispute test as a concrete case for the guest to adjudicate. For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give, drawing on the standard reply from the tradition where there is one. Please avoid questions that merely ask her to restate the doctrine, and do not ask her to retell the blind-men-and-elephant parable.
