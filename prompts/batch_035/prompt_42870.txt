I teach a graduate seminar on theories of well-being, and this past Thursday's session on hedonism got stuck on the heterogeneity problem. The problem as I pressed it: the pleasantness of eating a ripe fig, of finishing a proof, of an orgasm, and of sinking into a hot bath appear to share no property beyond the word 'pleasant' — no common feel, no shared intensity scale, no single duration profile — and this threatens not only the intelligibility of summing pleasures into one total but the very claim that pleasure is a single kind of thing that could be the sole good. During discussion, a colleague offered the familiar retreat: pleasure is not a sensation but a pro-attitude — each of those episodes is a state of affairs the subject takes favorably toward, and that taking is what they all have in common. My students thought this was a bait-and-switch: the hedonist needs pleasantness to be the thing that makes a life good, they objected, and if what unifies pleasures is an attitude rather than anything felt, the view either smuggles in a desire theory or leaves the value-bearer obscure.

I want to see whether the attitudinal line can actually be made to work, so argue for it: argue that the pro-attitude analysis dissolves the heterogeneity problem rather than merely renaming it. Make a committed case, not a survey — no 'philosophers have offered several responses'. Present the argument as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Across the five points I expect you to (i) state precisely what property unifies the heterogeneous episodes; (ii) explain why summing them into a single total becomes intelligible once they are unified by that property; and (iii) confront the bait-and-switch charge directly, saying what the value-bearer is and why it is not just a desire in disguise. Two constraints: do not run the argument through the higher/lower pleasures distinction, and do not lean on the claim that pain is unified in a way pleasure is not — the case should stand on its own.
