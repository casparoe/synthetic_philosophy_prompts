I run pre-departure orientation for a university study-abroad program — a few dozen students a semester, headed everywhere from Tokyo to Cairo to Berlin. I have maybe fifteen minutes with each of them and no philosophy training, and they constantly ask stuff like: do I have to tip there, is jaywalking rude, do I queue like at home. I need an actual decision procedure I can run in one advising session, not a lecture. A colleague of mine sorts every local convention into three buckets: ones that coordinate (which side to pass on, how you queue), ones that compensate (tipping), and ones that signal membership (dress, greetings). She says visitors are bound by the first kind absolutely, by the second only where skipping it stiffs a specific person, and by the third barely at all. I'm honestly not sure that carve-up holds up. Build me the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it terminates in a "follow it" or a "don't," and where it has to hand the call back to me because it's about values, not facts. Use my colleague's three buckets if they work, fix them if they don't. Then run it on three cases I actually get: an American student who insists on tipping in Japan because not tipping at home feels like stiffing the waiter; a student headed somewhere where boarding a bus is basically a contact sport, who want to know if elbowing past people is stealing from whoever was there first; and a student who'll be in a city where everyone crosses against the light and thinks waiting at the corner makes her look like a rube. Include at least one case where the procedure's answer goes against what I'd want to tell the student, and be upfront about what the procedure can't settle. Answer in American English.
