So last month at a family dinner my brother Daniel told a story that turned into a huge argument between him and his friend Priya, and I've been chewing on it ever since because honestly I couldn't referee it myself. The story: back in February he saw a cyclist go down hard on an icy road, and he stopped, called the ambulance, kept the guy warm with his own jacket, and waited with him. Then, laughing, he admitted the thing that was actually going through his head the whole time was that a woman he'd been wanting to ask out was driving right behind him and would see the whole thing. Priya pounced: she said that admission retroactively ruins the moral worth of what he did. The act was beneficial, sure, but moral credit tracks why you do it, and a deed done to look good in front of someone isn't morally good, any more than a vending machine that happens to dispense medicine deserves praise for it. Daniel came back that this makes morality a purity contest for the actor while the cyclist, the only person affected, gets the exact same outcome either way -- and that by her standard nobody in history ever did a good thing, since human motives are always mixed and mostly self-flattering. Priya said mixed is fine; her question is just which motive was doing the work, and the natural test is counterfactual: would he have stopped if the woman hadn't been there? Daniel objected that the counterfactual is unfair -- people have no clean access to their own motives, so it can't be a requirement for credit -- and anyway he claims it's not vanity all the way down, since he'd have felt awful driving past (he once did, and it bugged him for a year). Priya replied that guilt-avoidance is just another self-regarding feeling, so on his own logic it shouldn't count either. At that point they gave up and refilled their drinks.

I want you to adjudicate this properly, move by move. Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works on moral worth and the ethics of action, think it through carefully before answering (a little brainstorming on the general landscape of views about when an agent deserves moral credit would help), and then go through the exchange: for each step -- Priya's vending machine point, Daniel's purity-contest reply, the counterfactual test, the mixed-motives and self-knowledge objections, and Priya's guilt-avoidation comeback -- say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification. Then tell me who comes out more right overall, and, importantly, whether there's something both of them are missing that would dissolve part of the dispute -- I have a feeling they're sliding between two different questions ("was the act good?" vs. "is Daniel a good man?") but I'm not sure if that's actually their problem or just something I read somewhere. Don't go easy on either of them just because one of them is my brother.
