Our philosophy reading group spent the last few weeks on Simone Weil's short 1943 essay on abolishing political parties (written in London right before her death, published posthumously), and we've gotten stuck in a rut that I want a way out of. As I understand her case, it's roughly: a party is a machine built to manufacture collective passions, it exerts constant pressure on the minds of its individual members, its first and ultimate goal is its own unlimited growth rather than truth or justice, and anyone who joins one ends up handing over their judgment — they adopt positions because those positions are good for the party, and the very structure makes them unable to notice they're doing it. Hence parties shouldn't be reformed or tempered but abolished.

The rut is the standard objection everyone immediately raises: this proves too much. Newspapers, trade unions, churches, activist groups, nations, even football clubs all generate collective passions and deform their members' thinking in the same ways, and Weil doesn't want to abolish all of those. The two moves our group keeps cycling between are (a) saying her argument is therefore a failure or rhetorical exaggeration, and (b) trying to find some feature that distinguishes parties from all those other associations — something about parties being necessarily general-purpose rivals for state power rather than sectional, or something. Move (b) keeps failing because Weil's own text seems to condemn the passion-generating and judgment-absorbing features as such, and those features are everywhere.

I'd like you to argue for a different position, namely that both moves are wrong because they're looking for the wrong kind of distinguishing feature — in the content or purpose of the association rather than in the relation it claims over the person. The position: Weil's argument is best read as targeting not collective passion in general but the total, advance claim on the member's judgment — the promise, made before any question has even arisen, to think as the party thinks on whatever comes up. On that reading her conclusion is both narrower and wider than abolition of parties sounds: narrower, because most of what ordinary voters and sympathizers do (reading a party's stuff, voting for it, being glad when it wins) survives untouched, so the proves-too-much objection loses its grip on newspapers and unions read by people with their judgment intact; but wider, because the argument then condemns arrangements nobody calls parties — whipped legislative voting, signed election pledges, loyalty-styled ideological litmus tests within professional fields, activist organizations that require affirming a platform as a condition of belonging. Please actually make this argument: show why the reading is a plausible reconstruction of her case, defend the claim that the advance-promise feature does the real work in it and that the passion stuff is secondary or derivative, and engage the obvious counterargument that this collapses her radical abolitionism into a modest plea for independent-mindedness, which would make the essay much less interesting than it's usually taken to be.

One more thing: as you go, be honest about load-bearing steps. Tell me which part of the argument is most likely to fail, what a critic would press on hardest, and what exactly would have to be true — or what consideration would have to show up — for you to abandon the position and concede that Weil really is just making the familiar proves-too-much-vulnerable claim, or that move (b) with some better distinguishing feature was the right way out all along. Don't just flag weaknesses in passing; tell me what would change your mind.
