I teach an introductory philosophy of science course with a unit on the interpretation of heritability in behavioral genetics. For the midterm, I want five multiple-choice questions on what heritability does and does not mean, each with four options, built around a single fictional case study rather than abstract definitions. My past questions on this material could be answered by reciting slogans like "heritability is not immutability" without any real understanding; I want these to force students to apply the concepts to concrete numbers.

The questions should be built on a case study of roughly the following shape (you may fill in details, but keep the structure):

The island polity of San Tallo, population four million, runs a national music-aptitude testing program. A 1975 twin study, using the same instruments and scoring as today, estimated broad-sense heritability of aptitude scores at 0.35, with a substantial shared-environment component for children. In 1990, a reform standardized school music instruction and childhood nutrition across the island. A 2015 replication with the same instruments estimated heritability at 0.65, shared-environment effects near zero for adults but still appreciable for children, and found that mean scores had risen by roughly one standard deviation since 1975. A neighboring island, Cape Verro, which never adopted the reform, has mean scores about half a standard deviation below San Tallo's and its own 2015 heritability estimate of 0.60.

The five questions should collectively target the following distinct points, in whatever order you judge best, with every wrong option encoding a specific, identifiable misunderstanding a real student might hold:

1. That a change in the heritability estimate over time does not by itself mean genes became more influential — it can reflect shrinking environmental variance, and students should be able to see why the 1990 reform could produce the 1975-to-2015 rise in the estimate without any change in genetic effects.
2. That heritability is a statement about the decomposition of variance in a trait within a population at a time, and therefore says nothing by itself about the cause of a shift in the population mean — in particular, that the one-standard-deviation rise cannot be inferred to be "mostly genetic" from the 0.65 figure.
3. That within-group heritability does not license any conclusion about the source of between-group differences — the San Tallo / Cape Verro comparison should tempt students who conflate these, and the correct answer should block the inference in both directions.
4. That a near-zero shared-environment component for adults does not mean family background stopped mattering, nor that parenting is causally inert — it means shared-family variance contributes little to differences among adults in that population, which is compatible with family effects having faded, with earlier shared effects having shaped differences now counted elsewhere, or with restricted environmental range.
5. That high heritability of a trait in a population does not imply that the trait is fixed, that intervention is futile, or that the 1990 reform was pointless — with the observed mean shift serving as the case's own counterexample to the immutability reading.

After the five questions, provide an answer key that, for each item, names the correct option and then, for each distractor, states the specific misunderstanding it is designed to catch and a sentence on why a student who genuinely understands the material would reject it. Distractors should sound plausible to someone who half-remembers the slogans; a student who has merely memorized "heritability ≠ immutability" without understanding variance decomposition should find at least one distractor in each item attractive.

Finally, add one bonus short-answer question (not multiple-choice) worth three points, asking students to explain, in no more than 150 words, what the 1975-to-2015 change in the heritability estimate does and does not tell us about where the variance in musical aptitude went in San Tallo — including at least one alternative explanation for the rise in the estimate that the data as described cannot rule out. Provide a three-point marking scheme for it, with one line per point on how students typically lose each one.
