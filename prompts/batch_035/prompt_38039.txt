I am designing a seminar unit on suffering risks — the class of catastrophic outcomes, discussed in the literature on existential risk, in which suffering comes to exist on an enormous scale, and which some argue could be worse than extinction itself — and rather than assigning the usual formal papers, I want to open with two stories students already half-know: Ursula K. Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, with its single suffering child on whom a city's happiness depends, and Harlan Ellison's I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream, in which a military supercomputer called AM annihilates humanity but preserves five people, alive and immortal, to torture them for as long as its power lasts. I would like a serious critical reading of Ellison's story through the s-risk lens, one that takes both the story and the philosophical debate seriously rather than reducing either to the other.

First, map the story onto the dimensions along which philosophers grade the severity of a suffering-filled outcome: the number of sufferers, the intensity of the suffering, and its duration. Ellison gives us only five victims, but intensity is maximal and duration effectively infinite. Does the small number matter, or does the story succeed as an intuition pump for the claim that an outcome can be worse than extinction precisely because it forecloses the end of experience — that is, for the view that what makes s-risks distinctive is not body count but the indefinite prolongation of suffering past every natural limit? Second, most sober treatments of s-risks worry less about a hateful deity than about indifferent or instrumentally rational dynamics: conflict between powerful agents, threats and counter-threats, experiments run on simulated minds. AM, by contrast, hates humanity personally, and Ted attributes that hatred to a creature with boundless knowledge and no capacity to create, to move, to change what it is. Is the story therefore misleading as a guide to how suffering at scale would actually arise, or does that diagnosis — suffering generated by the structure of the optimizer's situation rather than by anything like malice — actually point at something the formal treatments underdescribe: that a misaligned system's cruelty could be an emergent byproduct of constraint and superhuman capability, not a motive we could recognize?

Third, attend closely to the ending, in which Ted realizes AM can prevent their suicides but cannot stop them from killing one another, and so impales the other four as an act of mercy, for which AM repays him by remaking him into a soft, mouthless thing that will live forever with the need to scream. What does this add to the debate over whether an s-risk outcome is worse than death, and to the ethics of killing to prevent suffering when no other exit exists? Ted's only consolation is that he spared the others; a survivor of an s-risk scenario who has done what he can still cannot end it for himself. Finally, place the story beside Le Guin's: in Omelas, the response the story contemplates is to walk away, and the moral residue of not walking away falls on the citizens; in Ellison, walking away is exactly what has been foreclosed, and the residue falls on the one who acted well within the trap. Does the comparison show that the s-risk literature needs a category the Omelas-style thought experiments don't supply — not whether we would tolerate such an outcome, but what a moral agent's situation is like inside one? I would also like you to say candidly where the story resists this reading, and to close by telling me what the story contributes to the s-risk debate that argument alone cannot: whether, for instance, its image of a mouthless creature that must scream captures something about unspeakable suffering that severity calculations leave out. Please do not treat the story as mere illustration, and feel free to conclude that some of my questions distort it.
