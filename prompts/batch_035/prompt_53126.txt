I serve on a small advisory panel to our national statistics office, which is deciding whether to make subjective wellbeing a headline indicator alongside GDP. At our last meeting, two panel members — a behavioral economist, Marcus, and a social epidemiologist, Lena — spent an hour arguing about whether the international happiness rankings measure anything real and whether a government should ever aim to top them. I stayed out of it and took notes, and I'd like a referee's account of the exchange. Here is the substance, in order.

(1) Marcus opened by denying that the rankings measure happiness at all. The headline figures, he said, rest on people answering a single question asking them to place their own life on a ladder from worst to best possible life, zero to ten. Answers to such questions are heavily shaped by cultural reporting norms: some nations grade themselves harshly by habit, others avoid extreme answers, others are culturally disposed to modest self-assessment. So the league table ranks response styles as much as it ranks lives, and a country's position tells you more about how its people talk about themselves than about how they feel.

(2) Lena answered that Marcus had smuggled in an ambiguity between moment-to-moment affect and life evaluation. Nobody claims the Finns feel the most joy; the measure asks people to evaluate their lives as a whole, and life evaluation is exactly the kind of thing a country's institutions can be good or bad for. Moreover, if the numbers were mostly cultural noise, they would not track the objective conditions they in fact track — social trust, safety from violent crime, freedom to make major life decisions, low corruption. The correlations do the work of ruling out the pure-response-style story.

(3) Marcus conceded some of that but reloaded with hedonic adaptation. People, he said, adapt to their circumstances, so satisfaction scores measure the gap between life as lived and life as expected, and expectations are trainable. Two populations could report identical average scores while living very different lives, because the one with lower expectations is easier to please. He noted this is why rising national income has historically failed to deliver rising average satisfaction — the classic finding in that literature — and concluded that a government chasing the top of the table is chasing a treadmill it can only climb by managing citizens' expectations downward.

(4) Lena replied that adaptation is incomplete, not total. People measurably fail to adapt to unemployment, chronic pain, caregiving strain, and daily commuting misery, and the same literature Marcus invoked shows that within a country, at a moment, income and satisfaction move together robustly. And Scandinavian expectations, she said, are not mysteriously low — they are realistic because security, trust, and functioning institutions keep disappointing things from happening. High scores there ride on the absence of dread, not on diminished ambition.

(5) Marcus then made his policy move. Even granting the measurement, aiming to top the table is a Goodhart problem: the moment average satisfaction becomes a national target, the cheapest path to moving the number stops being making lives better and becomes adjusting how lives are interpreted. He pointed to something concrete from our own panel's consultations: a ministry draft proposal for secondary-school modules in what it called realistic contentment, explicitly teaching teenagers to calibrate their aspirations so that adult life satisfaction measures would improve. That, he said, is the measure devouring the goal, and a government that runs the rankings as a target will manufacture exactly such proposals.

(6) Lena's answer was that this argument proves too much, since any target, GDP included, can be gamed, and we don't conclude GDP should not be a target — we conclude you watch for gaming. But her harder point was that the line between gaming the metric and genuinely improving wellbeing may not exist where Marcus assumes. Cognitive behavioral therapy works substantially by changing how people interpret their circumstances, and we do not call recovering depressives victims of Goodhart's law. So the school modules might be manipulation or might be genuinely valuable emotional skill-building, and the metric itself cannot tell us which — which means you pair subjective indicators with objective ones and look for divergence, rather than abandoning the whole enterprise. Marcus closed by saying that if the indicator cannot distinguish, from the inside, genuine improvement from managed expectations, that was precisely his case against making it the target, not a reply to it.

I could not referee this and did not want to. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each numbered step, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or clearly wrong, and why, engaging with the argument as actually made rather than with the standard textbook version of it. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing across the whole exchange.
