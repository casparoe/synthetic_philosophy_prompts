I'm a retired engineer who reads history for pleasure, and last year I read Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men and Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners back to back, then followed the exchanges between the two authors that came afterwards. What struck me is how much the two sides agree on: what Reserve Police Battalion 101 did at Józefów and in the villages around Lublin, how many men took part, that the men were offered the chance to step out and almost none did, and that those who did suffered no real consequences. Yet from the same record Goldhagen concludes the perpetrators were driven by an eliminationist antisemitism pervasive in German society, while Browning concludes that ordinary men shaped by conformity, deference to authority, and the gradual dulling of inhibitions will do such things in many societies, not only Germany. As far as I can tell, in the decades since, each side has mostly restated itself, and nobody has moved. I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who was right. Is there a hidden factual dispute here after all — about how antisemitic German society actually was before 1933 — or is the real disagreement about what an explanation has to accomplish, that is, whether pointing to a motive allegedly shared by an entire culture explains anything more than restating the behavior it is meant to explain? Or are the two men answering different questions under one heading, one about why Germany produced the Holocaust and the other about how otherwise ordinary human beings become killers? And given your diagnosis, could anything have ended the argument — new archival evidence, studies of non-German perpetrators, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — or was the impasse guaranteed from the start?
