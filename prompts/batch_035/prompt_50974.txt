I am a final-year PhD student in computational biology, and my department's wet-lab groups bring us sequencing data from small studies — a dozen tumour samples here, thirty patients there — and the recurring argument is always the same: how many entities should the model posit? Is the variant-allele-frequency pattern better explained by two tumour subclones or three? Should the methylation profiles be clustered into four groups or five? Does a modest correlation across time points warrant a latent factor, or is it noise with a face on it? At present the answer is settled by whoever argues hardest, or by whatever cutoff is the software default, and reviewers rightly push back. My own reading leads me to believe the relevant resource is the Occam's-razor tradition running through Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and minimum description length: the idea that a model is a code for the data, that complexity must be paid for in bits of description length, and that the fit-versus-complexity trade-off can in principle be made principled rather than a matter of taste. But I cannot ask my collaborators to run a mixture over all computable hypotheses, and half the relevant quantities are uncomputable or inestimable at our sample sizes in any case.

Please write a decision procedure my group could actually follow when choosing among models of differing complexity. It should set out the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a plain instruction not to posit the extra component, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of scientific values — interpretability, downstream clinical use, what a reviewer will accept — rather than of compression. It should be honest about the gap between the ideal theory and anything computable in an afternoon: in particular about the points at which the description length of a hypothesis is a judgement call dressed up as a number — the coding of priors over parameters, the choice of language in which the model is described, the fact that Kolmogorov complexity is only defined up to an additive constant that no finite experiment can dwarf. Where the theory cannot deliver, the procedure should say so rather than manufacture false precision.

Then run the procedure on two cases drawn from our recent work: (a) variant allele frequencies across forty loci in a single tumour biopsy, where a three-subclone model improves the fit only marginally over two; and (b) clustering RNA-seq profiles from twenty-eight patients into four versus five groups, where the fifth group consists of five samples and would be biologically interesting if real. Include at least one run in which the procedure returns the answer the collaborator will not like — for instance, refusing to license the subclone that the postdoc's favourite mechanistic story requires — and say what, if anything, the postdoc could legitimately do about it within the procedure rather than by overriding it.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: the situations in which two researchers following it in good faith could still reach different conclusions, the class of errors it is structurally blind to (for example, a shared misspecification that makes the wrong simple model compress well), and what evidence from outside the dataset would be needed to settle the residual disagreements.

Constraints: the procedure must be executable by a wet-lab postdoc with R and a spreadsheet in under an hour per analysis; it may not presuppose sample sizes at which asymptotic or consistency arguments bite; and each step should be marked as either derived, estimated, or asserted outright.
