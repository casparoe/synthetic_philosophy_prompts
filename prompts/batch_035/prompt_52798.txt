The literature on manipulation versus legitimate persuasion seems stuck in a loop, and I want a diagnosis of the stall rather than another entry in it. As far as I can tell, the pattern is this: someone proposes a definition of manipulation — in terms of covert influence, bypassing the target's capacity for critical reflection, exploiting decision-making defects, imposing unaccepted costs, influencing through non-rational routes — then someone else constructs a counterexample (a benevolent nudge that fits the definition, a straightforward lie that doesn't), and the cycle repeats. Meanwhile, a parallel debate about why manipulation is wrong at all — whether it violates autonomy, treats a person as a mere means, or merely tends to cause harm, and whether covert benevolent manipulation is even wrong — has stalled in exactly the same way, with everyone agreeing that paradigm cases like guilt-tripping and dark patterns are manipulative and agreeing about the facts of those cases, yet not moving an inch on the definitions or the grounds of the wrong. My working hypothesis is that the definitional dispute is a normative dispute in disguise: each proposed definition quietly encodes a verdict about which features of a person's agency are owed respect, so the counterexample exchange is really the sides disagreeing about which influences on decision-making they find objectionable, while talking as if they were disagreeing about the meaning of a word. Is that right? Diagnose the disagreement — verbal, substantive, or two groups answering different questions under one heading — using what the various sides actually say, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, a reframing of the question, empirical findings about how non-rational influence actually affects people, or an argument that the impasse reflects something about the concept itself and should be expected to persist.
