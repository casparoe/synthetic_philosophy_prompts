I teach a philosophy of history seminar and have written a handout arguing from Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation to a conclusion I can't swallow, and I'd like to know exactly where it goes wrong rather than simply dismissing it. The chain runs:

(1) Nietzsche's claim in that essay is that history is justified only insofar as it serves life — the vitality and capacity for future action of the living — never by devotion to truth for its own sake.

(2) He grants that monumental history, which presents the great deeds of the past as models for future greatness, necessarily distorts: it smooths the past into monumental outlines, treats as comparable things that are not, and drifts towards something close to free poetic invention.

(3) He holds that each of his three modes is legitimate exactly when it serves life — monumental history for the man of action who needs precedents, antiquarian history for the preserver, critical history for the sufferer who must destroy a past in order to live.

(4) So the historian's duty of truth is always conditional: fidelity to the record binds only up to the point where life demands otherwise.

(5) What serves life at the level of a culture, rather than an individual, is whatever sustains that culture's cohesion and vigour.

(6) So where a fabricated founding myth demonstrably increases a culture's cohesion and vigour — imagine a ministry polling a civic-vitality index before and after installing an invented national history in its schools — that myth is not merely excusable but positively justified; and the critical historians who then demolish it, sapping that vigour, are doing decadent work.

I can't accept (6), but I'd also rather not give up (1), which seems to me the essay's genuine insight. Tell me which premise or inference to reject, why it looked fine to me, and what each alternative rejection costs — including the cost of keeping (1) and giving up something else I haven't listed. If you think the chain is actually valid and Nietzsche simply has to bite the bullet here, say so plainly rather than rescuing me. And if I've just misread the essay — if, say, nothing in it supports (4), or (5) trades on an equivocation the text itself blocks — tell me that too; I'd rather be corrected than flattered. Please answer in British English.
