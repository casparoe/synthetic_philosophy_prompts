I serve on the programme committee of a small foundation that funds three-year postdoctoral fellowships in political philosophy, and this cycle we can support four fellows working on the cluster of problems descending from Sen's 1979 Tanner Lecture, "Equality of What?" — the question of what exactly a society committed to egalitarian justice should be equalising, and the ensuing quarrel between welfare egalitarians, resource egalitarians, and defenders of the capability approach. I am not asking for a survey of the positions; I know roughly how the map looks. I am asking for a prioritised research agenda: which open questions in this area most deserve four able philosophers' next three years, and in what order? For context, our remit is explicitly theoretical — we fund conceptual work, not empirical policy evaluation — though we care whether the theory could eventually be picked up by statisticians, welfare economists, or the people who design household surveys and social indicators.

To seed your thinking, here are the open questions as the committee currently sees them, though I am happy for you to add, split, or reject items. First, there is the question of whether the relational-egalitarian critique associated with Anderson and Scheffler has made the metric question obsolete — if the point of equality is the standing of citizens in social relations rather than the distribution of some underlying stuff, is a three-year fellowship on the metric simply answering a question nobody should be asking any more, or does relational equality itself quietly need a metric to fill in its content? Second, there is the measurement problem: can the capability approach be made operational — comparable across persons, aggregable, usable in an index — without collapsing into either a resources metric or a disguised welfare metric, and is its notorious indeterminacy a bug to be engineered away or the whole point? Third, there is the money-metric worry: whether resource-based views, once they let economists price heterogeneous bundles of goods, smuggle in more welfarism than their defenders admit. Fourth, the luck-egalitarian quarrels — brute luck versus option luck, expensive tastes, adaptive preferences — strike some of us as increasingly baroque; do they still do real work, or should a serious agenda retire them? Fifth, does the choice of metric have any traction in non-ideal policy at all (disability compensation, education finance, healthcare rationing), or do the rival metrics converge once you get down to cases? And sixth, a question one of our trustees keeps raising: whether the metric question and the population-ethics question are entangled in ways the literature has systematically ignored.

What I want from you is a defended ordering, not a list. For each question you would prioritise, tell me why it sits where it does in the ranking: how many other questions depend on its answer, whether it is tractable by philosophers rather than economists or statisticians, whether it is neglected relative to its importance, and what a plausible answer would actually unlock downstream. Tell me also which questions you would deliberately deprioritise and why, even if they are fashionable — including, if you believe it, that some of my six are pseudo-questions. And tell me what would change the ranking: what developments over the next few years, whether in the literature, in official statistics, or in public debate, should move a question up or down the committee's list before the next funding round. The deliverable we need is a funding rationale someone could defend to our board, so the reasoning has to be explicit and the trade-offs honest — especially where you are trading tractability against importance.
