Write a satirical internal strategy memo, roughly 700–800 words, from the fictional Ministry of National Mood of a mid-ranked country whose incoming government has made a top-three finish in the next world happiness report an explicit manifesto commitment. The voice should be deadpan, professional bureaucratic prose — numbered recommendations, an executive summary, a risk register, the whole apparatus of a government strategy document — and the humor should come entirely from the real measurement problems, not from invented absurdities. A well-being scientist reading it should wince in recognition rather than object.

The satire should work because the memo's logic is genuinely sound given its premises. In particular, the author — an earnest, competent official — should have noticed that the headline measure is a life-evaluation question (the Cantril ladder, in which respondents rate their own life against the best possible life they can imagine, on a zero-to-ten scale) rather than a measure of experienced feeling, and should exploit the difference with a straight face. Cheapest route to a higher score: not improving lives but managing the denominator — aspiration-lowering campaigns, a modest proposal to gently disillusion the citizenry about what is possible, since a citizen who has recalibrated "best possible life" downward rates the same life higher. Other recommendations should follow the same pattern from real features of the enterprise: exploiting the fact that respondents in different countries use the zero-to-ten scale differently, so a national "use the whole ladder" numeracy drive yields gains without any change in well-being; timing survey season; nudging mood at the moment of questioning, since a life evaluation asked on a bad-weather day is not the life evaluation asked on a good one; and a committee to look into why the Danes keep beating everyone and whether their confidence can be acquired by import.

At least one passage should be a dissenting annex from a welfare economist who is concerned but preoccupied with the wrong thing — say, she doubts the ladder target on the grounds that long-run happiness does not rise with income the way the growth lobby assumes, and therefore the memo should have picked a different indicator to game, an objection that concedes everything the memo needs. Another item, ideally buried in the risk register, should acknowledge that people adapt to improvements and revert to baseline, and propose managing this through scheduled disappointments.

The memo should never state, but should make unmistakable to an attentive reader, the underlying questions: whether a ranking built from a single evaluative question with an imagined-ideal anchor measures happiness or something more like managed expectations; whether a government optimizing its position on such a measure is doing good or Goodharting; and whether the whole premise — a country "competing" in a ranking its authors never intended as a tournament — has quietly replaced well-being with position. The final recommendation may gesture at this and then resolve it in favor of the ranking anyway.

Invented names and titles are fine; do not cite or quote real sources, and do not name real countries except in passing comparisons of the sort the report itself invites. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the measurement literature and perfectly readable to someone who knows only that such rankings exist.
