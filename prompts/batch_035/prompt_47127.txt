I teach an intermediate philosophy of language seminar, and our unit on radical interpretation is coming up. The students know the textbook picture: an interpreter with no prior grasp of the speakers' language correlates public assent and dissent with observable circumstances, and must fix meanings so that the speakers come out believing mostly truths and holding mostly rational preferences, with charity doing the heavy lifting. What I want for class is a case that strains the two halves of the package against each other, because the seminar so far has treated "interpret them as mostly right about the world" and "interpret them as rational in what they want" as if they always pull in the same direction, and I suspect that convergence is an artifact of the stock examples.

Construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications:

1. The setting is ordinary, empirical, and culturally comprehensible — a village, a workshop, a marketplace. No aliens, no inverted physics, no ideal interpreter with unlimited data. The scenario itself should be a single rich paragraph.
2. The community's linguistic data must confront the interpreter with a genuine forced choice: any assignment of meanings that makes the speakers' beliefs come out mostly true makes their preferences come out badly irrational — not merely unusual, unfamiliar, or unfashionable, since risk-seeking, asceticism, and elaborate ritual lives should not count as irrationality — and any assignment that makes their preferences come out rational requires attributing widespread, systematically false beliefs about those very same matters. Truth-charity and rationality-charity must trade off against each other sentence by sentence, and the tradeoff must be generated by structural facts about the community's situation and practice rather than stipulated. Simply decreeing that the speakers are irrational or that their environment deceives them is cheating.
3. The case must be engineered so that the standard escape routes fail: it must not be open to the interpreter to reinterpret the offending utterances as expressive rather than belief-ascribing (the rain-dance move), to carve the community into subgroups with different meanings, or to blame the interpreter's own defective evidence for the mismatch.
4. After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification: lay out the two competing assignments, show what each optimizes on, and show exactly where the tradeoff bites.

Then three analyses. First, identify which structural feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the case when that feature is removed — does it collapse back into the familiar situation where the two constraints reinforce rather than oppose each other? Second, give the strongest Davidsonian response: can the Davidsonian deny that truth-charity and rationality-charity are separable constraints that can be traded off against each other at all, such that my specification in (2) misconceives the method from the start? Or must they instead argue that a community presenting these data would simply be uninterpretable in principle? I want you to work out which of these is the better line. Third, consider whether a Lewisian interpreter — one who fixes belief and desire simultaneously by fitting the speakers to decision theory — does any better here, or merely faces the same fork with heavier machinery.

For calibration of what counts as cheating: the case is meant to drive a seminar discussion of whether charity is one constraint or several, so it must not smuggle in a verdict on that question. Both readings of the case — the one on which the Davidsonian has an easy dissolution and the one on which the package genuinely splits — must remain live. Keep the analyses as brief as the scenario is dense.
