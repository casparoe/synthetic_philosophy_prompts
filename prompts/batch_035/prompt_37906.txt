I'm putting together a position statement for a wild animal welfare reading group (we take turns presenting our "philosophical profile" and everyone else pokes holes in it), and before I embarrass myself I want to know if my views actually cohere. Here's roughly where I've landed: (1) I'm a hedonistic utilitarian — only the felt quality of experience counts, so species, ecosystems, and biodiversity have no moral status beyond the welfare of the individual animals that make them up. (2) I think most wild animal lives are probably not worth living — the typical wild animal is something like an insect or a small fish that starves, gets parasitized, or is eaten alive before accumulating much in the way of positive experience. (3) That means permanently destroying habitat is pro tanto good: paving over a wetland so nothing can ever live there again prevents enormous numbers of bad lives from coming into existence. (4) And yet I donate monthly to a rewilding charity and I genuinely cheered when wolves came back to my region. (5) I'm confident enough that insects are sentient that their numbers dominate my whole wild animal welfare calculus. (6) On the other hand I believe we should NOT act on habitat destruction or anything else yet, because our estimates of net welfare in wild populations are so noisy that any intervention could easily backfire — the correct move for now is to fund research and wait. (7) Finally, I think climate change is a moral catastrophe, full stop.

So: does this package hang together, and if not, which commitment has to go? I can see how someone might wave away the rewilding thing as an aesthetic preference, but given (1) I'm not sure I'm allowed to have that preference do any moral work, so please be honest about whether (4) survives contact with (1) and (3) or whether I'm just being sentimental. Similarly the climate change one: is it consistent with (3), or does my "destroying habitat prevents bad lives" logic commit me to something uncomfortable there? And I genuinely can't tell whether (6) is a stable form of humility or just me refusing to act on the very conclusions I claim to believe in (2) and (5) — if the estimates are too noisy to act on, aren't they too noisy to believe?

Please structure the answer as: a "Bottom line" of two or three sentences saying whether the set is consistent and which commitment is most at risk; then "Genuine tensions, worst first" with a paragraph on each real conflict; then "Apparent tensions that dissolve" covering the ones that only look bad on the surface; and finally "The cheapest repair" saying what single revision would fix the most and what it costs me. Rank by severity rather than presenting everything as equally damning — I'd rather know what's fatal versus what's just awkward.
