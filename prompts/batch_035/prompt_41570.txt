So I work on fair ML stuff, and somewhere along the way I absorbed what I guess is the received view in the field: that there's a deep incompatibility between the fairness criteria people actually care about, so any nontrivial scoring system has to violate at least one of them — the classic case being that you can't have calibration within groups and equal error rates across groups at the same time when base rates differ (the Chouldechova / Kleinberg-et-al style results). The math is proven, obviously, so this isn't a request to re-derive anything. What I want is for you to play it straight and treat the popular conclusion — the one that shows up in papers' intros and in policy discussions, something like "fairness criteria are mathematically incompatible, so it's all just value judgments about which unfairness to tolerate" — as the thing under examination, and tell me what would have to be true for that conclusion to be wrong or badly misleading.

Here's what I suspect matters but want you to work out properly: the theorems only bite given specific background conditions — imperfect risk prediction, base rates that genuinely differ, a fixed outcome variable, a threshold/binary-decision setting. So the interesting question is which of those conditions could fail, be relaxed, or be chosen differently in the design of the whole system: changing the decision problem (deferral, randomized decisions, multi-threshold policies), redefining the label, intervening on base rates themselves rather than treating them as given, dropping calibration in favor of something weaker. For each escape route, tell me whether it's actually live — where people have worked-out alternatives in the literature or in deployed practice, with actual content — versus merely logically possible, i.e. something you can gesture at but nobody has made work. And then the honest-assessment part: is the standard conclusion well insulated (in which case say so — maybe the impossibility results really are robust and the hand-wringing is fine), or has the popular version quietly become a different and stronger claim than the theorems support, one that's kind of unfalsifiable and does rhetorical work the math can't back up? I'd want the candidate defeaters ranked from "this would genuinely show the received view is wrong" down to "merely awkward for it," and I'd like you to be explicit about which of these the fair-ML community would itself concede versus what only outside critics push.

A bit of context on where I'm coming from: I'm a grad student, my advisor is fairly skeptical of the whole framing and keeps saying the impossibility results are "definitions dressed as theorems," which is exactly the kind of thing I can't tell whether is a deep point or a cheap shot. So if part of your answer is that it's a cheap shot, or a deep point only in some places, that's genuinely the thing I most want to know. Also fine if your conclusion is that the popular pessimism is overblown but the theorems are still doing real work — just show me where the line falls. No need for a bibliography, just the reasoning.
