I'm an academic advisor at a mid-size state university, and I see students back to back all day in 20-minute slots about decisions they treat as huge: which major, whether to transfer, whether to take a leave to go work or travel, whether to just drop out. A philosopher friend of mine kept going on about transformative experience stuff at a party — the idea that for some choices you literally can't know what it'll be like until you've done it, and can't trust your current preference because the choice will change you into someone with different preferences. I think half my job is exactly these cases, but I've been advising them like they're regular decisions with better or worse info.

So what I want is a decision procedure I can actually run inside a 20-minute meeting. First, questions to ask in order to sort whether a decision is genuinely this transformative kind or just an ordinary hard choice where more research helps — and it has to be questions I can ask a 19-year-old, not questions that presuppose the theory. Then, for each bucket, what advice is even possible: I suspect for the transformative ones the honest move is about reversibility and exit costs rather than trying to predict outcomes, but tell me if that's wrong or incomplete. Mark the points where the procedure has to stop and hand it back to the student because it's their values, not facts, and be honest about which steps are just my judgment dressed up as a checklist. Then run it on three real-ish cases: a student torn between accounting and studio art, one deciding whether to take a semester off to care for a sick parent, and one deciding whether to study abroad next year. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'm pretty sure I'd tell the studio art kid to major in accounting and minor in art, and if the procedure says thats the wrong frame entirely, I want to see exactly where it says so and why.
