My friend and I got into it big time last night over animal consciousness, specifically over which animals "actually feel things" vs. which are just running programs. We went case by case and I realized my gut verdicts don't obviously hang together, so I want to know if there's a principle underneath them or if I'm just vibes-ing it.

My verdicts:

Octopus: yes, obviously. They solve problems, escape tanks, seem to have moods and even individual personalities. No hesitation.

Fish: yes, they feel pain. My friend did the usual "that's just nociception, reflexive withdrawal, no actual felt pain" move, and I pointed to the studies where fish trade off food against avoiding a place where they got injected with mild acid — a reflex doesn't weigh options, was my line.

Crabs and lobsters: honestly wavering. The UK declared decapods sentient back in 2022, and there are those hermit crab experiments where crabs abandon a perfectly good shell after getting shocked in it and then trade up for a better one, which sounds a lot like my fish evidence. But their nervous system is so distributed — is that even the right architecture for one feeling thing? I flip on this one.

Bumblebees: back and forth constantly. They play with little balls, rolling them around for no reward, and there's a study where a sugary treat seems to put them in something like an optimistic mood. But come on, a bee brain is tiny. Then again, a roundworm (C. elegans, 302 neurons) I say NO to without hesitation, so somewhere between 302 neurons and a million I apparently draw a line for no stated reason.

Ants: no individual ant feels anything, I'm pretty sure. But here's where my friend called me insane — I said the colony as a whole seems to *want* things, like there's one agent there. He thought crediting the superorganism was ridiculous. I actually still half think it.

So: is there a principle that captures all this? My tentative one was something like "flexible trade-offs in pursuit of its own good" — if the animal weighs options and gives something up to avoid harm or get something better, that's enough. But that can't be right as stated, because even the roundworm does simple approach/avoid. So maybe it needs a sophistication clause? Or maybe the whole thing is that I trust behavior in invertebrates I find charismatic (octopus) and distrust it in ones I don't (crab), which would be embarrassing.

What I want from you: (1) state the best principle (or principles, if you think my verdicts split into groups), as precisely as you can, with the case that motivates each clause; (2) apply it to cases I didn't list — cuttlefish, jumping spiders, garden snails, say — and tell me what it says; (3) be upfront about which of my verdicts your principle simply cannot save, especially the octopus/crab asymmetry and the bee dithering; and (4) tell me what would change your mind on whichever of my verdicts you're most confident I've gotten wrong, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail — like whether the trade-off criterion is secretly just picking up vertebrate-style learning rather than anything about experience. Don't be diplomatic, I'd rather know if my list is mostly vertebrate bias wearing a costume.
