Wigner's 1960 essay "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences" gets read one of two ways: as a quasi-metaphysical claim — the success of mathematics in physics outruns anything a selection effect could explain, so the world must have some genuinely mathematical character — or as a cultivated expression of wonder that isn't really in the business of being true or false. A friend of mine takes the first reading to be plainly correct, and puts his credence in the following proposition at about 0.75: "the effectiveness of mathematics in fundamental physics is not fully explained by selection effects, convenience, and the choice of which problems physicists work on; some further fact about the world's structure is required to explain it." I put my credence at about 0.35, but I suspect our disagreement is not where I currently think it is, so I want an outside number with its derivation. Decompose it as you see fit, but I'd want you to cover at least these terms: (a) the probability that the proposition is even truth-apt rather than a posture of wonder that dissolves under pressure; (b) given truth-aptness, the probability that the selection-effect story leaves a genuine residue — and here I want you to grapple with an asymmetry that is awkward for both sides: the effectiveness is spectacular in fundamental physics yet markedly less so in biology, economics, and climate science, which is awkward for Wigner (why would the world's mathematical character be so domain-limited?) and also awkward for the selection-effect reply (if mathematicians simply cultivate whatever turns out to work, the ineffectiveness elsewhere should look more like noise than like a stable pattern); and (c) if there is a residue, the probability that it amounts to "the world is mathematical" rather than only the weaker deflation that physics, by construction, keeps the fragments of the world that fit the mathematics it already has.
