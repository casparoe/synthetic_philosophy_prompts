I write novels for a living, and at a recent seminar on Leibniz the discussion of the best of all possible worlds kept turning on an analogy I know intimately from the inside: God's choice among possible worlds is like an author choosing among possible drafts of a novel, where every scene is bound up with every other, and where the dark scenes — the deaths, the betrayals — earn their place by setting off the beauty of the whole. The lecturer presented this as Leibniz's own picture (he did like to compare God to an author), and used it to answer the objection from evil: just as a novel without conflict would be worse, not better, so a world without suffering would be less perfect, and Leibniz's God simply did the best an author can do. As a novelist, I find the mapping compelling in places and suspect in others: a novel is written for readers, not characters; the character who suffers is in no way compensated by the reader's delight; a draft can be revised piecemeal while a possible world presumably cannot; and an author has no criterion of the "best" draft that is independent of an audience.
