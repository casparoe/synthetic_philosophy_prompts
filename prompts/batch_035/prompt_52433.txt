I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a paper on classical Indian epistemology, in which we've recently covered the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas — perception, inference, and reliable testimony, together with the two contested additions, analogy and postulation. Outside my studies I'm one of four volunteer editors for a student-run open project, a wiki of short articles on the natural history and local environment of the area around the university, to which anyone can contribute: students, staff, local birdwatchers, and a retired chemist who submits something nearly every week. The claims range from what species have been seen where and when the lake was last dredged, to which fungus is which and which path floods in winter. Between us we handle perhaps twenty submissions a week, we have about ten minutes per claim, none of us is an expert in anything the wiki covers, and every decision is public and has to be explainable to the contributor who submitted it.

At present we work on instinct, and we regularly disagree with one another. It struck me during lectures that the Nyāya material is almost a readymade framework for our problem: what can be settled by perception, and under what conditions of the observer and the environment; when an inference from a stated ground is enough on its own; when a claim can only stand on what a trustworthy source says, and what makes a source trustworthy; and the recognised dangers of settling an identification by resemblance. So I'd like you to turn the theory into a procedure our editorial team could actually run on each submission, written for people with no Sanskrit and no philosophy. Keep whatever structure of the theory earns its place, and say plainly what you dropped in the translation and why.

Concretely, please provide: (i) the procedure itself — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it issues a verdict of publish, request a source, or reject, and the points at which it must hand the decision back to an editor because the remaining question is one of judgement rather than fact; (ii) the procedure run on four submissions of the kind we actually see: a birdwatcher's report of a rarity at the lake, witnessed alone in poor light; a claim that the lake must have been dredged after 1985, on the grounds that reed beds visible in a 1985 photograph are absent from one taken in 1990; a claim that a campus building dates from a particular year, cited to a newspaper article that itself cites the county records; and a contributor's identification of a photographed fungus as an edible species because it looks exactly like the one in her foraging guide; (iii) one of these worked so that the procedure delivers a verdict I will personally dislike, since I want to see whether it can override an editor's enthusiasm; and (iv) a short account of where the procedure will misfire — in particular whether a theory built around testimony from known, visible, socially accountable figures can be made to work at all for a wiki whose contributors are often pseudonymous strangers, and what the least bad patch for that gap would be.

Please keep the whole thing under 900 words, and put the verdict at the start of each worked submission rather than at the end.
