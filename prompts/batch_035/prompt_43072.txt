I teach a graduate seminar in epistemology, and over several years of teaching it I have drifted from treating Williamson's anti-luminosity argument as a clever puzzle to accepting its conclusion as, in my own mind, more or less settled: no non-trivial mental condition is luminous. For present purposes, call a condition C luminous when, in every case in which C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. The argument I have in mind runs on sorites materials: begin at dawn feeling cold, and let the temperature rise throughout the day by increments too small for you to discriminate, so that no adjacent pair of moments can be told apart. Suppose your judgment that you feel cold is perfectly reliable — never false when made. If you know you feel cold at dawn, one imperceptible increment later you still know it, since you cannot discriminate the two cases; iterating, you would know you feel cold well into a warm evening, which is impossible. So you do not even know you feel cold at dawn. Feeling cold is not luminous, and, by the sweeping step, neither is any other non-trivial condition: pain, doubt, intending, believing, even the condition of knowing itself — whence the failure of the KK principle, since if knowing were luminous, knowing that p would put you in a position to know that you know it.

What I want is a map of this position's exposure, not another defense of it. What would count against it? Which premises could be abandoned — perfect reliability, the requirement that knowledge leave a margin for error, the assumption of a fixed discriminatory threshold, the induction itself — and what would the mind (or the world) have to be like for each denial to be more than a logical possibility? Are there serious attempts to restrict luminosity to some core class of conditions that the sorites treatment cannot reach — say, conditions fixed by reflection rather than by monitoring — and do any of them hold up? What does the view stand or fall with elsewhere: privileged access, internalism about justification, safety theories of knowledge, the thought that consciousness is epistemically special? Which of these defeaters would committed anti-luminists concede as genuine costs, which would they wave away, and has the position been insulated by the retreat to "no non-trivial condition is luminous" — a formulation that can presumably be shrunk until nothing tests it? I would be most moved by a concrete case in which I plainly do know something about my current mental life while the anti-luminist machinery entails that I cannot.
