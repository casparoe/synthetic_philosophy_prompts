For my seminar on animal ethics I have to produce a piece of satire rather than yet another essay, and I've settled on a target: the way debates about speciesism quietly sort beings into moral categories as if moral status were an administrative matter. Write me a mock bureaucratic document — an intake application form issued by a fictional "Office of Moral Consideration," with numbered sections, eligibility checkboxes, and fine print — that a species must complete to be granted moral status. Around 600 words, but under 700 absolute maximum; leave white space as you see fit, but the form itself should not exceed that limit.

The comedy has to come from the philosophy being right, not from absurdist decoration. So: the form should process a human infant and a pig in adjacent booths whose paperwork is nearly identical except for the stamp they'll receive, making the argument from marginal cases do the satirical work without anyone naming it. Somewhere there should be a section where "species membership" appears as a required field, followed by an official note insisting that the field is never used in the determination — and a checkbox that uses it anyway. Include an "upgrade to personhood" pathway that requires evidence of self-consciousness or rational agency, so the dog fails on the mirror test while a corvid passes on tool use and the clerk is visibly uncomfortable with the result. Work in at least one contractualist exclusion (a section for beings who cannot enter into agreements or reciprocal arrangements, with a footnote about what they are owed "indirectly"), one sliding scale of the degree of consideration granted (platinum, gold, basic sentience), and a clause where the magnitude of an interest and the possession of an interest are quietly conflated in the fine print. If you can find room, a telos-related line — something about the office needing to know what a "characteristic life" of the applicant looks like — would be welcome, but don't force it.

Tone: deadpan throughout, the voice of a well-meaning bureaucracy that believes it has solved the controversy, never winking at the reader. The satire should be legible to someone who knows the Singer–Singer-critic literature well enough to recognize the argument from marginal cases, the charge that speciesism is an appeal to species membership per se, and the standard Kantian and contractualist moves, but also readable to someone who has only taken intro ethics. Do not have any character editorialize about how terrible the office is; the office should seem proud of its fairness, and the reader should draw the conclusion alone. No quotations from real philosophers and no citations — the office cites only itself.
