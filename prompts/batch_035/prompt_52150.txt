And here is the student's answer, copied word for word:

"Putnam's point is that two speakers can be exactly alike internally and still mean different things by the same word. On Earth 'water' refers to H2O, and on Twin Earth it refers to XYZ, even though the speakers on the two planets are identical in the head and nobody in 1750 could have told the liquids apart. So the extension of the word is fixed by what's actually in the local environment, not by anything about the speakers. As Putnam puts it, "meanings" just ain't in the head. In fact the word behaves something like an indexical: 'I' picks out different people for different speakers without any difference in their heads, and 'water' likewise picks out whatever liquid bears the right relation to the stuff in the speaker's environment.

The division of linguistic labor explains how this is compatible with ordinary ignorance. Most people who use 'water' couldn't say what its chemical composition is, and they don't need to: the community's experts know, and the layman's word gets its extension from them. If the chemists later work out the microstructure, the word had been tracking it all along. Putnam makes the same point with his other famous example, the patient who tells his doctor he has arthritis in his thigh: the community's usage of 'arthritis' fixes what the patient means, so his word misapplies even though from the inside it feels like he means just what the doctor means. Both cases show that whether a person's word applies to something can be settled by facts about the community and the world that the person has no access to.

As for 1750 specifically: since the two liquids were observationally identical back then, Earthians and Twin Earthians meant the same thing by 'water' — the word expressed the same concept on both planets, something like 'watery stuff'. The meanings only came apart later, once chemistry had identified H2O and XYZ, and from that point 'water' was effectively two different words. So 'water is H2O' wasn't true yet in 1750; it only became true once the chemists established the identity. The overall consequence is that meanings aren't in the head, so two speakers in the same psychological state can differ in what they mean, and psychology can't be purely individualistic if it individuates beliefs by their contents."
