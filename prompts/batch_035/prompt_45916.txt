I'm in a grad reading group that has now spent two semesters on the extended mind thesis, and the whole debate feels frozen in a way I'd like help diagnosing rather than adjudicating. One one side there's the Clark-and-Chalmers parity intuition: if some part of the world functions, in the moment, in a way that we'd count as cognitive were it done in the head, then it just is part of the cognitive process — Otto's notebook counting as part of his memory because it plays the same functional role that Inga's biological memory plays for her. On the other side you have the internalists, mainly Adams and Aizawa with their coupling-constitution fallacy charge — the fact that an external item is causally coupled to a cognitive process doesn't by itself make it part of that process — and Rupert, who'd rather park external resources under a separate heading about embedded cognition than grant them the extended label. The frustrating part is that after 25+ years both camps seem to accept the same cases (the notebook, counting on your fingers, doing algebra with pencil and paper, a smartphone full of offloaded memories), agree on basically all the facts, and still nobody has moved an inch. Each new round just swaps in newer gadgets and replays the same exchange.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to actually evaluate and not just nod along to: the dispute has quietly become verbal, a fight over who gets the word cognition, that persists because it's dressed up as a substantive metaphysical claim about where the mind ends and the tool begins. But I have doubts about my own diagnosis, because the two sides really do produce different science downstream of it — the extensionists write cognitive science with different units of analysis, while the internalists keep studying brains — so maybe they're answering two different questions under one heading, one about explanatory units in cognitive science and one about a folk-conceptual claim about the boundaries of persons, and the label fight is just the symptom of that conflation. Or maybe it's genuinely substantive and merely stuck, because nobody has a non-question-begging mark of the cognitive, so every argument bootstraps from a criterion its own side already accepted.

So, what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Substantive, verbal, two distinct questions running under one name? Ground whatever you say in what each side actually claims, not in a survey of the literature, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a principled mark of the cognitive, some experimental or formal result, an agreed stipulation to split the vocabulary, or nothing at all — and whether the impasse means the question is ill-posed rather than just hard. My advisor is convinced it's all terminological and I half agree, but the fact that people build actual research programs on the extended side makes me suspect it can't just be words. Also, if you think the question is ill-posed, say what a well-posed replacement would look like — I need something to bring back to the group besides "it's semantics, everyone go home."
