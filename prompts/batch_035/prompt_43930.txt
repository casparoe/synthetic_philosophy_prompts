Consider the 1996 Sokal affair: the physicist Alan Sokal published a deliberately nonsensical, jargon-laden article in the cultural-studies journal Social Text and then announced the parody himself, after which the matter became a sustained public controversy carried on almost entirely by newspapers, magazine essays, and later online discussion. Analyze this episode through Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public in The Present Age and The Moment — the public as an abstraction that everyone and no one belongs to, the press as its instrument, the levelling of all distinction, the diffusion of responsibility into anonymous chatter, mockery as the age's substitute for passion. I'm not asking for the science-wars question of who was right about truth and objectivity; I want the Kierkegaardian mapping done carefully and then run in both directions. Which features of the episode does the theory genuinely illuminate — say, the way the controversy became a matter of taking sides in an abstract public opinion rather than a discussion among accountable individuals, or the role of the anonymous crowd of commentators? And where is the theory silent or stretched? In particular: does it have any resources for evaluating the ethics of Sokal's own act of deception, given Kierkegaard's own practice of pseudonymous, indirect communication, or is it simply mute there? Finally, does the episode confirm the levelling thesis, or does it present a case in which the press-and-public dynamic, whatever its vices, exposed a genuine epistemic failure — something Kierkegaard's picture of the public as pure irresponsible chatter seems unable to accommodate? Please be honest about the points where the theory cannot be made to speak rather than forcing it.
