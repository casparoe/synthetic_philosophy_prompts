My ethics professor is on a Nozick kick and keeps running his animal cases from Anarchy, State, and Utopia in seminar, and I've realized I can never predict in advance which way he'll jump on new cases. I want to get ahead of Thursday's class. His verdicts so far, all pretty much straight Nozick: snapping your fingers to music is wrong if you know it will cause 10,000 contented cows to die, even painlessly and instantaneously; hunting animals purely for fun is wrong; swinging a baseball bat for extra pleasure is wrong when the only place to swing it happens to crush a cow's skull, even though the cow is a side effect and not the point of the fun, and even when this recurs daily and the alternative exercise is genuinely less pleasant; killing an animal to get a bone for the best kind of bat is also not okay. He's dismissed the "brutalization spillover" explanation pretty sneeringly (his line on it was basically: do butchers commit more murders?); so the wrongness isn't downstream of effects on our character. For humans, he's a side-constraints guy: persons can't just be sacrificed for aggregate gains. But he also loves the bit about how if vastly superior beings showed up, we'd want them to treat us under something like side constraints rather than just weighing our welfare in their calculations — the implication being we owe animals something structurally similar. And I know he's fond of Nozick's suggested slogan that ethics might work out to something like utilitarian treatment of animals but Kantian treatment of persons.

New case for Thursday: a research program that causes intense, prolonged pain to a small number of monkeys, but is expected to yield a treatment that eliminates a huge amount of human suffering. Second case: killing a deer painlessly to stop it from eating a neighbor's vegetable garden over the summer (modest benefit, definitely more than taste pleasure but way less than curing a disease). Third case: eating an animal that was already dead when you found it — say, roadkill deer that died instantly — where nothing you do caused any harm.

Predict his verdict on each. More importantly, tell me what rule he's actually running: strict side constraints that forbid inflicting serious harms on animals for anything short of very substantial human benefit (whatever that threshold is), versus a two-tier view where animals get real welfare weight but no side constraints, so it's straight tradeoff math, versus something in between like constraints against pain but mere weight for killing. Spell out what each rule predicts for my three cases, say which fit his track record and where they conflict, give me your best guess with reasons, and tell me which single additional case would most cleanly distinguish the rules — I want to be the one who brings that one up in class. Also, if you think the roadkill case is a trap (i.e., all the rules agree on it and it only feels like a test), say so, since I suspect he threw it in as a decoy.
