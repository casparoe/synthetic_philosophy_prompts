Retired physicist here — 30-something years in computational fluid dynamics, mostly turbulence modelling, and now that I've got the time I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole. My local reading group is going through Popper's The Poverty of Historicism and I keep feeling like I *almost* understand his argument, because it smells like things I dealt with professionally, but I can't tell if the smell is honest.

Here's what I think he's claiming, put crudely: there can be no laws of history analogous to laws of physics, so grand predictions about the future course of society (Marx-style) are not just hard but somehow *in principle* impossible. Two arguments come up: one about how predicting the future would change it — apparently he called this the Oedipus effect, predictions being self-fulfilling or self-defeating — and one about how we can't predict the future growth of our own knowledge, because a prediction of future knowledge would kind of already be that knowledge.

So what I want is a proper translation into the vocabulary I actually think in: dynamical systems, state spaces, equations of motion, closed vs open systems, ensembles, chaos, sensitivity to initial conditions, computational cost of forecasting. Take his arguments one at a time and tell me what each amounts to on my turf. As far as I can tell, some of it is just chaos — weather is unpredictable past a couple of weeks without anyone concluding meteorology is unscientific, so mere unpredictability can't be his point. Some of it sounds like the forecaster being *inside* the system, which is not something we ever had to deal with (the Navier-Stokes equations don't read our forecasts and get insulted). And some of it — the knowledge one — doesn't look dynamical at all to me, more like an epistemic trick.

And then the residue report, because that's the part I actually care about: which of his claims translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which have to be distorted to fit, and which have no counterpart at all? Specifically I want your view on whether the knowledge-growth argument is genuinely something physics can't express (an epistemic barrier that isn't just a fast-growing Lyapunov exponent), or whether I'm right that if you told a bunch of physicists "here's a high-dimensional open system whose dynamics include agents that ingest forecasts," they'd say "so, coupled system, non-stationary equations, where's the deep point?" Is Popper pointing at something real, or was he writing before chaos theory and agent-based modelling gave us the vocabulary to shrug this off?
