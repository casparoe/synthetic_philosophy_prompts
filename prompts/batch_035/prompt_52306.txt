My book group has just finished Silas Marner (I lost the vote, we are normally a thrillers kind of crowd), and I've somehow volunteered to lead next month's discussion on the religion angle. I'd love a proper philosophical reading of it.

Eliot obviously wasn't a Christian by the time she wrote it -- she'd spent her twenties translating German biblical criticism, Strauss's Life of Jesus and then Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity, the book arguing that God is basically humanity's own best qualities projected outward. So you'd expect her fiction to be a wrecking ball, and in a way it is: the whole plot is set off by the lot-drawing at Lantern Yard, where Silas's sect lets God decide whether he stole the money, God (or the church's machinery, same thing from Silas's point of view) gets it dead wrong, and the actual truth only surfaces sixteen years later out of a stone quarry. Everything genuinely good in the book -- Dolly Winthrop's neighbourliness, Eppie, the whole Raveloe recovery -- comes through human attachment rather than doctrine, and Eppie replacing the stolen gold is Feuerbach's point in miniature: the love religion points at, arriving without the religion.

But then the novel keeps resisting that neat reading. Dolly's simple faith, church bells and all, is treated with total tenderness, not as a confusion to be diagnosed. And the ending flirts with exactly the providence the book has spent two hundred pages undermining -- Eppie arrives on the night Silas loses everything, like a consolation sent from somewhere, and Silas is allowed to feel there was meaning in it. Plus the Lantern Yard revisit: a factory has swallowed the place where the sect was, and nobody even remembers any of it. Is that the death of the old religion, or just the old religion being replaced by industrial modernity?

So: read the novel through Eliot's actual position on religion -- agnostic about doctrine but keeping Christianity's moral emotions, the Feuerbach line that our religious feelings are real feelings about real humans, just misaddressed. Where does the novel dramatise that better than her essays and translations ever could, where does it complicate or push back on her, and which scenes should I actually make the group look at? Please be fair to both sides -- I don't want to turn up with a simple religion bad, love good take, since two of the members are churchgoers and one of them will definately notice. No need to quote the text, I don't have my copy to hand. And please write in British English -- were a Yorkshire lot and I'll never hear the end of it otherwise.
