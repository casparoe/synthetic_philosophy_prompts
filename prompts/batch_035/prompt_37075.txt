I'm a graduate student straddling bioethics and decision theory, and in every seminar on paternalism — and increasingly in conversations about preference elicitation for recommender systems and AI assistants — we hit the same wall: some agent's stated preference deserves respect, and some other, superficially similar preference is a "bias" that may permissibly be resisted, corrected, or overridden. Everyone gestures at the distinction; nobody can say what it consists in. Before I read another round of papers on adaptive preferences or debiasing, I want the specification first. Please act as a philosopher working at the intersection of metaethics, rational choice theory, and moral psychology, and write the desiderata: what any adequate account of the preference/bias distinction must get right. I'd like you to think carefully and start by brainstorming the hard cases before committing to the list, because the requirements should be motivated by cases, not by loyalty to any particular theory. The purpose to fix on: the account has to be usable by a third party — a clinician, a policymaker, or a system that must decide whether to defer to what someone says they want — so mere classification in the abstract isn't enough; the distinction has to track something about when overriding is and isn't permissible, or it does no work.

Some of the cases I'd want the list to be built around, though feel free to add others: the smoker who enjoys smoking and wants to quit (an unwanted desire that seems fully "hers"); the sour-grapes case, where someone retroactively ceases to want what was foreclosed to them; the obsessive collector whose monomania is perfectly coherent diachronically, satisfies every second-order endorsement test, and yet strikes many people as defective; and the person whose religiously or culturally grounded preference is dismissed as "bias" by outsiders who happen to dislike it — a case where the wrong classification licenses something close to domination. Beyond cases, I want to know which distinctions the theory must respect: preference versus belief, preference versus compulsion, first-order versus second-order attitudes, and content versus structure. And I have a suspicion I'd like you to assess: that any purely structural criterion (coherence, stability, well-being consequences, second-order endorsement, informed-desire under full information) is either too weak, letting the sour-grapes preference through, or too strong, condemning ordinary but messy human tastes; while any content-based criterion smuggles in contested substantive values and fails the detectability requirement that a third party classify without first settling every normative dispute. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly — that result would be the most valuable thing you could give me.

Please mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a camp preference, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate accounts. Then, briefly, run three familiar tests against the list — the informed-desire test, the coherent-preference test, and an autonomy-based test — but keep that part short; I'm interested in the criteria, not the scorecard, since I'll be doing the evaluating myself in my thesis.
