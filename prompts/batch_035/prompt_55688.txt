For an extended essay at my school I have been looking into the Ellsberg paradox, and I have hit a wall that I would like your help understanding. It is not the paradox itself that confuses me but the fact that the argument about it does not seem to go anywhere, and I want to know what kind of disagreement it actually is.

Here is what I mean. In Ellsberg's urn problems, people prefer to bet on a draw from an urn with a known 50/50 split of colours over an urn with an unknown split, even when the payoffs are symmetrical, and they make choices that, taken together, no single assignment of probabilities can explain. Since Ellsberg's 1961 paper, one side — following Savage's tradition — has treated this as a demonstration that people are irrational or at least that their intuitions are unreliable, since the choices violate the axioms that expected utility theory rests on. The other side has treated the behaviour as reasonable: if someone tells you an urn's contents were chosen by an adversary, or you simply know nothing about how it was filled, then refusing to put a sharp probability on the draw is not a failure of reasoning but an honest response to missing information, and theorists have built serious frameworks around betting in a way that does not require pretending to know more than you do. What strikes me is that both sides have had the same hypothetical choices in front of them for over sixty years, agree on what people actually choose, and yet neither has moved.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about the urns at all. One side is answering a question about what makes a set of choices consistent with each other, while the other is answering a question about what it is rational to do when your information is genuinely incomplete — two different questions travelling under the single name of rationality. But I am a sixth-form student and I suspect this is too neat, and that there might be a real substantive dispute underneath it, perhaps about whether probabilities are things you must always have, or about whether the normative axioms were ever meant to apply to people's raw preferences in the first place.

So my question for you: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, is it a hidden disagreement about what a theory of rational choice is for, is it a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for without begging the question, or is it a genuine unresolved substantive dispute that is simply hard? Support your diagnosis with what each side actually says, not just a survey of the positions. And then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or whether the impasse itself is a sign that the question, as both sides pose it, is ill-posed. I would like an answer I can actually use in my essay, so please reason it through rather than summarising the literature back to me.
