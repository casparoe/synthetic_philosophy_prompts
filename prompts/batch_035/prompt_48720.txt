I sit on the governance review group of Hillcrest Housing Co-operative, a student-run co-op with about 700 resident members. We're revising our bylaws, and the group has become stuck on a boundary question that I suspect is an old philosophical problem wearing institutional clothes: who counts as part of our co-op for voting purposes?

At present only resident members vote. Three proposals would widen that, each backed by a different faction. The first would give votes to subletters — around 90 non-members living in our buildings under short-term agreements while members are on placement abroad; they pay the same rent and are subject to the same house rules and disciplinary procedures, but have no say in making them. The second would give votes to the eleven live-in wardens, who are employees rather than members; the argument is that they're affected by nearly every governance decision but currently have to be 'consulted' in a way nobody is obliged to take seriously. The third, pushed hardest by a group of final-year members, would create proxy or successor votes for people on our waiting list — roughly 200 students who will live here next year and will bear the consequences of rent and capital decisions taken now, but who have no present say.

There's also a fourth, quieter faction who think we've drawn the boundary too widely already and should be talking about narrowing it: they point out that our bulk-purchasing and building decisions affect traders and neighbours in the surrounding streets who will never be members of anything, and that if we start expanding the franchise on the ground of being affected, we have no obvious place to stop — you can't put a landlord in Ascot on the ballot because our refurbishment budget moved the price of his scaffolding.

I have to circulate a position paper before our meeting in a fortnight, and I want it to be a proper decision memo, not a survey of views: a concrete recommendation for a revised franchise, with the reasoning behind it. I'd like the reasoning to be principled — some articulated test for who belongs in the decision-making body, not a case-by-case horse trade — and to say honestly where the test draws lines that some faction will hate. It should also take the strongest argument for the losing side seriously, whichever side that turns out to be; my inclination is toward the subletters, but I want to be shown the best case against my own position rather than reassured. Finally, I want the memo to address the slippery-slope worry about the affected-but-distant (the scaffolding landlords of the world) rather than wave it away, since our group will seize on any hand-waving there.

Please keep the memo under 600 words. Assume it will be read by intelligent students with no philosophy background, so jargon should be either avoided or explained in a sentence when it earns its place.
