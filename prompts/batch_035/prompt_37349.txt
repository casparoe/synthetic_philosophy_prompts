I'm a novelist — literary fiction, mostly, though my last two books are what my editor cheerfully calls "unremittingly tragic." I've been asked to write a substantial afterword for an anniversary edition of the novel of mine people most often describe as devastating, and rather than the usual craft memoir I want to say something honest about why readers willingly submit themselves to a book that has made a number of them cry on public transportation. The trouble is that when I write down what I actually believe about this, I can no longer tell whether I'm describing one coherent view or several views wearing each other's coats. So I'd like you to audit my commitments the way a professional philosopher of art would: sharply, and ordered by severity, not as a catalogue of disconnected remarks. Please think carefully and deliberately before answering — start by laying out what each commitment is doing, then tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other and which only seem to, and for each real tension say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me. Don't flatter me by flagging everything as a problem, and don't assume you know which commitment I'm most attached to, because I don't.

Here is the set. First: the pity and fear a reader feels for a fictional character are genuine emotions — the same kind of state, whatever that comes to, as pity for a real person in distress — and I have no patience for the suggestion that they are make-believe, attenuated, or merely "as if" versions of the real thing. Second: nobody who loves tragedy is a masochist; whatever the pleasure of tragedy is, it is not pleasure taken in suffering considered in itself. Third: the distinctive satisfaction of tragedy is cathartic — the reader wants the pity and fear wound up and then discharged, and the pleasure of tragedy is substantially the pleasure of that release, the way scratching an itch is pleasurable. Fourth: the deeper source of the pleasure is contemplative — the mind's satisfaction in grasping the necessity, or at least the intelligibility, of the catastrophe; tragedy is a cousin of geometry, and the reader who understands why it had to happen this way has received the thing tragedy has to give. Fifth: emotional force in tragedy is maximized by restraint and distance — the coldest narration produces the strongest effect, and I have built my own practice on that conviction, while overt manipulation of the reader's feelings yields weaker work, not stronger. Sixth, and finally: I find that rereading a tragedy — when the outcome is known, the suspense is gone, and the reader can no longer be surprised or distressed in the ordinary way — is at least as moving, often more so, than the first reading, and I would want any account of tragic pleasure to explain that rather than explain it away.

So: are these jointly tenable? If not, which one has to go, or which pair needs a genuine reconciliation rather than a shrug? And where two of them only look to be in conflict — where one is quietly rescuing the other — please say so, because I honestly cannot tell which of my beliefs are doing the load-bearing work and which are just sentiment I've absorbed from other novelists at festivals.
