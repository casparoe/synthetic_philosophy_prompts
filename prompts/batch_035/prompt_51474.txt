I am preparing an essay on whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I would like your comments on it before I expand my notes into a full draft. The position it defends is, as far as I know, not the standard one: it grants the deprivationist most of what she wants about death, but insists that she has not yet earned the asymmetry between the nonexistence that follows a life and the nonexistence that precedes it. Below are condensed notes. Please judge them on their internal merits; no bibliography, citations, or references to the literature are wanted, and none are needed — engage the philosophical positions themselves, from your own understanding of them.

NOTES — "The Double Feature: A Partial Defense of Lucretius"

§1. The argument and the reply. The Epicurean argument, as usually reconstructed, is this: for something to be bad for a person, there must be a subject whom it is bad for and, arguably, a time at which it is bad for her; but during a person's life her death has not yet arrived, and once it arrives there is no longer anyone for it to be bad for; so death — as opposed to dying — is not bad for the one who dies. The orthodox reply, in the tradition of Nagel and later Feldman, is deprivationism: death is bad for you when it deprives you of the goods the rest of your life would otherwise have contained. The harm need not be datable within your biography; the badness of the betrayal you never discover is not located at any moment you experience, and neither is this.

I set aside the separate question of when death is bad for you; nothing below turns on locating the harm at a time, and I am content to let the deprivationist place it where she likes — within the life, at its end, or nowhere in particular.

§2. The Lucretian challenge, made precise. Lucretius observed that the time before your birth and the time after your death are mirror images: long stretches of the world without you in it, on either side of your life. Nobody regards the eons before their birth as a misfortune; by parity, Lucretius urged, we should not regard the time after death as one either. Deprivationists tend to wave this off, but the challenge has bite once the deprivation analysis is stated as a comparison. Write S's actual life as running from b to d. The analysis says: S's death at d is bad for S because the prudential value of S's life truncated at d is lower than the value of the relevant counterfactual continuation. The same analysis can be run at the other end: the value of S's life beginning at b is lower than the value of the relevant counterfactual life beginning earlier. If the analysis is good at one end, it is good at both.

§3. The Double Feature. To hold fixed everything except the direction of the addition, consider this case. You are offered, at no cost, a choice between two extensions of your life, equal in length (one year) and equal in experiential quality. Under option A the year is appended at the back: you live on to d+1. Under option B the year is prepended at the front: a person exists from b−1 whose life consists of an extra initial year and then, from b onward, exactly your actual life, experiences and all; by stipulation, nothing from b onward is altered. Then:

(1) If the value of a life is the sum of the values of its segments — the assumption the deprivation comparison presses us toward, since it compares lives by their contents — then A and B confer exactly the same benefit on you. A deprivationist who resists this additivity owes an account of why a year at the front of a life counts differently from a year at the back; that debt comes due in (3).

(2) What addition confers, subtraction removes. By parity of reasoning with the standard argument that dying at d is worse for you than dying at d+1 would have been, being born at b is worse for you than being born at b−1 would have been: each costs you the same extra year of goods.

(3) The usual defenses of the asymmetry fail, or cost more than they appear to. The identity defense says the prepended year would not have been yours: the person existing from b−1, however psychologically connected to you, is at best a counterpart. The obvious ground is the essentiality of origins — you could not have begun to exist earlier than you did — which is a heavyweight metaphysical thesis; and note what it concedes: that no degree of psychological connectedness, even stipulated maximal as it is here, suffices for the ownership of goods when origin varies. The modal defense says the earlier-birth counterfactual is more remote than the later-death one; but the Double Feature holds distance fixed at one year in each direction, so remoteness cannot be doing the work. What remains is the bias toward the future: we care about goods to come and are largely indifferent to goods gone by. That bias does distinguish the cases — but at a price.

(4) The price is this: if the only remaining ground for treating death as worse for you than late birth is a temporal bias, then the badness of your death is not an intrinsic feature of your life or its ending. It is partly a fact about the direction in time we happen to face. The Epicurean can press the point home: a harm whose badness depends on our point of view is a projection of concern, not a misfortune of the person. (Parfit's discussion of our bias toward the future is the natural reference point; I claim no novelty for the observation, only for its deployment here.)

(5) I nevertheless accept the parity rather than deny it. Being born later than one might have been is bad for one, in the very sense in which dying earlier is. Our failure to regret the eons before our birth is explained by the forward bias, not by the absence of a harm — just as tranquility, before the discovery, about a betrayal already committed does not show that one has not been harmed. The verdict is uncomfortable: it implies that everyone who could have existed earlier, with additional goods, has been correspondingly harmed. But it is not incoherent, and discomfort is not a refutation.

(6) Conclusion. Lucretius's argument, properly run, does not vindicate Epicurus. It vindicates a symmetry: either the nonexistence before your birth and the nonexistence after your death are both bad for you, or neither is. Deprivationists should accept the first horn openly, rather than leaning silently on the bias toward the future; and if they cannot stomach the lost eons, they owe an account of what, other than that bias, breaks the mirror.

WHAT I WOULD LIKE FROM YOU

Please structure your comments under the following headings:

1. Accuracy and fairness. Are the reconstructions of the Epicurean argument and of the deprivationist reply fair, or do any of them attack a straw man? Is anything said about Lucretius, Nagel, Feldman, or Parfit inaccurate as a characterization of their views? (I mean characterizations of the positions, not textual points.)

2. Step-by-step assessment. Evaluate claims (1) through (6) individually. Identify the weakest inference in the chain and either repair it or explain why it cannot be repaired.

3. The ownership criterion. The notes treat a counterfactual life as yours when it keeps your actual life fixed and only adds to it — at the back in option A, at the front in option B. Is that criterion legitimate, or does it beg the question against the essentiality of origins, which says that no life beginning at b−1 is your life at all, however much of it overlaps with yours? In particular: does step (3) succeed against the identity defense, or does ownership of goods depend on something that psychological connectedness, however maximal, cannot capture?

4. Verbal or substantive? Suppose a reader accepts (1) through (3) but resists (5), insisting that late birth, unlike early death, simply is not bad for anyone. Is the remaining dispute a substantive disagreement about whether death and late birth harm us, or a verbal one about the expression "bad for you"? Please give a reasoned answer rather than a compromise.

5. The strongest opposing essay. What would a determined Epicurean press hardest against these notes, and should the notes preempt that line of attack, or would preemption weaken the essay by making it defensive?

6. Overall verdict. Is the thesis interesting enough to be worth developing, and what is the single most valuable revision you would make to the notes as they stand?
