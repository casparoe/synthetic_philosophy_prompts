So, background: I'm a retired solid-state physicist (35 years of neutron scattering, mostly) who has gotten seriously into philosophy of science since retiring, and the continuing-ed program at my local university has somehow let me run a 90-minute session for their philosophy of science discussion group. About 14 adults, mixed backgrounds, comfortable with logic but no math beyond high school algebra. We've been circling Occam's razor and, thanks to my pushing, induction, and I want them to actually feel why a short theory isn't just prettier but pays off in prediction -- and also where the idea gets slippery. I have a half-formed idea and need you to turn it into something I can actually run.

My sketch: I generate symbol sequences (coin flips, letters, whatever) from hidden rules -- some deterministic with noise, some biased, maybe one near-random -- and the group has to write down "theories" as short instruction cards for predicting the next symbol. They win points for correct predictions but pay points for every word or symbol their theory card uses, so they run headfirst into the fit-versus-brevity tradeoff themselves instead of me lecturing about it. Then I want a second phase where two subgroups get different instruction languages -- one where "repeat the block you've seen" is a single reserved word, one where they have to spell everything out -- so they discover that simplicity depends on your language, but only up to a fixed cost, which I gather is roughly the invariance theorem (correct me if I'm mangling it). Design the whole thing: materials, exact rules, the payoff scheme (no real money), a timeline that fits 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto priors and description length without turning into me talking for 40 minutes.

Two more things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- the group includes a retired accountant who will try to game any scoring rule you hand me, so stress-test it against him specifically. Second, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: I know the real Solomonoff story involves summing over infinitely many hypotheses and is uncomputable, and I suspect that part can't be gamified at all -- tell me clearly how much I just have to assert at the very end, and how to do it without deflating the room. I'd rather they leave feeling the razor in their bones with the caveats stated plainly, than protected from the caveats. Also if there's a moment where someone in the room will independently reinvent something like "you can't tell a simple rule from a lucky complicated one without more data," build the activity so that moment is likely, because that's worth more than anything I could say upfront.
