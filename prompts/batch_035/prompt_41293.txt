I'm building thought experiments for a seminar on causal explanation in history, and I want one aimed at a specific blind spot in redundancy-based reasoning. The familiar pattern: when an event is overdetermined — when it would have occurred through some other route even had the candidate cause been absent — the but-for counterfactual test returns "not a cause," and this result is routinely deployed to deflate historical claims of the form "X caused the war" on the grounds that "it would have happened anyway." I want a case built to order in which that deflation goes through for the event itself, and yet the agent in question is still, and rightly, the central causal explanandum of the episode — because what the agent caused was not the event but the overdetermination of the event. Before the agent's act, the outcome is contingent and avoidable; after it, the outcome is overdetermined; the act's causal work is performed entirely on the redundancy structure.

Constraints:

1. Realistic. A plausible sequence of diplomatic, institutional, or organizational decisions — cabinet politics, procurement schedules, treaty deadlines — in a fully fictional but believable setting. No real historical figures or events, no science fiction, and no stipulated foreknowledge: nobody in the story may know more than a realistic decision-maker of the period could know.
2. The overdetermination must be generated by the agent's act within the scenario, not stipulated by the setup. If the case works only because the narrator asserts that a second route to the outcome existed all along, it fails.
3. The scenario itself should be one paragraph.

After the scenario, demonstrate that the case meets the specification:

- Run the standard tests on the agent's act — the but-for counterfactual, the nearest-possible-world check, and the structural historian's question of whether the outcome would have arrived anyway — and show that each returns the deflationary verdict for the event. This matters because the case must not be accused of smuggling its conclusion in through a favorable test.
- Then show why the agent is nonetheless the central cause of the episode, identifying exactly which feature of the case is doing the work.
- Say what happens to the case if that feature is removed.
- Finally, give the strongest attack a defender of the pure counterfactual test would mount against the case — presumably by denying that causing an overdetermination is a causal relation at all — and assess whether the case survives it.

The point of the case: to test whether redundancy deflations of historical causation, which are usually treated as settling the matter, miss an entire class of causes — those whose product is necessity itself.
