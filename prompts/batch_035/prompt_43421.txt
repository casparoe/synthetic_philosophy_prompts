Next term I'm leading a political philosophy reading group on migration justice, and before our first session on whether states may exclude would-be immigrants, I want to circulate a one-page statement of my own position so the others can interrogate it. I've written up my commitments, but a colleague looked it over and said the package "smells inconsistent" without saying where — which was unhelpful and also a little nerve-wracking. Rather than walk into the seminar exposed, I'd like an audit first. Here is the set: (a) there is no general human right to immigrate; would-be immigrants have no claim of justice to enter a country whose members have not admitted them; (b) there is, however, an unconditional human right to exit — a right to leave any country, including one's own — and no state may attach conditions that make leaving effectively impossible; (c) the right to exclude rests on collective self-determination: a legitimate people governing itself is entitled to set the terms of its own membership, roughly the way Wellman defends it in his exchange with Cole; (d) I firmly reject any proprietary reading of that ground — citizens do not collectively own the territory or its infrastructure, and I'm quite sympathetic to the thought that natural resources belong to humanity at large; (e) birthplace is morally arbitrary brute luck, and I find the old comparison between citizenship-by-birth and feudal hereditary privilege genuinely powerful — it's a serious injustice that life prospects track the accident of where one is born; (f) on cases: a democratic state may legitimately prefer immigrants expected to be net fiscal contributors, but it may not exclude on cultural grounds — "we want to remain a Christian country" is not an acceptable basis for restriction; and (g) on brain drain: wealthy states' active recruitment of doctors and nurses from poor states is unjust, and those states are entitled, perhaps obliged, to restrict such recruitment even though the individuals themselves have done nothing wrong.

What I'd like is a severity-ranked audit, not a lecture on the open-borders literature. For each genuine tension, tell me which commitments are implicated, what the trouble is, and what would have to give — noting what each candidate repair costs elsewhere in the set. Please also flag anything that only looks like a tension, since I'd rather not preemptively abandon a view that's actually fine; I've been told, for instance, that (b) is vacuous without a matching right to enter somewhere, and I half-suspect that objection is shallower than it sounds, but I'd like your considered view rather than a reflexive defense. And at the end, name the single cheapest repair that would clean up the most, assuming I want to keep the overall shape of the position: closer to Wellman than to the open-borders side, but with the luck-egalitarian conscience intact.
