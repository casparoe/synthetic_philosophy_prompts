Take the Dreyfus controversy over early artificial intelligence and analyze it through the lens of the frame problem, running the analysis in both directions.

The episode, in brief: in a 1965 RAND memorandum, "Alchemy and Artificial Intelligence," and then in What Computers Can't Do (1972), Hubert Dreyfus — drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty — argued that intelligence rests on embodied, informal skills that resist formalization, and that the field's optimism (Herbert Simon's contemporaneous predictions of world-champion chess computers and imminent machine discovery of new mathematical theorems) was unwarranted. The AI community responded with ridicule. In 1969, in "Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence," McCarthy and Hayes had defined the frame problem as a technical challenge in logic: how to state the effects of actions without writing out endless axioms for all the obvious non-effects. In the 1980s philosophers such as Dennett broadened it into a problem about relevance — how a mind or machine manages to consider only what matters. The narrow, technical version was largely settled by the end of that decade, and today's large language models exhibit seemingly relevant, common-sensical behavior with no explicit frame machinery anywhere.

Be disciplined about the mapping, and say where the lens is silent rather than forcing it to speak. In particular: Was Dreyfus, avant la lettre, pointing at the frame problem, or at something it does not capture — skill acquisition, embodiment, background know-how? Was the technical solution of the 1980s a solution to what Dreyfus was pointing at, or a solving-away by narrowing? And what does the LLM era do to the analysis — do these models dissolve the frame problem, circumvent it, or relocate it (to the context window, to their occasional bizarre failures of relevance, to the humans curating their training data)? Distinguish carefully between the narrow frame problem and the wide one throughout, and be explicit when a claim you want to make belongs to neither.
