I'm a second-year PhD student in economic theory, TA-ing an undergraduate elective on social choice, and I want a two-stage thought experiment for the final problem set in which students get to watch the Gibbard–Satterthwaite escape hatch close in real time. The theorem as we teach it: any deterministic social choice function over at least three alternatives, defined on the unrestricted domain of preference rankings, that is non-dictatorial and onto is manipulable — some voter can gain by misreporting. The familiar escape is restricting the domain: with single-peaked preferences over a one-dimensional policy space, the median of reported ideal points is strategy-proof. So stage one: a realistic committee — five members of a university department choosing one of ten budget levels for the coming year by the median rule — in which everyone's preferences are single-peaked. You must show, voter by voter, why truthful reporting is dominant: exhibit the logic that misreporting one's peak can only leave the median where it is or move it away from the misreporter's true ideal point. Stage two: one member is replaced by a new hire whose preferences are psychologically plausible but not single-peaked — I have in mind an institutional cliff, e.g., her lab survives only above a threshold funding level, and she separately believes a maximal budget would force a dean-level intervention that resets everything, so her ranking has a spike at the top and a dip in the middle. Then exhibit, with the full arithmetic — true rankings, reported peaks, the median before and after — a profitable manipulation she can carry out.

After the scenario, do the check, and here is where I want care: in stage one, say which premise of the theorem fails to hold and why the theorem therefore leaves room for strategy-proofness; in stage two, note the subtler point that the domain is *still* restricted, so the theorem does not strictly guarantee manipulability — and yet the manipulation is sitting there. Explain what the theorem is and isn't doing in each stage, and identify which feature of the case does the work (I suspect it is the shape of the profile rather than anything about the rule, but I want your view). Constraints: no abstract three-letter ballots — the stakes and the language should be realistic throughout; the manipulation must be verifiable by hand in under a minute, with no combinatorial search; and the non-single-peaked preference must arise from the institution (thresholds, outside options, dissolution rules), not from arbitrary perversity. Nothing may be stipulated: both the dominance argument and the manipulation must fall out of the arithmetic. Finally, give me the defender's response — say, that non-single-peaked voters are rare and identifiable before the vote, or that the rule should be patched — and an honest assessment of whether any patch could restore strategy-proofness on the enlarged domain, or whether the real lesson is that strategy-proofness holding on a knife-edge domain was never what made the median rule attractive in the first place.
