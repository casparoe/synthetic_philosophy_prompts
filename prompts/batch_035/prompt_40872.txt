For the past few months I've been stuck on a question I picked up from a popular book about consciousness: the idea that mind might be baked into matter itself, so that even an electron has some sliver of experience. I never studied philosophy, so I've been turning it over on my own, and what haunts me is not whether it's true but what would follow if it were. When I raised it at dinner, two of my friends got into an argument they've now asked me to referee, and I'm honestly out of my depth.

It went like this. (1) Ruth said that if matter is sentient down to the particle, then ordinary life is a moral catastrophe: every kettle I boil, every cube of ice I melt, every spoonful of salt I stir into soup involves trillions upon trillions of tiny experiences, and at least some of them must be unpleasant. Even if each one is almost nothing, the numbers are so astronomical that they should outweigh everything we normally count, the way a million pennies is still more than a hundred dollars. So at the margin I ought to do less of it: fewer hot meals, a colder house, slower walks. (2) Dan said bare experience isn't the same as suffering — an electron could have some minimal glimmer without anything being at stake for it. Suffering needs valence, the badness, and nothing in the bare idea that matter is conscious says the glimmers are good or bad for their owners; a subject with no valence has no welfare to harm. (3) Ruth answered that physics is full of fundamental quantities — mass, charge — so if experience is fundamental, valence could be too, and she's at least entitled to think turbulent hot water contains some bad experiences somewhere. And even granting each one almost no weight, almost nothing times an unthinkable number is still something, so Dan's dismissal doesn't get off the ground. (4) Dan said this proves too much: on her view breathing, walking, and digesting all create or destroy micro-experiences, so every action whatsoever is a moral disaster, and a view that condemns everything condemns nothing. He added that maybe tiny scattered glimmers never pool at all — perhaps goodness and badness only exist in unified subjects, the ones with nervous systems, so no amount of particle-level shimmer adds up. (5) Ruth replied that proving too much shows her view is hard to act on, not that it's false — we already live with terrible things happening constantly — and that she'd settle for a weak conclusion: some slight pressure toward gentler ways of doing things, maybe slow cooking instead of a roiling boil.

Please adjudicate move by move: tell me who has the better of each of the five moves and why, what both of them are missing that would change the picture, and who comes out more right overall. I'd also like you to say how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and to single out the one move you'd least want to bet on.
