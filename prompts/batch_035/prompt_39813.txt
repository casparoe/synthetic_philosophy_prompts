Our state board of education is revising the high school world history standards, and I'm on the subcomittee that has to vote next month. One draft standard says students should be able to explain why the policy of appeasement made the Second World War inevitable. Three pages later, in the historical-thinking skills section, the same draft instructs teachers to wean students off hindsight bias and off the assumption that historical outcomes were somehow foreordained. Nobody else on the subcommittee seems to have noticed that these two requirements sit awkwardly together. The options on the table are: keep the appeasement standard as written, soften it to something like made war more likely, or cut it entirely. Write the memo I'll circulate before the vote. Recommend one option, and go beyond just flagging the tension — I want to know whether teaching an inevitability narrative is a defensible pedegogical simplification (you have to say something examinable) or whether it quietly commits us to a substantive causal claim, namely that the causes of the war were jointly sufficient and every counterfactual escape route was closed off, which is a much stronger claim than most historians would actually sign. And if your recommendation is to cut the inevitability language, tell me what replaces it, since a standard like discuss whether the war was inevitable risks turning into a debate-club exercise with no right answers at all.
