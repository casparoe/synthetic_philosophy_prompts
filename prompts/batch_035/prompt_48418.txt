Construct a decision scenario, for use in a teaching unit on the difference between certain and uncertain evidence, in which an agent receives two pieces of uncertain evidence in succession and the order of arrival determines what the agent ought to do. The specifications are as follows.

(1) The setting is ordinary: a workplace, a manager, ordinary stakes — no idealized agents, no exotic devices. (2) Each piece of evidence is soft: the agent learns no proposition outright; each observation arrives with a confidence strictly between 0 and 1 (a second-hand report of a renewal, a half-heard remark, a glance at incomplete figures). (3) The two observations must concern distinct propositions. (4) Updating proceeds by Jeffrey conditioning: an observation of E with confidence q replaces the agent's credence in any hypothesis H by q·Pr(H|E) + (1−q)·Pr(H|¬E). State the prior credences, the relevant conditional probabilities, and the confidence levels explicitly, using simple numbers. (5) Under one order of arrival the final credence must fall on the opposite side of a decision threshold (say 1/2) from the final credence under the other order, and you must show the arithmetic for both orders, so that the order-dependence is demonstrated rather than stipulated. (6) The order-dependence must not be manufactured by anything beyond the mechanics of soft updating: no self-undermining evidence, no observation that alters the content of the later one, and nothing in the story that privileges one order epistemically.

After the scenario: (a) walk through both orderings with the numbers; (b) identify which feature of the case does the work and what happens to the construction if that feature is removed — in particular, you should be able to explain why the case could not have been built had both observations concerned the same proposition; (c) give the two standard defenses of Jeffrey conditioning their best run — first, that the confidence weights attached to the second observation should themselves have been revised in light of the first, so the inputs, not just the outputs, are order-dependent facts; second, that the agent should really have conditionalized, hard, on the occurrence of the observations themselves; and (d) say whether either defense leaves the order-dependence looking like a bug in the rule or a faithful description of how noisy evidence actually accumulates. Keep the whole thing tight: one paragraph for the scenario, and the analysis no longer than the arithmetic requires.
