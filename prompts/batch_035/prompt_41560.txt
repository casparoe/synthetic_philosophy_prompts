I lead a lunchtime reading group at a robotics software company: six colleagues, strong on reinforcement learning and control theory, no philosophy. Next session is ninety minutes at a whiteboard, on embedded agency — what changes for an agent when it is part of the environment it acts in, rather than a clean optimizer sitting outside it. Don't explain the topic to me; plan how I should explain it to them. Structure the response in the following sections, in this order, at roughly the lengths given.

1. "Dependency order" (~250 words). The sequence the ideas must come in, and why that order. My instinct is that I first have to lay out the standard RL picture — agent on one side, environment as a box, reward signal arriving from outside — and then visibly break it, but tell me exactly which pieces must be in place before the next can be introduced, and what goes wrong if I introduce them out of order.

2. "The carrying example" (~150 words). The one concrete case that should carry the whole session. It must involve an agent that models a world which contains itself, but it must not be a human or any conscious being, or the group will drift straight into philosophy of mind. Say what the example must have and what it must lack.

3. "Misconceptions" (~300 words). The errors this audience will form, in the order they will form them, and where in the sequence each should be headed off. My guesses: that embedded agency is just embodiment (having a body and sensors); that it collapses into partially observable MDPs, which they know cold; and that the fix is simply "model yourself too," which misses the regress that any self-model short of the whole agent leaves something unmodeled. Correct me where I'm wrong and add what I've missed.

4. "Check" (~80 words). One question I can ask at the end whose answer would show they have actually understood the problem, not merely found it intriguing.

5. "What to leave out" (~100 words). What I should openly decline to cover in ninety minutes — I'm braced for someone to raise self-reference paradoxes and decision-theoretic puzzles — and how to say so honestly without making the topic look deeper or more settled than it is.

6. "Settled vs contested" (~150 words). I'm aware that embedded agency as a technical term comes largely from a particular research program in AI safety rather than from a mature literature, and that some of its framing — the talk of a Cartesian boundary, the claim that embedded agents cannot in principle fully model themselves — is contested or simply underdeveloped rather than agreed. In this final section, separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is contested or program-specific, and do not present one program's framing as the consensus of the field.

Throughout, assume the whiteboard is the only prop and that the group will interrupt; the plan should be robust to that.
