Hi! So I'm a novelist (mid-list, don't worry, you haven't heard of me), and I'm stuck on a scene and I think maybe a poem can get me unstuck. Let me explain.

My current book has a minor character, an elderly woman named Vera, who is sincerely, quietly devout — believes in God, in an afterlife, the whole package — but she is not stupid and she is not using belief as a metaphor. Every time I write her, I end up making her either a bad apologist (doling out arguments for God's existence that she'd lose) or a secret aesthete (treating her faith as basically poetry that she happens to take seriously). Both feel wrong. I kept thinking the problem was that I needed to decide what Vera is claiming, and then a friend who knows philosophy said, very Wittgenstein of you, but maybe she isn't claiming anything at all in the factual sense — that religious belief might be less like holding a hypothesis and more like something that shows itself in how a whole life goes, the way it's patterned, what it holds steady. That clicked with something I half-remember from his lectures on religious belief from the late 30s — the ones published as Lectures and Conversations on Aesthetics, Psychology and Religious Belief — but I read them years ago and honestly I just have the mood of them, not the arguments.

So here's what I want from you. You're a philosophy professor who specializes in Wittgenstein and also, conveniently, has strong feelings about poetry. Before writing anything, think carefully and do a bit of brainstorming on what he actually seems to think is going on with religious believers in those lectures: not agreeing or disagreeing on evidence, not applying a picture, the way a picture can regulate a life without being used to draw a conclusion, and how this connects to his bigger idea of a form of life. Get that right first, because the poem has to be right at that level or it's just decorative.

Then write me a sonnet. The octave should build the misleading picture I keep falling into — Vera as someone taking a stand on a disputed factual question, offering reasons, defending a position. The volta should be the moment that picture breaks: the realization that she isn't taking sides in an argument at all, that nothing in her life is being used as evidence. And the closing couplet should say, as precisely as sonnets allow, what kind of thing her belief actually is instead. Accurate enough that a philosopher of religion wouldn't wince, but it still has to work as a poem — I want to be able to actually read it aloud and feel Vera in it.

Typos in this note are all mine, thanks for looking past them. Take your time. I'd rather wait a day for something true than get something clever in ten minutes.
