Rules of evidence seem to demand support for everything: a document needs a witness to authenticate it, a witness's identification can be probed for the quality of her view, and any assertion can be met with a demand to know how she knows. Yet the law cuts this regress off at fixed points. Judicial notice lets a court accept certain facts without any evidence at all; legal presumptions, together with burdens of proof, declare a winner when support runs out on both sides. What view of the structure of justification does this arrangement presuppose? Is judicial notice a foundationalist stopping point at which some facts are simply self-warranting — and if so, why does the law confine it to matters of common knowledge or facts not subject to reasonable dispute, rather than letting it apply more broadly? Or are presumptions and burdens pragmatic rules that sidestep epistemology, decreeing an outcome rather than tracking where justification actually lies? Could a coherentist instead read the same practice as tolerating an open-ended web in which testimony, exhibits, and background regularities mutually support one another, with no need for a bedrock? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology as best you can, indicate where the practice is underdetermined between these readings, and explain what would have to change in courtroom practice if the law instead embraced an explicit infinitism, on which any chain of support must in principle be extendable on demand.
