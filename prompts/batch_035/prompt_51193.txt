For well over a decade I have read the rationalist blogs and forums more or less daily, which means I have watched the same fight about decision theory break out in the comment threads perhaps fifty times: someone posts Newcomb's problem, a confident two-boxer and a confident one-boxer talk past each other, someone brings up the smoking lesion, someone else announces that one-boxer-style theories dissolve the entire dispute, and nobody's position moves an inch. My growing suspicion, after all those years, is that many participants are sorting themselves by tribal affiliation rather than by what they would actually do in the cases — that if their verdicts were checked against their labels, a fair number of the labels would come apart from the verdicts.

So I would like to commission a sorting quiz for the blog: which decision theory do you actually implement? Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options (flee to Aleppo, stay in Damascus, and, where the case allows it, flip a fair coin and let it decide), plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The positions that must appear as leaves: causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, functional or updateless-style decision theory, and the tickle-defended version of evidential decision theory. Add further leaves if the case space requires them, and mark any answer pattern that nobody could coherently hold as uninhabited, with one sentence on why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The cases must include: (a) Newcomb's problem with the predictor's accuracy stated, so the key can register whether the reader one-boxes; (b) the smoking lesion, in two forms — the plain version, and a version in which the agent has already learned, before deciding whether to smoke, whether she has the lesion; this second form is the question that should split plain evidential decision theory from its tickle-defended variant, so write it so that a reader can answer it without ever suspecting that; (c) Death in Damascus, both the plain version and a version in which the agent may flip a fair coin and let it choose between the two cities, so the key can catch readers whose real view is that randomizing is the uniquely rational response when both deterministic options have been predicted to end badly; and (d) at least one case of your own construction, not a standard textbook one, designed to separate whatever pair of leaves the standard cases leave entangled. Flag honestly any pair of leaves the quiz still cannot separate after all this, rather than papering over it — my readership will test the key against their own answers with suspicious care, and a false claim of clean separation will be found out within a day.

Constraints: no question may ask which theory the reader endorses, may use any of the theories' names, or may gesture at the literature; every question must be answerable by someone with no background at all, purely on the strength of the case as described. Keep the prose economical — quiz, options, and key together should fit on one long page. Assume a mixed audience: longtime regulars who will scrutinize the scoring key, and newcomers who have never heard of any of the positions and must be sorted by what they say about cases alone.
