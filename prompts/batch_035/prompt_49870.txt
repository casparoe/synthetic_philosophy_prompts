my roommate takes a seminar on formal epistemology and came home with an arguement he's unbearably smug about. some background first so we're on the same page — this is all standard textbook stuff, no need to look anything up.

ordinary Bayesian updating treats experience as making you certain of something. you learn E flat out, and your new credence in any H is just your old credence in H given E. Richard Jeffrey's complaint, back in the sixties, was that experience rarely works like that — a dim glimpse through fog moves your beliefs around without ever handing you a proposition you're sure of. so he proposed probability kinematics (everyone just calls it Jeffrey conditioning): take a partition, meaning a set of mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive ways things might be, like {the witness is lying, the witness is mistaken, the witness is telling it straight}, and let the experience shift your credences in those cells from p_i to q_i. then recompute everything else by holding your conditional credences inside each cell fixed (the rigidity condition). done.

now his argument.

(1) a theory of learning from experience owes us a distinction: it has to be able to rule some credence changes OUT — to say this shift counts as learning and that one is just wishful thinking, or panic, or changing your mind so a bet comes out right. if it can't do that, it isn't a theory of rational learning, it's a notation.

(2) here's the math fact he leads with. fix where you are now (a credence function P) and anywhere you'd like to end up (P'), and suppose P' doesn't assign positive credence to anything P had already ruled out. then you can get from P to P' with one Jeffrey update — you just pick the partition right. the trick is to bucket the possibilities by how much more likely P' takes them to be than P did, i.e. by the likelihood ratio, and make those buckets your cells, with the new cell probabilities set to whatever P' says they should be. he says you land on P' exactly when the ratios cooperate, and as close as you like otherwise — I took that part for hedging, honestly.

(3) so for ANY destination there's a partition and a set of cell probabilities such that Jeffrey conditioning on them gets you there. the inputs can always be chosen after you've already seen where you want to go.

(4) a procedure whose inputs can always be chosen after the fact, so that any destination whatsoever comes out as having learned something, filters nothing. it's like a lawyer who can construct a defense of any verdict: the defense is formally fine, it just doesn't discriminate.

(5) so Jeffrey conditioning, as a theory of rational learning from uncertain evidence, is normatively empty. it can't tell a dim glimpse from wishful thinking, because both are just destinations and every destination is reachable. he adds that ordinary conditioning escapes this only because it can reach just the small family of distributions you get by conditioning on single events — which is exactly why he thinks the liberal version should scare me more.

then he goes for the kill: so the whole idea that perception is a kind of evidence quietly collapses, since the paradigm use case was the dim glimpse, and now anything at all can be dressed up as one.

I can't accept (5), but I also can't find the bad step. my instinct says the trouble is in (2), since that's the fancy step, but he walked me through it with actual numbers and it checks out as far as I can tell. if I had to keep one premise it'd be (1) — if that goes, I don't know what the theory is supposed to even be for, and he knows I think that, which is partly why he's smug.

also, part of me wonders whether the whole thing turns on what the theory was ever supposed to do. he keeps saying it's only about how a local change propagates once it has happened, not about which local changes are warranted — but if that's the escape hatch, I want to know what it costs. it sounds like it concedes that the formalism by itself can never rule out wishful thinking, and that everything hangs on some unformalized story about where the q_i come from. is that a price worth paying, or is it just giving up (1) with extra steps?

so: which premise do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? and if the real answer is that the argument is valid but the conclusion is smaller than he claims, say that too — he'd hate it, which is a point in its favor.
