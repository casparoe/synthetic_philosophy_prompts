Every year I teach a survey course on philosophy in the classical Islamic world, and every year the unit on Avicenna's flying man argument goes the same way. The students are charmed by it, grant it its conclusion, and then, when I press them, concede that the conclusion is being delivered by the two features everyone complains about: the subject has been created as a fully formed adult with no past, and he hangs in a void in which nothing whatsoever is happening to him — no sensation, no thought, no experience at all. The students suspect, and I half agree, that a mind conjured into existence under those conditions is not a fair test of anything. For next year's syllabus I want a version of the case with both suspect features stripped out, and I would like your help constructing it.

Here is the specification. Build a case in which a subject, at some moment, is aware of her own existence while nothing about her body is before her mind: she neither affirms nor denies any limb, no internal sense presents her with anything bodily, and her awareness of herself at that moment does not run through her body at all. Two devices are banned. First, no miraculous creation: the subject must have an ordinary past — a childhood, memories, settled habits — and an ongoing mental life; she is not a newly minted adult. Second, no void: she is not suspended in darkness with nothing occurring; she may be thinking, remembering, feeling an emotion, perhaps even hearing something. The setting must be realistic — clinical or everyday, no technology that does not exist — and the scenario itself should run to one paragraph. Most importantly, the absence of bodily awareness must be generated by the setup rather than stipulated: a reader of your paragraph should be able to say why, in that moment, nothing bodily is present to her, not merely be told that this is so. A case that simply asserts that the subject happens not to be attending to her body has cheated, since attending-to is exactly what is in question.

After the scenario, please do three things. First, run Avicenna's own reasoning over your case: show that the subject affirms her existence without affirming her body, and that no bodily faculty is doing the affirming — the parallel to his claim that the flying man would affirm his self even if one of his limbs were missing, and that the affirmation is not performed by any of the external or internal senses. Second, identify which feature of your case is doing the work that sudden creation and the experiential void did in the original, and tell me what happens to the conclusion if that feature is subtracted. Third, give me the strongest attack on the case from the embodied-self side — a Lockean who holds that personal identity runs through consciousness connected to the body, or an Aristotelian hylomorphist who denies that the self can be aware of itself at all except as embodied — together with the best Avicennian rejoinder to that attack. And if, in the course of building it, you come to think the specification is not satisfiable — that every realistic case quietly stipulates what it was supposed to generate — I would much rather have that diagnosis, argued for honestly, than a case that only appears to comply.
