I've been reading Leibniz on the best of all possible worlds, and a picture keeps turning up, in his own texts and in the secondary literature: the possible worlds as rivals competing for existence, each striving to be actual, the more perfect ones exerting a stronger claim, so that the best possible world comes out on top more or less by itself. Someone in my seminar said this is like natural selection: a selection process without a selector, where the outcome is fixed by the internal natures of the competitors rather than by anyone's decision. My study group has been using this to soften the problem of evil: if the actual world resulted from a competition among possibles rather than from a divine choice, God is no more to blame for evil than natural selection is to blame for the extinction of the less fit — God comes out more like the environment than like an agent.

Please audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of fitness, of the population of competitors, of selection pressure, of the environment — and then say where the mapping breaks: which features of natural selection have no counterpart here (there are no actual populations, no variation, no competition for scarce resources), and which features of the source the analogy quietly imports into the case of God, notably the assumption that being a condition rather than an agent absolves one of responsibility. Then tell me whether the theodicy conclusion survives. On Leibniz's own account, God still surveys the possibilities and chooses, with complete knowledge; so is the selection-without-a-selector picture doing real work in the argument, or is it decoration on a story in which God remains very much a selector — and if the latter, does the conclusion it was supposed to support collapse? If you think some other comparison would carry the honest conclusion better, say what it is.

Please write your answer in British English.
