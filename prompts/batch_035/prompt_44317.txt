So a bit about me first: I retired a few years back after 30-odd years in condensed matter physics, mostly superconductors and a lot of time yelling at vacuum equipment. Since then I've gotten really into philosophy of science, mostly over my head but enjoying it. I run a little reading group — four retired scientists and one very patient ex-philosophy-teacher — and we just worked through Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, the one where he argues you judge a theory by the accuracy of its predictions and shouldn't hold its wildly unrealistic assumptions against it. People apparently call this the F-twist. Half our group thinks Friedman just said out loud what every working scientist already does, and the other half thinks it's a scandal. I want to settle it properly at the next meeting, or at least find out where everyone actually stands.

What I'd like is a decision tree we can hand out. It should start from a starting point all of us accept — something like: "this model made a correct, novel prediction, but one of its central assumptions is flatly false and we know it's false." Then each branch node should be a question a working scientist could answer without knowing any philosophy jargon (no question like "are you an instrumentalist?"). Some candidate questions, use or replace these as you see fit: Did the false assumption actually do any work in deriving the prediction, or would a true-enough substitute have gotten us there anyway? Is the assumption false the way "frictionless plane" is false — a deliberate simplification of a real target system — or false the way nobody even aimed at the truth? Would we expect the idealization to be removed as the science matures, or does it have to stay in place forever? Is it false because the omitted factor is negligible in our domain, false because we've wandered outside the domain where it holds, or just a placeholder to be replaced by the real story later? And do we care about explaining how the world actually works, or only about organizing predictions?

The leaves should be named positions — Friedmanite instrumentalism, some Galilean/idealization view, mechanist positions that demand the real mechanism, fictionalism about models, structural realism, whatever else belongs there. Build the tree from the logical space first, i.e. figure out the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, and THEN locate the actual named positions in that space, not the other way around. If some combination of answers looks like a position nobody holds, note it and say briefly why (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?). And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually seperate — I'd rather know the instrument is blunt in places than have it papered over. Maybe 7-9 leaves total. No reading list or citations please, we already have the Friedman essay and thats not the point — we need the map.
