At a workshop on persistence next month I am defending a paper arguing for a view I call custodial continuity: the identity of an artifact over time is fixed neither by material continuity nor by continuity of form, but by an unbroken, intentional chain of custody — a socially sustained practice of some person or institution maintaining, using, and taking responsibility for the object as the same one. On this view, the gradually repaired ship of Theseus is the original, because the repairs belong to a single ongoing custodial practice, while the ship reassembled from the scavenged old planks is a new ship, because acquiring custody of the parts is not inheriting custody of the vessel. My central argument turns on a variant I have constructed: suppose the crew is barred from the shipyard for ten years while a preservation society carries out exactly the replacements the crew had planned, in the same order, from the same timber stock. If matter or form were what mattered, this case and the ordinary gradual-repair case should receive the same verdict; nearly everyone wants to say the society's ship is different, or at least hesitates; my view explains the difference cleanly — custody was severed, the original ceased, and a successor began.

The audience is a working group of about fifteen metaphysicians. Roughly half are endurantists who hold that persistence is an absolutely determinate, mind-independent fact, and who will treat any appeal to social practice as a subject change from metaphysics to sociology. Two are committed mereological essentialists for whom the replacement of a single plank already destroys the original. Two or three are perdurantists who will say the whole dispute is merely verbal, and that my question is really about which temporal segments our sortal concepts group together. One is a historian of material culture who studies how museums decide when a heavily restored object is still the one on the placard, and will come armed with real restoration cases in which the custodial story and ordinary judgments come apart. The organizer, who invited me, is sympathetic but keeps pressing a sharpened version of my own worry: if a derelict is abandoned and later re-adopted by a community that sincerely treats it as the same ship, my view makes it the same ship — doesn't that make identity hostage to collective attitudes in a way that should embarrass even a conventionalist?

Prepare my prep sheet. Do not open with a summary of my paper or any preamble: the first sentence must be the single most dangerous objection stated in full, and everything after is the reasoning. The sheet should then give the objections I will actually get from this audience — not the ones I would get from a general philosophy crowd — ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way the relevant subgroup would put it, in their own vocabulary, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Include at least one objection about whether custodial continuity can handle fission — two rival crews simultaneously maintaining the same derelict, each claiming to be the original's custodian — and one from the historian about the museum cases. Mark clearly which points I should concede up front and which I must hold no matter what. And include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
