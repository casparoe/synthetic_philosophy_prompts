I'm taking an introductory course on rational choice theory, and a pattern has come up twice now that I can't find the right concept for. When I compare two options that differ only slightly—two nearly identical pairs of headphones where one gets two percent better battery life, or two apartments where one commute is two minutes shorter—I honestly cannot judge that one is better than the other; the difference is just too small to register. My roommate says that if I treat every tiny difference as making no difference to which option is better, then by chaining enough tiny differences together I'm committed to saying that an apartment with a two-hour commute is just as good as one next door to my office, since each step along the chain was individually negligible. And the same thing seems to happen in reverse: when our landlord raised the rent fifteen dollars a month, nobody would have said the place became a genuinely worse deal, but after ten years of such raises the rent has doubled and everyone agrees it is now clearly worse relative to what it was.

Everyone I've described this to tells me it's just the heap-of-sand paradox, but I don't think it is, and that's what I want help pinning down. The heap paradox is about when a vague word applies to a thing as grains are added or removed. My pattern is about a relation—something being better than something else—holding or failing to hold between options, and it's not obvious to me that a relation between comparisons should behave the way the word 'heap' does. Nor is it obvious what the right response is: my roommate concludes that at least some tiny differences must make a real difference even though nobody can perceive them, while a friend of mine concludes instead that comparing options across long chains is somehow illegitimate, and I can't tell which of them—if either—is responding to the right structure. Does this configuration have an established name in the philosophical literature? If it does, I'd like to know how exactly it differs from the heap paradox and whether the standard responses to the one carry over to the other. If it doesn't, please coin a term for it and define it carefully, including where its edges are—for instance, whether it matters that the tiny differences are merely unnoticed rather than genuinely imperceptible, or whether the chain runs across a ranked list of options held all at once versus across time like the rent increases.

I'm not looking for a bare label; I want the actual carving—what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and which nearby concepts it gets confused with—since the label alone won't tell me whether my roommate's solution and my friend's solution are both available, only one of them is, or both miss the point. Please answer in American English.
