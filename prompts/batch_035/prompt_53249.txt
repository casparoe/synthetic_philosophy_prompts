In Tolstoy's What Is Art? (1897) the whole theory rides on one analogy: art is an infection. The artist recalls a feeling he once experienced and expresses it through external signs; the audience catches the feeling from those signs the way one catches a disease from a carrier, and Tolstoy proposes the strength of this infection as the measure of artistic quality. He leans on the analogy to license two conclusions he cares about: that sincerity is the master virtue of art — the artist must himself have the disease before he can pass it on, so imitated symptoms make for counterfeit art — and that the refined art of the educated classes, transmitted without genuine infection, is worthless by its own criteria. Audit the analogy as an argument. Give me the correspondence table (artist, work, audience, and feeling as pathogen, host, symptom, carrier — whichever mapping is really intended), then identify where the mapping breaks. I'm most interested in the normative gap: in epidemiology a trait is evaluated from the pathogen's point of view (what spreads), whereas Tolstoy wants to evaluate art from the host's point of view (what is good for those infected), and I suspect he can close that gap only by quietly importing his separate religious criterion of worthwhile feelings — in which case infectiousness was never the real measure, just the rhetorical packaging. So: does his claim that the best art is the most infectious survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the analogy collapse into a different and better one — say, art as persuasion rather than contagion — and if so, what does the replacement analogy change about his verdict on the art of his own class?
