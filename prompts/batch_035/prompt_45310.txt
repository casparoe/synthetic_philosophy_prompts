I'm a postdoc in gravitational physics, and this semester I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar mostly out of curiosity about how outsiders see what we do. Last week the instructor presented what she called the problem of old evidence, and it has been stuck in my head since. The setup: by 1915, the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion had been known for over half a century — Le Verrier had flagged it, and whole families of fixes had been tried. When general relativity accounted for the anomaly exactly, everyone took this as powerful confirmation of the theory. I still take it that way; I'd bet my career on the direction of that judgment. But the Bayesian story of confirmation says evidence supports a hypothesis insofar as learning it would raise the hypothesis's probability, and Mercury's perihelion wasn't learned in 1915 — it was background knowledge the whole time, so it seemingly couldn't raise anything's probability by any amount at all. The instructor seemed to treat this as a genuine open wound in Bayesian confirmation theory; the physicists in the room shrugged that obviously we just mean something like counterfactual reasoning, imagine you didn't know about the perihelion, and the philosophers told us that the counterfactual is notoriously ill-defined — you can't just delete one fact from Einstein's web of knowledge and keep the rest fixed.

I want your credence — a number, not a survey — that Bayesian confirmation theory, in some principled development rather than an ad hoc patch, can give the right answer about the perihelion case: that is, that it can deliver substantial confirmation of general relativity by already-known evidence, using machinery that doesn't collapse when you look at it closely. And I want the decomposition, because I suspect my number and the instructor's differ for identifiable reasons. Concretely, something like: your probability that the problem is a real defect rather than a misdiagnosis resting on some confusion about what "knowing the evidence" amounts to; conditional on it being real, your probability that the best repair turns out to be some form of counterfactual credence (imagining away the anomaly despite the definitional tangles), versus something like treating oneself as newly learning where the hypothesis lives in logical space so that its entailing the anomaly comes as news, versus a dissolution that grants confirmation can flow from accommodation after all and shows why that's fine; and whatever further terms you think the total credence depends on that I haven't listed. Give a number and a one-line justification for each, then show how you combine them into the total.

Two more things. First, the instructor floated the suspicion that the perihelion case is unrepresentative because the anomaly had no serious rival explanation at the moment GR arrived — she thinks cases like this, where accommodation of a known puzzle really does feel like confirmation, are doing most of the work making the problem look soluble, and that messier historical cases would expose any repair as tuned to this one example. Factor that in or push back on it. Second, tell me which single term in your decomposition the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or historical case would move that term the most — I'd like to bring something sharper than a shrug to the next seminar.
