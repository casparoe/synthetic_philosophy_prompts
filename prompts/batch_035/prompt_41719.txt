I teach in a philosophy department, though my own field is philosophy of law, and epistemology is adjacent enough that I am embarrassed by how shaky my grasp of the Wittgenstein literature is. A colleague in epistemology has been pressing a recent trade book on skepticism on me, and its chapter on the late Wittgenstein summarizes his position as follows: "Wittgenstein's last notebooks, published as On Certainty, contain his definitive answer to the skeptic. He argues that knowledge rests on certainties that are themselves more fundamental than any proof could be, things like the fact that I have two hands, or that the world has existed for more than a few minutes. These are things we know with total certainty, though not on the basis of evidence; they are self-evident to any rational person, much as the cogito was for Descartes. Once we see that these hinge certainties are self-evident, skepticism about the external world is simply refuted: the skeptic demands proof where proof is out of place, and can be shown to be wrong. Wittgenstein thus ends up where G. E. Moore ended up, agreeing that Moore really did know he had hands, but supplying the argument that Moore lacked. And since the hinges are the same for everyone and are immune to change, Wittgenstein also saw off relativism: anyone who doubted our hinges would be irrational, not merely different from us."

I suspect there are genuine distortions here, but I cannot locate all of them with confidence. My vague recollection is that on Wittgenstein's view hinge propositions are somehow not items of knowledge at all, which would make the book's phrase about knowing them with total certainty quietly question-begging; and I also half-remember the riverbed image suggesting that hinges can in principle shift, which sits badly with the claim that they are immune to change. I have even heard Wittgenstein described as closer to relativism than to its refutation, though I don't know whether that reading has legs or belongs to a fringe. So my request: give me a careful audit of this passage, part by part, sorting each of its claims into what a serious Wittgenstein scholar would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I am especially interested in the hinge-as-knowledge issue, in whether Wittgenstein can really be read as endorsing Moore's proof, and in whether the book's two headline conclusions — that skepticism is refuted and that relativism is refuted — each survive correction of the passage they are built on.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose only, the way you would write a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and begin with your overall verdict on whether the chapter can be trusted before a general reader.
