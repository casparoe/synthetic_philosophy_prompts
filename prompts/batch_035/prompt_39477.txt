I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've ended up, mostly through arguing with a colleague at work and a lot of YouTube, roughly committed to the view that taxation is theft — not as a slogan but as a serious claim about moral structure. The idea, as I'd put it: when I earn money by my own labour and a third party takes a cut under threat of punishment, with the fact that a majority or a parliament approved it doing no moral work that it wouldn't do if a gang voted to take my wallet, the taking has the same moral character as theft. I take the point that the state provides roads and courts I use, but I pay for those involuntarily in the first place, so it feels like the mugger who buys you lunch afterwards, or the protection racket that does, in fact, protect the shop. And the move I find most persuasive is the forced-labour one I've seen attributed to Nozick: if the state is entitled to 30% of my wages, it's entitled to 30% of my time, and I can't see the principled difference between confiscating the product of my hours and compelling the hours themselves.

I'd like to know what would actually count against this view — not a lecture on why it's wrong, but a map of its exposure. Which cases, intuitions, analogies or arguments should genuinely worry me if I'm being honest with myself? For instance: is there a relevant difference between theft by an individual and expropriation by an institution everyone is born into before they own anything? Does the fact that I could leave the country, or that my property rights in the money were themselves created and enforced by the state rather than pre-existing it, undermine the consent framing I'm relying on — or are those just the standard dodges? I'm especially interested in which potential defeaters a thoughtful defender of the view would concede as counting against it, versus which they'd dismiss as confused, and in whether the view risks becoming unfalsifiable: if every counterexample gets absorbed by redefining what counts as genuine consent or genuine ownership, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just what a conceptual claim looks like?

Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and be specific about what would have to be true — about the origin of property rights, about the difference between taking and failing to give, about democratic authorisation, about the natural-rights story I'm implicitly leaning on — for the view to collapse or need major surgery. If you think some of my supporting intuitions (the lunch-buying mugger, the forced-labour equivalence) are doing more work than the core claim and would be the first thing to go, say so. And if you think the view is actually well insulated and the insulation is legitimate rather than epistemic vice, I'd want that conclusion stated plainly rather than hedged.
