i'm a grad student in philosophy applying for a departmental summer grant, about 8 weeks of funding, and the application needs a ~1000 word research proposal. here's my half-formed idea, which i want you to turn into the actual proposal:

the "hinge of history" hypothesis — the claim that our century is unusually influential compared to other centuries — is normally debated as a first-order factual question. people argue about base rates of technological change, or about whether people in the past also believed they lived at the hinge and were wrong, etc. but here's the thing that interests me: if enough people believe we're at the hinge, that belief itself changes how the century behaves. some AI labs race to build powerful systems faster partly because they think the stakes are uniquely high *now*. grantmakers shift money toward this century's problems. talented people pick careers in AI safety or AI development instead of whatever they'd otherwise do. so the belief is causally entangled with the fact. it could be partly self-fulfilling, and it could also in some versions be self-undermining — a mad dash now might burn through the influence that later centuries would otherwise have had, which would be weird (the belief that we're the hinge makes us not-the-hinge).

what i want in the proposal:

1. the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer — something like: under what conditions does widespread belief that time t is the hinge raise, lower, or leave unchanged the probability that t actually is the hinge?
2. why the existing debate doesn't touch this. the standard back-and-forth is all first-order; almost nobody models the belief and the fact as feeding into each other. (don't do a lit review, i'll handle citiations myself.)
3. what i'd actually do week by week. i'm imagining mostly conceptual work with a toy model: a few parameters like how strongly belief affects effort, how effort affects the outcome, how the outcome changes later centuries' leverage. no heavy math, this is philosophy — more like carefully specifying a model and seeing which combinations of assumptions give self-fulfilling vs self-undermining results.
4. what would count as failure. i'm honestly worried the whole thing collapses into "beliefs affect behavior, news at eleven," so the proposal should include a paragraph on why the feedback structure here is more interesting than that, and should be upfront that if the parameters turn out to be so underspecified that anything can be derived, the project fails.
5. a realistic scope check — is this actually doable in 8 weeks, and if not, what's the trimmed version that is?

keep the tone appropriate for a funding committee but don't pad it. no biblography. if you think part of my framing is confused (e.g. maybe "later centuries' leverage" needs more careful definition than i've given it), fix it in the proposal rather than silently keeping my vagueness.
