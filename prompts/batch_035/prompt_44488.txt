I've been following the epistemic akrasia debate for years now, first through the academic literature and then, more urgently, through the forecasting and calibration community I actually belong to, where it has a practical edge the journals don't quite capture. As I understand the impasse: everyone agrees on the first-order facts, agrees the agent has higher-order evidence that her belief-forming process was unreliable in this very instance, and yet the field has been static for a decade. The deflationist says higher-order evidence is just evidence — integrate it into your credence like anything else, so no rational agent ever ends up believing p while believing her total evidence doesn't support p. The steadfasters and the akrasia-permissive crowd say otherwise: sometimes the rational move is to hold the first-order credence fixed while judging that holding it is irrational by your own lights, because the higher-order evidence either points nowhere in particular or arrives too late to guide anything. The same stock cases get re-run endlessly — the misleading deflater, the reason you can't locate, the Moore-paradoxical combination — and nobody moves an inch. My forecasting group has its own live version: our aggregation pipeline is known to have a bias, we can't tell which of our current credences it contaminated, and half the group thinks the meta-knowledge should make us discount everything while the other half thinks a discount applied to all beliefs equally is no discount at all, just noise.

My working hypothesis is that the stalled debate isn't really about cases at all but about what 'epistemic rationality' is doing in the sentence: one side is evaluating states of belief (is this credence fitting, given everything, including the facts about its own pedigree?), while the other side is issuing directives to an agent mid-inquiry (what should you do now, holding what you hold?). If that's right, the two sides could each be fully correct on their own question and the dispute would be verbal — but I can also see it the other way, as a substantive fight over whether norms must be action-guiding at all, which would make the verbal diagnosis a way of dodging the real issue.

So: diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal over 'rational,' substantive about the norms, or two questions under one heading? Support the diagnosis with what the sides actually say rather than a survey of positions, and be explicit about what would count as progress given your answer — a case that separates the interpretations, a formal result, a distinction nobody has drawn — or an honest argument that nothing should be expected to move it and why. And tell me whether my forecasting group's pipeline dispute is the same disagreement in work clothes or a genuinely different problem, because if the diagnosis doesn't have implications for agents who actually have to submit a number, I'm not sure what it's for.
