I'm taking an introductory course in biomedical ethics, and we've spent two weeks on consent without ever landing on an actual definition of what makes consent valid, so I've been trying to work out my own position by going with my gut on cases. My verdicts so far: A patient who signs a surgical consent form after the surgeon explains the procedure in dense technical language she plainly doesn't follow, and who never asks questions, has not given valid consent — the form means nothing. A woman who agrees to have sex with a man who has told her he's divorced when he's married has given valid consent, and I can't quite explain why the lie doesn't bother me more. The same woman agreeing to have sex with a partner who has falsely claimed a vasectomy has not given valid consent, and that contrast is one I feel strongly about but can't yet justify. An employee who signs a non-compete agreement after being told that the alternative is termination that same day has not consented validly, yet the same employee accepting a low wage because the local job market is brutal does seem to have consented, somehow. A mildly tipsy friend who gets a tattoo has consented validly; the same friend, equally tipsy, signing over power of attorney has not. And finally, my grandmother, who has mild early-stage dementia, validly consented to her flu shot, and I'd say also validly to moving into assisted living, but could not validly leave her entire estate to a caregiver she met three weeks earlier.

I'd like to know whether there is a single principle, or perhaps a small set of conditions, that captures all of these verdicts — something along the lines of consent being valid when it is informed, competent, and uncoerced — but I suspect those three standard headings won't survive contact with my cases. The lie about the vasectomy and the lie about the divorce both seem like failures of informedness, yet only one invalidates; the threat of firing and the pressure of the job market both look like constraints on the choice, yet only one looks like coercion. Maybe the real distinction runs through materiality, or through whether the deceived party would have had a realistic chance to reconsider had the truth been available, or through a graded notion of competence that shifts with the stakes rather than sitting at a fixed threshold. Please give me your best candidate principle or principles, stated as precisely as you can, with the case that motivates each clause, and then tell me honestly which of my verdicts your account cannot save — and if the price of saving all of them is a hopelessly baroque principle, say that too, and tell me which verdict to abandon. I'd also like your account's verdict on two cases I deliberately haven't decided: a research subject who initial every page of a forty-page consent packet without reading it, and a person who consents to sex while equally drunk as their partner, each having plausibly compromised the other's capacity. Please write your answer in British English.
