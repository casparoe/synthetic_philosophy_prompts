Been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days and I now run a small meetup -- about 10 people, mixed crowd (a few programmers, a nurse, one econ grad student who thinks he already knows all this), we get a rented room for 2 hours. Next session I want to tackle coherence arguments for expected utility maximization: the idea that if your preferences violate the axioms you can be money-pumped, so you're not just wrong, you're not even a coherent agent. Everyone in this scene has *read* that argument a hundred times. Nobody has ever felt it. So: design a game where they choose their way into being pumped and only see the ledger at the end.

Specifics:

- The violations have to come out of their own choices, no lecture up front. I'm imagining a series of pairwise picks between bundled options (trip packages, or pizza-and-movie bundles, whatever you think gets people to reveal real preferences), arranged so a normal person judging each trade on its own plausibly ends up with A over B, B over C, and C over A -- while the facilitator quietly plays bookie, taking a small cut at every step.
- Payoffs need to work without cash: tokens, or getting out of cleanup duty, something with actual stakes.
- The debrief has to map the pump onto transitivity specifically. I do NOT want people walking away thinking "money pump = you violated independence." Keep that distinction crisp, and maybe flag that Allais-type stuff is a different failure mode this game doesn't touch.
- Handle the escape hatch everyone who's done some reading will reach for: "I'd just see it coming and refuse the later trade." Design things so refusing is either unavailable or visibly costs them, and the debrief should be honest about whether that escape actually defeats the coherence argument or only works because agents like us can't credibly precommit.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run (someone will game it, someone will sulk about the bookie's cut, I assume), and be frank about what the activity can't reproduce. The real theorem is about preferences over lotteries, and if my game is all sure outcomes I'm only pumping them on intransitivity, not showing why *expected utility* in particular. If you think I should sneak in a couple of gamble choices to gesture at the independence axiom, say so, but keep the whole thing runable by someone who is definately not a math person.
