I'm a novelist, four books in, all historical fiction, and right now I'm deep in a novel set on an English country estate in the 1880s. My protagonist is a woman who genuinely believes empire is a kindness, votes for women would be a disaster, and her servants are lucky to serve her — I've written her that way because I think that's who she'd be, and I've resisted having the narration wag a finger at her every five pages. My agent is fine with it, but my beta readers split into two camps. One keeps flagging passages saying readers will think these are MY views if I don't distance myself somehow. The other camp, led by my friend Diana, keeps shutting the first camp down with a line she got from a writers' conference panel on historical fiction: "The past is a foreign country; they do things differently there." It's the famous opening sentence of L. P. Hartley's The Go-Between, and Diana deploys it like a full argument — past people are just different, so judging my protagonist by our standards is like calling a foreigner barbaric for eating with their hands. Case closed, or so she thinks.

But it bugs me that nobody at the workshop actually spelled out what the sentence is claiming, and I suspect it's doing several different jobs at once depending on who says it. When Diana says it, is she making the near-trivial point that customs and beliefs vary across time (which nobody denies, and which wouldn't by itself settle anything about whether the first camp's worry is legitimate)? Or the methodological point that understanding a past person requires reconstructing their context before appraising them? Or the much stronger claim that moral appraisal just doesn't reach across centuries, that my protagonist's views aren't wrong-for-her even if wrong-for-us? Or even some almost relativist claim that "wrong" only ever means wrong-by-the-standards-of-some-time, so there is no further question once you've located her in hers? And there's a nagging version where it's really a claim about me and my craft rather than about her at all — something like "fiction about the past is ethnography, not a morality play," which sounds more like a theory of the novel than a theory of history.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims this one sentence could be making, from the ones that are platitudes through the substantive and defensible ones down to the ones that are probably false, and for each tell me whether it actually supports what Diana used it for — namely, telling my beta readers to stop worrying about whether I need to morally signal distance from my own character? I want a menu of readings I can bring back to the group, with a verdict on each, not just one favored interpretation. Also worth flagging if any reading would actually support the FIRST camp instead, since I have a feeling the foreign-country analogy cuts both ways — we do manage to disapprove of things happening in actual foreign countries, after all.
