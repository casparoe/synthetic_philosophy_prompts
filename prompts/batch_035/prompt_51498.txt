I am an examiner marking take-home essays for a third-year undergraduate paper on moral philosophy. The set question was: "Why do promises bind? Defend the account you find most plausible against its most serious rival, and state clearly whether, on your account, it can ever be right to break a promise for the sake of the greater good." Below is one student's submission. Please give it a mark out of 100, using the usual UK undergraduate bands, and then supply the comments that justify the mark, working through the essay point by point so that each element the student got right or wrong is separately identified and assessed. Where the student makes a substantive claim about a philosopher's position, your comments should say whether that claim is accurate, and you should be precise about it; the essay cites primary texts, and part of your job is to check that it cites them for views they actually contain. Please write the whole of your response, including the mark and the comments, in continuous prose of the sort one finds in a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic text, no tables. Please also write throughout in British English.

The student's submission reads as follows.

"Why do promises bind? The natural first thought is that when I promise, my act of will somehow creates an obligation, rather as signing a document creates a legal one. Hume saw that this will not do. There is no natural obligation that springs from a bare volition: nothing in the operation of my will, considered on its own, obliges me to anything. The obligation must instead be artificial, generated by a convention established for the common interest. That is Hume's account of promises in the Treatise: promises are conventions of the same kind as money and property, invented because life goes better with them than without them.

Rawls later gave this the sharper form of the practice conception of rules. On this view a promise binds not because the individual act of promising has good consequences, but because there is a practice of promising with constitutive rules, and the obligation is created by the rules of the practice. When I promise, I invoke a rule of the practice, roughly a rule that one who utters the words in the right circumstances is to do the thing promised, and the justification of that rule belongs to the practice as a whole and not to the particular case. It follows that I cannot justify breaking this promise by appealing to the good consequences of breaking it, any more than a batsman who has hit the ball for four can claim six because of how well he hit it. So far as it goes, this is the right account: it explains both why the obligation is directed, owed to the promisee rather than to the world at large, and why it applies even when breaking the promise would be more useful.

Scanlon agrees with the direction of this answer, and his expectation principle makes the point in terms of what the promisee may reasonably rely on. On Scanlon's view, the wrong of breaking a promise consists in setting back the promisee's interests by defeating a reasonable expectation, so the seriousness of the wrong varies with the size of the reliance loss: the more the promisee has staked on my word, the graver the wrong, and where nothing has been staked there is little wrong done.

What, then, of breaking a promise for the greater good? Since the practice is justified by the good consequences of there being such a practice, the justification is ultimately consequentialist. It follows that the practice must license exceptions whenever breaking a promise would produce better consequences overall, and so the utilitarian is right at the level of individual promises after all, even though the practice-level story explains why we normally speak as if promises were exceptionless."
