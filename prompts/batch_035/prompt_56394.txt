I'm teaching a philosophy seminar on evil and responsibility, and after working through Arendt's Eichmann in Jerusalem we're screening Jonathan Glazer's The Zone of Interest (2023), about Rudolf Höss, the commandant of Auschwitz, and the domestic life his family builds in the house adjoining the camp wall. Please give me a reading of the film through Arendt's banality of evil that runs in both directions: where the film seems made for her thesis, and where it quietly refutes it.

On the vindicating side, there is the film's central formal choice: we never see inside the camp. Evil appears only as administration — approving a crematorium design, protecting the lilac bushes, a promotion, a garden, sounds over the wall — which is close to Arendt's picture of wrongdoing without demonic roots, perpetrated by a functionary rather than a monster. On the resisting side, Höss is not the Eichmann of Arendt's portrait: he is a convinced believer and a proud technician of mass killing (at one point he tells his wife that he passed a Berlin party working out how to most efficiently gas the guests), and his retching on the staircase at the end, cut against present-day janitors scrubbing the displays of the Auschwitz museum, suggests an interiority that thoughtlessness leaves no room for. Please also weigh Bettina Stangneth's argument, based on the Sassen papers from Eichmann's Argentine exile, that the real Eichmann was an ideological antisemite who staged himself as a mere bureaucrat for the court.

Where does the film dramatize something Arendt's report misses, where does it distort her, and what does the final cut to the museum add to the debate — does banality survive as a category, or does the film replace it with something else, like the normalization of horror rather than its thoughtlessness? I'd like a reading that takes both the film and Arendt seriously enough to say where each goes wrong.
