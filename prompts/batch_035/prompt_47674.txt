I'm taking a philosophy of mind class this semester and we just did the conceivability/possiblity stuff (zombies, Kripke, etc.), and my study partner cooked up an argument that's supposed to be a reductio of the whole conceivability-possibility link, but I can't find where it slips. Here it is:

(1) If people at a time can coherently conceive that p -- I mean they really sit down, try to imagine it in detail, and find no contradiction, not just shrug and say "who knows" -- then it was possible, at that time, that p.
(2) Back in, say, 1990, before Wiles's proof, mathematicians could coherently conceive that Fermat's Last Theorem was false: nobody had a proof, they could describe a counterexample scenario without running into any contradiction they could detect, and plenty of them spent real effort on approaches that would have proven it false if they'd worked out.
(3) Whether p is possible doesn't depend on what year it is -- modal facts aren't created by us proving things. So if it was possible in 1990 that FLT was false, it's possible now.
(4) Wiles proved FLT, and a proved theorem is necessary. So it's not possible, now or ever, that FLT was false.
(5) Contradiction. So one of (1), (2), (3) has to go.

Here's my problem. My term paper is supposed to be on the zombie argument, so I can't just chuck (1) -- if conceivability doesn't track possibility at all, my paper is dead in the water. And (3) seems obviously right; if (3) has to go, then what's possible literally changed the moment Wiles finished, which sounds insane to me. So I want to say (2) is the false one, but I can't put my finger on what the 1990 mathematicians were actually missing. My friend's comeback is that if I patch (1) with some "ideal conceivability" clause, I've made the principle cheap and unfalsifiable, and that the exact same doubt he's raising now applies to my zombies anyway (am I ideally conceiving them, or just failing to spot an entailment?).

Tell me which premise to give up, why it looked fine going in, and what rejecting it costs me -- and specifically whether my friend is right that the "ideal conceivability" move saves the zombie argument only by making the conceivability-possibility link vacuous. Answer as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
