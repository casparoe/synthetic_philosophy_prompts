I am a novelist four years into a book narrated from inside the July Crisis of 1914, told by four characters — a Serbian consular official, a Habsburg staff officer, a London shipping clerk, a Berlin socialist printer — each of whom understands the coming war as the work of exactly one cause: the assassination, the alliance system, naval rivalry, the timetables of the general staffs. The book's formal problem is that the history appears to be overdetermined all the way down — remove any one of these and there is plausibly still a war in 1914 or 1917 — while narrative is constitutively selective; a novel cannot narrate all the jointly sufficient causes at once and remain a novel. Before I commit the book to any causal picture, I want the specification rather than the theory: please write the desiderata that any adequate account of overdetermined and multi-causal explanation in history must meet, fixing the purpose yourself as licensing or vetoing a novelist's choices about which causes to foreground.

Among the cases I would expect such an account to get right: the two fires that merge and jointly destroy a house, either of which alone would have sufficed; the preempted backup, the assassin stationed further along the route whose bullet was never needed; a cause that figures in no single sufficient set of which its removal would change nothing, and yet without which everything would have run differently. The distinctions it must not collapse include cause versus explanation, overdetermination versus redundancy, and causal contribution versus explanatory salience. I would add one requirement of my own, which you should assess rather than assume: that the theory say when narrative emphasis on one member of an overdetermining set is merely a choice of contrast class and when it is a distortion — and if that requirement turns out to be confused, tell me why.

Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudices of one school (counterfactualists will demand what regularity theorists will refuse to grant), and identify — this is the part I most want — whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, since if adequacy is unachievable in principle, that is a fact my narrators are entitled to know. Then, briefly and only briefly, tell me how a counterfactual-dependence account, an INUS- or NESS-style regularity account, and a contrastive account fare against the list: enough for me to see which one forgives a novelist's selectivity and which one condemns it as falsification.
