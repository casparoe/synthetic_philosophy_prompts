I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind, and every year the Chinese Room produces more trench-digging than argument. I want to force the discussion into explicit credences. Please give me yours: a number, not "it depends," for the following precisely fixed proposition — not "no machine could ever think," but that running a program with the right input-output profile is not by itself sufficient for understanding; i.e., that computationalism about the mind is false, and that the Chinese Room, in its strongest form, establishes this. Decompose the credence into roughly these terms, adjusting as you see fit: (1) the probability that the intuition the room pumps — the man shuffling symbols understands nothing — tracks a real fact about where understanding resides rather than a cognitive illusion about which objects we are willing to attribute understanding to, in light of how intuitions mislead in nearby cases; (2) the probability that Searle's distinction between intrinsic and derived intentionality can be made non-question-begging — that is, explained without simply building in the conclusion; (3) the probability that the systems reply fails even for the augmented room where the man has internalized the rulebook and the whole system, so that there is no longer a clear boundary to point to; and (4) any further terms you think belong, each with a number and a one-line justification. Then combine them into your overall credence. Finally, tell me which single term carries the most weight, what argument or evidence would most move it — and which term you would identify as the most likely location of the whole dispute, since in my experience students who find the argument obviously sound and students who find it obviously bankrupt usually turn out, on inspection, to be disagreeing about exactly one of these terms.
