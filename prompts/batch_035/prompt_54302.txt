The setup as I understand it. Textbook Bayesian updating treats your evidence as certain: you see the coin land heads, you set your probability of heads to one, and you recompute everything else. But Richard Jeffrey's point was that a lot of real observation isn't like that — there's his old tablecloth-by-candlelight thing, where you squint at the thing and can't quite tell if its green. So Jeffrey's rule (probability kinematics, I think it's called) lets you update on uncertain evidence: you nudge the probability of "green" partway toward one, and the rest of your beliefs get dragged along proportionally. I believe I've got that much right.

Here's what I can't square, and what the column currently hangs on. The number you nudge to — where does it come from? As far as I can tell the rule doesn't say. It's however much the experience moved you. So the formula governs the redistribution, but the actual point of contact with the world is just... a felt intensity? If that's right, then once Bayesians admitted evidence is uncertain, they admitted that learning is subjective exactly where it touches reality, and everything after that is bookkeeping. And it gets worse: it looks like an escape hatch. Murky evidence only counts against your theory as much as you felt it did, so nothing can ever really refute you — a Popperian would say the whole point of a severe test is that it hurts you regardless of how it strikes you. My draft column says, roughly, "the Bayesians' own rule concedes that evidence is only as strong as your gut says it is." Is that fair? It feels too clean, which usually means I'm wrong somewhere.

Two smaller things, also for the piece:

2. Would it be legitimate to use Jeffrey-style updating as a model of slowly souring on a news source you used to trust — no single clean disconfirmation, just a drift, week after week — or is that a different animal from what the rule was built for?
