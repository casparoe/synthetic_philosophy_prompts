So my uncle got really into philosophy podcasts this year, and at thanksgiving he gave me his whole rundown of the pragmatist theory of truth, Pierce vs William James edition. Roughly what he said: Peirce started it back in the 1870s with the idea that the meaning of a belief is just the practical effects you'd notice if you acted on it, and since true beliefs are the ones whose effects actually pan out, truth just IS whatever the community of scientists ends up agreeing on — so if the scientific consensus someday settled on something false, it would thereby be true, which Peirce was apparently fine with. Then James took the same idea and made it livelier: truth is what works, so a belief in God is true for you if it works for you. My uncle's takeaway was that James basically just ran with Peirce's view and Peirce went along with the direction of it but thought James was sloppy. He also said the whole thing was pretty much refuted once people pointed out that wishful thinking 'works' by James's standard, since comforting beliefs make you feel good, so pragmatism ends up making wishful thinking true.

I don't know enough about either guy to push back. Is this fair? Go through it piece by piece and tell me what's accurate, what's a simplification, and what's just wrong. For what it's worth im inclined to think he's right about James being a relativist, because that's the version I'd always heard — but the Peirce part where scientists agreeing on a falsehood makes it true sounds too dumb to be what anyone actually held, so I suspect something got lost in translation there.
