I am a retired developmental geneticist who has spent the past two years reading philosophy, and I have volunteered to lead a ninety-minute session of our lifelong-learning philosophy circle on origin essentialism — the view, associated with Kripke, that you could not have developed from a different sperm and egg, and that the individual who would have developed from a different gamete pair would simply not have been you. The audience is a dozen educated retirees with no philosophical training but strong general knowledge; they will arrive assuming this is a question my old field can settle empirically. Do not explain the view to me; plan how I should explain it. Specifically: in what order the pieces must come (I suspect the modal machinery — the distinction between 'you couldn't have had that origin' and 'whoever had that origin couldn't have been you' — has to be in place before the genetic material is even mentioned, but I may be wrong); which single example should carry the session, and what it must have and lack, given that the most tempting example for this audience, identical twins, is exactly where their biological intuitions will betray them; which misconceptions they will predictably form — I anticipate 'this is genetic determinism in fancy dress,' 'the other individual would have been so similar to me as to count as nearly me,' and 'the question is about personality rather than identity' — and at what point in the sequence each should be headed off; and one closing question that would let me distinguish genuine understanding of the claim from polite nodding. Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to cover in a single session and how to name the omission honestly.
