I've been trying to sort out what I actually believe about whether you can intend to do something you'll have no reason to do once the intending has done its work. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases, and I want to know if there's a single principle behind them or whether some of them have to go.

1. The classic one: a billionaire offers me $1M if at noon today I intend to drink, tomorrow, a vial of stuff that will make me violently ill for a day but do no lasting harm. The money is paid at noon today regardless of what happens after, and drinking the toxin tomorrow does nothing for me then. Verdict: I don't think I can get the million by just deciding. When I try to intend, I catch myself knowing I won't drink, and the intending won't stick. So: no money, unless I use some trick.

2. Same, but before noon I sign a contract with my sister: if I don't drink the toxin tomorrow, I pay her $50,000. Verdict: now I think I can genuinely intend, that I really will drink, and that setting this up was rational. The side-commitment gives tomorrow-me a reason.

3. Same as (1), except the billionaire doesn't interview me — he has a brain scanner that checks whether I'm in a "settled resolve" state at noon. And suppose I can get hold of a drug that produces exactly that neural state without any intention to drink actually being there. Verdict: I'd take the drug and the money, and I don't feel like I did anything wrong — he paid for the wrong thing. Though when I say that out loud it sounds kind of terrible, so hold this one loosely.

4. A kidnapper releases me on condition that at noon today I intend never to reveal his face, and suppose he can reliably tell whether I'm intending. Verdict: I think I can form that intention and that tomorrow I'll keep it — but I notice the case differs from the toxin one, because tomorrow-me still has a reason to stay quiet (the guy knows where I live).

5. Variant of (1) where the billionaire is careless: I get the million at noon if I intend to drink, but per a clause he tacked on the money is clawed back unless I actually drink tomorrow. Verdict: I intend, I drink, I keep the money — barely feels like a puzzle. (Control case, basically.)

The hard pair, I think, is (2) versus (3): in (2) I say the side-commitment makes the intention formable because tomorrow-me gets a reason, but in (3) I let myself collect without intending at all, which makes it look like what mattered in (2) was never really forming the intention, just securing the payout. Also, I can't tell whether my "no trick, no money" verdict in (1) is a psychological claim (I *can't* intend that) or a rationality claim (I *shouldn't*), and I suspect any principle has to pick one.

So: is there one principle that captures all five verdicts, or close to it? If yes, state it precisely — precisely enough that given a new case you can tell me whether the intention is formable and/or rational, and give me its verdict on a couple of cases I didn't list. In particular I want its verdict on the case I keep going back and forth on: like (2), but the commitment device is only my own word. Before noon I stand up at a family dinner and swear to everyone that I will drink the toxin, and I'm the sort of person who has never once broken a public vow and would be genuinely wrecked by doing it. That looks like (2), except the penalty is self-imposed and enforcing it tomorrow depends on me caring tomorrow — which is exactly whats in doubt. If your principle can't save all my verdicts, tell me which one to drop and why, but please take a side: pick a principle and defend it, don't hand me a menu of options and let me chose.
