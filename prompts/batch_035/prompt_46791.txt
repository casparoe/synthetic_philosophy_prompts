I run a weekly seminar on translation theory for fifteen second-year students — mostly monolingual English speakers with a smattering of school French — and I have one ninety-minute session to spend on something I've been mulling ever since reading Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: the thought that when a text is written under tight formal constraint, its form is not packaging wrapped around a content but part of what the text says, so that 'faithful translation' fractures into several mutually incompatible faithfulnesses. Rather than lecturing on this, I want an activity in which they run into the fracture themselves. My working plan: I hand them a short original quatrain I wrote for the purpose (so they can't look it up anywhere) — "Grey the window, grey the rain; / Kettle humming, low and slow; / Cats curled up beside the pane; / Nowhere else I need to go." — seven syllables to the line, ABAB rhyme, a rather dreamy, unhurried domestic mood. They would first 'translate' it into plain prose under the instruction to preserve everything that is said, then translate it again under a stack of formal constraints, and we would compare what survived and what quietly died at each step. But the details are up to you.

Please design the full session: phases, precise instructions given to participants at each phase, timing, group structure, and any voting or scoring mechanism you think helps. The point of the exercise must not be announced up front — they should discover it in the comparison, not be told it. The debrief should map specific moments in their own outputs onto the distinction between carrying propositional content and carrying what the form itself contributes: tone, music, brevity, the sense that no word in the original is idle.
