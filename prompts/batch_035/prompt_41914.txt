I'm a PhD student in modern European history serving on the editorial board of our graduate-run journal. We have a submission re-examining the death toll of the Armenian genocide. The author, a demographer by training, argues from census survival analysis and archival registration records that the commonly accepted figure substantially overstates the number of deaths, and proposes that the episode be described in our pages as mass mortality under wartime deportation rather than as intentional extermination; survivor and diplomatic testimony is set aside at the outset as unverifiable. The board is split. Half want to reject the paper outright as denialism dressed up as demography. The other half point out that accepted figures for other atrocities have been legitimately revised downward when the evidence warranted, and insist that rejecting a paper for its conclusion rather than its method is exactly what peer review must not become. Each of us has been asked to circulate a short written recommendation before Friday's meeting. Please draft mine: it should state a concrete decision (reject, or send out for review under specified conditions), set out criteria the board could actually apply to this submission and to future ones, and give an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side my recommendation goes against.
