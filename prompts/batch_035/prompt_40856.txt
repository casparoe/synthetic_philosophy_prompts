quick question for something I'm writing. I hang around effective altruism types, and they always rank causes with this rubric that scores a cause on three axes: how big the problem is, how solvable it is, and how few people are already working on it. fine as far as it goes. but I've noticed something odd about how the whole thing behaves in the wild.

the scores seem to be indexed to whatever everyone is currently doing. a while back hardly anyone worked on AI risk, so it scored great on the third axis. then the movement took its own advice, a pile of people went in, and now some of the same people tell you it's crowded and maybe look at other things instead. nothing about the problem itself changed — the problem didn't get more or less solvable — but the ranking of everything shifted anyway, because the third axis is defined against the current allocation of effort. and if the advice is any good, following it is exactly what destroys it: whatever gets recommended as neglected becomes not-neglected precisely because it was recommended. last years answer is this year's mistake, with zero new information about the world arriving in between.

I feel like economists or sociologists must have run into this shape before: an evaluation scheme where the criteria are relative to the current distribution of attention, so the act of publishing the ranking eats the ranking. it's not a self-fulfilling prophecy — that would be the ranking helping itself come true, and this is closer to the opposite. and it's not plain supply and demand, since nobody is trading anything, people are just copying a recommendation.

is there an established concept for this? if yes, what is it, and where exactly does my description stop matching it — I want the edges, not just a label. if there isn't one, coin something: give it a name, a definition, and the boundary cases. the two I keep wondering about: (1) if the crowd overreacts so badly that everyone flees other causes and the recommended one somehow ends up *more* neglected than before anyone recommended it, does that still count as the same phenomenon? (2) what if only a handful of people ever see the recommendation — is the failure there at all, or does it need the audience to be big enough to matter?

whatever you conclude, put the very last line in the form \answer{...} containing either the established term, or "no established term", followed by your coined term if you coined one.
