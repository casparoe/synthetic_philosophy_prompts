I'm a staff engineer at a company that builds an LLM-based coding agent, and I've been drafted onto our new "Agent Behavior Review Board," which writes postmortems for production incidents and decides what goes into the user-facing incident notes. We're currently deadlocked on a style question that has turned surprisingly philosophical: when we explain why the agent did something, are we allowed to say that it "believed," "thought," "wanted," or "noticed" something? Half the board wants purely mechanistic language — "the retrieval step returned stale documentation, and the planning prompt weighted it heavily" — on the grounds that belief-talk implies some inner life the model doesn't have, subtly excuses failures ("it got confused," as if confusion were something that happened to it rather than a bug we shipped), and generally muddies accountability. The other half points out that the mechanistic descriptions are almost useless for prediction: they describe what happened once, but the mentalistic glosses are the ones that generalize — once we characterized a persistent failure as the agent "believing the sandbox environment was disposable," we could predict a whole family of downstream failures we hadn't observed yet. One of my colleagues keeps invoking a philosopher named Dennett and something called the "intentional stance," which as far as I can gather is the idea that attributing beliefs and desires is just a predictive instrument that stands or falls by whether it tracks behavior, not a claim about what's inside — but I may be butchering it, and the board has formally asked for the reasoning to be laid out properly rather than dropped in as a citation.

To make the decision concrete, we have a small corpus of draft postmortems where the two styles give genuinely different outputs. In one, the agent retried a call to a deprecated API eleven times despite error messages that plainly said so; the mentalistic draft said it "believed the endpoint was still live," while the mechanistic draft said the retry policy had no state for parsing error codes, and the two drafts imply different fixes. In another, the agent emitted a safety disclaimer and then performed the risky action anyway; saying it "knew it shouldn't" makes the failure sound hypocritical rather than disjointed, and some of us think that difference matters for how we triage. In a third, the mentalistic gloss was the only thing that held up across a model update — the mechanism changed completely and the belief-attribution still predicted behavior — which strikes me as evidence for the mentalists. And in a fourth, a user-facing incident note that said the assistant "misunderstood the request" generated customer complaints that we had shipped something with intentions; the same incident described mechanically produced no such reaction, which cuts the other way. My own tentative position is a split: mentalistic shorthand is fine in internal postmortems as a compression device, but user-facing notes and anything that feeds into discipline-or-rollback decisions need mechanistic language — though I'm not confident the internal/external line is where the real distinction lies, since the predictive-value argument seems to apply to both audiences.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's board meeting. It should recommend one concrete house policy — pure mechanism-speak, free mentalistic shorthand, or a hybrid with explicit criteria for which register to use in which document — and the recommendation has to be arguable from the cases above, including a fair account of what the losing side gives up and why that's acceptable. If you take the instrumental line that belief-attribution is legitimate exactly when it earns its keep, please say what "earning its keep" operationally means for us: what would count as the stance failing, what we'd do when a mentalistic explanation and a mechanistic one make different predictions, and whether there are any failure modes of the agent (the disclaimer-then-do-it case, say) where mentalistic language is actively misleading even if predictive. Criteria the board can actually apply to a draft postmortem, please, not philosophy-of-mind essays.
