My friend and I stayed up far too late last night arguing about the Meno problem — whether and why knowledge is better than a mere true belief — and by the end we had a stack of her verdicts that I cannot fit to any single principle. I'd like you to act as a professional epistemologist and help me make sense of her track record, predict her verdict on a new case, and tell me what further case would best sort out the competing interpretations. Please think carefully, begin by brainstorming the candidate rules before committing to any prediction, and respond in American English.

Her verdicts so far:

1. On Plato's two guides to Larissa — one who knows the road, one who merely has a true belief about it — she said the knower's belief is better, and better in a way that matters even for this one trip, not merely because knowledge is more useful on future trips.
2. A doctor who reaches the correct diagnosis through a sound procedure versus one who lands on it by lucky guess: the first doctor's belief is better, and would remain better even if both patients recover identically.
3. A commuter who believes the train leaves at 8:00 from a year-old timetable that happens to still be correct: no knowledge there, and the commuter is in no worse a practical position than someone who checked the current timetable — yet she still insisted the person who checked has the better belief, full stop.
4. A gambler whose hunches have paid off across hundreds of one-off bets: no knowledge, and she denied that the hunches gain any value with each success.
5. A person who believes a bridge is safe because a genuinely reliable but unfamiliar measuring instrument reported it, though she has no idea the instrument is reliable: knowledge, and better than a lucky guess at the same truth.

The new case: a museum will incinerate two sealed vaults tonight, unopened and unexamined. Two archivists each hold a true belief about which vault contained the forged artifact. One read it in an accession ledger she had no reason to distrust but never cross-checked; the other simply guessed and happened to be right. The belief will never guide any action and never be tested again — the vaults burn either way.

Is the ledger-reader's belief better than the guesser's, and why? Identify the rules that fit her earlier verdicts (for instance, is she valuing non-accidentality, redeployability, stability, or being right for the right reason?), explain which of those rules converge and which split on this new case, give your best prediction of her verdict with your reasons, and propose the further case that would most cleanly discriminate between the remaining candidate rules.
