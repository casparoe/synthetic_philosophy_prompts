At a departmental reading group on Kant's ethics, a colleague presented a short argument meant to show that the Formula of Universal Law, taken on its own, forbids nothing at all — not even the false promise that Kant himself uses as his example of a perfect duty. Her argument ran as follows:

1. The Formula of Universal Law evaluates maxims, not acts; a maxim is a policy of the form: in circumstances C, I will do A for the sake of end E.
2. The C-position in a maxim is filled by whatever circumstances the agent takes herself to be in when acting. The formula itself places no restriction on which believed circumstances may occupy that position, and an agent can take herself to be in any circumstance she is actually in — including circumstances no one else could share, such as standing at this counter, on this afternoon, being asked this question by this loan officer.
3. If the circumstances in C are specific enough that only one agent at one time could possibly occupy them, then willing C's maxim as a universal law requires only that whoever is in those exact circumstances act on it — which, in that case, is a demand on a single performance.
4. A single performance of any act that is possible at all can be willed as a universal law without contradiction: the world containing it is consistent whatever else it contains, including a fully functioning institution of promising.
5. So by describing her maxim with sufficiently specific circumstances, an agent can pass the contradiction-in-conception test for any possible act — including the false promise.
6. The parallel holds for the contradiction-in-will test: an agent whose maxim's circumstances obtain only for her can will its universalization without thereby willing the loss of anything she depends on for her own ends.
7. Therefore the universalizability tests constrain nothing, unless some restriction on how specific C may be is imposed from outside the formula.

She anticipates the obvious reply — that such maxim descriptions are gerrymandered, and that the honest description of the false promisor's maxim mentions needing money and having no intention of repaying, not this afternoon and this loan officer. Her rejoinder: any restriction on how circumstances may be described either comes from somewhere other than the formula, in which case the formula is not doing the work Kant claims for it, or else the restriction must be stated as a principle, and she doubts any principle can be stated that both blocks her cases and preserves Kant's own examples.

I believe the argument is unsound, but each time I try to say where it slips, I find myself gesturing at what maxims really are, which is precisely the restriction she is demanding a statement of. Which premise or step should be rejected, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (1) and the verdict that the false promise fails the test; if those cannot both be kept, I want a clear account of what I am trading away and for what.
