I'm on the committee of a small housing co-op — about 40 units in a converted mill in Manchester, we own it collectively and the big calls get made by majority vote at quarterly meetings. Right now we're fighting about the courtyard: half the members want to rip up the old paving and put in bike storage and a bin area, the other half want to keep it as a shared garden. The fight itself is honestly boring, but the argument we've got into around it has become genuinely weird and I keep going back and forth on it.

The thing is, it's not only members who are affected. A bunch of the flats are sublet — some of the tenants have lived here longer than plenty of the actual members and use the courtyard every day, but they get no vote because they're not in the co-op. There's a café next door whose terrace looks straight onto the courtyard and would end up looking at a bike shed. A few members have basically moved abroad and only turn up for the votes that touch the service charge. And my partner keeps saying, well, what about the people who buy in next year — the decision will bind them harder than it binds those of us who might sell up and leave. My friend Sam reckons the rule is obvious: everyone whose interests are affected by a decision gets a say in it, full stop. My other friend thinks that's madness — on that logic the courier who cuts through the alley gets a vote, or the person who'd have bought one of the garden flats but now won't bother, and where does it even end? Someone at the last meeting said it should run the other way: you get a say if you're bound by the decision and have to live under it, which sounds cleaner but seems to leave the café owner out entirely, and that doesn't feel right either — he'll be staring at the shed for the next decade.

So help me out here: is there any principled way to work out who should get a vote, or is it always going to be some messy pragmatic fudge? It'd be useful if you could tell me whether either of my friends' rules is actually workable, and what happens when you push each of them hard — Sam's rule especially, since he gets quite smug about it. Oh, and please write your answer in British English — the American spellings do my head in. Ta!
