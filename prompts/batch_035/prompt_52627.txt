In a class debate about overpopulation, a classmate shut down my side by saying that Malthus was "right, just early": his prediction that population would outrun food supply only looked wrong because fossil fuels and the Green Revolution bought humanity a couple of centuries, so nothing was ever really falsified and the crunch is still coming. I can't put my finger on what's wrong with this move, but it feels like a way of never having to admit a prediction failed. I've since noticed people say the same thing about peak oil and other resource scares that never arrived on schedule. What precise claims could "right but early" actually be making in this context? That the theory issued a conditional prediction whose conditions happened not to be met? That it never committed to a date in the first place, so no missed deadline counts against it? That it described a real tendency that technology has so far outrun? That a stopped clock is eventually right about anything? For each reading, please tell me whether it's fair to Malthus's actual thesis, whether it leaves the theory falsifiable at all, and whether it would support my classmate's further claim that today's doom predictions about overpopulation deserve credence on this basis. Also be blunt if my framing is confused -- for instance, if "falsified" isn't really the right notion to apply to a two-hundred-year-old social theory, or if some readings of the slogan are perfectly legitimate and I'm wrong to smell a trick.
