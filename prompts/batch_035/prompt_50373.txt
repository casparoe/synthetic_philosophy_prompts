My roommate and a friend of his have been going at it for a week over the absent-minded driver puzzle, and on sunday they finally asked me to referee, which went badly because I couldn't decide either. Maybe you can do better.

The setup, in case you dont know it: a driver is driving home at night. He's absent-minded, so at any intersection he can't remember whether he's already passed one. There are two identical intersections on his route, and at each he can either exit or continue. Exiting at the first gets him home with utility 0, exiting at the second gets utility 4, and continuing past both gets utility 1. He'd like to exit at the second one, but since the intersections look identical and he can't recall whether he's passed one already, he can't just tell himself 'exit at the second.' The standard analysis: let p be the probability he continues at a given intersection; the expected payoff is p² + 4p(1−p), maximized at p = 2/3, for a value of 4/3. If he could remember, he'd exit at the second and get 4.

(1) My roommate says the punchline is that rationality is hostage to memory: a perfectly rational agent who is absent-minded gets 4/3, a third of what an ordinary person with a functioning memory gets, and no amount of cleverness at the wheel fixes that. So the real lesson is about precommitment — an agent like that should be willing to pay quite a lot for any device (a notebook, a string on his finger) that restores his memory, and what decision theory tells him to do 'at the moment of choice' is basically worthless in this situation.

(2) His friend thinks that's the wrong moral. Nothing is broken; the driver just has self-locating uncertainty. When he's at an intersection he doesn't know which intersection he's at, which is the same kind of problem as not knowing which day it is in Sleeping Beauty, and it has an answer: he should condition on the observation 'I am at an intersection' and act on the posterior. Decision theory handles it fine once you take the observation seriously.

(3) My roommate says he can't condition, because the relevant probability isn't available to him. The probability that he's at the second intersection is the probability that he continued at the first — which depends on the very policy he's now trying to evaluate. The 'prior' changes with his own choice, so it's circular: there's no fixed background against which to update.

(4) His friend answered that this isn't a paradox, it's just game theory. A sequence of decisions by one agent with imperfect recall is exactly a game played between his time-slices, and equilibria of games are 'circular' in the same way all the time — each player's best response depends on the other players' strategies, which depend back on his. Nobody thinks that breaks decision theory, so why would it here?

(5) My roommate wasn't satisfied. In an actual game, he says, the players are different people with different payoffs, and the probabilities live in different heads. Here there's one payoff and — on his view — one person. If every intersection is experienced by the same driver, then whose credence is 'the probability that I'm at the second intersection'? It looks like a credence with no owner, and he doesn't see how you do Bayesian conditioning with a credence that no one in particular has.

(6) His friend's last word was that maybe that's the actual lesson: questions about who, where, and when you are infect decision theory much more broadly than people admit, and the absent-minded driver is just the smallest, tidiest place where the infection becomes visible.

I honestly could not referee this. Can you go through the six moves one at a time, say for each whether it's right, mostly right, or off base (with a brief reason), and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing?
