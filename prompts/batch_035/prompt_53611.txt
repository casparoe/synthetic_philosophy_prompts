Before I crossed over into political philosophy I spent over a decade as a survey statistician at a polling agency, so sampling theory is honestly my first language. Lately I keep getting dragged into debates about citizens' assemblies and lottocracy — the idea that we should replace or at least supplement elections with randomly selected lay citizens, on the model of ancient Athens or jury service. The trouble is nobody ever puts the arguments in my terms. So: restate the case for and against sortition in the vocabulary of sampling. Treat the electorate or the citizenry as a population and the selected assembly as a sample; use the concepts I actually work with — sampling frames, coverage error, stratification, unequal selection probabilities, design effects, and the distinction between a sample that merely matches the population on observables and one that is genuinely random. Where does the lottocrats' case translate cleanly? Their complaint that elections are self-selected — that candidates and voters opt in, which in my world is textbook nonresponse bias — seems like a genuine point in my terms, and I'd like that confirmed or corrected. What has to be distorted to fit? Their talk of the assembly as a microcosm of society looks to me like a claim about margin of error that no sample of 150 satisfies, but I might be being uncharitable. And what has no expression at all in my vocabulary — the idea that representatives owe constituents accountability or a mandate, or that choosing one's rulers is a right people exercise rather than a burden imposed on them by someone else's random draw? Then give me the residue report: does what won't translate point to a gap in my vocabulary, or to something the philosophers' claim was never precise about in the first place? Keep the translation honest — don't smuggle in normative premises and present them as if they were part of the sampling framework. And please write the answer in British English.
