(1) For predictions about a particular individual — whether a patient survives five years, whether a startup fails — there's a fact of the matter about the correct reference class: roughly the narrowest class that matches every feature causally relevant to the outcome. If the patient is a 40-year-old nonsmoking woman, you predict off 40-year-old nonsmoking women, not "adults." Saying "it all depends on which reference class you pick, so there's no fact of the matter" is lazy relativism.

(2) Gott-style Copernican reasoning is basically just good induction done right. If you observe some ordinary ongoing thing at a random moment in its lifespan, you're probably not seeing it near its very beginning or its very end, so the remaining lifetime is probably within a factor of a few of the current age. IIRC Gott visited the Berlin Wall in 1969 when it was 8 years old and walked away thinking it probably wouldn't last dramatically longer than it already had, and that turned out fine — the Wall fell 20 years later, right in the expected range. But then he wrote a piece in Nature in 1993 applying the same logic to the human race and concluded we'd probably get at least a few thousand more years but not much past a few million, and that's where I think the method goes off the rails. The Wall part was sound though.

(3) The Doomsday argument fails. My birth rank — I'm somewhere in the first hundred-odd billion humans — should not lower my credence that humanity lasts millions of years. (4) Anthropic selection effects in a multiverse are legitimate and are what answers fine-tuning.
