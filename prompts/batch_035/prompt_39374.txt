Got into a huge fight with my sister at dinner about whether there's a duty to remember terrible things, and I realized mid-argument that my own gut verdicts are all over the place, so here they are. A country keeping archives and memorials of a genocide it committed: obviously right. That same country requiring 10-year-olds to visit the memorial every year: also fine, actually. A country whose textbooks quietly compress its colonial atrocities into one paragraph: wrong. A victim community holding yearly ceremonies naming their dead: right, and beautiful. A victim community teaching kids that the descendants of the perpetrators are their enemies: wrong, even though it's built on the same facts. A perpetrator's grandchild who refuses to feel guilt and just lives her life: fine by me, honestly. But a whole perpetrator generation collectively deciding the whole thing is history now after 40 years: not fine. And I can't decide about a village that wants to demolish an old camp site and build housing because the state has built a proper museum elsewhere — half of me says forgetting is happening, half of me says that's just practical land use. My tentative principle was something like: the duty to remember tracks how close the wrong was to what Margalit calls radical evil rather than ordinary wickedness, and it binds collectives but not individuals. But I'm not sure that saves the grandchild case and the history-now case at the same time, and it says nothing about the village. Can you find a principle that gets all of these, or close to it, and tell me which verdicts have to go if they can't all be saved? And tell me what the resulting principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a truth-and-reconciliation-style amnesty where the deal is full public testimony now in exchange for the record being sealed after a generation. Keep it under 350 words.
