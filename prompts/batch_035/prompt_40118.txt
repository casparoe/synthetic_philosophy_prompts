So last week I tried to call out my brother for constantly flaking on plans, and he hit me with "you're literally the king of flaking" and, annoyingly, he's not wrong, I've bailed on stuff plenty. It's been sitting with me because my gut says his flakiness doesn't automatically make my blame illegitimate, but in other situations my gut goes the other way. So I've been making a list of hypocrisy-and-blame cases and recording my snap verdicts, and I want someone to tell me if there's an actual principle behind them or if I'm just being self-serving. Here's the list:

1. My friend Priya, who is chronically, flagrantly late to everything, got genuinely angry at me for being 40 minutes late to her birthday dinner. Verdict: she had no right to be angry at me, at least not out loud. Her blame had no standing.

2. My uncle quit smoking fifteen years ago and regularly tells my cousin she should quit too, with real feeling. Verdict: he has standing. His smoking history doesn't disqualify him.

3. A coworker who gossips moderately blames another coworker whose gossiping is far worse and actually destructive. Verdict: the blame is fine, standing intact.

4. Someone who used to do the exact thing, stopped, and has apologized to the people affected, now blames a friend for still doing it. Verdict: standing, obviously.

5. Someone says to their sister: "I know I'm the worst about this, I missed dad's surgery for a work thing, but not showing up to mom's memorial really hurt her and was wrong." Verdict: standing, and I'd even say strong standing — the candor seems to add something.

6. Same situation, except the person never volunteers that they missed dad's surgery, but would freely admit it if asked, and doesn't think they were justified. Verdict: ...I keep flip flopping. Part of me says identical to case 5, part of me says something is lost when the confession doesn't happen.

7. Someone who embezzled $50k from a past employer calls out a coworker for stealing $20 from the petty cash. Verdict: no standing, worse than case 1 even.

8. Someone who has never cheated but is a workaholic who neglects their spouse blames a friend for cheating — the underlying disloyalty is arguably the same "in kind," just via a different route. Verdict: standing, and this one feels easy even though on paper it's structurally similar to case 7.

So: is there one principle here, or do I need two? The obvious candidate my brother would push is something like "you have standing only if you're not now doing the thing," but that doesn't survive case 3, and maybe not case 5. Something about severity or proportionality might handle 7, but then case 8 needs explaining, since the workaholic's neglect could be worse for the spouse than a one-night stand would've been. And whatever principle you land on, tell me straight: which of my verdicts does it have to overturn, and what does it predict for the case I keep chewing on — my mom texting-while-driving-shaming me even though she did it herself for years and only quit because of an accident scare last month? Does a threat-of-consequences-driven stop count, or does it need to be a change of judgment?

If it helps, distinguish for me between blame being unfair versus blame just being aimed by the wrong mouth, if those come apart. And please end with a last line of the form

Verdict: <the principle in a short phrase, plus the verdict number you'd overturn>

so I can actually extract your answer from the wall of text I'm expecting.
