I sit on the Heritage and Commemoration Commission of Harrow Falls, a mid-sized city, and we must decide the text for a memorial to the Eastgate Orphanage fire of 1931 before the dedication at the end of October. The case has split the commission, and I would like the written opinion I will circulate before our July meeting.

The documentary record — the coroner's inquest, two surviving newspaper accounts, and the fire department's log — holds that the alarm came in at 10:12 p.m., the first engines arrived within half an hour, and four children died. The descendants' association, drawing on oral histories collected over three generations, remembers otherwise: that the response was delayed because Eastgate was the poor immigrant ward, that the toll was eleven, and that the count was quietly lowered ahead of a bond campaign meant to restore the city's image. Our archivist complicates matters from both directions. On one hand, the records were kept by the very government the association accuses, the newspaper was owned by the mayor's family, and the death registers for Eastgate are known to be incomplete for those years — so the record's tidiness is partly an artifact of who kept it. On the other hand, the association's own traditions disagree among themselves about what made the difference that night — a delayed response or a locked stairwell door — and the figure of eleven first appears in writing only in the 1970s.

Our precedent cuts both ways: in 2016 we ruled that a bridge dedication would follow the documented record over a neighborhood's contrary tradition, and a ledger surfaced two years later that vindicated the neighborhood. Two compromise proposals are on the table: a plaque bearing the record's numbers with a QR code linking to recorded testimonies, or two panels side by side, one stating the record and one stating the memory. The association has withdrawn from the working group and says it will boycott the dedication if the plaque "corrects" them to four dead.

Please draft the memo I will circulate: a concrete recommendation, with wording the commission could actually adopt and a decision path that runs to October; the reasoning behind it; and an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the side you reject. If you believe memory and record are each entitled to their own standing rather than one trumping the other, say exactly what that amounts to in a physical memorial — what a visitor should read, and on whose authority. Write in American English.
