I'm a novelist, and I've spent the last two years writing a book in which nearly everyone blames everyone else, which has made me a connoisseur of the ways blame gets litigated rather than the blame itself. One move I keep finding, both in life and in my drafts, I cannot name properly, and it's now blocking a chapter because I can't tell whether a certain character is doing something shabby or something defensible.

The pattern runs like this. Someone wants to blame another person for a fault — lateness, a selfish act, an affair, what have you. Before delivering the blame, they first confess to the same fault in themselves, emphatically and at some length, and then proceed to the blame. The confession does not soften the blame; it seems to license it. "I'm the worst offender here, God knows I've done the same thing, and worse, so believe me when I say—" and then the harshest judgment of all follows, delivered with a kind of authority the person could not otherwise claim. The audience, disarmed by the confession, grants the blame a free passage it would never have granted an open hypocrite. I've watched a man at a dinner party spend five minutes on his own failings as a father and then demolish another guest's parenting so thoroughly that no one could object without seeming to defend neglect.

The nearest thing I know is the tu quoque move, or the charge of hypocrisy, that people raise to take away someone's standing to blame — the "look who's talking" defense. But in my pattern the accusation is never available, because the blamer got in first and made it himself. It's as if the confession is a payment made in advance that purchases the right to judge. And I notice a second, darker use of it: people who cultivate a general, ambient self-reproach — a standing habit of self-criticism about their own class, their own generation, their own vices — and who then blame members of that group with unusual savagery, apparently on the theory that no one can call them a hypocrite. The self-blame is real enough, and it never restrains the other-blame in the slightest; the two run on parallel tracks and never meet.

So my questions. Is there an established name for this — the preemptive confession that secures standing to blame? Philosophy presumably has a literature on what it takes to have standing to blame and on hypocrisy, and I'd like to know which part of it, if any, covers my pattern. Is it genuinely different from ordinary hypocrisy, or merely hypocrisy with better timing — and does it matter that the confession is sincere? Is there anything to the thought that self-blame ought to make one more rather than less hesitant to blame others, so that the confessing blamer is in some way worse than the plain hypocrite, who at least hasn't added a false display of humility to the ledger? If no term exists, coin one for me and draw its boundaries, because I need to know what to call this character, and whether to forgive her.
