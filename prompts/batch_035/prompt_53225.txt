Our formal epistemology seminar has spent like three weeks on the question of whether learning can be probabilistic and it feels completly stuck, so I want a diagnosis rather than another survey. On one side you've got the people who say all rational updating is really just conditioning on something you're certain of: if you wake up only fairly confident it rained overnight, then strictly speaking what you learned with certainty was some experiential thing, and Jeffrey conditioning is at best a shortcut for conditioning on the full state. On the other side you've got the radical probabilists going back to Jeffrey himself, saying there just needn't be any certainties at the base — probability judgments can be revised by other probability judgments and that's fine, in fact it's the honest description of how perception works. And the specific pressure points have all been on the table forever: the rigidity assumption doing hidden work in Jeffrey conditioning, the fact that it's non-commutative so the order in which you get two uncertain signals changes where you end up (which looks irrational), van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin case where the update seems to move you further than it should, and the Williamson-style push that evidence just is knowledge and so has to be certain. Everyone knows these arguments, nobody moves. My own hypothesis is that the fight isn't really about the formalism at all — both camps accept the same math — but about whether experience even comes in propositional, nameable chunks, so one side is answering a question about ideal agents and the other a question about actual cognizers without noticing. Is that right? Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two different questions under one heading? And be honest about whether anything — a theorem, an empirical result about how perception actually works, some conceptual distinction — could settle it, or whether this is one of those debates that's stalled because the question is ill-posed.
