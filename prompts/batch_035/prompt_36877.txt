Two friends of mine got into an argument at dinner last week about voting, and because there's an election coming up in my district that is expected to be reasonably close, and I'm genuinely unsure whether to bother, I'd like a referee's verdict on their exchange. I'll number the moves as they happened.

(1) Maya said voting is instrumentally irrational: the chance that a single ballot decides the outcome is astronomically small, while the costs — time off, the queue, getting registered in a new district — are certain. Multiplying a near-zero probability by even enormous stakes yields an expected benefit below the certain cost, so the expected value is negative.

(2) Dev said this argument proves too much. By the same logic no one should ever make a tiny contribution to a collective outcome: switching to a green electricity plan, cutting a little consumption for the climate, or refraining from flicking one wrapper out the car window, since a single wrapper makes no perceptible difference to the state of the roadside. He said a vote is like the wrapper: part of a pattern whose total is what matters.

(3) Maya answered that the analogy runs backwards. With the roadside, the bad thing is additive — each wrapper contributes a real, if tiny, amount to the total mess, so there is a small but nonzero effect to point to. An election is a step function: if my candidate loses by three thousand votes, my ballot did literally nothing, and even a win by one vote means the outcome would have been the same without me. For the expected value to be positive at all, there has to be a real chance of an exact or near-tie in which I'm pivotal, and in a large electorate that chance is vanishingly small.

(4) Dev conceded the step-function point but said she was ignoring a second causal channel: turnout is socially contagious. People who vote pull their family, friends, and coworkers along with them, and those people pull others in turn. So a decision to vote is not an isolated act whose effect stops at the ballot box; even on a strict causal accounting, one vote can end up generating several more, and the expected effect should be computed over that cascade, not over the single ballot.

(5) Maya replied that even granting contagion, it only helps Dev's case if the people pulled along vote the way I do; otherwise I'm mobilizing my opponents' supporters as much as my own, and the cascade could net out to nothing. And she accused him of a bait-and-switch: the original question was whether voting is rational, but his talk of "doing your share" and the wrapper example keeps smuggling in fairness and moral duty, which are not efficiency calculations, and she suspects his contagion story is an attempt to dress an ethical point up as an expected-value one.

I honestly could not tell who was winning. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right about each of the five steps, whether Maya's step-function argument really breaks the analogy with the wrapper, whether Dev's contagion channel survives her reply about whom it ends up mobilizing, and whether, once the moral reasons enter at step (5), the two of them are even arguing about the same question anymore. Finally, since this actually bears on what I do on election day, tell me plainly what you take the upshot to be for a voter in my position. As you go, please indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your adjudication that you are least sure of.
