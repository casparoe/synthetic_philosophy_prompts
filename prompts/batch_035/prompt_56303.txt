I'm putting together some material for a phil bio reading group I run (mostly grad students, a couple of synthetic biology people who always bring weird examples), and the next theme is biological function. Everyone shows up already knowing the bare etiological vs. causal-role contrast, so I don't want another "here's what each side says" summary. What I want is the spec first: a list of what any adequate theory of biological function actually has to deliver, with the motivating case behind each requirement, before we even look at the contenders.

So write me the desiderata. The obvious ones are in the mix somewhere -- it has to explain malfunction (a diseased heart still has the function of pumping even when it can't), it has to distinguish functions from mere accidental benefits (the nose holding up glasses isn't for holding up glasses), and it has to keep function talk normative in the right way, whatever that turns out to mean. But I especially want the harder cases given real weight: novel or recently modified traits with little to no selection history for what they now do, exaptation-type stories like feathers originally for thermoregulation, vestigial structures that have largely lost their original use, and lab-built organisms where a function is simply assigned by design rather than inherited through any lineage of selection. Also the swampman-style worry: an exact duplicate of a functioning organism with no evolutionary history at all, and whether intuition's verdict there is actually a datum or just a hangover from thinking about artifacts like chairs. Then there's the question of whether a theory of function needs to do double duty -- explaining what a trait is for in a whole organism AND licensing functional explanations like "the zebra stripes confuse predators" in a way that makes such explanations truth-apt. Some people treat that as one job, some as two, and I suspect that assumption is doing a lot of quiet work in the debate.

Mark which of your requirements you think are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are really just the etiological camp (or the causal-role camp) smuggling its own conclusions in as a desideratum, and flag any pair or subset you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- if that's right, the list itself is basically a result, and that's the part I most want for the discussion. No need for a full scorecard of every theory out there, just a quick pass at how a Wright/Neander-style selected effect view and a Cummins-style causal-role view do against your own list, and where each one seems to be paying a cost you'd only notice once you've written the requirements down. My own hunch, which you can push back on, is that the lab-organism requirement and the malfunction requirement pull in opposite directions, but I haven't been able to make that precise.
