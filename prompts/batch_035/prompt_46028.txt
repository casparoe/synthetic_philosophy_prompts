my housemate thinks she has cracked the paradox of tragedy and her argument is bugging me because each step looks fine but the conclusion is that nobody has ever enjoyed a sad play, which can't be right. She calls it her "there's no such thing as a good cry" argument. Here it is, numbered the way she wrote it on our whiteboard:

(1) We voluntarily watch tragedies, and rewatch them, and pay money to see new ones, so we desire the experience of watching them.

(2) A desire for an experience is a desire for it under the aspect of enjoying it — you can't desire an experience except as something you take yourself to enjoy. She says this is just what desiring an experience is, not an extra assumption.

(3) The experience of watching Othello essentially contains distress at Desdemona's death. Not distress standing next to the experience — the distress is at that, and a version of the play that didn't produce it wouldn't be the tragedy.

(4) If you enjoy an experience, you enjoy its essential components. Enjoying a whole while flatly not enjoying an essential part of it is not enjoyment of the whole, just like loving a team while hating every one of its players isn't loving the team.

(5) So we enjoy the distress at Desdemona's death.

(6) But enjoying distress at someone's death is enjoying something intrinsically bad, and that's incoherent — you can't be pleased by the very thing you are pained by.

(7) So either we don't desire the experience, against (1), or the enjoyment is incoherent, against (6). Either way nobody enjoys tragedy, and the "paradox" dissolves because there was never any phenomenon to explain.

The part that worries me is she's delighted that the argument generalizes: same reasoning shows nobody enjoys horror movies (fear is intrinsically bad), spicy food (pain), rollercoasters (terror), long-distance running (exhaustion). When an argument proves that much, I usualy figure it's broken somewhere, but I genuinely can't spot where. My gut says keep (1) and give up (4), but when I said that she asked whether I really want to claim you can enjoy a thing while failing to enjoy every essential part of it, and honestly that sounded almost as bad as her conclusion. Also possible I should be looking at (3) or (6), I realy can't tell.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when I first read it, and what does rejecting it cost you? And separately — does the argument generalize the way she wants, or is there something about tragedy specifically that makes her case look stronger there than in the spicy food case?
