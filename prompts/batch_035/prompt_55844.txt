Two of my friends (one a stats PhD student, the other doing philosophy of science, like me but further along) had a long argument at a pub about what probability claims actually mean, and I got lost halfway through. I'd love a proper referee report. Here's the exchange as best as I can reconstruct it, in order:

(1) Marta opened by saying she's a pretty unrepentant frequentist about it: a probability just IS a long-run frequency, so when someone says a coin has a 50% chance of landing heads, they're making a claim about the limiting relative frequency of heads in an indefinitely long sequence of flips. She conceded there's no such thing as the probability of a single flip, properly speaking -- single-case probabilities are just shorthand or a manner of speaking.

(2) Dev called that a reductio of her own view: insurance companies price individual houses, doctors tell individual patients their five-year risk, and a quantum physicist will happily tell you the probability that THIS atom decays in the next minute. If frequentism can't make sense of any of that, it's not an interpretation of the probability we actually use, it's a proposal to stop using probability.

(3) Marta said fine, that's what propensity theories are for -- the atom has a dispositional property, a propensity, to decay with such-and-such chance, and the long-run frequencies are how the propensity manifests. Dev pounced: this just relabels the mystery. Saying the atom "has a propensity of 0.3 to decay" is saying "it will decay with probability 0.3," which was the thing we were trying to explain. Propensity talk adds a word, not an account. Marta shot back that the same complaint applies to any fundamental disposition -- "fragile" isn't circular just because glass breaks when it's fragile; dispositions are legit science everywhere.

(4) Dev then made his positive case for subjectivism: probabilities are degrees of belief, full stop. Coherence (no Dutch book) is what pins them to the axioms, and everything else -- frequencies, physics, symmetries -- is just input to forming those degrees of belief, not part of what "probability" means. He said this is the only reading under which it even makes sense to assign probabilities to one-off events like "this unspecified coin lands heads on the next flip" or an election outcome, where there's no repeated trial and arguably no objective propensity either.

(5) Marta's reply was that this collapses probability into psychology. If probability is just belief, then when two scientists disagree about the probability of a catastrophe, there's no fact of the matter for them to be wrong about -- one of them just has different beliefs, like preferring vanilla. And yet we clearly think one of them CAN be wrong, and can be criticized for it. Dev answered that he never said all priors are equally good -- evidence, calibration, and scoring rules let you criticize beliefs without pretending there's a mind-independent number out there. Marta said if you need frequencies to check whether beliefs are any good, it's looking a lot like frequencies are doing the real work and "probability = belief" is riding along as a label.

(6) Dev's closing move: he granted that frequencies matter enormously but insisted they enter as evidence, the way observations confirm theories. Nobody says a theory IS the set of observations that confirm it, so why say a probability IS a frequency? And he noted that two agents with different evidence SHOULD have different probabilities about the same event, which frequency talk can't even express without smuggling in an observer anyway.

They left it there and asked me what I thought, and honestly I could follow each move individually but lost the thread of who's actually winning.
