Simone Weil's 1943 essay on the abolition of political parties argues that parties are essentially machines for the manufacture of collective thought and collective passion, that they are structurally committed to unlimited growth, and that this second feature inevitably subordinates truth to the party's survival. She proposes as a remedy that candidates for office bind themselves by public, revocable pledges: never to join a party, never to accept party funds, and to publish their sincere views on public questions. Parties, on her account, are not a regrettable necessity of democracy but a mechanism by which individuals surrender their private judgment — the one faculty, in her view, from which sound public deliberation can proceed.

Now consider phenomena she never lived to see: political movements that claim to supersede the party machine — Macron's En Marche, launched outside the existing French parties and absorbing them; the Five Star Movement's use of an online platform to let "members" vote directly on policy; leaderless hashtag campaigns that coalesce into electoral forces without dues, cards, or committees; candidate-selection by mass online petition. Suppose Weil could study these cases in detail. Would she conclude that they vindicate her analysis — that they are parties in her precise sense, all the more dangerous for disclaiming the label and thereby escaping the skepticism voters reserve for overt partisanship — or would she see in any of them a genuine realization of her alternative: unmediated individual judgment aggregating into collective decisions without a party apparatus manufacturing the collectivity's thoughts?

I want a definite answer, not a survey of considerations: commit to the prediction you find most probable, derived as closely as possible from the actual structure of her argument rather than from her general reputation for severity, and say specifically what it commits you to. In particular: does platform-mediated collective deliberation count, on her criteria, as the manufactured collective passion she condemns, or as something her three-proposition argument fails to capture — and if the argument fails to capture it, does that failure expose a flaw in the argument or only a limit of its intended scope? Conclude by stating what Weil would predict, on your reading, about the trajectory of at least one such movement over its first decade, and note any point where you are extrapolating beyond what her texts can actually support.
