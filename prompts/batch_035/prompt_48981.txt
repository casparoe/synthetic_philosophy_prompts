I write novels — mostly literary thrillers about con artists, so I've spent alot of time thinking about how people get played. In three weeks I'm giving a talk at a small conference on persuasion in media (half digital-ethics people who spend their days on dark patterns and targeted ads, a couple of rhetoricians, and one very committed free-speech absolutist) where I'm going to defend a thesis I half believe and am building my next novel around: that the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion doesn't run through the techniques used — unreliable narration, withholding information, sequencing emotional hits, exploiting the reader's sunk investment in a character, all of it — it runs through whether the persuasion happens inside a frame the person knowingly entered. A reader who picks up a novel knows they're signing up to be worked over; a microtargeted ad is doing the same things to someone who never consented to the game. The people who just shout "that's art, this is commerce" I can handle. The digital-ethics crowd is the problem: they'll say the frame is doing no real work, since nobody reads terms of service either, and the parasocial trust a reader extends to a narrator looks exactly like the parasocial trust influencers exploit; they'll bring up the research on narrative transportation (immersed readers change beliefs and often don't even remember the source of the change) and ask why a belief installed by a novel I engineered to install it is less manipulated than one installed by an ad — after all, my novel was targeted too, I chose the genre, the jacket, the comps precisely to reach the reader most likely to fall for it. The free-speech guy will come from the other side and say my criterion quietly brands all emotional rhetoric as manipulation the moment you find someone who "didn't knowingly enter the frame." And I suspect the rhetorician will ask what happens to my nice frame story when the person entering it is a kid, or grieving, or half-asleep, or has read every book I've ever written and thinks she knows me.

Draft me the prep sheet: the objections in rough order of how much damage they'd do to my thesis, phrased the way each camp would actually phrase them (the ethics people in their vocabulary, the rhetorician in hers), each with a reply I can actually deliver in two or three minutes, plus an honest note on where the reply is thin. Include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks — and don't make it a strawman, make it the one that genuinely keeps me up. Also, at the end of the sheet, tell me how confident you are in the whole thing and which of your replies you're least sure would survive a smart follow-up, so I know where to keep hedging in the talk itself.
