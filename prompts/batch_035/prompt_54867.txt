I'm in a reading group working through Dennett on consciousness, and one analogy keeps doing all the heavy lifting: the self as a centre of gravity. As it gets explained to us, a centre of gravity isn't a little thing tucked away inside an object -- it's an abstract point you posit because it's fantastically useful for predicting how the object will behave, and nobody goes mystical about it or thinks physics has discovered a ghostly dot. Dennett extends this to the self: the self is a centre of narrative gravity, an abstract point around which the stories a person tells about themselves (and others tell about them) organise, and it earns its keep by predicting and explaining what the person says and does. Fair enough so far. But the podcast summary most of the group listened to, and half the group itself, now use the analogy to draw a much stronger conclusion: that the self is an illusion, that there's literally nobody home, that when I say "I decided" I'm reporting a fiction like reporting that the sun rose. One member -- a physicalist who normally loves Dennett -- thinks this is a sleight of hand, because a centre of gravity is a posit about a real distribution of mass, whereas the self-posit, if it's about anything, is about patterns of talking, and it's unclear what "illusion" even means when the thing posited is made of narratives in the first place.

Can you audit the analogy for me like the group discussion is tomorrow and I want to come in with the map? Specifically: give me the correspondence table (what plays the role of the mass distribution, the physical laws, the predictions of motion, the engineering usefulness, and so on), then say where the mapping genuinely breaks -- I can already think of a few candidates (a centre of gravity can't be wrong about anything, can't be depressed, can't discover it doesn't exist, and nothing it does feeds back into the object the way my self-story changes how I behave), but I want you to sort which of those breaks actually matter for the "illusion" conclusion and which are just noise. And the key question: does the strong conclusion -- self is an illusion, nobody home -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If you think there's a better analogy in the neighbourhood (a protagonist, a legal corporation, an attractor in a dynamical system, whatever), say what it gets right that the centre-of-gravity picture gets wrong. Please write the answer in British English.
