I'm co-teaching a comparative philosophy seminar on skepticism across traditions, and next week we pair the butterfly dream passage from the Zhuangzi with Borges' short story The Circular Ruins. I'd like a reading of the story through the lens of the Zhuangzi passage and the dream-skepticism debate it has come to anchor.

Here is the setup as I see it, and where I want help. In the Zhuangzi passage, a man dreams he is a butterfly, wakes, and then asks whether he is a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. The usual Western gloss turns this into a Cartesian worry: how do I know I'm not dreaming now? But there's a competing reading of the passage on which the point is not epistemic at all — it comes in the context of the equalization of perspectives in that chapter of the text, and the closing line about the transformation of things suggests the dream state and waking state are not ranked, but treated as phases of a single ongoing process, so that there is no privileged standpoint from which one could be certified as real. Descartes needs a criterion of waking because his project depends on recovering one; the Zhuangian reading suggests the demand for such a criterion itself presupposes the fixed self that the dream calls into question. Meanwhile, Borges' story seems engineered to test both readings: the wizard dreams a son into existence over a long period of nights, worries that the son might one day discover he is an illusion, arranges for him to be protected from fire as proof of his dream-status, and finally walks into a burning temple unharmed — learning from his own fire-immunity that he too is being dreamed by someone else.

I'd like you to address, in a sustained reading rather than a list of answers: first, whether the story dramatizes the epistemic reading or the transformation reading of the Zhuangzi — is the wizard's discovery an answer to a skeptical question or a dissolution of it, and does Borges' terror at the end read as a Cartesian anxiety that Zhuangzi would refuse to feel, or as something else entirely? Second, a student in my class plans to argue that the story is closer to Neoplatonism than to Daoism, since the wizard is a member of a hierarchy of dreamers that evidently bottoms out somewhere — the fire god who arranges his protection implies a real divinity orchestrating the dream — whereas Zhuangzi's transformation of things suggests no such base of the regress. Is that student right that the story needs a foundation, or does the detail of the fire-god's protection cut the other way, marking the wizard off as a dream only from a standpoint that the story itself withholds from us? Third, there is an important asymmetry I want you to weigh: the wizard's proof that he is a dream is empirical — the fire does not burn him — whereas Zhuangzi's conclusion is precisely that no empirical test could settle the matter, since any test would be administered within one of the two states. Does the story, by giving its protagonist a test, quietly take a side the Zhuangzi leaves open, or is the fire scene better read as showing the test's emptiness, since the wizard learns he is dreamed only in the moment when being dreamed ceases to matter to him?

Please be candid about where the reading strains: point out the scenes and structural choices that resist a Zhuangian framing rather than smoothing them over, and close by saying whether you think the story adds anything to the dream-skepticism debate that the standard arguments — Descartes' demon, say, or the more modern point that no experience can certify its own veridicality — do not already contain. Assume familiarity with the story's plot and major scenes; do not quote from either text. Aim for something I could circulate to graduate students as a model of careful cross-traditional work.
