I'm a PhD student in library and information science, writing a dissertation on appraisal — the decisions archivists make about which records to keep permanently and which to let expire. The pattern I keep running into, in state archives and university special collections alike, is roughly this: records of governments, corporations, and prominent individuals are scheduled for permanent retention more or less because they exist and their creators had power; the everyday papers of ordinary people — families, workers, small organizations — are mostly left to perish or to survive by accident; and when archivists do set out to document marginalized communities, they typically do so retroactively, conducting oral history interviews decades after the fact rather than collecting communities' own records as they were made. There's a literature arguing that archives are active shapers of social memory rather than neutral storehouses — Trouillot's work on silences in the historical record is the usual touchstone — and I'm comfortable with that framing. What I can't find is an analysis at the level of the commitments the practice itself presupposes.

Please respond as a philosopher of history and archival theory, and think carefully before answering — ideally start with some brainstorming that lays out candidate theories the practice could be resting on. What view of what archives are for does this appraisal pattern presuppose? The candidates I can see are something like: that the archive's function is the accountability of the powerful, so we keep their records in order to police them; that historical value attaches to whatever shaped events, so we keep the winners' papers as causes; that evidence of the powerful is systematically scarce while traces of the powerless are abundant, so scarcity alone justifies the asymmetry; or that a record only counts as archival when its creator is institutional. Distinguish which of these the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits well with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined — several rival theories of historical value could license the same retention schedules, and I'd like to know whether the practice could survive a shift from the accountability view to something like a representativeness view without becoming a different institution altogether.

Then tell me what would actually have to change — in retention schedules, in what counts as a record, in the habit of documenting communities after the fact through interviews — if the presupposed view were replaced by the claim that archives should represent society rather than monitor power. I'm especially interested in the retroactive oral history pattern: is it a natural consequence of the presupposed view, or a symptom that the view is already breaking down under pressure it can't accommodate?
