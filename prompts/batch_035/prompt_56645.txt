I've been reading about pain asymbolia and it has unsettled my working assumption that pain is bad in virtue of how it feels. As I understand the syndrome, certain patients with (I believe) lesions in or near the anterior cingulate cortex will report that a stimulus is painful, that it is sharp or burning, even that it is intense, yet they show no distress, no withdrawal, no wish for it to stop, and sometimes seem amused by it. My first thought was that nothing bad is happening to these people, and that this shows the badness of pain lives not in the sensation but in the aversion to it. But when I line up my gut verdicts on a range of cases, they don't quite arrange themselves around that single idea, and I'd like help finding the principle, if there is one.

Here are my verdicts. The asymbolic patient during a painful procedure: reporting the pain as intense but shrugging, joking, not wanting it stopped — I say nothing bad, or almost nothing, is happening to her. A patient on a pain-numbing drug who still rates her pain a seven out of ten but only mildly minds it: something bad is happening, a little. A patient undergoing surgery under inadequate anesthesia, who later remembers nothing and who, plausibly, never minded it in any ordinary sense: something very bad happened. A patient with congenital insensitivity to pain who accumulates burns, fractures, and a chewed-up tongue without ever feeling anything: her condition is awful, but it seems to me the badness is in the damage and the danger, not in any missing feeling. A friend who meditates and reports genuinely intense pain which she notices fully and dislikes only faintly: I want to say that is quite bad — worse than the drug patient — even though the dislike is similar. And a mild ache that sends someone into panic and misery: bad, perhaps worse overall than the ache itself warrants, and I'm inclined to think the suffering is located in the panic rather than the ache. Finally, a masochist's welcomed pain: not bad at all, as far as I can tell.

What I can't see through is the contrast between the asymbolic case (intense pain, no dislike, nothing bad) and the meditation case (intense pain, faint dislike, quite bad), and even more the contrast with the unremembered surgical pain (intense pain, no dislike, very bad). If badness tracks dislike, the asymbolic and the surgical patient come out the same, and I don't think they do. If badness tracks the felt intensity, the asymbolic and the meditator come out the same, and I don't think they do either. So: is there one principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts — perhaps something that appeals to whether the capacity for aversion is intact, or whether the pain would be disliked by the person under reflection, or some distinction I haven't found between pain that fails to register as bad and badness that fails to be registered? Please state the principle (or two or three rival candidates) precisely, show which clause earns its keep on which of my cases, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, a person with advanced dementia in pain, or an animal in pain — and be honest about which of my verdicts your best principle has to overturn. I'd also be glad to know which side of the debate this leaves me on: the view that the badness is in the feel itself, or the view that it is in the aversive attitude, or something that splits the difference.
