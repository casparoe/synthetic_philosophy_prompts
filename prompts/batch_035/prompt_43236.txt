I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've ended up in too many arguments lately about whether insects count morally, whether a thermostat could ever count, and what either answer would mean for the systems my colleagues and I build. The pattern in these arguments is always the same: someone points at one vivid case, someone else points at another, and nothing generalizes. Before I engage again, I want the specification first -- think of it as writing the acceptance tests before choosing an implementation. Please write the desiderata a theory of moral status has to meet, given the working assumption that sentience comes in degrees rather than as a binary property: the cases such a theory must get right (from insects on up, and from thermostats on down), the distinctions it cannot be allowed to collapse (e.g., between whether a being has any moral status at all and how much it has; between low status and our uncertainty about status), and the results from neuroscience and animal behavior it cannot flatly contradict -- with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. Please also flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the popular candidate theories. Two specific things I want addressed as part of the spec: whether my instinct is right that the whole problem resembles thresholding in signal processing -- a continuous quantity forced through a binary cut, where the real questions are where the cut lands and whether it should exist at all -- or whether that analogy is a category error; and what the theory must say about aggregation, since a status that comes in tiny degrees only matters practically if it can be summed across, say, ten to the eighteenth insects. I'd rather you not include a scorecard of named theories -- the criteria are what I want to think against.
